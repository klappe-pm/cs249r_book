1.  **A team scales a well-tuned training run from 8 accelerators to 25,000. Per-device arithmetic throughput stays constant, yet end-to-end wall-clock time per step roughly doubles, and the job now experiences hardware-induced interruptions every few hours. Applying the scale-moment analysis, which explanation is most consistent with the evidence?**

    1.  The arithmetic units on each accelerator are the bottleneck, so the team should enable lower precision to double local matrix throughput.
    2.  Distribution has exposed additional fleet costs and routine component failures; communication, straggler waiting, and recovery are candidate causes of the longer steps, requiring correlated measurements.
    3.  The kernel compiler is failing to fuse operations at fleet scale, increasing redundant high-bandwidth memory traffic on each device.
    4.  The input data loader has become single-threaded, starving all 25,000 GPUs uniformly.

    *Answer*: The correct answer is B. Constant per-device arithmetic throughput does not explain the longer end-to-end step or establish that local throughput is high. Correlated kernel, collective, link-utilization, barrier, and recovery measurements are needed to distinguish exposed transfers from delayed participants or other costs; cross-cut saturation together with exposed collective time would support a bisection-bandwidth diagnosis. Under an independent all-required-component failure model, increasing device count also increases job interruption frequency. The observations justify fleet-level investigation, not a specific compiler, input-loader, or arithmetic diagnosis.

    *Learning Objective*: Distinguish evidence of fleet-scale execution costs and failure exposure from a confirmed diagnosis of their mechanisms.

2.  **Meta reported 419 unexpected interruptions during a 54-day snapshot of Llama 3 training on 16,384 H100 GPUs, averaging one interruption about every three hours. Explain why this cadence motivates automated recovery rather than manual restart after every interruption.**

    *Answer*: A recurring manual response would add recovery delay to hundreds of interruptions, wasting substantial accelerator time. The interruptions included hardware, software, network, and host issues, so improving one component cannot address every cause. Automated diagnosis, checkpointing, and restart limit the progress lost at each interruption. The observed cadence motivates recovery engineering but does not establish a universal sub-minute recovery requirement.

    *Learning Objective*: Explain why fleet-scale failure cadence forces automated recovery and fast checkpointing rather than reliance on individual component reliability.

3.  **True or False: If individual data center accelerators achieve a 99.9% annual uptime reliability, a synchronous training job requiring 10,000 such accelerators simultaneously will experience hardware failures only a few times per year.**

    *Answer*: False. Availability is a fraction of time, not a failure count, so 99.9% uptime alone cannot determine annual interruption frequency. Under independent component failures, simultaneous availability of all required devices is $(0.999)^{10{,}000} \approx 4.5 \times 10^{-5}$. Estimating interruption cadence additionally requires a failure-rate model.

    *Learning Objective*: Critique the misconception that high individual component reliability guarantees low interruption rates in large synchronous clusters.

4.  **A frontier model training run utilizes 25,000 independent GPUs, where each GPU has a modeled mean time to failure (MTTF) of 50,000 hours. Under an exponential independent failure model, what is the expected system MTBF, and approximately how many job interruptions will occur per 24-hour day?**

    1.  System MTBF is 2.0 hours, resulting in approximately 12 interruptions per day.
    2.  System MTBF is 50 hours, resulting in approximately 0.48 interruptions per day.
    3.  System MTBF is 50,000 hours, resulting in approximately 0.00048 interruptions per day.
    4.  System MTBF is 24 hours, resulting in exactly 1 interruption per day.

    *Answer*: The correct answer is A. For independent components, the system mean time between failures (MTBF) is the per-component mean time to failure (MTTF) divided by the component count, so 50,000 hours / 25,000 = 2.0 hours. Over a 24-hour day, the expected number of cluster interruptions is $24\text{ hours} / 2.0\text{ hours} = 12\text{ failures per day}$. Dividing by 1,000 instead of 25,000 incorrectly yields 50 hours, assuming system MTBF equals component MTBF ignores the fact that any single device failure halts the unreplicated run, and assuming exactly 1 failure per day contradicts the underlying component failure rate.

    *Learning Objective*: Calculate cluster-level MTBF and daily interruption frequency from per-component reliability and fleet size.

5.  **Order the following landmark training milestones by the number of accelerators utilized during training, from smallest to largest: (1) PaLM (6,144 TPUs), (2) AlexNet (2 GPUs), (3) Llama 3 (16,384 GPUs), (4) BERT-Large (64 TPUs), (5) Megatron-LM (512 GPUs).**

    *Answer*: The correct order is: (2) AlexNet (2 GPUs), (4) BERT-Large (64 TPUs), (5) Megatron-LM (512 GPUs), (1) PaLM (6,144 TPUs), (3) Llama 3 (16,384 GPUs). AlexNet (2012) trained on 2 GTX 580 GPUs; BERT-Large (2018) scaled to 64 TPU v2 chips; Megatron-LM (2019) demonstrated model parallelism on 512 V100 GPUs; PaLM (2022) scaled across two pods totaling 6,144 TPU v4 chips; and Llama 3 (2024) scaled to 16,384 H100 GPUs across an InfiniBand fabric. Reversing PaLM and Llama 3 or placing BERT after Megatron-LM violates the verified historical accelerator scaling trajectory.

    *Learning Objective*: Order landmark model training runs by their accelerator fleet scale to trace the exponential growth of ML clusters.

1.  **Which responsibility assignment matches the fleet stack described in this section, while retaining ML frameworks and applications as parts of the system?**

    1.  Infrastructure handles request admission; Distribution sets fairness policy; Serving supplies network links; Governance runs gradient collectives.
    2.  Infrastructure places training jobs; Distribution cools accelerators; Serving defines privacy requirements; Governance answers inference requests.
    3.  Infrastructure supplies accelerators and fabrics; Distribution handles collectives, job placement, and recovery; Serving handles requests and deployment; Governance sets security and responsible-operation requirements.
    4.  Infrastructure supplies accelerators and fabrics; Distribution handles collectives; Serving owns training-job scheduling and recovery; Governance replaces applications.

    *Answer*: The correct answer is C. The fleet stack groups eight rows into four bands. Infrastructure supplies the physical substrate; Distribution includes distributed execution and Reliability & Control, where training-job scheduling and recovery live; Serving adds request handling and deployment pipelines; Governance sets assurance and responsible-operation requirements. ML frameworks still execute models and applications still use them. The upper bands add responsibilities rather than replace frameworks or applications. The other assignments confuse physical resources, distributed control, request serving, and policy.

    *Learning Objective*: Locate physical, distributed-execution, serving, and governance responsibilities in the fleet stack without treating its bands as replacements for frameworks and applications.

2.  **Contrast the impact of a 10% performance slowdown (straggler) on a single worker node in a 1,000-worker MapReduce job versus a 1,000-accelerator synchronous ML training job. Explain why the two architectures respond so differently.**

    *Answer*: A slow MapReduce task can delay downstream reduction and overall job completion; its effect is not its slowdown divided by the worker count. Other independent tasks can nevertheless finish, and slow work can be retried or replicated. In synchronous training, a persistent slow worker can delay every shared update. If that worker determines each step’s critical path, its 10% slowdown can approach a 10% step-time penalty across the job.

    *Learning Objective*: Analyze how synchronous barrier coupling makes fleet-scale ML training hypersensitive to tail worker latency compared to independent-task distributed systems.

3.  **True or False: Because MapReduce and synchronous distributed ML both distribute data across thousands of nodes, an ML training cluster can adopt MapReduce’s straggler-mitigation technique of simply re-executing lagging tasks in parallel on spare nodes without pausing other workers.**

    *Answer*: False. MapReduce workers execute stateless, independent partitions that produce final outputs independently. Synchronous ML training maintains shared state (model weights) updated synchronously across all ranks via an AllReduce barrier every step. Re-executing an iteration on a spare node while other workers continue would violate synchronization invariants, causing model weights to diverge or forcing all workers to stall until the replacement finishes the collective exchange.

    *Learning Objective*: Explain why independent-task fault tolerance strategies cannot directly apply to barrier-synchronized ML fleets.

4.  **Under the Warehouse-Scale Computer (WSC) perspective required for the ML fleet, the data center is treated as the computer: the orchestrator acts as the operating system, distributed storage acts as the local disk, and the network fabric acts as the \_\_\_\_.**

    *Answer*: system bus. In a warehouse-scale computer, high-bandwidth interconnect fabrics (such as InfiniBand or NVLink networks) replace the local motherboard bus, transporting gradient and activation tensors between distributed compute units.

    *Learning Objective*: Infer the WSC system-bus architectural analogy for datacenter network fabrics in ML fleets.

5.  **When scaling an autoregressive model from a single GPU to a 10,000-accelerator cluster, an engineer observes that the local CUDA arithmetic kernels execute without modification. Why does distributed execution nonetheless introduce failure modes that never occur on a single machine?**

    1.  Accelerators in large clusters automatically switch to lower-clock power states that disable floating-point exception traps.
    2.  Operating systems disable host-to-device PCIe DMA on multi-node clusters, forcing all memory through unbuffered network sockets.
    3.  Multi-GPU compilers alter the underlying mathematical definitions of GEMM operations to save bisection bandwidth.
    4.  Distributed coupling introduces network partitions that cause state divergence, stragglers that stall global synchronization barriers, and collective communication deadlocks.

    *Answer*: The correct answer is D. Distributed coupling introduces unreachable peers, stragglers, and mismatched participation in collective operations. These can stall progress or require coordinated recovery even when local arithmetic is healthy. Packet loss alone need not deadlock a reliable transport, and a partition does not automatically cause divergent updates in a system that halts safely. The other choices invent changes to local arithmetic or PCIe behavior.

    *Learning Objective*: Identify emergent failure modes in ML fleets that arise from distributed coordination and network coupling rather than local kernel execution.

6.  **A performance engineer profiles two distributed training jobs running on the same 1,024-GPU cluster connected by 200 Gb/s InfiniBand. Workload Alpha transfers 10 MB of gradient data per step while executing $10^{14}$ FLOPs locally per GPU. Workload Beta transfers 1 GB of gradient data per step while executing $10^{13}$ FLOPs locally per GPU. Using the communication intensity (${\text{CI}}$) ratio, how will these workloads respond as additional GPU nodes are added to the cluster?**

    1.  Workload Alpha will scale poorly because its low CI indicates the GPU compute units are starving for network data.
    2.  Workload Beta has 1,000 times higher network bytes per local FLOP and is more exposed to communication limits; actual scaling also depends on effective bandwidth and partitioning.
    3.  Both workloads will achieve identical linear scaling because CI measures single-device memory bandwidth rather than inter-node network traffic.
    4.  Workload Beta will scale more efficiently because higher network byte volume improves the link utilization of optical InfiniBand transceivers.

    *Answer*: The correct answer is B. Alpha’s communication intensity is $10^{-7}$ bytes/FLOP and Beta’s is $10^{-4}$ bytes/FLOP. Beta therefore imposes 1,000 times more network demand per unit of local work. This indicates greater exposure to communication costs, not a guaranteed saturation point: compute throughput, effective bandwidth, partitioning, and overlap determine the boundary. CI concerns network traffic, not local memory traffic.

    *Learning Objective*: Calculate and compare communication intensity (${\text{CI}}$) ratios to predict whether distributed workloads will scale or hit network bandwidth limits.

1.  **A project team observes a validation loss decrease after doubling its training compute budget. Under the fitted power law ${\mathcal{L}}({X}) = {A_{\mathcal{L}}} {X}^{-{\alpha_{\text{scale}}}} + {C_{\mathcal{L}}}$, with positive amplitude and exponent, what follows for another doubling within the same regime?**

    1.  The next doubling must yield the same absolute loss decrease because scaling is linear in arithmetic space.
    2.  The next doubling yields a smaller absolute loss decrease; a smaller percentage decrease additionally depends on the loss floor.
    3.  The next doubling immediately prevents any measurable loss reduction.
    4.  The next doubling yields twice the absolute loss decrease because the scaling exponent compounds.

    *Answer*: The correct answer is B. Each doubling multiplies loss above the floor by the same factor, so the absolute decrease shrinks. With a zero floor, the percentage decrease remains constant; a positive floor makes the percentage decrease shrink too. Constant absolute improvements would imply a different relationship.

    *Learning Objective*: Interpret power-law scaling equations to predict diminishing returns on loss reduction as compute budgets expand.

2.  **An organization has a 512-GPU cluster for two weeks and a domain corpus of 5 billion tokens. What evidence is needed to choose between a larger model trained for fewer steps and a smaller model trained over repeated epochs?**

    *Answer*: The data budget alone does not determine the better allocation. Compare training and held-out validation curves across feasible model sizes and token-repetition schedules under the same compute budget. Additional capacity helps only if validation performance improves; repeated epochs may also remain useful but can show diminishing gains or memorization. Choose the allocation supported by those measurements and check that its measured execution cost fits the two-week window.

    *Learning Objective*: Formulate a training strategy for the data-limited scaling regime by trading model capacity against token repetition.

3.  **True or False: Empirical scaling laws show that model loss is determined solely by the total raw FLOP budget expended during training, regardless of whether those FLOPs are allocated to model parameter count or training token volume.**

    *Answer*: False. Chinchilla compute-optimal scaling (Hoffmann et al., 2022) found within its studied regime that model loss depends critically on balanced co-scaling of parameters and tokens (${D} \approx 20 {P}$). Allocating a fixed FLOP budget to an excessively large model on too few tokens (as in GPT-3) or an undersized model on too many tokens results in suboptimal validation loss compared to the compute-optimal balance point on the IsoFLOP frontier.

    *Learning Objective*: Critique the misconception that compute budget alone guarantees optimal loss irrespective of the token-to-parameter allocation ratio.

4.  **According to the Chinchilla compute-optimal scaling laws (Hoffmann et al., 2022), training tokens and model parameters should scale approximately proportionally at a ratio of roughly 20 tokens per parameter. If a team designs a compute-optimal 70-billion-parameter model, what training dataset size is required, and what was the historical scaling imbalance of earlier models such as GPT-3 (175B parameters on 300B tokens)?**

    1.  350 billion tokens; earlier models like GPT-3 were overtrained because token counts exceeded parameter counts.
    2.  70 billion tokens; earlier models like GPT-3 were compute-optimal but lacked sufficient inference context length.
    3.  14 trillion tokens; earlier models like GPT-3 were severely data-saturated and suffered from parameter memorization.
    4.  1.4 trillion tokens; earlier models like GPT-3 were undertrained relative to their parameter size due to training on only ~1.7 tokens per parameter.

    *Answer*: The correct answer is D. Chinchilla compute-optimal scaling establishes that dataset size ${D}$ and parameter count ${P}$ should satisfy ${D} \approx 20 \times {P}$. For a 70B parameter model, the optimal dataset size is $70 \times 10^9 \times 20 = 1.4 \times 10^{12}$ tokens (1.4 trillion tokens). Earlier models like GPT-3 (175B parameters on 300B tokens, a ratio of ~1.7:1) were substantially undertrained; allocating that same compute budget to a smaller model trained on more tokens would have achieved superior loss with lower inference serving costs. Proposing 350B tokens uses an incorrect 5:1 ratio, 70B tokens assumes a 1:1 ratio, and 14T tokens overstates the requirement by an order of magnitude.

    *Learning Objective*: Calculate compute-optimal token-to-parameter allocations using the Chinchilla ratio and diagnose historical undertraining.

5.  **In compute-optimal language model training, the Chinchilla scaling law establishes that for a balanced allocation of compute, the training dataset size in tokens and the model parameter count should scale proportionally, targeting a ratio of approximately \_\_\_\_ training tokens per parameter.**

    *Answer*: 20. Hoffmann et al. (2022) demonstrated that optimal training loss for a given compute budget requires roughly 20 tokens per parameter, showing that models like GPT-3 were significantly undertrained.

    *Learning Objective*: Identify the Chinchilla token-to-parameter ratio for compute-optimal language model pretraining.

6.  **Explain why extrapolating training progress from a 10B-parameter single-node run to a 100B-parameter distributed run using a simple log-log power-law curve can result in multi-million-dollar budget overruns. What systems factor does naive scaling-law extrapolation omit?**

    *Answer*: A loss scaling law relates useful training work to model quality; it does not predict how peak hardware throughput becomes wall-clock progress. Communication, synchronization, memory limits, and overlap can change effective utilization when the workload is distributed. A plan must measure these costs on the intended partition and fabric. Otherwise, dividing a useful-FLOP estimate by advertised throughput can substantially underestimate the hardware time and budget needed to reach the target loss.

    *Learning Objective*: Analyze how distributed efficiency degradation at fleet scale causes real training progress to diverge from nominal scaling-law predictions.

1.  **A data center operator claims that upgrading per-node server availability from 99.9% (‘three nines’) to 99.99% (‘four nines’) makes an unreplicated 10,000-GPU training cluster as reliable as a small research workstation. Applying the reliability gap equation, in which all-node availability ${A_{\text{all}}}$ is the per-node availability ${A_{\text{component}}}$ raised to the number of required nodes, why is this claim invalid?**

    1.  The upgrade has zero effect because cluster availability depends exclusively on network switch packet drop rates rather than server uptime.
    2.  The upgrade increases simultaneous all-node availability to 99.99%, but software bugs in PyTorch cause the remaining downtime.
    3.  The upgrade increases simultaneous all-node availability from near zero ($(0.999)^{10{,}000} \approx 4.5 \times 10^{-5}$) to approximately 36.8% ($(0.9999)^{10{,}000} \approx 0.368$), meaning the cluster is still in a degraded or failed state over 63% of the time.
    4.  The upgrade reduces cluster MTBF because four-nines hardware operates at higher thermal thresholds that accelerate silent data corruption.

    *Answer*: The correct answer is C. Under independent component failures, the probability ${A_{\text{all}}}$ that every required node is operational simultaneously is the per-node availability ${A_{\text{component}}}$ raised to the number of required nodes. For 10,000 nodes and ${A_{\text{component}}} = 0.999$, ${A_{\text{all}}} \approx 4.5 \times 10^{-5}$ (virtually zero). Increasing individual availability to $0.9999$ brings simultaneous cluster availability to $(0.9999)^{10{,}000} \approx 0.368$ (36.8%). While this is a substantial improvement, the unreplicated cluster is still unavailable 63.2% of the time, proving that high component reliability cannot eliminate the need for automated checkpointing and fault tolerance. Claiming zero effect ignores component reliability math, asserting 99.99% cluster availability ignores the 10,000-node exponent, and suggesting four-nines hardware lowers MTBF contradicts standard reliability definitions.

    *Learning Objective*: Apply the multiplicative reliability gap equation to quantify simultaneous availability in large-scale accelerator clusters.

2.  **Using the CAP theorem (Consistency, Availability, Partition Tolerance) as an analogy for distributed ML training, explain how a synchronous AllReduce training system and an asynchronous parameter-server system make opposing trade-offs during a network partition.**

    *Answer*: Synchronous training can halt when a required worker is unreachable because the shared update needs every required contribution. An asynchronous design may let reachable workers continue against available parameter state while tolerating stale updates. This illustrates a progress-versus-freshness trade-off, but gradient freshness is not CAP’s atomic consistency property. Availability during a partition still depends on which workers and state replicas remain reachable and on the system’s protocol.

    *Learning Objective*: Apply the CAP theorem framework to evaluate the consistency-availability trade-off between synchronous and asynchronous distributed training.

3.  **True or False: In the CAP theorem as applied to physical distributed systems, Partition Tolerance is an optional configuration setting that network engineers can choose to disable in high-end data centers to achieve simultaneous atomic Consistency and Availability.**

    *Answer*: False. Partition Tolerance is not an optional architectural choice or software toggle; it is a physical reality of distributed infrastructure where network cables, transceivers, switches, or nodes can fail or experience delays. Because partitions will inevitably occur in any physical network at scale, a distributed system can only choose how to respond when a partition happens: by halting to preserve Consistency (CP) or continuing with stale state to preserve Availability (AP).

    *Learning Objective*: Critique the misconception that Partition Tolerance is an optional system parameter rather than an unavoidable physical constraint.

1.  **An engineering team instruments a 2,048-GPU training cluster and breaks down the 1.5-second step time: 0.9 s is spent on GPU matrix multiplications, 0.4 s is spent in NCCL ring transfers across the InfiniBand fabric, and 0.2 s is spent waiting at the barrier for stragglers and fault-heartbeat checks. How does this step time map onto the C$^3$ taxonomy?**

    1.  0.9 s Compute (${C}_1$), 0.6 s Communication (${C}_2$), 0 s Coordination (${C}_3$)
    2.  1.3 s Compute (${C}_1$), 0.2 s Communication (${C}_2$), 0 s Coordination (${C}_3$)
    3.  0.9 s Coordination (${C}_3$), 0.4 s Communication (${C}_2$), 0.2 s Compute (${C}_1$)
    4.  0.9 s Compute (${C}_1$), 0.4 s Communication (${C}_2$), 0.2 s Coordination (${C}_3$)

    *Answer*: The correct answer is D. In the C$^3$ taxonomy: Compute (${C}_1$, the math) is the local execution of matrix math on accelerators (0.9 s); Communication (${C}_2$, the wire) is data transfer across network links (0.4 s for NCCL transfers); and Coordination (${C}_3$, the logic) is state synchronization, barrier waits, straggler handling, and fault checks (0.2 s). Conflating barrier waiting with network transfer obscures the root cause of idle time, grouping matrix math with communication misattributes silicon execution, and reversing Compute with Coordination misclassifies the workload dimensions.

    *Learning Objective*: Classify distributed step-time profiling measurements into the Compute, Communication, and Coordination dimensions of the C$^3$ taxonomy.

2.  **The chapter describes D·A·M (Data, Algorithm, Machine) as the nouns of the workload and C$^3$ (Compute, Communication, Coordination) as the verbs of the fleet. Explain how distributing an Algorithm across multiple nodes gives rise to Communication (${C}_2$), and how distributing Data across multiple nodes gives rise to Coordination (${C}_3$).**

    *Answer*: When an Algorithm is partitioned across nodes (e.g., tensor parallelism or pipeline parallelism), intermediate activations and gradients must be exchanged across the network fabric between forward and backward operations, directly generating Communication (${C}_2$, data on the wire). When Data is distributed across nodes (e.g., distributed data parallelism), the fleet must ensure deterministic mini-batch sampling without duplicate reads, track global dataset pointers, and synchronize gradient aggregation barriers and checkpoint state across all worker ranks, directly generating Coordination (${C}_3$, the logic of distributed state management).

    *Learning Objective*: Explain the projection of single-node D·A·M workload components onto fleet-scale C$^3$ execution costs.

3.  **A team profiles a distributed training step and measures ${T_{\text{compute}}}/{N} = 0.8\text{ s}$, ${T_{\text{comm}}}({N}) = 1.0\text{ s}$, ${T_{\text{sync}}}({N}) = 0.2\text{ s}$, with zero communication overlap (${T_{\text{overlap}}} = 0$). The compute-time fraction is ${f_{\text{compute}}} = 0.8 / 2.0 = 0.40$. Which engineering intervention is most directly indicated by the Fleet Law as a diagnostic instrument?**

    1.  Replace existing GPUs with next-generation accelerators that deliver $2\times$ higher peak FLOP/s, since local compute is the primary path to speedup.
    2.  Investigate feasible gradient AllReduce overlap with the backward pass (${T_{\text{overlap}}}$) and whether fabric bandwidth limits the dominant ${T_{\text{comm}}}$ term before choosing an intervention.
    3.  Increase the number of GPU worker nodes ${N}$, which will proportionally reduce both ${T_{\text{comm}}}$ and ${T_{\text{sync}}}$.
    4.  Switch to a fully asynchronous parameter server architecture, since eliminating ${T_{\text{sync}}}$ will automatically cut ${T_{\text{comm}}}$ to zero.

    *Answer*: The correct answer is B. In the Fleet Law (${T_{\text{step}}} = {T_{\text{compute}}}/{N} + {T_{\text{comm}}} + {T_{\text{sync}}} - {T_{\text{overlap}}}$), communication (${T_{\text{comm}}} = 1.0\text{ s}$) represents 50% of the total 2.0 s step time, while ${f_{\text{compute}}} = 0.40$ identifies the modeled compute share, not measured accelerator idle time. Investigating fabric limits or feasible overlap with backward-pass compute (${T_{\text{overlap}}}$) targets the largest measured term; an upgrade still requires evidence that available bandwidth is the limit. Buying faster GPUs would only cut the 0.8 s compute term (e.g. to 0.4 s), reducing total step time to 1.6 s while lowering ${f_{\text{compute}}}$ to $0.4 / 1.6 = 0.25$. The smaller compute fraction does not mean the faster configuration makes less progress. Increasing node count ${N}$ typically increases ${T_{\text{comm}}}$ and ${T_{\text{sync}}}$ rather than reducing them, and asynchronous updates do not eliminate network data transfer.

    *Learning Objective*: Apply the Fleet Law diagnostically to select system optimizations targeting the dominant step-time component.

4.  **Order the following steps in the execution of a simplified, non-overlapped synchronous data-parallel training step with an explicit pre-collective barrier from first to last: (1) Execute forward pass matrix math locally, (2) Apply updated weights using optimizer step, (3) Synchronize gradients across all workers via AllReduce, (4) Execute backward pass and compute local parameter gradients, (5) Wait at barrier for all ranks to complete local computation.**

    *Answer*: The correct order is: (1) Execute forward pass matrix math locally, (4) Execute backward pass and compute local parameter gradients, (5) Wait at barrier for all ranks to complete local computation, (3) Synchronize gradients across all workers via AllReduce, (2) Apply updated weights using optimizer step. The step begins with local forward arithmetic, followed by backward gradient computation; workers then reach the synchronization barrier where gradients are communicated across all nodes via AllReduce; once collective reduction finishes, each worker updates its local model weights with the optimizer step. Reversing forward and backward passes or attempting to apply weight updates before AllReduce gradient synchronization violates optimization logic.

    *Learning Objective*: Order the operational phases of a synchronous distributed training step from forward execution to optimizer weight update.

5.  **Under the Fleet Law decomposition (${T_{\text{step}}}({N}) = {T_{\text{compute}}}/{N} + {T_{\text{comm}}}({N}) + {T_{\text{sync}}}({N}) - {T_{\text{overlap}}}$), a team training on 1,024 GPUs uses a hypothetical single-device compute baseline ${T_{\text{compute}}} = 1{,}024\text{ s}$, raw network communication time ${T_{\text{comm}}} = 0.8\text{ s}$, barrier synchronization time ${T_{\text{sync}}} = 0.2\text{ s}$, and an actual measured step time of ${T_{\text{step}}} = 1.6\text{ s}$. What is the value of ${T_{\text{overlap}}}$, and what does it signify?**

    1.  ${T_{\text{overlap}}} = 0.4\text{ s}$; it represents the communication time successfully hidden behind concurrent backward-pass compute.
    2.  ${T_{\text{overlap}}} = 1.0\text{ s}$; it represents the total exposed network stall time.
    3.  ${T_{\text{overlap}}} = 0.2\text{ s}$; it represents the time spent recovering from straggler delays.
    4.  ${T_{\text{overlap}}} = 0\text{ s}$; because communication and computation cannot execute concurrently on GPU architectures.

    *Answer*: The correct answer is A. Substituting into the Fleet Law: ${T_{\text{step}}} = ({T_{\text{compute}}} / {N}) + {T_{\text{comm}}} + {T_{\text{sync}}} - {T_{\text{overlap}}} \implies 1.6\text{ s} = (1{,}024 / 1{,}024) + 0.8 + 0.2 - {T_{\text{overlap}}} \implies 1.6 = 1.0 + 1.0 - {T_{\text{overlap}}} \implies 1.6 = 2.0 - {T_{\text{overlap}}} \implies {T_{\text{overlap}}} = 0.4\text{ s}$. This term quantifies the 0.4 seconds of gradient communication that ran concurrently with local tensor arithmetic, reducing total exposed step overhead. Conflating overlap with total communication time, confusing it with straggler recovery time, or claiming overlap is impossible on modern GPUs contradicts basic asynchronous CUDA stream execution.

    *Learning Objective*: Calculate communication overlap (${T_{\text{overlap}}}$) using the Fleet Law and explain its physical significance in distributed step efficiency.

6.  **True or False: According to the displacement-of-overhead heuristic, switching from synchronous training to asynchronous parameter updates eliminates coordination overhead from the system entirely.**

    *Answer*: False. Displacement of overhead is a diagnostic heuristic, not a conservation law. Asynchronous updates can reduce barrier waiting but introduce stale gradients and may require more optimization steps. The net benefit must be measured as useful progress to a target quality; eliminating one wait does not establish that all coordination costs disappear.

    *Learning Objective*: Apply the displacement-of-overhead heuristic to explain why relaxing synchronization barriers relocates rather than eliminates system overhead.

1.  **An infrastructure team argues that the four bands of the fleet stack (Infrastructure, Distribution, Serving, Governance) can be optimized in complete isolation by independent teams without cross-layer coordination. Why does the chapter reject this architectural view?**

    1.  The fleet stack is a strict constraint-propagation hierarchy where lower layers physically constrain upper layers: facility power and network fabric bisection bandwidth constrain feasible distribution strategies, which constrain serving tail-latency SLOs, which in turn determine whether runtime governance invariants can be enforced.
    2.  The fleet stack requires all four layers to be compiled into a single monolithic C++ binary that executes directly on GPU bare metal.
    3.  The fleet stack only applies to single-GPU workstations and breaks down whenever multiple nodes are connected.
    4.  The fleet stack is an administrative corporate organization chart rather than a technical systems model.

    *Answer*: The correct answer is A. The fleet stack is defined as a layered constraint-propagation framework: decisions at the bottom constrain possibilities at the top. Physical infrastructure limits (e.g. 200 Gb/s link limits and facility power budgets) bound which distribution strategies (tensor vs pipeline parallelism) are viable; distribution choices bound serving latency and throughput envelopes; and serving architectures determine how data lineage, access control, and auditability invariants are maintained at runtime. Proposing monolithic binary compilation misrepresents software architecture, claiming it applies only to single nodes contradicts the fleet-scale definition, and viewing it as an administrative org chart misses its technical engineering purpose.

    *Learning Objective*: Analyze how the fleet stack acts as a constraint-propagation hierarchy where lower-layer physical decisions bound upper-layer operational capabilities.

2.  **During capacity planning for a frontier model, an engineering leadership team doubles the target model parameter count from 100B to 200B while keeping the target calendar training window fixed. Explain how this model-side change cascades into concrete requirements on the training data and on the fleet’s infrastructure.**

    *Answer*: If the plan retains the approximate Chinchilla allocation, doubling parameters from 100B to 200B doubles the token target from roughly 2T to 4T. Parameter and optimizer memory also double at fixed precision. Since ${O_{\text{train}}} \approx 6{P}{D}$, useful training FLOPs quadruple. Finishing in the same time therefore requires four times the effective aggregate compute throughput, which may require more than four times the devices if utilization falls. Data delivery, placement, and communication must be checked separately; no fixed network multiplier follows from parameter count alone.

    *Learning Objective*: Trace how a change in model size cascades into data and infrastructure requirements under a fixed calendar window.

Archetype A (GPT-4/Llama-3): Communication (dense gradient/activation synchronization across thousands of GPUs); Archetype B (DLRM): Coordination (sharding 10 TB+ embedding tables with $\mathcal{O}({N}^2)$ AllToAll routing under \<100 ms tail latency); Archetype C (Federated MobileNet): Compute (watt-scale power and memory limits on local edge devices).

The archetypes probe communication for dense distributed LLM workloads, coordination for sparse DLRM routing and placement, and compute for MobileNet’s per-device envelope. 4.  **Order the four bands of the fleet stack from bottom (physical foundation) to top (mission context): (1) Governance, (2) Infrastructure, (3) Distribution, (4) Serving.**

    *Answer*: The correct order is: (2) Infrastructure, (3) Distribution, (4) Serving, (1) Governance. 1.  **On the 1,024-GPU reference job, a modeled training step of 8.71 s splits into 7.25 s of local computation, 0.53 s of pipeline bubble, and 0.93 s of exposed tensor-parallel and gradient exchange. Accelerators twice as fast halve the computation and the bubble, which is idle time measured in stage computation, but leave the exchange unchanged. What happens to step time, and what does this imply for the purchase decision?**

    1.  Step time falls from 8.71 s to about 4.82 s, a $1.8\times$ speedup rather than $2\times$; the unchanged communication caps the gain.
    2.  Step time falls from 8.71 s to about 4.36 s because doubling accelerator throughput halves every distributed cost.
    3.  Step time falls to about 5.09 s, a $1.7\times$ speedup, because only the 7.25 s of computation halves while the bubble and the exchange stay fixed.
    4.  Step time falls to about 5.75 s, a $1.5\times$ speedup, because faster accelerators expose twice as much of the gradient exchange.

    *Answer*: The correct answer is A. The bubble is idle time measured in stage computation, so it halves along with the computation. The halved terms take (7.25 + 0.53)/2 = 3.89 s; adding the unchanged 0.93 s exchange gives 4.82 s, and 8.71/4.82 is about 1.8. Faster arithmetic helps, but its datasheet ratio does not carry through the communication term, and the larger the share of the step that communication occupies, the further the gain falls short. The purchase decision needs the measured fleet decomposition and any additional changes to memory, links, or overlap. These step values are modeled, not measured.

3.  **A common fallacy treats edge ML systems as simply smaller, resource-constrained versions of cloud servers. Using the chapter’s autonomous vehicle example (a car traveling at 120 km/h with a 100 ms inference delay), explain why edge deployment constraints differ qualitatively rather than merely quantitatively from cloud data centers.**

    *Answer*: At 120 km/h, a vehicle moves about 3.33 meters in 100 ms, so processing delay contributes directly to reaction distance. Edge deployment must therefore satisfy the application’s physical deadline as well as device-specific power, thermal, and connectivity limits. Dedicated cloud cooling and elastic capacity cannot simply be assumed on the device. These are workload-specific constraints, not a universal battery lifetime or throttling multiplier for all edge hardware.

    *Learning Objective*: Analyze how physical safety, latency-distance physics, and strict thermal limits create qualitative differences between edge and cloud ML systems.
