# V2-01.07 The Fleet Stack and the Lighthouse Archetypes

[[Home|Home]] · [[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/Volume 2 Overview|Volume Overview]] · [[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/00 Introduction/01 Introduction/V2-01 Introduction|V2-01 Introduction]]

^sec-vol2-introduction-foundational-concepts

When a distributed step stalls on exposed communication or synchronization, the Fleet Law and the C$^3$ dimensions tell us which cost dominates, but not which layer of the system caused it or which workloads will trigger it next. Three architectural maps work together to close that gap: the fleet stack locates system responsibility, C$^3$ and the Fleet Law diagnose execution cost, and the lighthouse archetypes test those costs across representative production workloads. The fleet stack, traced upward from the hardware of one node in [[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/00 Introduction/01 Introduction/V2-01.03 From Single Node to Fleet#^sec-vol2-introduction-engineering-crux|section 1.2]], arranges the fleet into eight rows grouped into four ascending bands ([[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/00 Introduction/01 Introduction/V2-01.03 From Single Node to Fleet#^fig-fleet-stack|figure 4]]). From the bottom, those rows are Facility and Accelerators, Fabric and Storage, Distributed Execution, Reliability and Control, Runtime and Serving, Operations, Assurance, and Governance and Sustainability. Capabilities fixed in the lower rows, from facility cooling and RDMA fabrics up through distributed execution, constrain the serving runtimes and governance mechanisms above them. Those four bands correspond to the book’s four parts: the physical fleet, distributed ML, the fleet in production, and the responsible fleet. The chapter map at [[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/00 Introduction/01 Introduction/V2-01.08 The Structure of This Textbook#^sec-vol2-introduction-structure|section 1.7]] follows that dependency order.

Six **engineering practices** guide decisions at every band of the stack: instrument first, reserve headroom for recovery and tail latency, expect bottlenecks to migrate, treat failure as steady state, compound small efficiency gains across the fleet, and co-design hardware with algorithms. For example, faster accelerators can expose a network bottleneck; instrumentation identifies that migration, and hardware co-design aligns the fabric with the workload. The practices describe how a fleet engineer works. The principles of distributed ML systems, among them that communication can dominate a step and that failure is routine, describe what the physics of the fleet makes true, whatever the practice. Each part of this book establishes its share of those principles. **[[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/04 The Responsible Fleet/17 Conclusion/V2-17.03 Six Principles of Distributed ML Systems#^sec-conclusion-six-principles-distributed-ml-systems-746a|Six Principles of Distributed ML Systems]]** gathers them into six, one or two for each part, and pairs each with the practice it demands.

### Three Systems Archetypes

^sec-vol2-introduction-archetypes

A single benchmark workload cannot test every band of the fleet stack, because a dense language model, a sparse recommendation service, and an on-device federated learner fail in completely different places. Three lighthouse archetypes at scale recur across the book where they illuminate a chapter’s central constraint; no chapter must force all three into its argument. Together they sample distinct C$^3$ regimes by stressing communication, coordination, or compute in different ways.

#### Archetype a (GPT-4/Llama-3)

^sec-introduction-archetype-gpt4llama3-b02f

The training state of a dense language model with hundreds of billions of parameters cannot fit in the accelerator memory of a single server. Splitting the model across thousands of accelerators using 3D parallelism (data, tensor, and pipeline) risks exposing enough gradient and activation traffic to make the network the binding bottleneck. GPT-4 and Llama 3, the autoregressive transformer architectures behind this archetype, generate text one token at a time. At single-accelerator scale, autoregressive language models are memory-bandwidth probes. At the scale of hundreds of billions of parameters, and in some proprietary systems potentially more, training demands EFLOP/s of sustained compute distributed across thousands to tens of thousands of accelerators. Serving requires memory bandwidth sufficient to load billions of weights for every generated token. Because gradient and activation traffic crosses the network every step, Archetype A probes the communication dimension of C$^3$, although its actual dominant term depends on the model, partition, topology, and degree of overlap.

Where a chapter needs a concrete instance, Archetype A is a 175B-parameter dense transformer of the GPT-3 class, chosen because its configuration is published while GPT-4’s is not, trained with mixed precision and the Adaptive Moment Estimation (Adam) optimizer. Its training state occupies 16 bytes per parameter (BF16 weights and gradients, FP32 master weights, and two FP32 Adam moments), or 2.8 TB. A resumable checkpoint omits the gradients and needs 14 bytes per parameter, or 2.45 TB. The reference cluster is 1,024 H100 GPUs, arranged as 128 nodes of 8 accelerators each. Each data-parallel worker is one replica of the model spread over 64 GPUs, with tensor parallelism across the 8 GPUs of a node and a pipeline 8 nodes deep, so the cluster holds 16 replicas. Without sharding, the training state then comes to 43.8 GB per GPU, which fits in one H100’s memory. One training step is one optimizer update over a global batch of 1,536 sequences (3,145,728 tokens), with one gradient reduction across the replicas, and the modeled step takes about 8.7 s. A chapter that studies a larger cluster or a different model says explicitly how it varies this reference.

#### Archetype B (DLRM at Scale)

^sec-introduction-archetype-b-dlrm-scale-fa6d

Where a dense language model spends most of its memory on weights that every token uses, a production recommender spends its memory on sparse embedding tables that reach 10 TB or more across hundreds of nodes. It serves roughly 10 million queries per second (QPS) within a \<100 ms tail-latency budget. DLRM, the deep learning recommendation model that Meta published as a reference architecture for personalized content ranking, derives its capacity from dense matrix multiplications, as LLMs do, and also from large embedding tables that map billions of user and item features into dense vectors. Those tables far exceed any single accelerator’s memory, so they are sharded across devices while each worker processes its own slice of the request batch for the dense layers.

Because a worker’s requests reference embedding rows stored on many other devices, serving and training must coordinate AllToAll exchanges, collective operations in which every worker sends a distinct subset of looked-up vectors to every other worker across $\mathcal{O}({N}^2)$ potential communicating pairs. The resulting byte volume and contention depend on the request batch, partitioning, and network topology. In the C$^3$ taxonomy, the AllToAll bytes are measured as Communication, but Archetype B stresses coordination, because sparse feature routing, shard placement, request scheduling, and embedding-update consistency determine whether that traffic can be served within tail-latency limits.

#### Archetype C (Federated MobileNet)

^sec-introduction-archetype-c-federated-mobilenet-65a3

Both of those workloads run inside managed data centers. Federated learning instead trains a model across millions of varied smartphone-class devices whose power budgets span roughly 2 W to 10 W (a few watts sustained, with brief bursts at the top of that range), without ever moving raw user data off the device. MobileNet, a family of efficient convolutional neural networks designed for on-device execution under tight memory and power budgets, serves as the reference workload for this federated learning setting. Microcontroller-class sensors that run on hundreds of kilobytes of memory and milliwatts of power lie at an extreme below this archetype.

In the C$^3$ taxonomy, Archetype C probes the per-device compute envelope: local execution must fit within the device’s power, memory, and thermal limits. That local constraint does not by itself determine the bottleneck of a federated round. Uploading updates can expose communication limits, while selecting available participants and waiting for their results can expose coordination limits. The fleet challenge is coordinating model updates across millions of such compute-constrained, unreliable devices using federated averaging, which periodically averages device updates at a coordinator. Data distributions that are not independent and identically distributed (i.i.d.) and intermittent participation affect the resulting learning process; convergence claims must state the assumptions under which they hold.

The three archetypes are constraint probes, not a catalog of model families. The LLM case asks whether dense synchronization can keep thousands of accelerators useful, the DLRM case asks whether sparse feature routing can meet tail-latency budgets, and the federated MobileNet case asks whether local device constraints can be coordinated into a statistically coherent update. Each of the three canonical workloads pairs a C$^3$ diagnostic regime with the fleet challenge it raises ([[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/00 Introduction/01 Introduction/V2-01.07 The Fleet Stack and the Lighthouse Archetypes#^tbl-vol2-lighthouse-archetypes|table 3]]), and [[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/00 Introduction/01 Introduction/V2-01.08 The Structure of This Textbook#^sec-vol2-introduction-structure|section 1.7]] traces how the chapters climb the fleet stack to meet them.

| **Archetype**                         | **C$^3$ diagnostic regime**          | **Fleet challenge**                                                                                                                                                                                                          |
|:--------------------------------------|:-------------------------------------|:-----------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------|
| **Archetype A (GPT-4/Llama-3)**       | Communication (fleet-wide)           | Partition hundreds of billions of parameters, and potentially larger proprietary models, across thousands to tens of thousands of GPUs using 3D parallelism without the network becoming the bottleneck.                     |
| **Archetype B (DLRM at Scale)**       | Coordination (routing and placement) | Shard 10 TB+ embedding tables across hundreds of nodes; process millions of QPS with \<100 ms tail latency while managing sparse feature routing, shard placement, and exchanges among $\mathcal{O}({N}^2)$ potential pairs. |
| **Archetype C (Federated MobileNet)** | Compute (per-device envelope)        | Coordinate learning across millions of compute-constrained, unreliable edge devices using federated updates; raw data cannot leave the device.                                                                               |

Table 3: **Lighthouse Archetypes at Scale**: The three canonical workloads in representative diagnostic regimes, with the fleet-scale challenge each raises. These are constraint probes, not invariant bottleneck assignments; workload, partitioning, topology, and overlap determine the binding cost. The MobileNet row describes the local device envelope, not the bottleneck of a complete federated round.

**Self-Check: Question**

1.  An infrastructure team argues that the four bands of the fleet stack (Infrastructure, Distribution, Serving, Governance) can be optimized in complete isolation by independent teams without cross-layer coordination. Why does the chapter reject this architectural view?

    1.  The fleet stack is a strict constraint-propagation hierarchy where lower layers physically constrain upper layers: facility power and network fabric bisection bandwidth constrain feasible distribution strategies, which constrain serving tail-latency SLOs, which in turn determine whether runtime governance invariants can be enforced.
    2.  The fleet stack requires all four layers to be compiled into a single monolithic C++ binary that executes directly on GPU bare metal.
    3.  The fleet stack only applies to single-GPU workstations and breaks down whenever multiple nodes are connected.
    4.  The fleet stack is an administrative corporate organization chart rather than a technical systems model.

2.  During capacity planning for a frontier model, an engineering leadership team doubles the target model parameter count from 100B to 200B while keeping the target calendar training window fixed. Explain how this model-side change cascades into concrete requirements on the training data and on the fleet’s infrastructure.

    1.  Archetype A: Coordination (edge battery); Archetype B: Compute (matrix math); Archetype C: Communication (InfiniBand).
    2.  Archetype A: Compute (single-thread CPU clock); Archetype B: Communication (local PCIe); Archetype C: Coordination (disk I/O).
    3.  Archetype A (GPT-4/Llama-3): Communication (dense gradient/activation synchronization across thousands of GPUs); Archetype B (DLRM): Coordination (sharding 10 TB+ embedding tables with $\mathcal{O}({N}^2)$ AllToAll routing under \<100 ms tail latency); Archetype C (Federated MobileNet): Compute (watt-scale power and memory limits on local edge devices).
    4.  All three archetypes share the identical dominant constraint: local GPU HBM capacity.
