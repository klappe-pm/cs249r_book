# V2-01.06 The C³ Taxonomy and Foundations of Scale

[[Home|Home]] · [[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/Volume 2 Overview|Volume Overview]] · [[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/00 Introduction/01 Introduction/V2-01 Introduction|V2-01 Introduction]]

^sec-vol2-introduction-c-cube

An idle accelerator does not identify its own bottleneck. It may be waiting for local operands, a gradient transfer, or a failed peer. The C$^3$ lens introduced at the scale moment separates these explanations and gives the preceding examples a common diagnostic language. On a single machine, diagnosing performance follows the **Data · Algorithm · Machine (D·A·M) taxonomy**. In that framework, Data governs what information the model learns from; the Algorithm defines the mathematical operations to execute; and the Machine supplies the silicon and memory hierarchy executing those operations.

↪ The D·A·M taxonomy as a complete single-node framework, with its diagnostic summary, roofline bound, and scorecard, is developed in **[[05 Reference/Volume II - Scaling/V2 Single-Machine Foundations#^sec-appdx-dam-taxonomy|The D·A·M Taxonomy]]**.

When workloads stretch across thousands of interconnected nodes, the execution bottleneck shifts. Data-center hardware is procured and cabled around three physical pillars: Compute, Network, and Storage. At runtime, however, distributed execution spends wall-clock time in three distinct operational dimensions: Compute (${C}_1$), Communication (${C}_2$), and Coordination (${C}_3$). Together, they form the C$^3$ taxonomy. Storage is not absent from this runtime model. It serves as the persistent physical substrate spanning all three dimensions: an extension of the node memory hierarchy in Compute (${C}_1$), a high-throughput streaming pipeline across Communication (${C}_2$), and the durable checkpointing engine required for fault-tolerant Coordination (${C}_3$).

Each dimension leaves different evidence. Suppose a training job’s step time grows by half after its cluster doubles. If time inside each node’s kernels grew while their model FLOPs utilization (MFU), the fraction of peak arithmetic throughput spent on the model’s own operations, fell and memory-bandwidth counters saturated, local execution is responsible. If link utilization and collective timing rose, transfers are; if barrier waiting and recovery records grew, coordination is. The three diagnoses call for different interventions, and optimizing the wrong mechanism leaves the end-to-end bottleneck in place. As in single-machine systems, the governing limit is the *binding constraint*: the bottleneck whose relaxation yields an end-to-end reduction in training time or serving latency. The C$^3$ taxonomy is therefore an operational diagnostic loop: identify the binding axis, apply the appropriate systems intervention, and remeasure, because relaxing one limit frequently shifts the bottleneck to another.

**Definition 1.2: The C³ taxonomy**

***C$^3$ taxonomy*** is a diagnostic framework that classifies distributed machine learning execution time and system bottlenecks along three operational dimensions: Compute (${C}_1$, the math), Communication (${C}_2$, the wire), and Coordination (${C}_3$, the logic). The goal is to identify which dimension binds fleet-scale performance.

1.  **Significance**: The diagnostic power is concrete even before inspecting complex distributed traces. If accelerators are computing at low MFU because tensor cores starve for local operands or un-fused kernels, the binding axis is Compute (${C}_1$). If accelerators sit idle waiting for AllReduce gradient transfers or activation tensors across the network fabric, the binding axis is Communication (${C}_2$). If progress halts due to straggler-induced barrier waiting, node recovery rollbacks, or preemptions under gang scheduling (which allocates all of a job’s workers at once or none of them), the binding axis is Coordination (${C}_3$).
2.  **Distinction**: Unlike data center procurement categories (Compute, Network, Storage), which classify physical hardware assets, the C$^3$ taxonomy classifies runtime execution cost. Storage does not execute model arithmetic; rather, it serves as the persistent physical substrate across all three dimensions (local NVMe in ${C}_1$, remote dataset streaming in ${C}_2$, and durable checkpointing in ${C}_3$).
3.  **Common pitfall**: Treating the three dimensions as independent variables. Upgrading to faster accelerators (${C}_1$) often converts a compute-bound job into a communication-bound job (${C}_2$). Sharding model states to eliminate memory redundancy (${C}_1$) increases collective communication volume (${C}_2$) and state coordination complexity (${C}_3$). The dimensions move together.

### The Three Dimensions of the Fleet

^sec-vol2-introduction-three-dimensions

Each dimension of the C$^3$ taxonomy governs a distinct physical layer of the fleet and exposes a different operational bottleneck.

#### Node Compute Substrate

^sec-vol2-introduction-dim-compute

When operands cannot reach a node’s arithmetic units fast enough to keep tensor cores busy, the memory wall binds Compute (${C}_1$, the math). This axis covers execution within the server node across arithmetic units, high-bandwidth memory, and local Non-Volatile Memory Express (NVMe) storage. Improving MFU on this axis requires reducing local data movement, for example through operator fusion, rather than merely increasing peak arithmetic throughput.

↪ The memory wall, from high-bandwidth memory (HBM) bandwidth to the Roofline Model that locates it for one accelerator, is developed in **[[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/01 The Fleet/02 Compute Infrastructure/V2-02.04 The Memory Wall#^sec-compute-memory-wall|The Memory Wall]]**.

#### Network Communication Backbone

^sec-vol2-introduction-dim-comm

When gradient, activation, or dataset-shard transfers saturate a cluster’s finite bisection bandwidth, they reach the network wall and expose communication time (${T_{\text{comm}}}$) during which accelerators wait for remote data. Communication (${C}_2$, the wire) captures this data movement between worker devices across both scale-up fabrics inside a server and scale-out networks between servers; reducing bytes transferred or overlapping transfers with useful work can shorten that exposed time.

#### Orchestration Coordination Layer

^sec-vol2-introduction-dim-coord

Because a synchronous job waits for its slowest required participant, a single straggler or node fault can stall thousands of healthy peers even when both local kernels and network links have spare capacity. Coordination (${C}_3$, the logic) captures the schedulers, synchronization barriers, and checkpointing protocols that manage the reliability gap ([[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/00 Introduction/01 Introduction/V2-01.05 Constraints of Scale#^eq-reliability-gap|equation 2]]) and bind separate, failing machines into one distributed computer. Its objective is goodput, the rate of useful training progress after wasted and repeated work are accounted for. It is one of several measures that share the name, and **[[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/03 The Fleet in Production/09 Fleet Efficiency/V2-09.03 ML Goodput#^sec-performance-engineering-goodput-family|One word, several measures]]** tells them apart. Overlap where dependencies permit and bounded recovery rollback improve that rate.

### Projection from D·A·M to C³

^sec-vol2-introduction-projection

When a model or dataset that fit on one server is split across a cluster, the single-machine D·A·M components do not disappear; each one generates a distinct pattern of fleet-level compute, communication, and coordination work. The single-machine D·A·M taxonomy and the fleet-scale C$^3$ taxonomy therefore operate in tandem. D·A·M describes the workload: the *nouns* of the system (the training data, the executed algorithm, the hardware substrate). The C$^3$ taxonomy describes the execution cost: the *verbs* of the fleet (computing the math, communicating the results, coordinating the state). The C$^3$ taxonomy projects that workload onto a distributed fleet.

Stretching an algorithm across nodes creates communication (${C}_2$), such as the activation and gradient exchanges that tensor and pipeline parallelism add between partitions of one model. Stretching data across nodes also moves shards and gradients over the fabric (${C}_2$), as when AllReduce synchronizes data-parallel replicas, and it requires coordination (${C}_3$) through distributed samplers, gradient-aggregation barriers, and non-blocking checkpointing. Finally, managing hardware faults across a warehouse-scale machine requires orchestration and dynamic node recovery (${C}_3$). Fleet-scale ML systems engineering requires solving the cross-products of D·A·M and C$^3$ rather than addressing each dimension in isolation.

### The C³ Intersection Landscape

^sec-vol2-introduction-c3-landscape

The dimensions interact at their pairwise intersections, where an intervention along one axis alters costs across another. At the intersection of compute and communication (${C}_1 \cap {C}_2$), overlapping transfers with arithmetic can hide network latency, but only while independent computation remains available. Between compute and coordination (${C}_1 \cap {C}_3$), checkpointing preserves training progress against failure, yet it consumes execution resources; its frequency and placement trade present overhead against future recovery loss. Across communication and coordination (${C}_2 \cap {C}_3$), collective synchronization depends jointly on transfer completion and participant readiness, so faster physical links alone cannot eliminate straggler waiting.

At the center (${C}_1 \cap {C}_2 \cap {C}_3$), all three constraints converge simultaneously. Partitioning a model determines both which device computes each operation and what information must cross the network; recovering a failed partition requires restoring the corresponding shared state. These dependencies explain why parallelism, state sharding, and serving policies must be designed together. Each dimension contributes one term to the distributed step time, and each pairwise overlap names an interface where the dimensions trade costs by hiding latency, preserving progress against failure, and synchronizing collectives ([[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/00 Introduction/01 Introduction/V2-01.06 The C³ Taxonomy and Foundations of Scale#^fig-c3-taxonomy|figure 14]]).

Each pairwise lens names the interaction between two dimensions: hiding network latency behind computation, preserving training progress against failure, and synchronizing collectives. The diagram organizes competing explanations for the same observed delay. A diagnosis may begin with one dimension or with a coupled mechanism, such as checkpoint traffic delaying a collective. Surrounding these three operational dimensions are the non-negotiable operating invariants developed across Part IV, the Governance band of the fleet stack. They are the thermodynamic energy and cooling limits that constrain data centers, the adversarial security and differential privacy guarantees required of distributed pipelines, the robustness against silent corruption (a fault that changes a result without raising any error) and distribution shift (live inputs that move away from the training data), and the systemic governance, auditing, and safety invariants needed when operating at societal scale.

### The Fleet Law

^sec-vol2-introduction-fleet-law

The C$^3$ intersection landscape defines *where* distributed execution spends time and where trade-offs bite. The Fleet Law turns that diagnostic map into a first estimate of distributed step time. Its terms are a simplified accounting model, so a prediction must state how work is divided and which costs overlap. On a single machine, execution time is governed by the iron law of performance, ${T}= {D_{\text{vol}}}/{\text{BW}} + {O}/({R_{\text{peak}}} \cdot {\eta_{\text{hw}}}) + {L_{\text{lat}}}$, decomposing into data movement, computation, and overhead. At fleet scale, that single-machine view no longer suffices. [[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/00 Introduction/01 Introduction/V2-01.06 The C³ Taxonomy and Foundations of Scale#^eq-fleet-law|Equation 3]] establishes the **Fleet Law**, which decomposes the idealized distributed training step into the terms of the C$^3$ engine:

$$
{T_{\text{step}}}({N}) = \frac{{T_{\text{compute}}}}{{N}} + {T_{\text{comm}}}({N}) + {T_{\text{sync}}}({N}) - {T_{\text{overlap}}} \tag{3}
$$

^eq-fleet-law

Here ${T_{\text{compute}}}$ is the compute term of the iron law above, the time one device spends on the forward and backward passes of one training step. Each term in the Fleet Law maps directly onto a region of the C$^3$ landscape in [[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/00 Introduction/01 Introduction/V2-01.06 The C³ Taxonomy and Foundations of Scale#^fig-c3-taxonomy|figure 14]]:

- $\frac{{T_{\text{compute}}}}{{N}}$ is the Compute (${C}_1$) term, assuming ideally divisible local arithmetic for a fixed workload distributed across ${N}$ devices.
- ${T_{\text{comm}}}({N})$ is the Communication (${C}_2$) term, capturing the wire transit time moving gradients, activations, and parameter shards across the network fabric.
- ${T_{\text{sync}}}({N})$ is the Coordination (${C}_3$) term, capturing time consumed by barrier semantics and straggler waiting, plus each step’s average share of run-level costs such as cluster scheduling decisions and failure recovery.
- ${T_{\text{overlap}}}$ is the Overlap (${C}_1 \cap {C}_2$, ${C}_1 \cap {C}_3$, ${C}_2 \cap {C}_3$) term, representing communication or coordination hidden behind useful compute via techniques such as interleaving forward and backward work across pipeline stages, running transfers on separate execution streams, and writing checkpoints asynchronously.

The Compute term has two readings, and an analysis must state which one it uses. The arithmetic idealization used below assumes that local memory can supply operands fast enough to sustain the modeled compute rate, so the term holds arithmetic alone. A measured local-execution reference can instead include local memory stalls and dispatch costs in the Compute term, provided those costs divide with the work as assumed.

Doubling the device count shows why the subtraction matters. The local compute term falls to ${T_{\text{compute}}}/(2{N})$, but communication and barrier synchronization persist, and the shorter compute window leaves less room to hide transfers, so ${T_{\text{overlap}}}$ shrinks as well. The step on $2{N}$ devices therefore takes well over half as long as the step on ${N}$ ([[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/00 Introduction/01 Introduction/V2-01.06 The C³ Taxonomy and Foundations of Scale#^fig-fleet-law-step|figure 15]]).

![[90 Assets/Volume II/01 Introduction/Fleet Law Step f4045ac21e.svg]]

Figure 15: **Anatomy of a Fleet Law Step**: Each row is one training step for the same workload, with local computation on the upper lane and communication and synchronization on the lower lane. Read the step length from the bracket, not from any single bar: overlap (gray band) removes only the transfer time that runs beneath compute, so doubling the devices halves ${T_{\text{compute}}}/{N}$ yet leaves ${T_{\text{step}}}(2{N})$ well past the ideal half-length step (dashed line), by the hatched span. Bar lengths are illustrative, not measured.

^fig-fleet-law-step

Weak scaling (growing the workload with the device count), load imbalance, memory-capacity changes, and algorithmic changes require modifying these terms rather than treating ${T_{\text{compute}}}/{N}$ as invariant. Measurements must also separate transfer from coordination time: a barrier interval that includes an unfinished transfer cannot be charged in full to both terms. Subtract only overlap supported by the execution timeline. [[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/00 Introduction/01 Introduction/V2-01.06 The C³ Taxonomy and Foundations of Scale#^eq-fleet-efficiency|Equation 4]] rewrites this decomposition as the compute-time fraction. It measures useful arithmetic under the arithmetic idealization and local execution under the measured-reference convention:

$$
{f_{\text{compute}}} = \frac{{T_{\text{compute}}}/{N}}{{T_{\text{step}}}({N})} \tag{4}
$$

^eq-fleet-efficiency

This decomposition is a diagnostic instrument. When ${f_{\text{compute}}}$ drops below 0.5, more than half the step lies outside the modeled Compute term; under the arithmetic idealization, that means outside useful local arithmetic. The C$^3$ taxonomy identifies where the time goes. If ${T_{\text{comm}}}({N})$ dominates, upgrade interconnects or overlap communication with computation. If ${T_{\text{sync}}}({N})$ dominates, consider asynchronous methods or reduce barrier frequency. If ${T_{\text{compute}}}/{N}$ dominates, the modeled local execution sets the step time. The **displacement of overhead** is a diagnostic heuristic: reducing one overhead can relocate cost among the three dimensions. Asynchronous training removes some coordination barriers but introduces gradient staleness, in which workers apply updates computed from an older copy of the model. Pipeline parallelism can reduce some communication exposure but adds bubble time, the intervals in which pipeline stages sit idle waiting for work.

The compute-time fraction describes how a single ${N}$-device configuration divides its own step time. A production team also needs a scalar that compares that distributed step against a single-device baseline to show whether adding devices actually speeds up the job. The scaling efficiency (${\eta_{\text{scaling}}}$) of the fleet is the ratio of ideal parallel step time to actual step time:

$$
{\eta_{\text{scaling}}} = \frac{{T_1}}{{N}\times {T_N}}
$$

 where ${T_1}$ is the single-device time for the same work and ${T_N} = {T_{\text{step}}}({N})$ is the step time on ${N}$ devices. When local execution divides cleanly by ${N}$, ${T_1}/{N}$ matches ${T_{\text{compute}}}/{N}$ and the two ratios agree. In practice, partitioning a workload across more devices can also shrink per-device batch slices enough to lower local kernel utilization or add recomputation work, which reduces ${\eta_{\text{scaling}}}$ even before communication and synchronization time are counted. **[[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/02 Distributed ML/06 Distributed Training/V2-06.05 Scaling Efficiency and Convergence#^sec-distributed-training-scaling-math|The mathematics of scaling efficiency]]** develops the measure in full.

↪ The component decomposition that maps each term of ${T_{\text{step}}}({N})$ to measurable activities is developed in **[[05 Reference/Volume II - Scaling/V2 The C³ Taxonomy#^sec-appdx-c3-taxonomy-component-decomposition|Component decomposition]]**.

**Systems Perspective 1.1: Amdahl's distributed pitfall**

The Fleet Law applies Amdahl’s reasoning ([[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/00 Introduction/01 Introduction/V2-01.12 Source References#^ref-amdahl1967|Amdahl 1967]]) to a strong-scaling experiment: a fixed amount of useful work is divided across more devices while communication and coordination terms are measured for that configuration. A common shortcut reads a measured 20 percent network share as a 5$\times$ speedup ceiling, but Amdahl’s bound applies only if that 20 percent of the original workload remains irreducibly serial as compute is accelerated. A network share measured at one device count is not automatically invariant; changing the partition, topology, collective algorithm, or overlap can change both its absolute time and its fraction of the step. The Fleet Law therefore diagnoses the scaling limit of a stated workload and configuration rather than asserting a universal network fraction.

Fleet efficiency has several measures, and each answers a different question:

- Percent of peak compares achieved arithmetic throughput with peak throughput over a stated measurement interval, and the result depends on what it counts as arithmetic.
- MFU is percent of peak that counts only the model’s own forward and backward work, and GPT-3’s roughly 20 percent is a measure of this kind. GPT-3’s estimate averages over the training run, so it includes idle time within that interval; a measurement restricted to active kernels uses a different denominator.
- Hardware FLOPs utilization is percent of peak that counts every executed operation, including activations discarded to save memory and recomputed during the backward pass. Megatron-LM’s 52 percent is a measure of this kind, so it runs higher than the MFU of comparable runs, which the PaLM report puts at 30.2 percent for Megatron-Turing NLG and 46.2 percent for PaLM ([[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/00 Introduction/01 Introduction/V2-01.12 Source References#^ref-chowdhery2022palm|Chowdhery et al. 2022]]).
- The compute-time fraction asks how much of one step belongs to the modeled local-execution term.
- Scaling efficiency asks how close ${N}$ devices come to an ${N}$-fold speedup over one.
- Goodput widens the window from one step to the whole run by discounting work lost to failures and repeated after restarts; Meta’s report of more than 90 percent effective training time for Llama 3 is a measure of this kind.
- Google’s ML productivity goodput shares the name but answers a fleet-wide question, the fraction of capacity that becomes useful work, as the product of scheduling, runtime, and program goodput ([[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/00 Introduction/01 Introduction/V2-01.12 Source References#^ref-wongpanich2025fleet|Wongpanich et al. 2025]]). **[[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/03 The Fleet in Production/09 Fleet Efficiency/V2-09.03 ML Goodput#^sec-performance-engineering-measurement-scale|ML Goodput]]** defines the three factors and the boundary at which each one counts a loss.

A fleet can score well on one measure and poorly on another, so a claim about fleet efficiency must name the measure and measurement interval it uses.

The Fleet Law, the compute-time fraction, and scaling efficiency together describe the time side of fleet efficiency. They separate modeled local execution from exposed communication and synchronization costs and compare the resulting step time with the single-device baseline. Fleet efficiency also has an energy side, because every exposed transfer and every idle accelerator still draws power.

### The Energy Accounting Boundary

^sec-introduction-energyscale-invariant-a0d6

While the Fleet Law accounts for time, comparing fleet designs on energy requires a fixed **energy accounting boundary**, the set of energy consumers that every design in the comparison is charged for. The boundary is what stays fixed, not any particular efficiency value. [[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/00 Introduction/01 Introduction/V2-01.06 The C³ Taxonomy and Foundations of Scale#^eq-energy-scale-invariant|Equation 5]] defines **fleet energy productivity** (${\rho_{\text{energy}}}$), measured in FLOP/J, within a simplified operational boundary:

$$
{\rho_{\text{energy}}} = \frac{{O_{\text{useful}}}}{{E_{\text{device}}}+ {E_{\text{cooling}}}+ {E_{\text{network}}}} \tag{5}
$$

^eq-energy-scale-invariant

where ${O_{\text{useful}}}$ is useful work in FLOPs. The three energy terms cover compute devices, attributable cooling, and the network fabric, without counting the same consumption twice. Host, storage, and other facility loads must be added explicitly for a complete operational estimate; embodied impacts, the environmental costs of manufacturing the equipment, lie outside this boundary. Comparisons must use the same boundary and useful-work definition. The resulting Pareto frontier balances minimizing ${T_{\text{step}}}$ with maximizing ${\rho_{\text{energy}}}$.

↪ Power usage effectiveness, the ratio of total facility energy (including cooling and power conversion) to IT energy, and what it leaves out are developed in **[[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/04 The Responsible Fleet/15 Sustainable AI/V2-15.05 Energy Measurement and Modeling#^sec-sustainable-ai-facilitylevel-power-metrics-5558|Facility-level power metrics]]**.

The 175B reference job connects the time and energy accounts, because every second of exposed communication or pipeline idle time both delays the step and keeps accelerators and links drawing power. Notebook [1.1](https://mlsysbook.ai/vol2/01_introduction/01_introduction.html#nbk-introduction-coordination-energy-tax) computes both costs for that job on 1,024 and on 8,192 GPUs.

**Napkin Math 1.1: The time and energy cost of scaling out**

**Problem**: The 175B reference job runs on 1,024 H100 GPUs as 16 replicas of 64 GPUs, with a global batch of 1,536 sequences per step. On 8,192 GPUs, the same batch spreads over 128 replicas of 12 sequences each instead of 96. How do the modeled compute-time fraction and the energy of one step change between the two clusters?

**Physics**: The step model used here divides each step into the replica’s local computation, the tensor-parallel exchanges inside each node, the data-parallel gradient exchange across the fabric, and the pipeline bubble, the time stages sit idle while the pipeline fills and drains. The gradient exchange runs during the backward pass, and the model hides 85 percent of it behind that computation, leaving the rest exposed. These are modeled values, not measurements.

**Analysis**: On 1,024 GPUs, local computation takes 7.25 s of the 8.7 s step. Tensor-parallel exchanges add 0.90 s and the bubble 0.53 s, while only 0.03 s of the 0.21 s gradient exchange is exposed. The compute-time fraction is 83 percent. On 8,192 GPUs, local computation falls to 0.91 s and tensor-parallel traffic to 0.11 s, because each replica carries fewer sequences. The gradient exchange stays near 0.22 s, and the bubble does not shrink at all, because it is set by the time one sequence spends in each pipeline stage. With fewer sequences to keep the pipeline busy, that fixed idle time fills 33 percent of the step. The step shrinks only to 1.6 s, a 5.5× speedup for 8× the GPUs, and the compute-time fraction falls to 57 percent. Because the compute term is the replica’s whole local workload, this fraction is also the step’s scaling efficiency, 0.83 and 0.57.

**Energy**: Both clusters perform the same optimizer update, so the useful FLOPs in [[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/00 Introduction/01 Introduction/V2-01.06 The C³ Taxonomy and Foundations of Scale#^eq-energy-scale-invariant|equation 5]] are identical. Charging each transmitted bit once at 35 pJ/bit, the gradient exchange costs about 2.9 kJ of fabric energy per step on the smaller cluster and about 24.9 kJ on the larger, because 8× as many GPUs each send nearly the same gradient slice. The accelerators matter more. The step occupies 8,921 GPU-seconds on the smaller cluster and 12,983 on the larger, and an accelerator idle in the bubble or waiting on an exchange still draws power. If each accelerator draws similar power whether it computes, exchanges, or waits in the bubble, fleet energy per step grows with those GPU-seconds, so energy productivity falls as the fleet grows, even though each step finishes sooner.

**Systems insight**: The model prices links at line rate, hides a fixed share of the gradient exchange, treats tensor-parallel exchanges as unoverlapped, and ignores stragglers, so a measured step at either scale can differ. The trend is the lesson. Adding accelerators to a fixed batch shrinks the compute term in proportion, while the bubble and the gradient exchange do not shrink, so exposed communication and pipeline idle time claim a growing share of each step. [[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/00 Introduction/01 Introduction/V2-01.03 From Single Node to Fleet#^sec-vol2-introduction-ci-ratio|Section 1.2.4]] found the same pressure on the network side, where the gradient exchange climbs from 6 percent to 53 percent of fabric balance.

↪ Why the pipeline bubble grows as each replica receives fewer microbatches is developed in **[[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/02 Distributed ML/06 Distributed Training/V2-06.06 Model Parallelism#^sec-distributed-training-systems-systems-pipeline-parallelism-8748|Pipeline parallelism]]**.

↪ The energy that exposed collectives and idle, synchronization-stalled accelerators add to a distributed training run is measured in **[[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/04 The Responsible Fleet/15 Sustainable AI/V2-15.08 Training Vs. Inference Energy Analysis#^sec-sustainable-ai-distributed-systems-energy-optimization-5e83|Distributed systems energy optimization]]**.

How does this step time map onto the C$^3$ taxonomy?

    1.  0.9 s Compute (${C}_1$), 0.6 s Communication (${C}_2$), 0 s Coordination (${C}_3$)
    2.  1.3 s Compute (${C}_1$), 0.2 s Communication (${C}_2$), 0 s Coordination (${C}_3$)
    3.  0.9 s Coordination (${C}_3$), 0.4 s Communication (${C}_2$), 0.2 s Compute (${C}_1$)
    4.  0.9 s Compute (${C}_1$), 0.4 s Communication (${C}_2$), 0.2 s Coordination (${C}_3$)

A team profiles a distributed training step and measures ${T_{\text{compute}}}/{N} = 0.8\text{ s}$, ${T_{\text{comm}}}({N}) = 1.0\text{ s}$, ${T_{\text{sync}}}({N}) = 0.2\text{ s}$, with zero communication overlap (${T_{\text{overlap}}} = 0$). The compute-time fraction is ${f_{\text{compute}}} = 0.8 / 2.0 = 0.40$. Which engineering intervention is most directly indicated by the Fleet Law as a diagnostic instrument?

    1.  Replace existing GPUs with next-generation accelerators that deliver $2\times$ higher peak FLOP/s, since local compute is the primary path to speedup.
