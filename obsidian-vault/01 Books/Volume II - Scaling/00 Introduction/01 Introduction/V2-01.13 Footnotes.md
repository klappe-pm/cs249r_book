# V2-01.13 Footnotes

[[Home|Home]] · [[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/Volume 2 Overview|Volume Overview]] · [[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/00 Introduction/01 Introduction/V2-01 Introduction|V2-01 Introduction]]

^footnotes

<sup>1</sup> **Tensor processing unit (TPU)**: Google’s custom application-specific integrated circuit (ASIC) for machine learning, whose matrix units are systolic arrays, grids of multiply-accumulate cells that pass data between neighboring cells ([[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/00 Introduction/01 Introduction/V2-01.12 Source References#^ref-jouppi2017|Jouppi et al. 2017]]). At fleet scale, the inter-chip network matters as much as the array: TPU v4 joins its chips with an optically reconfigurable interconnect, so the topology of the network, not only the speed of each chip, determines how well many accelerators cooperate ([[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/00 Introduction/01 Introduction/V2-01.12 Source References#^ref-jouppi2023|Jouppi et al. 2023]]).

^fn1

<sup>2</sup> **Hardware failure rates at scale**: The arithmetic below uses an assumed per-GPU mean time to failure and independent failures to illustrate how interruption frequency grows with fleet size. Production job interruptions include software, networking, and maintenance as well as accelerator faults. Meta’s Llama 3 report provides an observed example of this broader interruption category ([[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/00 Introduction/01 Introduction/V2-01.12 Source References#^ref-dubey2024llama|Dubey et al. 2024]]); it does not establish a universal annual GPU failure rate.

^fn2

<sup>3</sup> **InfiniBand**: A data-center interconnect whose defining feature for ML is remote direct memory access (RDMA), which bypasses the OS kernel to move data directly between application memory on different machines at microsecond-scale latency. HDR InfiniBand provides about 25 GB/s line-rate bandwidth per link and NDR about 50 GB/s; that per-link bandwidth, relative to the computation each worker performs between exchanges, decides whether large-model training is compute-bound or communication-bound. RDMA-capable Ethernet (RoCE) now offers comparable line rates.

^fn3

<sup>4</sup> **Warehouse-scale computer (WSC)**: Barroso et al. ([[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/00 Introduction/01 Introduction/V2-01.12 Source References#^ref-barroso2019|2019]]) frame the data center itself as the computer. For ML fleets, power delivery, cooling topology, optical network layout, and bisection bandwidth become first-order constraints; the power limit of a 100 MW facility can cap training throughput before any single accelerator’s TFLOP/s does.

^fn4

<sup>5</sup> **Tokens**: Subword units produced by algorithms like byte-pair encoding, which iteratively merges the most frequent character pairs in a corpus. Token vocabulary size creates a direct systems trade-off: larger vocabularies can reduce sequence length for some languages and domains, but they expand the embedding table roughly in proportion to vocabulary size. GPT-3 used a 50,257-token vocabulary; moving to 100K+ tokens in newer models therefore trades tokenization efficiency against additional embedding memory.

^fn5

<sup>6</sup> **FLOPs vs. FLOP/s**: FLOPs denotes total computational work (operations performed), while FLOP/s denotes hardware throughput (operations per second); confusing the two leads to incorrect cost estimates. GPT-3 required 3.14 × 10²³ FLOPs of work; an A100 delivers 312 TFLOP/s of throughput. Dividing work by peak throughput gives ideal wall-clock time, the roughly 32 years on one A100 computed at the scale moment. Actual time is approximately $\text{work}/({\eta_{\text{hw}}} {R_{\text{peak}}})$, where the hardware utilization ${\eta_{\text{hw}}}$ is the achieved fraction of peak throughput, so utilization below one multiplies ideal time by $1/{\eta_{\text{hw}}}$.

^fn6

<sup>7</sup> **Scaling law saturation**: Power-law relationships with a baseline loss term approach that floor rather than predict unbounded loss reduction. Downstream task utility may also stop improving even while the fitted loss decreases. That gap between loss and utility motivates testing data efficiency or model compression against task utility and resource cost; neither technique guarantees that a genuine information limit can be overcome.

^fn7

<sup>8</sup> **CAP (consistency, availability, partition tolerance) theorem** (Brewer’s theorem): Brewer formulated the conjecture ([[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/00 Introduction/01 Introduction/V2-01.12 Source References#^ref-brewer2000|Brewer 2000]]), and Gilbert and Lynch ([[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/00 Introduction/01 Introduction/V2-01.12 Source References#^ref-gilbert2002|2002]]) later proved it for asynchronous networks, meaning networks with no timing bounds.

^fn8
