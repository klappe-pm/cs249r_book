# V2-01.04 AI Scaling Laws

[[Home|Home]] · [[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/Volume 2 Overview|Volume Overview]] · [[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/00 Introduction/01 Introduction/V2-01 Introduction|V2-01 Introduction]]

^sec-vol2-intro-ai-scaling-laws-a043

The seven to eight orders of magnitude of training compute traced at the scale moment, from AlexNet to GPT-4-class estimates near $10^{25}$ FLOPs, were not an accident of ambition. Each order-of-magnitude increase in compute demanded a further expansion of the machine learning fleet, and organizations paid for those expansions because the return on them had become predictable. Predictable is not the same as free. Measured learning curves estimate the quality a training budget buys, while device throughput, execution efficiency, and the completion deadline determine how much fleet capacity that budget requires.

Rich Sutton’s “bitter lesson” articulated the underlying principle: performance in machine learning is primarily driven by applying general methods at large scale rather than encoding human knowledge into algorithms ([[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/00 Introduction/01 Introduction/V2-01.12 Source References#^ref-sutton2019bitter|Sutton 2019]]). Scaling laws formalize this observation quantitatively. Model loss improves sublinearly as a power-law function of compute, dataset size, and parameters, ${\mathcal{L}}({X}) \propto {X}^{-{\alpha_{\text{scale}}}}$ for a resource ${X}$ (stated in full, with its irreducible floor, in theorem [1.1](https://mlsysbook.ai/vol2/01_introduction/01_introduction.html#thrm-introduction-power-law-scaling-formulation)) ([[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/00 Introduction/01 Introduction/V2-01.12 Source References#^ref-kaplan2020scaling|Kaplan et al. 2020]]; [[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/00 Introduction/01 Introduction/V2-01.12 Source References#^ref-hoffmann2022chinchilla|Hoffmann et al. 2022]]). Each equal-sized loss improvement therefore requires disproportionately more resources, a systems consequence this book later names as the empirical scaling law. Each scaling dimension (parameters, data, and compute) interacts with infrastructure constraints differently, making multi-dimensional efficiency optimization essential at production scale.

### Empirical Evidence for Scaling Laws

^sec-vol2-intro-empirical-evidence-scaling-laws-0105

Over the longer history of AI, the growth rate of training compute broke sharply upward around 2010 ([[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/00 Introduction/01 Introduction/V2-01.12 Source References#^ref-sevilla2022|Sevilla et al. 2022]]). A fit to Epoch AI’s records of notable models gives growth of about 1.5× per year before 2010, close to Moore’s law, and of about 4.3× per year in the deep learning era ([[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/00 Introduction/01 Introduction/V2-01.04 AI Scaling Laws#^fig-compute-trends|figure 7]]). The scale moment already decomposed that post-2012 climb into accelerator count, run duration, and delivered rate per accelerator ([[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/00 Introduction/01 Introduction/V2-01.02 The Scale Moment#^fig-training-compute-trend|figure 1]], panel b); that decomposition’s lower rate of about 3.9× per year comes from fitting only the runs from 2012 through 2024 that report all three factors. When compute demand outpaces Moore’s law by that much, misallocating a training budget becomes prohibitively expensive, turning empirical scaling laws from a research curiosity into an engineering necessity.

The fastest stretch was steeper still. ([[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/00 Introduction/01 Introduction/V2-01.12 Source References#^ref-sevilla2022|2022]]).

^fig-compute-trends

Kaplan et al. ([[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/00 Introduction/01 Introduction/V2-01.12 Source References#^ref-kaplan2020scaling|2020]]) demonstrated that transformer-based language-model loss follows predictable power-law relationships with model parameters, dataset size (measured in tokens), and total computational budget. The result was not a license to increase every dimension blindly; it showed that model quality depends on coordinated allocation across those resources, a point later sharpened by Chinchilla’s compute-optimal scaling analysis ([[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/00 Introduction/01 Introduction/V2-01.12 Source References#^ref-hoffmann2022chinchilla|Hoffmann et al. 2022]]).

Across parameter scales from $10^3$ to $10^9$, larger models are more sample-efficient, reaching any target loss with substantially fewer training tokens ([[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/00 Introduction/01 Introduction/V2-01.04 AI Scaling Laws#^fig-kaplan-scaling|figure 8]], panel a). Each token costs a larger model more compute, yet an early-stopped larger model still attains the target loss with less total compute than a smaller model trained to its plateau (panel b). Compute-efficient training therefore stops far short of full convergence.

Because each equal loss improvement costs disproportionately more, allocation grows more consequential as budgets grow. The question is how to divide a budget between the size of the model and the training data it consumes.

### Compute-Optimal Resource Allocation

^sec-vol2-intro-computeoptimal-resource-allocation-541a

A team that starts from a balanced allocation and spends the same training-compute budget on a model twice as large, with half the training tokens,[[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/00 Introduction/01 Introduction/V2-01.13 Footnotes#^fn5|⁵]] ends up with a higher-loss model that also costs twice as much memory and bandwidth to serve. Empirical studies of large language models (LLMs) show that, for a fixed computational budget and within the studied scaling regime, balancing model size and dataset size minimizes training loss.

Holding the budget fixed and varying model size, so that a larger model receives fewer tokens, traces a U-shaped IsoFLOP curve of training loss against parameter count, whose valley marks the best model size for that FLOP budget ([[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/00 Introduction/01 Introduction/V2-01.04 AI Scaling Laws#^fig-compute-optimal|figure 9]], panel a). As the budget grows, the valley moves toward larger models, and the optimal parameter count and the optimal number of training tokens rise along coordinated power laws (panel b), so compute-optimal training advances model size and token volume in tandem. Projected to the budget of Gopher, an earlier DeepMind language model, the fits call for 63B parameters trained on 1.4T tokens, about $5.3 \times 10^{23}$ FLOPs in total,[[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/00 Introduction/01 Introduction/V2-01.13 Footnotes#^fn6|⁶]] which is how compute-optimal frontiers guide resource allocation before cluster execution begins.

![[90 Assets/Volume II/01 Introduction/Cell 27 Output 1 203282be96.png]]

Figure 9: **Optimal Compute Allocation**: For a fixed computational budget, language-model loss depends on how the budget is divided between model size and training data. (a) IsoFLOP curves for nine budgets from $6 \times 10^{18}$ to $3 \times 10^{21}$ FLOPs, drawn from the paper’s parametric loss fit; each valley marks the best model size for its budget, and the valleys move toward larger models as the budget grows. (b) Compute-optimal parameter and token counts from the paper’s IsoFLOP analysis, projected to the Gopher budget, where they reach 63B parameters and 1.4T tokens. The two panels use different fitting methods from the same paper, so their optima do not coincide exactly. After Hoffmann et al. ([[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/00 Introduction/01 Introduction/V2-01.12 Source References#^ref-hoffmann2022chinchilla|2022]]).

^fig-compute-optimal

Within the studied regime, the Chinchilla result indicates that model size and training tokens should scale approximately proportionally for compute-optimal training, with a ratio on the order of 20 training tokens per parameter ([[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/00 Introduction/01 Introduction/V2-01.12 Source References#^ref-hoffmann2022chinchilla|Hoffmann et al. 2022]]). The allocation is a trade along a fixed product. Training a dense transformer costs about ${O_{\text{train}}} \approx 6{P} {D}$ FLOPs for ${P}$ parameters and ${D}$ training tokens, two per parameter per token in the forward pass and four in the backward pass (**[[05 Reference/Volume II - Scaling/V2 Single-Machine Foundations#^eq-appdx-dam-training-flops|equation 4]]**). A fixed budget therefore fixes the product of model size and tokens, and the ratio chooses the point along it. The projection in [[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/00 Introduction/01 Introduction/V2-01.04 AI Scaling Laws#^fig-compute-optimal|figure 9]], 1.4T tokens for 63B parameters, or about 22 tokens per parameter, sits close to that ratio. This corrected earlier guidance that favored making models larger while training them on comparatively fewer tokens. The objective is loss at a fixed training-compute budget. A deployment that serves the model repeatedly must also account for inference cost; its preferred allocation need not minimize training compute alone.

When sizing GPT-3, the authors used scaling-law extrapolations to choose model size and training data ([[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/00 Introduction/01 Introduction/V2-01.12 Source References#^ref-brown2020language|Brown et al. 2020]]; [[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/00 Introduction/01 Introduction/V2-01.12 Source References#^ref-kaplan2020scaling|Kaplan et al. 2020]]). They trained an established transformer architecture with 175B parameters and approximately 300 billion tokens, about 1.7 tokens per parameter. Against the roughly 20 tokens per parameter of the later Chinchilla results, that point was undertrained relative to compute-optimal token allocation ([[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/00 Introduction/01 Introduction/V2-01.12 Source References#^ref-hoffmann2022chinchilla|Hoffmann et al. 2022]]).

The practical decision is how to divide a fixed budget among model size, data, and training duration, and a compute-optimal allocation answers it only in useful computational work. Converting that work into elapsed time requires a separate systems model. Communication can grow relative to per-worker computation as a fixed workload is split more finely, creating bandwidth bottlenecks that reduce effective utilization. At multi-thousand-GPU scale, utilization depends on partitioning and interconnect. Narayanan et al. ([[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/00 Introduction/01 Introduction/V2-01.12 Source References#^ref-narayanan2021|2021]]) report Megatron-LM runs on 3,072 A100 GPUs at 52 percent of peak device throughput and note that slower interconnects or communication-heavy partitions hinder scaling.

### Power-Law Scaling and Operational Regimes

^sec-vol2-intro-mathematical-foundations-operational-regimes-9afe

Even when a cluster sustains high utilization on a compute-optimal run, scaling parameters and tokens in lockstep works only while both resources remain available and the loss curve stays well above its irreducible floor. That floor, left out of the power law as stated with the bitter lesson, explains why the same fleet can enter different operational regimes. The following analysis takes three cuts through these regimes, each with its own fleet consequence. The first cut is how compute and data are allocated, which decides the resource to buy next. The second is where diminishing returns set in with dataset size, which decides when storage and data pipelines matter more than accelerators. The last is when in the model’s lifecycle computation is spent, which decides the part of the fleet, training or serving, that carries the load.

**Theorem 1.1: Power-law scaling formulation**

Scaling laws are expressed formally as power-law relationships. The general formulation is:

$$
{\mathcal{L}}({X}) = {A_{\mathcal{L}}} {X}^{-{\alpha_{\text{scale}}}} + {C_{\mathcal{L}}}
$$

where loss ${\mathcal{L}}$ decreases as resource quantity ${X}$ increases, following a power-law decay with rate ${\alpha_{\text{scale}}}$, plus a baseline constant ${C_{\mathcal{L}}}$, the irreducible loss (a constant of the fit, not one of the C$^3$ labels). Here, ${\mathcal{L}}({X})$ represents the loss achieved with a resource quantity such as parameters, tokens, or compute; ${A_{\mathcal{L}}}$ and ${C_{\mathcal{L}}}$ are task-dependent constants; and ${\alpha_{\text{scale}}}$ is the scaling exponent that characterizes the rate of performance improvement. A larger value of ${\alpha_{\text{scale}}}$ signifies more efficient performance improvements with respect to scaling.

For a fleet, the exponent is the price list. Halving the reducible term ${A_{\mathcal{L}}} {X}^{-{\alpha_{\text{scale}}}}$ requires multiplying the resource ${X}$ by $2^{1/{\alpha_{\text{scale}}}}$, so the smaller the exponent, the larger the multiple of accelerators, tokens, or training time that each further step in quality demands. These power-law predictions find empirical validation in the family of learning curves plotted in [[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/00 Introduction/01 Introduction/V2-01.04 AI Scaling Laws#^fig-loss-vs-n-d|figure 10]]. Across six model scales spanning from 393.2K to 708M parameters, test loss descends predictably as dataset size grows from $10^7$ toward $3\times 10^{10}$ tokens, with smaller architectures flattening into parameter-capacity plateaus while larger models sustain power-law error reduction.

#### Resource-Constrained Scaling Regimes

^sec-vol2-intro-resourceconstrained-scaling-regimes-062d

The first cut follows which resource runs short. The capacity plateaus show why collecting more tokens for a project whose cluster cannot train a larger model strands data in storage. The opposite imbalance, buying more accelerators for a project whose dataset is already exhausted, instead risks wasting hardware on overfitting. Applying scaling laws in practice is therefore a scarcity diagnosis that asks which resource prevents balanced growth first. Compute budget, data availability, and model size create three regimes with different optimal responses.

In a compute-limited regime, compute scarcity restricts scaling potential despite abundant training data; measured learning curves guide the allocation between model size and training tokens within that budget. In a data-limited regime, computational resources exceed what the available dataset can support, meaning that increasing model size is useful only if validation measurements show that the available data can support additional capacity. Between these extremes lies the optimal regime, where compute and data grow in balance following compute-optimal scaling laws, as DeepMind demonstrated with Chinchilla by outperforming much larger models through proportional scaling of model size and training data ([[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/00 Introduction/01 Introduction/V2-01.12 Source References#^ref-hoffmann2022chinchilla|Hoffmann et al. 2022]]). Recognizing which regime governs a given project prevents common inefficiencies: over-parameterized models with insufficient training data, or under-parameterized models that waste available compute. For the fleet, the diagnosis decides where the next investment goes. A compute-limited project needs accelerator-hours, while a data-limited one gains more from the storage and data pipelines of **[[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/01 The Fleet/04 Data Storage/V2-04 Data Storage#^sec-data-storage|Data Storage]]** than from additional accelerators.

^fig-loss-vs-n-d

The second cut through the operational regimes follows the return on additional data as a dataset grows, which decides when buying data stops paying. On log-log coordinates, dataset scaling passes through three regions ([[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/00 Introduction/01 Introduction/V2-01.04 AI Scaling Laws#^fig-data-scaling-regimes|figure 11]]). In the initial Small Data Region, generalization error lingers near the upper Best Guess Error ceiling, the error of a model that has learned almost nothing from its data. As data volume expands into the central Power-Law Region, error drops sharply along a constant slope where data investment yields predictable returns. Finally, in the Irreducible Error Region, the curve flattens asymptotically against the Bayes noise floor, where further dataset expansion provides negligible gain. That floor is the error that remains even for a perfect predictor, because the inputs do not fully determine the labels. The systems consequence depends on the region. In the Power-Law Region, finite high-quality data, not compute, can become the binding constraint, which is why data efficiency complements brute-force scaling. In the Irreducible Error Region, more data of the same kind stops paying.

↪ The data wall, and the storage traffic a fleet incurs when it generates, filters, and versions synthetic training data, are developed in **[[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/01 The Fleet/04 Data Storage/V2-04.08 Versioned Data and Commit Boundaries#^sec-storage-synthetic-fuel|The synthetic fuel line]]**.

An observed plateau does not by itself establish that the irreducible floor has been reached, however. Limited coverage, insufficient model capacity, and incomplete optimization can also flatten a learning curve, so the engineering task is to rule out these remediable limits before purchasing more data or compute.

The third cut follows lifecycle stage, because additional compute can be spent on the foundation run, on adapting the model afterward, or on each request in deployment ([[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/00 Introduction/01 Introduction/V2-01.04 AI Scaling Laws#^fig-scaling-regimes|figure 12]]). Pretraining scaling (orange curve) increases the resource investment in the foundation run. Posttraining scaling (blue curve) adapts an existing model through fine-tuning or preference optimization (training on human rankings of its outputs). Test-time scaling (green curve, “long thinking”) spends additional computation on a particular request through intermediate generation (writing out reasoning steps before the answer), search, or sampling, trading serving latency and throughput for a possible quality gain. Each phase stresses a different part of the fleet. Pretraining is one long, failure-sensitive foundation run whose scaling-law gains must be realized in wall-clock time. Posttraining replaces that single large job with many smaller, coordination-heavy runs. Test-time scaling moves computation into the serving path, where it competes with latency targets. The schematic curves do not establish a universal efficiency ranking among these choices, so the full treatments belong with their workloads. A resource plan must compare measured task improvements and costs before choosing where to spend the next increment of compute.

### Sustainability and Cost Implications

^sec-vol2-intro-sustainability-cost-implications-0473

GPT-3’s training run alone occupied about 148,000 GPU-days and drew an estimated 1,287 MWh of electricity ([[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/00 Introduction/01 Introduction/V2-01.12 Source References#^ref-patterson2021carbon|Patterson et al. 2021]]), and later frontier runs use more accelerators for longer. While scaling laws trace a path to lower loss, they also expose rapidly escalating resource demands whose training and deployment costs grow faster than the quality gains they buy.

Where that electricity is generated matters as much as how much is drawn. The same training run can differ by roughly 41× in CO<sub>2</sub> emissions depending on grid location, from about 20 g/kWh of CO<sub>2</sub> for Quebec hydropower to about 820 g/kWh for Poland coal. The siting of the cluster is therefore a first-order infrastructure decision. Financial cost scales with the same accelerator-days and megawatt-hours, which limits who can do large-scale research at all.

↪ How site selection and grid carbon intensity set the emissions of the same training run is developed in **[[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/04 The Responsible Fleet/15 Sustainable AI/V2-15.03 The Scale of Environmental Impact#^sec-sustainable-ai-scale-environmental-impact-ac9a|The Scale of Environmental Impact]]**.

Scaling laws do not guarantee unbounded improvement. Each incremental performance gain must be weighed against its resource cost. As systems approach practical scaling limits, the emphasis shifts from scaling alone to efficient scaling: balancing performance, cost, energy consumption, and environmental impact.[[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/00 Introduction/01 Introduction/V2-01.13 Footnotes#^fn7|⁷]]

### When Scaling Laws Break Down

^sec-vol2-intro-scaling-law-breakdown-conditions-1f8c

Doubling a model’s parameter count without expanding its training dataset or interconnect capacity can stall loss improvements even as cluster cost doubles, because power-law fits hold only within the balanced regime in which they were measured. Most breakdowns at the edge of that regime share one root cause, growth in one dimension that outpaces the others. Hoffmann et al. ([[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/00 Introduction/01 Introduction/V2-01.12 Source References#^ref-hoffmann2022chinchilla|2022]]) show that many large language models were undertrained because model size grew faster than training-token budgets, and that compute-optimal training requires scaling parameters and tokens together. Hardware ceilings belong to the same family, because memory bandwidth, interconnect capacity, and I/O throughput cap how far a larger model can be distributed at all.

Each imbalance requires different evidence. Training measurements can reveal an unsuitable optimization schedule. Held-out evaluation can distinguish improved generalization from memorization. Execution traces can reveal whether bandwidth or I/O prevents the fleet from completing the planned work. A lower benchmark loss also does not establish robustness to adversarial or out-of-distribution inputs. Each possible breakdown type pairs with a candidate cause and a representative scenario ([[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/00 Introduction/01 Introduction/V2-01.04 AI Scaling Laws#^tbl-scaling-breakdown|table 2]]). The pairing guides investigation; it does not infer a cause from a plateau alone.

| **Dimension scaled**   |  **Type of breakdown**  | **Underlying cause**                           | **Example scenario**                          |
|:-----------------------|:-----------------------:|:-----------------------------------------------|:----------------------------------------------|
| **Model Size**         |       Overfitting       | Model capacity exceeds available data          | Billion-parameter model on limited dataset    |
| **Training Data Size** |   Diminishing Returns   | Saturation of new or diverse information       | Scaling web text beyond useful threshold      |
| **Compute Budget**     | Underutilized Resources | Insufficient training steps or inefficient use | Large model with truncated training duration  |
| **Imbalanced Scaling** |      Inefficiency       | Uncoordinated increase in model/data/compute   | Doubling model size without more data or time |
| **All Dimensions**     |       Saturation        | Exhaustion of learnable patterns in the domain | No further gains despite scaling all inputs   |

Table 2: **Possible Scaling Breakdowns**: The rows describe risks and representative scenarios, not inevitable consequences of increasing one resource. Training and validation measurements establish which failure mode, if any, applies to a particular allocation.

^tbl-scaling-breakdown

**Checkpoint 1.2: Applying scaling laws**

These questions check whether scaling laws are being used as resource-allocation tools:

**Compute vs. data regime**: identify the learning-curve evidence needed to choose between a larger model trained for fewer steps and a smaller model trained for more steps under fixed compute and limited data.

**Scaling breakdown**: explain the risks of doubling model parameters at fixed dataset size, and what training and validation evidence would distinguish undertraining from overfitting.

**Chinchilla optimality**: explain why Chinchilla optimality serves as a resource-allocation target and identify the minimized quantity.

**Compute vs. capability**: classify the claim as true or false: scaling laws predict that doubling compute always yields a doubling of model capability.

### Combining Efficiency with Scaling

^sec-vol2-intro-integrating-efficiency-scaling-a513

Data saturation, infrastructure bottlenecks, and diminishing returns set hard limits on what brute-force scaling can achieve. The same constraints, however, point toward solutions along three interconnected dimensions. When compute is the scarce resource, algorithmic efficiency uses techniques such as sparsity (computing with only a fraction of the weights) and distillation (training a small model to imitate a large one) to extract more capability per FLOP. When high-quality tokens run short, data efficiency uses curriculum learning (ordering examples by difficulty) and active selection (choosing the most informative examples) to extract more information per training example. When the cluster itself stalls on transfers or barriers, systems efficiency uses communication hiding and pipeline overlap, which run transfers behind useful computation, to extract more utilization per accelerator. Because each dimension addresses a different breakdown condition from [[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/00 Introduction/01 Introduction/V2-01.04 AI Scaling Laws#^tbl-scaling-breakdown|table 2]], identifying the limiting resource determines whether efficiency work should target the algorithm, the data, or the execution system. Systems efficiency is the subject of **[[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/02 Distributed ML/05 Collective Communication/V2-05 Collective Communication#^sec-collective-communication|Collective Communication]]** and **[[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/02 Distributed ML/06 Distributed Training/V2-06 Distributed Training#^sec-distributed-training-systems|Distributed Training]]**; the latter also develops algorithmic levers such as reduced precision and sparse mixture-of-experts execution (running each token through only a few of a model’s subnetworks). Data efficiency under device budgets returns in **[[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/03 The Fleet in Production/11 Edge Intelligence/V2-11.06 Data Efficiency#^sec-edge-intelligence-data-efficiency-c701|Data Efficiency]]**.

Scaling laws and efficiency techniques determine how much computation large models require and how well the fleet uses it. They do not, however, address the physical and logical constraints that the fleet itself imposes. Hardware fails, bandwidth saturates, distributed systems face fundamental impossibility results, and societal impact at scale demands governance. These constraints cannot be optimized away; they must be engineered around.

**Self-Check: Question**

1.  A project team observes a validation loss decrease after doubling its training compute budget. Under the fitted power law ${\mathcal{L}}({X}) = {A_{\mathcal{L}}} {X}^{-{\alpha_{\text{scale}}}} + {C_{\mathcal{L}}}$, with positive amplitude and exponent, what follows for another doubling within the same regime?

    1.  The next doubling must yield the same absolute loss decrease because scaling is linear in arithmetic space.
    2.  The next doubling yields a smaller absolute loss decrease; a smaller percentage decrease additionally depends on the loss floor.
    3.  The next doubling immediately prevents any measurable loss reduction.
    4.  The next doubling yields twice the absolute loss decrease because the scaling exponent compounds.

2.  An organization has a 512-GPU cluster for two weeks and a domain corpus of 5 billion tokens. What evidence is needed to choose between a larger model trained for fewer steps and a smaller model trained over repeated epochs?

3.  True or False: Empirical scaling laws show that model loss is determined solely by the total raw FLOP budget expended during training, regardless of whether those FLOPs are allocated to model parameter count or training token volume.

4.  According to the Chinchilla compute-optimal scaling laws (Hoffmann et al., 2022), training tokens and model parameters should scale approximately proportionally at a ratio of roughly 20 tokens per parameter. If a team designs a compute-optimal 70-billion-parameter model, what training dataset size is required, and what was the historical scaling imbalance of earlier models such as GPT-3 (175B parameters on 300B tokens)?

    1.  350 billion tokens; earlier models like GPT-3 were overtrained because token counts exceeded parameter counts.
    2.  70 billion tokens; earlier models like GPT-3 were compute-optimal but lacked sufficient inference context length.
    3.  14 trillion tokens; earlier models like GPT-3 were severely data-saturated and suffered from parameter memorization.
    4.  1.4 trillion tokens; earlier models like GPT-3 were undertrained relative to their parameter size due to training on only ~1.7 tokens per parameter.
