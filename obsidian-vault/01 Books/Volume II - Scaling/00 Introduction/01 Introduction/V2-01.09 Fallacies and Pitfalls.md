# V2-01.09 Fallacies and Pitfalls

[[Home|Home]] · [[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/Volume 2 Overview|Volume Overview]] · [[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/00 Introduction/01 Introduction/V2-01 Introduction|V2-01 Introduction]]

^sec-vol2-intro-fallacies-pitfalls-f804

The following fallacies and pitfalls capture architectural mistakes that waste development resources, miss performance targets, or deploy systems critically mismatched to their operating constraints. Each represents a pattern observed repeatedly in the transition from single-machine ML to the machine learning fleet.

**Fallacy**: *Accelerators twice as fast halve the training step.*

An accelerator upgrade that changes only arithmetic throughput shrinks the ${T_{\text{compute}}}/{N}$ term of the Fleet Law while leaving the communication terms unchanged. On the 1,024-GPU reference job of notebook [1.1](https://mlsysbook.ai/vol2/01_introduction/01_introduction.html#nbk-introduction-coordination-energy-tax), local computation takes 7.25 s of the 8.7 s modeled step, and the pipeline bubble, idle time measured in stage computation, adds 0.53 s. Accelerators 2× faster halve both, but the 0.93 s of exposed tensor-parallel and gradient exchange does not speed up, so the step falls only to about 4.8 s, a 1.8× speedup rather than 2×. The larger the share of the step that communication occupies, the further the gain falls short. The direction of the effect is general: relieving the Compute axis tends to expose Communication or Coordination as the binding cost, the displacement of overhead described with the Fleet Law. A real upgrade may also change memory capacity, transfer rates, or available overlap. An accelerator purchase must therefore be judged against the fleet’s measured step decomposition, not against the datasheet ratio.

**Pitfall**: *Assuming that reliable components make a reliable job.*

A per-GPU MTTF of 50,000 hours describes a part that rarely fails. A synchronous job, however, needs all of its parts at once. As [[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/00 Introduction/01 Introduction/V2-01.02 The Scale Moment#^sec-vol2-introduction-scale-moment|section 1.1]] showed, a job spanning 25,000 GPUs has an MTBF of about 2 hours, or roughly 12 interruptions per day. Component reliability characterizes one part; the reliability gap ([[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/00 Introduction/01 Introduction/V2-01.05 Constraints of Scale#^sec-vol2-introduction-reliability-gap|section 1.4.1]]) characterizes the job, whose availability decays exponentially with the number of parts it requires simultaneously. Recovery practices that suit a single machine, such as manual restart and infrequent checkpoints, fail at fleet scale even though no individual part has become less reliable.

**Fallacy**: *Edge deployment efficiency requirements are simply scaled-down versions of cloud requirements.*

The fallacy treats edge systems as resource-constrained cloud systems. Edge devices face qualitatively different constraints. For example, an autonomous vehicle traveling at 120 km/h covers 3.33 m during every 100 ms of processing delay, adding that distance to its reaction distance. While cloud deployments scale to kilowatts, a phone-class device of the kind Archetype C models sustains only a few watts. A cloud-optimized model with 95 percent accuracy and 50 ms latency might be unusable on an edge device where thermal throttling increases latency to 200 ms and drains the battery in under an hour.

**Pitfall**: *Treating a compute budget as a wall-clock performance prediction.*

This pitfall, sometimes called the linear scaling fallacy, confuses useful computational work with the time required to execute it. The empirical loss relation ${\mathcal{L}}({X}) = {A_{\mathcal{L}}} {X}^{-{\alpha_{\text{scale}}}} + {C_{\mathcal{L}}}$ estimates model quality within a validated regime; the Fleet Law separately estimates the time needed to perform that work. A team distributing a fixed training workload might predict a 3× improvement but achieve only 1.8× if coordination and communication overhead consumes 40 percent of the scaled step time. This shortfall does not by itself invalidate a loss scaling law. It shows why a resource plan needs both a model-quality estimate and a measured execution-cost estimate.

Accelerators twice as fast halve the computation and the bubble, which is idle time measured in stage computation, but leave the exchange unchanged. What happens to step time, and what does this imply for the purchase decision?

    1.  Step time falls from 8.71 s to about 4.82 s, a $1.8\times$ speedup rather than $2\times$; the unchanged communication caps the gain.
    2.  Step time falls from 8.71 s to about 4.36 s because doubling accelerator throughput halves every distributed cost.
    3.  Step time falls to about 5.09 s, a $1.7\times$ speedup, because only the 7.25 s of computation halves while the bubble and the exchange stay fixed.
    4.  Step time falls to about 5.75 s, a $1.5\times$ speedup, because faster accelerators expose twice as much of the gradient exchange.
