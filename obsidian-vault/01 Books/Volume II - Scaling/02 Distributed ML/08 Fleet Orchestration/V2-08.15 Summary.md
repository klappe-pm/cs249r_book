# V2-08.15 Summary

[[Home|Home]] · [[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/Volume 2 Overview|Volume Overview]] · [[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/02 Distributed ML/08 Fleet Orchestration/V2-08 Fleet Orchestration|V2-08 Fleet Orchestration]]

^sec-fleet-orchestration-summary

Fleet orchestration turns data center capacity into useful ML work by deciding which jobs hold which accelerators, where, and for how long. Orchestration spends Coordination, the third axis of C³, so that Compute and Communication capacity turn into useful progress. In Fleet Law terms (**[[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/00 Introduction/01 Introduction/V2-01.06 The C³ Taxonomy and Foundations of Scale#^eq-fleet-law|equation 1.3]]**), placement sets the communication term ${T_{\text{comm}}}({N})$ of every step, and preemption and recovery, amortized over the run, enter the coordination term ${T_{\text{sync}}}({N})$. Queue wait and gang admission are coordination costs too, but they delay the first step rather than lengthening any step, so they appear in end-to-end completion time rather than in the per-step Fleet Law. Multi-tenancy and quota policy decide how much of that time allocated compute spends doing useful work. Hardware capability sets the feasible choices; the scheduler turns those choices into useful allocations by preserving ownership, coordinating recovery, and matching placement to workload demand.

Feasibility is the first filter. Gang size, topology locality, multi-dimensional fit, and accelerator compatibility decide which allocations a job can actually run, and only then can a fairness policy choose among them (principle [[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/02 Distributed ML/V2 Distributed ML Principles#^pri-feasibility-before-fairness|15]]). Quota borrowing extends the same reasoning across teams, since a guarantee is worth what the platform can reclaim within its response-time objective rather than the number printed on the quota.

Placement changes the physical paths available to a workload. Aligning model parallelism with the network hierarchy reduces exposed communication when placement is the limiting constraint, but it cannot create fabric capacity that is not there. Elastic resizing and spot capacity widen the set of feasible choices by trading worker continuity for flexibility and price. ML-aware schedulers sharpen the choice among them with workload signals such as attained service, iteration boundaries, finish-time ratios, and goodput. Every one of these levers pays a transition or integration cost that must be measured against the progress it buys.

At serving time, the fleet scheduler preserves a capacity envelope of indivisible replicas with SLO headroom and moves the training-serving boundary on forecast demand, holding ready headroom for what the forecast misses. Request-level routing and batching stay with the serving system. When that envelope, the quotas around it, or the placements inside it go wrong, the visible symptom is usually a full queue beside idle accelerators. In practice, the root cause is more often a policy written for an earlier fleet than a defect in the scheduling algorithm.

**Key Takeaways: Scheduling is systems engineering**

- **Distributed scheduling is hard**: Cluster scheduling must coordinate allocation ownership across machines despite partial failures, network partitions, and stale observations. Control planes balance prompt recovery against the risk of authorizing a replacement while an old worker still runs.
- **Gang scheduling prevents deadlock but reduces flexibility**: Gang scheduling gives rigid execution groups atomic resource allocation to prevent hold-and-wait deadlocks, while backfilling and elastic resizing recover flexibility within the job’s minimum viable gang and transition constraints.
- **Fairness is measured over feasible bundles**: DRF equalizes each tenant’s dominant normalized share across GPUs, CPU, memory, and network, but only among bundles that already pass gang, topology, and compatibility constraints (principle [[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/02 Distributed ML/V2 Distributed ML Principles#^pri-feasibility-before-fairness|15]]). Slowdown and maximum feasible wait test whether the resulting policy avoids delay and starvation, and a quota guarantee holds only as far as lent capacity can be reclaimed in time.
- **Topology determines performance**: Where GPUs are placed within the cluster hierarchy changes the communication paths available to a job. Locality can reduce exposed communication, but waiting for a compact placement also costs time; compare end-to-end completion rather than iteration throughput alone.
- **Elastic and interruptible capacity must repay its transitions**: A forced preemption or spot interruption discards work since the last durable checkpoint and then pays reload and warmup, while a resize pauses the job. A resize pays only if the new scale persists long enough to amortize that pause, and spot capacity saves money only while the spot price, scaled by the ratio of billed to retained productive time, stays below the on-demand price.
- **Scheduling signals must match the objective**: Workload characteristics support different improvements: attained service addresses runtime uncertainty, iteration boundaries reduce interruption cost, finish-time ratios expose unfair delay, and goodput guides adaptive allocation. Each policy requires validation against the fleet’s workload mix and recovery costs.
- **Useful utilization creates economic capacity**: Reducing avoidable idle time, often by updating policies written for an earlier fleet rather than by changing the scheduling algorithm, can deliver more useful work from an existing fleet. Distinguish allocated, active, and productive capacity before valuing an improvement, and preserve the headroom required for recovery and latency targets.

^callout-takeaways--8-15-885cb061

Placing a tensor-parallel gang makes the displacement of overhead (principle [[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/02 Distributed ML/V2 Distributed ML Principles#^pri-conservation-overhead|14]]) concrete. Co-locating the gang keeps its collectives on NVLink and shrinks the exposed communication of every step, but the scheduler pays for that locality in coordination time while it waits for a feasible contiguous allocation. The bottleneck moves from collective bandwidth to queue delay or fragmentation rather than disappearing, so the right placement is the one that minimizes end-to-end completion time, not iteration time alone.

**What’s Next: From allocation to useful work**

An allocation shows that a job holds capacity, not that the capacity produces useful work. A training job can hold a complete allocation while it restarts, waits for input, or exposes communication in every step. A serving replica can hold a full node while its decode steps stream weights and leave most of its arithmetic idle. The productive tier says how much of an allocation became useful work but not which part of a job’s life lost the rest. The owners of scheduling, recovery, and execution therefore each see their own loss, and none can see which is largest. Those separate losses become one product whose factors can be compared once every hour of capacity is followed from the moment the fleet offers it until it becomes useful work, with each loss charged to the boundary where it occurs.
