# V2-08.08 Elastic Scheduling

[[Home|Home]] · [[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/Volume 2 Overview|Volume Overview]] · [[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/02 Distributed ML/08 Fleet Orchestration/V2-08 Fleet Orchestration|V2-08 Fleet Orchestration]]

^sec-fleet-orchestration-elastic-training

When cluster demand fluctuates, rigid allocation creates two categories of waste. During off-peak hours, a job running on 256 GPUs cannot absorb idle resources to speed up training. During peak demand, the same job cannot release excess resources to accommodate higher-priority work without being fully preempted. The scheduler’s only options are “let it keep all its resources” or “kill it entirely,” with nothing in between.

Elastic scheduling expands this binary choice. The recovery mechanics of elastic training show how a distributed job can adjust its worker count, adapt its batch policy, and reconstruct its communication group after a failure. From the scheduler’s perspective, the same capability solves a resource-allocation problem under fluctuating demand. A training job might start with 128 GPUs, scale up to 256 when resources become available, and scale back to 192 when higher-priority work arrives, provided each allocation supports a viable worker group. Resizing may pause training and restart workers from saved state. Whether worker processes restart depends on the framework; TorchElastic restarts its worker group when membership changes. The scheduler therefore chooses among supported allocation sizes, each with a transition cost, rather than assuming uninterrupted progress at any resource level.

↩︎ Batch-size and learning-rate adjustment and communication-group contraction during elastic recovery are established in **[[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/02 Distributed ML/07 Fault Tolerance/V2-07.11 Elastic Recovery#^sec-fault-tolerance-reliability-reliability-elastic-training-mechanisms-49b1|Recovery adaptation mechanisms]]**.

### The Scheduler-Framework Contract

^sec-fleet-orchestration-elastic-contract

If a scheduler reclaims four nodes from a running job by abruptly killing their containers, or adds four idle nodes without triggering a new rendezvous, the surviving ranks treat the change as a fatal collective timeout rather than an elastic resize. Resizing a live job without crashing it requires an explicit contract between the scheduler and the training framework. For planned resizing, the scheduler and framework coordinate resource release through defined signals and an agreed adaptation window. Unexpected failures can bypass that window, so the framework must also recover from valid saved state. Its recovery path reconstructs communication groups and either preserves the global batch or applies a validated batch-size and learning-rate policy. The worker-membership lifecycle therefore behaves as a state machine centered on active training ([[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/02 Distributed ML/08 Fleet Orchestration/V2-08.08 Elastic Scheduling#^fig-fleet-orchestration-elastic-state-machine|figure 11]]). Idle GPUs trigger scale-up, preemptions trigger scale-down, and host timeouts or ECC errors divert ranks to worker replacement; each path returns to active training once the communication group is rebuilt or a spare node joins.

![[90 Assets/Volume II/08 Fleet Orchestration/Elastic Training State Machine 8ff0e04b59.svg]]

Figure 11: **Elastic Training Worker Lifecycle and State Machine**: The state machine governing dynamic worker membership. Active training loops on step completion and leaves that state along three paths: idle GPUs trigger scale up, a preemption for higher-priority work triggers scale down, and a host timeout or ECC error triggers worker replacement. Each path rebuilds the communication group and returns to active training (dashed arrows), and a spot reclamation deadline can force a shrinking job straight into replacement. Worker processes may restart during resizing, so recovery requires the application to restore valid training state.

^fig-fleet-orchestration-elastic-state-machine

From the scheduler’s perspective, this contract is defined by three governing parameters: the elastic range, the transition cost, and the scaling efficiency curve. The elastic range, $[{N_{\text{min}}}, {N_{\text{max}}}]$, specifies lower and upper worker bounds; the job’s parallelism layout determines the valid allocation increments within them. The transition cost includes any checkpoint, restart, state redistribution, and rendezvous required by the runtime. Measuring the full interruption determines how frequently the scheduler can trigger reallocations before transition overhead wipes out any throughput advantage. Finally, the scaling efficiency curve quantifies throughput at each worker count relative to linear scaling. The marginal throughput of each added worker follows from that curve and indicates whether assigning idle GPUs to this job yields more aggregate progress than holding them for an assembling gang-scheduled workload. Together, these parameters provide the scheduler with the quantitative bounds needed to resize jobs dynamically without treating elasticity as free capacity.

The scheduler must also account for the effect of worker count on global batch size (${B_{\text{global}}} = {B_{\text{per-worker}}}\times {N}$), where the per-worker batch counts examples contributing to one optimizer update, including gradient accumulation. Holding the global batch constant requires each remaining worker to process more examples after a shrink. Larger microbatches consume more memory; additional accumulation steps extend the update time. Preserving the batch removes one source of optimization change but does not guarantee identical convergence. Allowing the global batch to change instead requires validating the learning-rate policy and resulting model quality, so throughput alone cannot determine the best allocation.

``` bash
# Launch elastic training with min 32 to max 128 workers
torchrun \
  --nnodes=4:16 \
  --nproc_per_node=8 \
  --rdzv_backend=c10d \
  --rdzv_endpoint=head-node:29500 \
  --max_restarts=3 \
  train.py
```

Listing 2: **TorchElastic Launch Configuration**: The `--nnodes=4:16` range configures the elastic worker group to admit 4 to 16 nodes, or 32 to 128 workers. A separate cluster scheduler or operator must acquire, launch, and release nodes within that range.

^lst-fleet-orchestration-torchelastic

TorchElastic sets the elastic range in its launch configuration as a minimum and maximum node count ([[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/02 Distributed ML/08 Fleet Orchestration/V2-08.08 Elastic Scheduling#^lst-fleet-orchestration-torchelastic|listing 2]]). There, the `--nnodes=4:16` specification configures TorchElastic’s rendezvous and worker-membership range as 4 to 16 nodes (32 to 128 workers with 8 processes per node). It does not acquire or resize cluster resources; a separate cluster scheduler or operator must place the nodes and start or stop elastic agents as capacity changes.

**Systems Perspective 8.2: Elastic vs. rigid scheduling**

Consider a congested cluster where a 128-GPU data-parallel job, with no model-parallel constraint on its worker count, waits 8 hours for a full gang allocation. This first comparison assumes throughput scales linearly with worker count, equivalent learning progress per processed example, and negligible resize overhead; the later scaling decision relaxes the throughput and transition assumptions.

**Rigid scheduling**: Wait 8 hours, then train for 24 hours at full throughput. Total wall-clock: 32 hours.

**Elastic scheduling** (min 32, max 128):

- Start immediately with 32 GPUs (throughput = 25 percent of full)
- At hour 2, when 64 GPUs free, scale to 64 (throughput = 50 percent)
- At hour 8, when the full gang frees and the rigid job would also start, scale to 128 (throughput = 100 percent)

Work completed during the 8-hour queue wait, in hours of full-scale training:

- Hours 0 to 2: 25 percent throughput $\times$ 2 hours = 0.5
- Hours 2 to 8: 50 percent throughput $\times$ 6 hours = 3
- Total: 3.5 hours of equivalent full-scale work

The elastic job completes in approximately 28.5 hours (8 hours of partial-scale training plus 20.5 hours at full scale), saving 3.5 hours compared to rigid scheduling. This improvement of about 11 percent comes entirely from using resources productively during what would otherwise be idle queue time.

The scheduler cannot treat every worker count as a valid elastic configuration. Model parallelism constrains the allocation increment when the resize policy keeps tensor and pipeline layouts fixed and adds or removes whole data-parallel replicas. For a 175B-parameter job that relies on 8-way tensor parallelism within each node and 8 pipeline stages across nodes, this policy operates at the granularity of a whole model replica of 64 GPUs. The pipeline depth of 8 is the smallest power of two at which each GPU’s share of the mixed-precision Adaptive Moment Estimation (Adam) training state fits in its HBM when that state is not sharded across replicas. The layout of **[[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/02 Distributed ML/06 Distributed Training/V2-06 Distributed Training#^sec-distributed-training-systems|Distributed Training]]** instead uses a shallower pipeline and shards optimizer state across the data-parallel group with ZeRO (**[[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/02 Distributed ML/06 Distributed Training/V2-06.04 Data Parallelism#^sec-distributed-training-systems-systems-memoryefficient-data-parallelism-zero-fsdp-0e69|Memory-efficient data parallelism with ZeRO and FSDP]]**). This job gives up that sharded layout on purpose. Keeping each replica’s state self-contained costs memory, but it lets the scheduler add or remove a replica without redistributing every other replica’s optimizer shards. The 175B examples in [[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/02 Distributed ML/08 Fleet Orchestration/V2-08.08 Elastic Scheduling#^sec-fleet-orchestration-elastic-policies|section 8.7.3]] assume these self-contained replicas. The job can scale between 128 nodes (1,024 GPUs) and 32 nodes (256 GPUs) one replica at a time, changing the data-parallel degree. Removing a single GPU from a tensor-parallel group would instead require a new sharding layout and redistribution of training state. Changing the pipeline degree would also require repartitioning stages and rebuilding the pipeline schedule. A runtime that supports these transitions can expose them to the scheduler, but their feasibility and measured costs belong in the resize contract rather than following automatically from data-parallel elasticity.

The transition cost also constrains the scheduler’s resize frequency. Each scaling event can interrupt useful work while the framework reconstructs its communication group and restores or redistributes state. The duration must be measured for the chosen runtime and checkpoint path. The scheduler must amortize this cost over the subsequent run duration, so small resize increments are only worthwhile if the job will run at the new scale for significantly longer than the transition window, a test that [[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/02 Distributed ML/08 Fleet Orchestration/V2-08.08 Elastic Scheduling#^sec-fleet-orchestration-elastic-policies|section 8.7.3]] applies to resize policies.

### Scheduler Integration

^sec-fleet-orchestration-elastic-scheduler-integration

Without scheduler integration, a job that declares an elastic range $[{N_{\text{min}}}, {N_{\text{max}}}]$ in its launch script still waits in the queue for its full node count and still gets killed outright when a higher-priority job arrives. Integrating the $[{N_{\text{min}}}, {N_{\text{max}}}]$ bounds into the scheduler lets it start a job early at the minimum, expand the job into newly idle capacity, or shrink it to release borrowed capacity without terminating the logical job. Which action wins depends on the job’s scaling curve and transition cost, and the policies below compare the choices by the work completed over the same time window.

Each action must respect the framework contract. The most consequential of the three is **graceful preemption**: when higher-priority work arrives, the scheduler first shrinks an elastic job toward ${N_{\text{min}}}$, reclaiming whole replicas at the next resize boundary, and resorts to checkpoint-and-preempt only when the final viable group itself must yield. Quota borrowing across teams, developed in [[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/02 Distributed ML/08 Fleet Orchestration/V2-08.12 Multi-Tenancy and Quotas#^sec-fleet-orchestration-multi-tenancy|section 8.11]], depends on this primitive whenever an owner reclaims capacity it lent. Scaling curves and transition costs determine whether the extra progress repays the disruption. Because batch changes can affect convergence, the test of a resize is time to target model quality, not throughput. Throughput counts examples per second, but a changed global batch can change how many examples the run needs to reach its target quality, so an expansion helps only when the faster rate outweighs any added examples.

### Elastic Scaling Policies

^sec-fleet-orchestration-elastic-policies

Adding resources to a running job does not always accelerate time-to-convergence. Communication and synchronization overhead limit throughput gains, requiring the scheduler to enforce elastic scaling policies based on empirical efficiency curves rather than resource availability alone. With fixed global work per step, a job’s scaling efficiency ${\eta_{\text{scaling}}}({k})$ at ${k}$ workers (definition [6.4](https://mlsysbook.ai/vol2/06_distributed_training/06_distributed_training.html#dfn-distributed-training-scaling-efficiency)) typically passes through three regimes. In the near-linear regime, per-worker computation dwarfs every fixed per-step cost. In the sublinear regime, pipeline fill and drain and the unhidden part of gradient synchronization become a visible share of each step. In the saturation regime, those fixed costs rival the shrinking computation itself. Assigning available GPUs to a saturated job can yield less useful progress than serving queued work. The lower allocation bound answers a different question. Below its **minimum viable scale**, a job may lack the memory or complete parallel groups needed to execute at all. A policy can impose a higher minimum to meet a deadline, even when a smaller allocation remains computationally efficient.

*Schematic throughput curve against worker count beside a dashed ideal linear diagonal from the origin. The curve follows the diagonal at first, falls away from it, and flattens, with the regions labeled linear, sublinear, and saturated along the horizontal axis.*

*Throughput falls away from ideal linear scaling and saturates.*

The 175B reference job shows these regimes with the chapter’s own numbers. Modeled at a fixed global batch with the step-time decomposition of the Fleet Law (**[[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/00 Introduction/01 Introduction/V2-01.06 The C³ Taxonomy and Foundations of Scale#^eq-fleet-law|equation 1.3]]**), its scaling efficiency falls from 87 percent at 256 GPUs to 83 percent at 1,024 GPUs and 69 percent at 4,096 GPUs ([[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/02 Distributed ML/08 Fleet Orchestration/V2-08.08 Elastic Scheduling#^fig-fleet-orchestration-scaling-efficiency-curve|figure 12]], panel a). Efficiency falls, yet quadrupling from 1,024 to 4,096 GPUs still raises throughput 3.3×, so a lower efficiency is a declining marginal return rather than a slowdown. Panel (b) locates the loss. Tensor-parallel communication shrinks along with each worker’s computation, so its share of the step never rises above 11 percent. The decline comes instead from pipeline fill and drain and unhidden gradient synchronization, which take roughly fixed time per step while each worker’s share of the batch shrinks. The pipeline bubble grows fastest of the three, for a reason the bubble fraction $({N_{\text{PP}}}-1)/({m}+{N_{\text{PP}}}-1)$ of **[[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/02 Distributed ML/06 Distributed Training/V2-06.06 Model Parallelism#^sec-distributed-training-systems-systems-pipeline-parallelism-8748|Pipeline parallelism]]** makes explicit. With the global batch fixed, each added replica takes a share of the sequences, so the microbatch count ${m}$ per replica falls while the stage count stays at 8, and the idle fraction rises.

The shaded window from 256 to 1,024 GPUs is therefore a policy choice, not an interval derived from these efficiencies alone. A minimum allocation could follow from a completion deadline or the job’s declared resource contract. It does not follow from insufficient per-worker computation at smaller scale, since with fixed global work fewer workers generally perform more work each. The upper limit likewise requires comparing additional useful progress with resize cost and the value of serving queued jobs. A real scheduler must obtain those inputs from profiling and the workload’s quality target.

The shaded window is an assumed admission policy; efficiency alone establishes neither its minimum nor its maximum. This logic extends to the temporal dimension, where the scheduler must weigh the immediate progress of a smaller allocation against waiting for a larger one. If a 512-GPU job can start immediately with 128 GPUs or wait for the full request, the better choice depends on the integration of throughput over time. The resize overhead (the cost of stopping, checkpointing, and restarting when the runtime requires them) must be amortized over the subsequent run duration. A policy of “opportunistic scaling” that resizes a job every time a single node becomes free can lose its throughput advantage to repeated interruptions, completing less useful work than a stable allocation.

**Napkin Math 8.3: The elastic scaling decision**

The comparison of elastic and rigid scheduling above assumed linear scaling and free resizing; this decision drops both assumptions. Consider a training job with a target batch size that requires 512 GPUs for the highest throughput among the configurations considered. Three scheduling strategies are evaluated over a window of 24 hours.

**Given**: Throughput at 128 GPUs is 1 epoch/hour (baseline). Throughput at 256 GPUs is 1.8 epochs/hour (90 percent scaling efficiency relative to that baseline). Throughput at 512 GPUs is 3.2 epochs/hour (80 percent scaling efficiency). Re-scaling cost is 10 minutes (0.17 hours) to checkpoint, re-shard, and restart.

**Strategy 1 (immediate start, no scaling)**: The job starts immediately with 128 GPUs and runs for the full 24 hours. Total work: $24 \times 1.0 = 24$ epochs.

**Strategy 2 (wait for the full allocation)**: The job waits in the queue for 4 hours until 512 GPUs become available, then runs for 20 hours. Total work: $20 \times 3.2 = 64$ epochs.

**Strategy 3 (elastic step-up)**: The job starts immediately with 128 GPUs. After 4 hours, the capacity that lets Strategy 2 start frees up, but the elastic policy caps growth at 256 GPUs so that the remaining GPUs stay available to queued jobs. The job pauses, scales up, and resumes. Phase 1: $4 \times 1.0 = 4$ epochs. Phase 2: $(24 - 4 - 0.17) \times 1.8 \approx 35.7$ epochs. Total work: 4 + 35.7 = 39.7 epochs.

The elastic strategy completes nearly 1.7× the epoch-equivalent work of the immediate small-scale run, but still trails the full allocation by about 38 percent. That comparison counts this job’s epochs, not the GPU-hours spent on them. Strategy 2 holds twice as many GPUs as Strategy 3 for most of the window and runs them at 80 percent scaling efficiency against Strategy 3’s 90 percent. The elastic job therefore does more work per GPU-hour, and the GPUs it leaves free serve queued jobs. Which strategy wins therefore depends on whether the fleet values this job’s completion time or the total work completed across all jobs. That gap shrinks when queue waits grow or peak capacity stays scarce. Re-scaling overhead is only 10 minutes, so the step-up strategy keeps its lead over the immediate small-scale run unless slow checkpoint transfer or compilation stretches that overhead beyond about 9 hours.

Placement and elasticity determine which allocations are feasible and how much useful progress they produce across competing jobs. Fleet economics adds the price of those choices. Every placement, preemption, and reservation consumes capacity that could serve another workload, so the scheduler must compare both useful progress and opportunity cost.
