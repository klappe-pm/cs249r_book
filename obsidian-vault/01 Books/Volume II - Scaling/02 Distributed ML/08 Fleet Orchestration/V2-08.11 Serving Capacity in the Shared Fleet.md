# V2-08.11 Serving Capacity in the Shared Fleet

[[Home|Home]] · [[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/Volume 2 Overview|Volume Overview]] · [[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/02 Distributed ML/08 Fleet Orchestration/V2-08 Fleet Orchestration|V2-08 Fleet Orchestration]]

^sec-fleet-orchestration-serving

When an e-commerce platform launches a major sale, requests can arrive faster than its serving replicas can process them. A training job may tolerate waiting in the admission queue; a serving request misses its deadline while additional capacity is still starting. To the fleet orchestrator, serving is therefore a claim on accelerators that must be honored quickly and that competes with training gangs for the same devices.

The orchestrator’s unit is the serving replica, a ready execution group with a loaded model that may occupy one accelerator, a device partition, or a multi-accelerator gang. A 175B-parameter model may make an eight-GPU tensor-parallel group the schedulable replica. Archetype B (DLRM at Scale) imposes the same constraint through its embedding tables: a replica whose tables are sharded across several accelerators can serve only when every shard is placed. Each replica has a measured request-processing capacity at a specified workload mix and latency target, and that measured capacity is the quantity the orchestrator uses to size and place replicas. It decides how many such groups exist, where they reside, how they are protected from interference, and which lower-priority allocations yield when serving demand grows. Language models add a memory dimension to the claim. Each in-progress response retains per-token attention state, the key-value (KV) cache, whose footprint grows with context length and concurrency even though the weights stay fixed, so a replica can exhaust its memory before it exhausts its compute.

How many replicas to run at each moment is a control problem with its own signals and delays. Accelerator utilization alone does not identify the capacity needed to meet a request-latency target, and loading large models can delay a new replica’s readiness. The controller must combine demand signals with measured startup time and ready reserve capacity; **[[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/03 The Fleet in Production/10 Serving at Scale/V2-10.14 Autoscaling and Capacity Control#^sec-inference-scale-autoscaling-capacity-control|Autoscaling and Capacity Control]]** develops that control loop. For the orchestrator, the immediate consequence is that reclaiming a device from training does not instantly make it available for serving. Three fleet-level questions follow, namely how serving workloads share devices without unacceptable interference, when sharing pays for itself, and where the boundary between training and serving pools should sit.

### Resource Isolation

^sec-fleet-orchestration-resource-isolation

When multiple inference workloads share physical hardware, noisy-neighbor contention[[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/02 Distributed ML/08 Fleet Orchestration/V2-08.18 Footnotes#^fn12|¹²]] arises whenever one workload’s resource consumption degrades another’s execution speed. On GPUs, shared execution units can limit concurrent kernels, while saturated HBM interfaces constrain memory-bound work. Contending working sets can evict lines from the shared L2 cache, and simultaneous host-to-device transfers can compete for PCIe bandwidth. Aggregate power and temperature limits can also reduce operating clocks. These mechanisms connect the placement decision to the request’s critical path. A placement that fits every model in memory can still miss its service-level objective if sharing extends execution or queueing enough to exhaust the latency budget.

Multi-Instance GPU (MIG)[[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/02 Distributed ML/08 Fleet Orchestration/V2-08.18 Footnotes#^fn13|¹³]] closes several of these channels in hardware by partitioning A100 (80 GB) and H100 (80 GB) GPUs into hardware-isolated instances. Unlike software-based GPU sharing, such as time-slicing that context-switches several processes over one whole device, MIG dedicates memory, cache, and compute resources to each instance, reducing interference through those partitioned resources. The A100 MIG profiles in [[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/02 Distributed ML/08 Fleet Orchestration/V2-08.11 Serving Capacity in the Shared Fleet#^tbl-fleet-orchestration-mig-profiles|table 6]] show the scheduling consequence. A single physical A100 becomes several fixed-size resources that the device plugin of [[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/02 Distributed ML/08 Fleet Orchestration/V2-08.06 Orchestration Paradigms#^sec-fleet-orchestration-kubernetes|section 8.5.2]] can expose as independently schedulable accelerators.

|:----------------|:--------------:|:------------:|:--------------------:|
| **1g.10gb**     |     10 GB      |    14 SMs    |   Small inference    |
| **2g.20gb**     |     20 GB      |    28 SMs    |   Medium inference   |
| **3g.40gb**     |     40 GB      |    42 SMs    |   Large inference    |
| **7g.80gb**     |     80 GB      |    98 SMs    |       Training       |

MIG therefore sits at the strong resource-isolation end of this design space. The operational implication is that hardware partitioning reduces cross-workload interference but turns partition choice into a placement constraint. MIG is available on supported MIG-capable NVIDIA data-center GPUs, profiles may require draining affected workloads before reconfiguration, and fixed partition sizes may not match workload requirements. The memory-fit and sharing examples take each labeled device or profile capacity as a conservative admission budget of that many decimal gigabytes, so an instance admits a working set no larger than its label. A workload that needs 15 GB of GPU memory leaves 5 GB of a 20 GB MIG instance budget unused or exceeds a 10 GB instance budget.

Software sharing offers different controls over execution, memory, and fault isolation; these do not form a single performance-versus-security scale. At the simplest level, **CUDA time-slicing** allows multiple processes to share a GPU by context-switching the compute resources. While flexible, it offers weak performance isolation because contexts compete for the entire device over time. NVIDIA’s Multi-Process Service (MPS)[[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/02 Distributed ML/08 Fleet Orchestration/V2-08.18 Footnotes#^fn14|¹⁴]] allows kernels and memory copies from different processes to overlap, reducing context-switch overhead and improving utilization when one process cannot fill the device ([[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/02 Distributed ML/08 Fleet Orchestration/V2-08.17 Source References#^ref-nvidiaCudaMps|NVIDIA 2026]]). In the shared-resource MPS configuration considered here, Volta and newer clients have distinct GPU virtual address spaces but compete for execution and memory-system resources. Additional partitioning controls depend on the hardware, software version, and selected mode, and those configured limits, not address-space separation, determine how much performance and fault isolation MPS provides.

GPU memory accounting must still prevent one model from consuming capacity reserved for another. A distinct virtual address space blocks direct addressing of another client’s allocations, but it does not create a fixed memory-capacity partition. Without enforceable per-client limits, a memory leak or unexpectedly large batch can exhaust shared device memory and disrupt colocated workloads. MPS therefore fits mutually trusting workloads whose aggregate memory footprint and interference have been measured; MIG or full-device assignment fits stronger trust and predictability requirements. At the host level, CPU core pinning and NUMA-aware placement complement these device controls by keeping inference processes off contended cores and away from remote memory, which shortens tail latency whenever those host paths lie on the request’s critical path.

The GPU mechanisms form a spectrum from flexible software sharing (time-slicing and MPS) through hardware partitioning (MIG) to exclusive assignment, and each point gives up packing efficiency for isolation ([[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/02 Distributed ML/08 Fleet Orchestration/V2-08.11 Serving Capacity in the Shared Fleet#^tbl-gpu-virtualization-isolation-spectrum|table 7]]). For serving, the choice turns on interference and memory accounting; when colocated workloads belong to different teams, it also becomes a trust-boundary decision, which [[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/02 Distributed ML/08 Fleet Orchestration/V2-08.12 Multi-Tenancy and Quotas#^sec-fleet-orchestration-security-isolation|section 8.11.3]] takes up with the rest of multi-tenant security.

| **Option**                  | **Isolation strength**                                             | **Efficiency and flexibility**                         | **Suitable workload or trust boundary**                                    |
|:----------------------------|:-------------------------------------------------------------------|:-------------------------------------------------------|:---------------------------------------------------------------------------|
| **Time-slicing**            | Separate contexts; shared performance and device fault domains.    | Flexible; no spatial reservation.                      | Trusted, bursty jobs.                                                      |
| **MPS**                     | Separate address spaces; shared performance and GPU fault domains. | Concurrent kernels; configurable limits.               | Mutually trusting jobs with measured demand.                               |
| **MIG**                     | Hardware-partitioned memory, cache, and compute.                   | Predictable; fixed profiles.                           | SLO-sensitive work; external tenants also need platform security controls. |
| **Full device passthrough** | Exclusive access to the GPU.                                       | Strongest device isolation; lowest packing efficiency. | Jobs that saturate the device or forbid sharing.                           |

Table 7: **GPU Virtualization Isolation Spectrum**: GPU sharing mechanisms trade device-resource isolation against packing efficiency. External tenant isolation additionally depends on the host, driver, identity, network, and storage security boundaries.

### GPU Sharing Economics

^sec-fleet-orchestration-gpu-sharing

The decision to co-locate multiple models on a single GPU trades potential capacity savings against interference. Choosing **exclusive access** avoids competition from another device tenant but can leave capacity unused when one workload cannot fill the device. Production evidence suggests such stranded capacity is common. On NERSC’s Perlmutter supercomputer, Li et al. ([[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/02 Distributed ML/08 Fleet Orchestration/V2-08.17 Source References#^ref-li2023|2023]]) found that 50 percent of jobs used less than 25 percent of their allocated GPU memory. Moving to **aggressive sharing** (via MPS or time-slicing) can admit more work, provided contention does not erase the gain or violate latency objectives. Exclusive device assignment still depends on the surrounding host, driver, identity, network, and storage controls for tenant security. The scheduler must therefore evaluate memory fit and measured service capacity together.

![[90 Assets/Volume II/08 Fleet Orchestration/Fleet Orchestration Sharing Fill 9d912ad721.svg]]

*Two stacked bars of the scenario's conservative 80 GB memory budget. Exclusive hosting: a 26 GB blue used segment and a wide gray dark segment. Shared hosting: a 52 GB blue used segment and a narrow gray dark segment. Sharing uses more of the scenario memory budget.*

*Sharing packs two tenants, cutting the unused memory budget from 67.5 percent to 35 percent.*

Using the same conservative admission budget, consider an A100 GPU with a 80 GB memory budget serving a 7B parameter model. The model weights in FP16 consume approximately 14 GB. With the scenario’s assumed KV cache and activation overhead, the total runtime footprint is roughly 26 GB. Exclusive access leaves 54 GB (67.5 percent) of the memory budget unused at this load. That budget is reclaimable only to the extent that it exceeds required runtime and burst headroom. A MIG partition (for example, a `3g.40gb` profile) leaves roughly 35 percent of the assigned partition budget unused; the remaining device capacity is subject to valid partition combinations. Packing two such models onto the same 80 GB device through MPS consumes 52 GB, leaving 35 percent of the device memory budget unused under the same footprint assumption. Reducing the device count also requires enough compute and bandwidth headroom to preserve throughput and latency under concurrent demand; fitting the models in memory is only the first admission condition.

This density introduces interference. When two workloads share execution and memory resources through MPS, their demands can overlap on SMs and memory bandwidth. A compute-bound model paired with a memory-bound model is a candidate for complementary placement because, in isolation, each saturates a different resource, but the bottleneck labels describe isolated behavior, not a guarantee of compatibility. Both workloads still use multiple resources, and concurrent demand can change the limiting resource. The scheduler must measure the pair’s throughput and tail latency across its expected load range before treating memory density as usable capacity.

For a heterogeneous fleet, this logic dictates a bifurcated strategy. Our 175B parameter model requires about 350 GB for FP16 weights alone; with the 80 GB per-device admission budget that means at least 5 GPUs before KV cache, activation buffers, runtime workspace, and tensor-parallel granularity are included. In practice, an 8-GPU tensor-parallel replica is the cleaner scheduling unit and is a candidate for exclusive access. Conversely, the dozens of 1B to 7B parameter support models, such as toxicity classifiers, query embedding encoders, and auxiliary generation models, are candidates for sharing. Packing these smaller models onto shared utility nodes frees the high-performance nodes for foundation-model replicas.

**Napkin Math 8.4: GPU sharing ROI**

Consider a fleet serving 100 distinct 7B models (26 GB footprint each) and 10 distinct 175B models (350 GB footprint each). Assume load testing has established that each proposed co-resident pair meets its throughput and tail-latency objectives with the required headroom and isolation. The memory-utilization percentages use the same conservative admission budgets. The example computes the resulting capacity reduction under the load-testing assumption; memory fit alone would not establish it.

**Strategy A (exclusive access, one model per GPU)**: The 7B models require 100 A100 GPUs (80 GB each), achieving only 32.5 percent memory utilization (100 $\times$ 26 GB used out of 8000 GB total). The 175B models require 80 GPUs (10 models $\times$ 8 GPUs each for tensor parallelism). Total fleet: 180 GPUs.

**Strategy B (MPS for small models, exclusive for large)**: Pack 2 models per A100 for the 7B models (2 $\times$ 26 GB = 52 GB, well within 80 GB), requiring only 50 GPUs at 65 percent memory utilization. The 175B models remain unchanged at 80 GPUs. Total fleet: 130 GPUs.

Under the load-testing assumption, sharing reduces the required fleet size by 27.8 percent (50 fewer GPUs). At \$2/GPU-hour, the released capacity is worth approximately \$876,000 annually at the scenario rate. It becomes a cash saving when the organization releases billed resources or avoids purchasing that capacity.

### The Training-Serving Resource Boundary

^sec-fleet-orchestration-boundary

Separate training and serving pools simplify capacity ownership but can leave useful work queued beside idle devices. Consider a serving pool reserved for peak traffic while a training pool remains backlogged. This **static partitioning** protects serving capacity from training demand, but unused serving resources cannot help the training queue. **Dynamic sharing** allows that borrowing only when the orchestrator can return ready serving capacity before it is needed. The mode-switching cost determines how much capacity can safely move between the pools.

Repurposing a GPU from training to serving can require saving training state, releasing the old allocation, launching the inference runtime, loading weights, and warming the replica. The orchestrator must measure the complete path to readiness, including any overlap between these operations. If that path takes minutes, reclaiming training capacity cannot cover a burst that exhausts the serving reserve in seconds. Forecasts can schedule transfers ahead of predictable demand, while already-ready headroom covers shorter bursts; the required reserve depends on forecast error and measured reclaim time.

Consider a daily traffic pattern with low demand at 3 AM, growth at 8 AM, a peak at 2 PM, and a decline at 10 PM. A forecast with a 30-minute lead allows the orchestrator to begin reclaiming training allocations at 7:30 AM, provided measured replica startup fits within that lead time. As traffic falls, it drains surplus replicas before returning their devices to training. Pricing that policy against a static split needs three quantities: what serving reserves, what it uses on average, and how much of the difference training can absorb without eroding the surge buffer. The example below assumes its surge buffer suffices; a production policy must size the buffer against its own burst distribution and forecast errors.

**Napkin Math 8.5: The diurnal GPU shift**

Consider a fleet of 1,024 GPUs managed under two regimes:

**Scenario A (static partitioning)**: The serving pool reserves 400 GPUs. That reservation is the forecast peak of 283 GPUs plus a 117-GPU surge buffer that absorbs forecast error and bursts shorter than a replica’s startup time. Average usage is only 150 GPUs (37.5 percent utilization). The training pool takes the rest of the fleet after the serving reservation and 6 GPUs held back as maintenance headroom, which leaves 618 GPUs, and its backlog keeps them at 100 percent utilization. Result: 150 GPUs + 618 GPUs = 768 active GPUs. Fleet utilization: 75 percent. The maintenance headroom stays unallocated in both scenarios.

**Scenario B (dynamic sharing)**: Serving holds forecast demand (average 150 GPUs) plus the same 117-GPU surge buffer. Training reclaims the rest of the serving reservation, which is the unused 250 GPUs on average minus the buffer and averages 133 GPUs over the course of the day (the medium-blue band in [[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/02 Distributed ML/08 Fleet Orchestration/V2-08.11 Serving Capacity in the Shared Fleet#^fig-fleet-orchestration-training-serving-diurnal|figure 15]]). Because each role switch costs minutes, the orchestrator moves this capacity on the forecast rather than chasing individual traffic spikes. Result: serving (150 GPUs) + training (618 GPUs + 133 GPUs) = 901 active GPUs (the second training term is reclaimed serving capacity). Fleet utilization: 88 percent.

Dynamic sharing recovers 133 GPUs of effective capacity. At \$2/GPU-hour, this creates approximately \$2.3M per year in value without purchasing a single additional accelerator.

Safe sharing requires both a measured interference budget and an enforceable right to reclaim capacity. Isolation mechanisms address the first requirement; they do not decide which team owns a device or when a borrower must release it. That second requirement is an allocation contract across teams, and it applies to every pool the orchestrator manages, serving and training alike.

1.  Why does the fleet orchestrator schedule a 175B-parameter serving replica as an eight-GPU group rather than as eight independent GPUs?

    1.  Kubernetes cannot address more than one GPU per pod unless the pod is marked as a gang.
    2.  The replica’s weights are sharded across a tensor-parallel group, so it can serve only when every member is placed; a partial placement delivers no serving capacity at all.
    3.  Eight independent GPUs would each need a full copy of the weights, which improves fault tolerance but wastes memory.
    4.  Grouping GPUs lets the orchestrator skip readiness checks for the replica.

    3.  MPS requires dedicated physical PCIe switches per tenant, whereas MIG operates over standard Ethernet cables.
    4.  MPS permits concurrent clients sharing execution and memory-bandwidth resources, while MIG partitions device compute and memory resources more strongly at the cost of fixed profiles and placement constraints.
