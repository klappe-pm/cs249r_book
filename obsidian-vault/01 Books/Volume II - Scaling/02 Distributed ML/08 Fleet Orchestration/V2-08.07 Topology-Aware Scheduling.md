# V2-08.07 Topology-Aware Scheduling

[[Home|Home]] · [[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/Volume 2 Overview|Volume Overview]] · [[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/02 Distributed ML/08 Fleet Orchestration/V2-08 Fleet Orchestration|V2-08 Fleet Orchestration]]

^sec-fleet-orchestration-topology-aware

A scattered allocation can place frequent collective traffic on shared cross-rack paths, exposing latency or bandwidth limits that a more compact allocation avoids. The penalty is set by how much of the job’s traffic crosses those paths and how much capacity the fabric provisions there, not by the rack count itself. The earlier feasibility rules already required compatible topology; topology-aware scheduling makes that requirement explicit in the placement objective, accounting for communication paths as well as raw compute.

### The Topology Hierarchy

^sec-fleet-orchestration-topology-hierarchy

Splitting a tensor-parallel group across node boundaries moves its per-layer activation exchanges off intra-node NVLink onto the external network fabric, where an H100’s per-direction NVLink rate is 9× the rate of one NDR port (**[[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/01 The Fleet/02 Compute Infrastructure/V2-02.06 The Node#^sec-compute-bandwidth-hierarchy|Bandwidth hierarchy]]**). Each level of a cluster’s communication hierarchy comes from the hardware that builds it and sets a distinct bandwidth and latency budget. GPUs inside a node exchange data over NVLink, the tier fast enough for the per-layer exchanges of tensor parallelism. Nodes under one leaf switch communicate over an external fabric such as InfiniBand or RoCE. Racks joined through spine switches add switch hops whose delivered bandwidth depends on concurrent flows and oversubscription even in a fat-tree. Cross-rack communication also adds tail latency variability: while median latency may be only slightly higher, p99 latency can spike during congestion events, creating straggler effects in synchronous training where the slowest communicator determines the pace for all workers.

↩︎ Bisection bandwidth and fat-tree network topologies are established in **[[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/01 The Fleet/03 Network Fabrics/V2-03.06 Level 3 Switch and Topology#^sec-network-fabrics-fat-tree|Fat-tree (Clos) networks]]**.

A 64-GPU job within one rack can avoid cross-rack paths that a spread allocation must use. It outperforms the spread allocation only when those paths add exposed latency or bandwidth contention; a non-blocking, uncongested spine need not reduce sustained throughput. The performance difference stems directly from the hierarchical communication analysis in **[[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/02 Distributed ML/05 Collective Communication/V2-05.08 Hierarchical Communication#^sec-communication-collective-operations-collective-operations-hierarchical-allreduce-1338|Hierarchical AllReduce]]**: a flat ring AllReduce runs at the speed of the slowest link in the ring, and cross-rack hops introduce both higher latency and increased contention risk. For synchronous training, every iteration waits for the slowest worker to complete its AllReduce, so even occasional cross-rack congestion inflates the Fleet Law’s communication term ${T_{\text{comm}}}({N})$ (**[[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/00 Introduction/01 Introduction/V2-01.06 The C³ Taxonomy and Foundations of Scale#^eq-fleet-law|equation 1.3]]**) on every step it touches.

The scheduler must therefore model the cluster as a hierarchical graph with discrete bandwidth boundaries ([[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/02 Distributed ML/08 Fleet Orchestration/V2-08.07 Topology-Aware Scheduling#^fig-fleet-orchestration-topology-hierarchy|figure 7]]). In the illustrated H100 fleet, per-direction bandwidth steps down from 450 GB/s across the NVSwitch inside a node to 50 GB/s per port under a leaf switch, and to roughly 12.5–25 GB/s per endpoint when balanced traffic saturates an oversubscribed spine. A node boundary can introduce a large bandwidth reduction, but a non-blocking rack or pod boundary need not reduce per-endpoint bandwidth. Oversubscription adds a separate constraint when traffic saturates shared uplinks. Topology-aware placement minimizes the communication cost of the job’s actual traffic pattern rather than applying a fixed penalty to every boundary.

For a 4-GPU collective group, the routing paths make that cost concrete ([[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/02 Distributed ML/08 Fleet Orchestration/V2-08.07 Topology-Aware Scheduling#^fig-topology-placement-paths|figure 8]]). In the illustrated contended fabric, scattering ranks across Rack A and Rack B forces a 3-hop leaf-spine-leaf path with about 12.5 GB/s of effective bandwidth per endpoint, a share of contended uplinks rather than a physical link rate. Consolidating all four ranks (G0–G3) under Rack A’s leaf switch avoids those shared uplinks and provides about 50 GB/s per GPU under the example’s load assumptions.

### Placement Algorithms

^sec-fleet-orchestration-placement-algorithms

The illustrated 4× bandwidth difference between the 1-hop leaf path and the 3-hop spine route ([[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/02 Distributed ML/08 Fleet Orchestration/V2-08.07 Topology-Aware Scheduling#^fig-topology-placement-paths|figure 8]]) stretches the transfer component by the same factor when transfer time is bandwidth-dominated. Crossing a spine does not impose that ratio independently of load and topology. Whether communication exceeds computation also depends on the model and overlap schedule, and repeated exchanges can amplify the exposed cost. Topology-aware placement algorithms assign jobs to nodes that reduce this cost, turning resource packing into graph optimization over the physical topology. A simple **locality cost**, defined in [[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/02 Distributed ML/08 Fleet Orchestration/V2-08.07 Topology-Aware Scheduling#^eq-fleet-orchestration-locality-score|equation 2]], penalizes allocations spanning multiple topology domains:

$$
\text{Cost}_{\text{locality}} = \sum_{i < j} {w}({d}({g}_i, {g}_j)) \tag{2}
$$

^eq-fleet-orchestration-locality-score

where ${g}_i$ and ${g}_j$ are GPUs assigned to the job, ${d}({g}_i, {g}_j)$ is their topological distance (0 for same node, 1 for same rack, 2 for different racks), and ${w}(\cdot)$ is a weighting function that increases with distance. The scheduler minimizes this score when selecting an allocation from the set of feasible placements. An illustrative heuristic assigns locality penalties of ${w}(0) = 0$ (same node, NVLink, no penalty), ${w}(1) = 1$ (same rack, one switch hop), and ${w}(2) = 10$ (different racks, multiple switch hops). These dimensionless weights are policy assumptions rather than measured latency or bandwidth ratios, so a deployment must calibrate them to its traffic and topology. An unweighted pairwise score still cannot distinguish heavily communicating rank pairs from pairs that rarely exchange data.

More sophisticated algorithms distinguish the communication requirements of different parallelism strategies. Frequent tensor-parallel activation exchanges favor a high-bandwidth, low-latency domain that meets the measured communication budget; that domain need not coincide with one server. Pipeline parallelism exchanges activations across stage boundaries rather than between every layer, but its tolerance for link delay depends on microbatch size and the pipeline schedule. Data-parallel gradient synchronization can overlap computation, making wider placement feasible when the exposed communication remains within budget. These patterns guide placement without establishing a universal ranking for every model or fabric.

[Algorithm 1](https://mlsysbook.ai/vol2/08_fleet_orchestration/08_fleet_orchestration.html#algo-topology-aware-placement) combines the all-or-nothing gang constraint of [[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/02 Distributed ML/08 Fleet Orchestration/V2-08.05 Core Allocation Constraints#^sec-fleet-orchestration-gang-scheduling|section 8.4.3]], the locality cost of [[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/02 Distributed ML/08 Fleet Orchestration/V2-08.07 Topology-Aware Scheduling#^eq-fleet-orchestration-locality-score|equation 2]], and an illustrative parallelism-to-topology mapping. The algorithm constructs feasible rank mappings, scores each allocation, and chooses the lowest illustrative locality score, leaving the job queued when none fits. This surrogate does not guarantee minimum communication time or completion time. A fleet-scale implementation must restrict that search using heuristics rather than enumerate every allocation.

$N$

Applied to a whole job, the same reasoning ranks placement strategies. The latencies in this comparison are illustrative, not derived. They are constructed to stay above the ring-AllReduce bandwidth bound for the payload, and they show the order in which placement removes delay rather than values a reader could recompute from topology names. For a 0.5 GB gradient synchronization on 64 GPUs, random placement spans multiple racks and spine switches and takes an assumed 120 ms. Each successive strategy removes a source of delay that the previous one leaves. Confining the group to a single rack removes spine hops and reduces latency to 85 ms. Rail-optimized alignment maps ranks so that every member of each collective group sits at the same local GPU index and therefore on the same network rail (developed in [[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/02 Distributed ML/08 Fleet Orchestration/V2-08.07 Topology-Aware Scheduling#^sec-fleet-orchestration-rail-optimized|section 8.6.3]]). Traffic that previously hopped between rails through the spine now stays within one rail’s switches, which cuts latency to 40 ms. Topology-optimal placement additionally avoids leaf ports that other jobs are using and reaches 25 ms, a 4.8× reduction ([[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/02 Distributed ML/08 Fleet Orchestration/V2-08.07 Topology-Aware Scheduling#^fig-topology-placement|figure 9]], where each bar also carries its algorithm throughput). This ratio compares whole collectives under four assumed placements, so it need not match the link-bandwidth ratio of the two-rack paths above.

A **hierarchical placement** algorithm applies these constraints from the most restrictive communication domain outward, matching each 3D parallelism dimension to the interconnect tier its traffic can tolerate ([[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/02 Distributed ML/08 Fleet Orchestration/V2-08.07 Topology-Aware Scheduling#^fig-fleet-orchestration-hierarchical-placement|figure 10]]). In the illustrated fleet, it places each tensor-parallel group within an NVLink-connected node, where the per-layer activation exchanges run at 450 GB/s. It places consecutive pipeline stages under the same leaf switch, where stage boundaries cross at 50 GB/s. It distributes data-parallel replicas across racks only when the once-per-step gradient synchronization fits the roughly 12.5–25 GB/s per endpoint that an oversubscribed spine leaves. This alignment reduces costly boundary crossings, but placement alone cannot reserve the fabric’s full bisection bandwidth against other jobs.

![[90 Assets/Volume II/08 Fleet Orchestration/Hierarchical Placement ccd895a83d.svg]]

Figure 10: **Hierarchical Placement for 3D Parallelism**: This placement example maps distributed training dimensions to physical interconnect layers. Tensor parallelism (TP), which exchanges activations at every layer, stays inside a node on NVLink (450 GB/s); pipeline parallelism (PP) maps across adjacent nodes under one leaf switch (50 GB/s); and data parallelism (DP), which exchanges gradients only once per step, spans racks through the oversubscribed spine. Bandwidths are per direction, as in [[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/02 Distributed ML/08 Fleet Orchestration/V2-08.07 Topology-Aware Scheduling#^fig-fleet-orchestration-topology-hierarchy|figure 7]].

^fig-fleet-orchestration-hierarchical-placement

Evaluating all possible placements for a 256-GPU job across a 10,000-GPU cluster is combinatorially intractable, so production schedulers rely on heuristic approaches. Common choices include greedy algorithms that build placements bottom-up (first fill NVLink domains, then fill racks, then spread across racks), tree-search algorithms with pruning that explore the most promising placements first, and scoring-based approaches that evaluate a sample of feasible placements and select the best. Long jobs can amortize a more expensive search, but frequent submissions, short jobs, and recovery bursts also consume scheduler capacity, making end-to-end completion time, including placement delay, the useful comparison rather than execution throughput alone. The same comparison governs how long a job should wait for a compact placement. Holding a job until enough GPUs free up under one leaf switch delays its start, and the wait pays off only if faster collectives recover more than that delay over the job’s run.

**Napkin Math 8.2: Placement impact on 3D-parallel training**

**Scenario**: Consider a 256-GPU training job using 3D parallelism: 8-way tensor parallel, 4-way pipeline parallel, 8-way data parallel.

**Good placement** (topology-aware):

- Tensor parallel groups: 32 groups of 8 GPUs, each confined to one 8-GPU node (NVLink: 450 GB/s per direction)
- Pipeline stages: 4 consecutive nodes within the same rack (1 switch hop, InfiniBand: 50 GB/s per direction)
- Data parallel replicas: 8 racks (3 switch hops through leaf-spine-leaf paths)

**Bad placement** (topology-unaware):

- Tensor parallel groups split across 2 nodes (InfiniBand instead of NVLink)
- Pipeline stages scattered across racks (3 switch hops instead of 1)
- Data parallel replicas randomly distributed

**Math**: The tensor parallel communication alone illustrates the impact. With NVLink delivering 450 GB/s per direction, a 1 GB activation transfer takes approximately 2.2 ms. Over InfiniBand at 50 GB/s, the same transfer takes approximately 20 ms, a 9× slowdown.

**Systems insight**: This calculation prices only the tensor-parallel component of the bad placement. Splitting a tensor-parallel group across nodes makes each activation exchange 9× slower, and the group performs such exchanges at every transformer layer of every step, so the penalty multiplies with model depth rather than appearing once. How much of it reaches step time depends on how much of the exchange was hidden behind computation, and it is why hierarchical placement gives tensor-parallel groups the fastest tier first. The scattered pipeline stages and data-parallel replicas add their own penalties on top, and the scheduler must profile the complete job before assigning a throughput benefit to the placement.

### Rail-Optimized Scheduling

^sec-fleet-orchestration-rail-optimized

Even when two nodes sit in the same pod, placing corresponding data-parallel shards on mismatched local GPU indices forces their gradient collective to cross between network rails through the spine instead of staying on a single rail switch. In rail-optimized topologies, corresponding GPU positions across nodes connect through parallel network “rails” that a suitable rank mapping can use concurrently. Balanced bandwidth depends on distributing traffic across those paths rather than concentrating it on a subset. Keeping a collective group on one rail avoids cross-rail transfers for that group, while the placement of other groups determines how much rail capacity it must share.

↩︎ Rail-optimized fabric architectures and rail-to-rail traffic isolation are established in **[[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/01 The Fleet/03 Network Fabrics/V2-03.06 Level 3 Switch and Topology#^sec-network-fabrics-rail-optimized|Rail-optimized topology]]**.

Rail-aware scheduling assigns corresponding model shards across data-parallel replicas to GPUs attached to the same rail. When intra-node parallelism partitions a model replica across the eight GPUs of a node, each data-parallel gradient collective reduces only one shard ${i}$ across replicas rather than combining all eight GPUs on the node. In the eight-GPU-per-node example, local GPU index ${i}$ connects to rail ${i}$. Placing every replica’s copy of shard ${i}$ at that same local index on its selected node means the data-parallel replica rank varies across nodes rather than across local GPU indices, so the collective for shard ${i}$ stays entirely within rail ${i}$. This alignment removes cross-rail transfers for that group, though other groups or tenants can still contend for the same rail’s links, and full dedicated bandwidth requires an explicit capacity and sharing contract.

Rail alignment therefore acts at two levels, and they belong to different components. Within a node it is a rank-mapping decision. The launcher or framework must give every member of a data-parallel group the same local GPU index on its node, which is what removes the cross-rail hops in the placement comparison above. A mapping that puts shard ${i}$ at index ${i}$ on one node and at another index elsewhere sends that group’s traffic between rails.

Across nodes, rail alignment constrains the scheduler only when allocations are smaller than a node. When each replica’s pipeline stage occupies a complete node and every node wires local GPU ${i}$ to rail ${i}$, any free node already offers every rail, so rail alignment does not narrow which racks the scheduler can choose. With partial-node allocations, a job can receive different local indices on different nodes, and the scheduler must then choose GPU positions as well as nodes so that each group’s members share a rail. Those positions can conflict with the tensor-parallel budget and the paths between pipeline stages when only particular GPU positions remain free. The packing cost therefore follows the allocation granularity and the available topology rather than an inherent conflict among the three parallelism dimensions. Even a single-node allocation can have meaningful topology constraints when GPUs occupy different interconnect, PCIe, or non-uniform memory access (NUMA) domains, so packing efficiency should be optimized only among placements that satisfy the job’s local and external communication contract.

### Topology Discovery and Dynamic Adaptation

^sec-fleet-orchestration-topology-discovery

When a spine switch fails or an optical link degrades and begins retransmitting, a static configuration file such as Slurm’s `topology.conf` or Kubernetes node labels still reports the rack as healthy and full-bandwidth. The scheduler then places communication-heavy gangs onto a crippled path. Runtime **topology discovery** supplements static maps with information exposed by the operating system and device libraries. NCCL uses system topology information, including the PCIe hierarchy and network devices exposed through paths such as `/sys/class/infiniband`, to identify communication paths and network interface card (NIC) proximity. The discovered graph describes reported connectivity; health checks and traffic measurements must establish whether those paths deliver the expected performance.

Cluster topology is an operating state, not a fixed schematic. Hardware failures, maintenance windows, and thermal throttling change the paths and effective capacity available to the scheduler. A spine switch failure in a redundant Clos network can leave connectivity intact while removing capacity. The effect on our 175B parameter model’s data-parallel AllReduce depends on rerouting, concurrent traffic, and how much bandwidth the job was using. If the failure increases exposed communication time, the scheduler must compare continuing at reduced throughput with the cost of moving to a healthier allocation. A degraded link can also pass binary up/down probes while cutting delivered bandwidth, so the scheduler needs performance evidence such as retransmission counts and collective bandwidth, the link-level telemetry of **[[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/01 The Fleet/03 Network Fabrics/V2-03.10 Monitoring and Debugging#^sec-network-fabrics-link-telemetry|Link-level telemetry]]**, as well as reachability.

A checkpoint-and-restart migration spends time saving state, releasing the old allocation, obtaining a new one, and restoring execution. Its value depends on measured transition cost and remaining work. If migration takes 10 minutes and removes a sustained 20 percent throughput loss, each later minute of training recovers a fifth of a minute of progress, so the move pays back in less than an hour of subsequent training. That comparison assumes a healthy destination is available and the gain persists. A practical policy also accounts for queue delay and requires evidence that placement causes the slowdown, such as rising collective time on the affected path, before it moves the job.

**War Story 8.2: Performance-aware health checks (2021)**

**Context**: OpenAI’s account of scaling Kubernetes describes automated health checks that remove misbehaving nodes from the schedulable pool ([[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/02 Distributed ML/08 Fleet Orchestration/V2-08.17 Source References#^ref-sigler2021openai-kubernetes-c906164d|Sigler 2021]]).

**Mechanism**: Passive checks monitor reachability, disks, and GPU errors, but error codes do not expose every hardware or driver problem. Active GPU tests exercise the device before admitting ordinary workloads and periodically during its lifetime.

**Fix**: Nodes enter with a preflight taint, a node marking that repels ordinary pods, which blocks normal scheduling until the tests pass. Failed passive checks cordon a node, marking it unschedulable for new work; more serious failures can trigger eviction requests.

**Systems lesson**: Reachability alone is not a sufficient admission test. A scheduler needs health evidence that reflects the resources its jobs depend on, together with a policy for removing suspect nodes from placement.

The general scheduling idea is quarantine: when runtime evidence says a node, rack, or link is degraded, the scheduler should stop placing sensitive work there even if the nominal resource count still looks available. Kubernetes implements this pattern through taints and tolerations,[[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/02 Distributed ML/08 Fleet Orchestration/V2-08.18 Footnotes#^fn9|⁹]] while Slurm clusters commonly use drain states, node features, or partition rules.

↩︎ Slow components that pass liveness checks while setting the pace of every synchronous step, and how to tell them from fail-stop failures, are established in **[[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/02 Distributed ML/07 Fault Tolerance/V2-07.10 Failure Detection and Recovery#^sec-fault-tolerance-reliability-reliability-distinguishing-stragglers-failures-285d|Stragglers versus fail-stop failures]]**.

The complementary problem is how jobs adapt when available resources change dynamically. Rather than treating resource allocation as fixed for a job’s lifetime, elastic training allows logical jobs to grow, shrink, and recover.
