# V2-08.14 Fallacies and Pitfalls

[[Home|Home]] · [[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/Volume 2 Overview|Volume Overview]] · [[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/02 Distributed ML/08 Fleet Orchestration/V2-08 Fleet Orchestration|V2-08 Fleet Orchestration]]

^sec-fleet-orchestration-fallacies

Those policy-infrastructure mismatches recur as a small set of misconceptions that can make an apparently healthy scheduler waste expensive fleet capacity. A cluster running at 98 percent utilization can look healthy while researchers have stopped submitting jobs because wait times have stretched into weeks. Each misconception below follows the same pattern, in which optimizing one visible metric degrades the outcome the fleet exists to deliver.

**Fallacy**: *More sophisticated scheduling algorithms always improve utilization.*

Engineers facing low utilization often reach for more advanced schedulers, assuming the algorithm is the bottleneck. As the debugging example in [[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/02 Distributed ML/08 Fleet Orchestration/V2-08.13 Debugging Cluster Utilization#^sec-fleet-orchestration-debugging|section 8.12]] demonstrates, the root cause is frequently policy misconfiguration, not algorithmic limitation. A simple FIFO scheduler with correct policies (capability-based matching, backfill with timeouts, appropriate gang scheduling constraints) often outperforms a sophisticated scheduler with incorrect policies. Before upgrading the scheduler, audit the policies: confirm that users are not requesting resources they do not need, that heterogeneous resources are properly exposed, and that gang-scheduling timeouts are configured. The audit recurs as workloads evolve, because a preemption grace period that suffices for one model can be too short for another to save state, and placement weights suited to an earlier topology can strand capacity after an expansion.

**Pitfall**: *Treating all GPU-hours as equal when measuring utilization.*

An unlabeled “GPU utilization” percentage can conflate allocation with activity or useful work. For example, 80 percent of fleet capacity might be assigned to jobs while 15 percent of the fleet sits in groups still waiting for their remaining members and another 5 percent waits for input. Only 65 percent is then allocated to complete groups, and at most 60 percent executes useful training in that snapshot. Such a breakdown comes from scheduler and runtime evidence, which an activity counter cannot supply. The allocated, active, and productive tiers of [[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/02 Distributed ML/08 Fleet Orchestration/V2-08.13 Debugging Cluster Utilization#^sec-fleet-orchestration-resource-accounting|section 8.12.1]] keep these measurements distinct, with productive capacity tied to a stated workload objective and the job’s fault-free rate.

**Fallacy**: *Gang scheduling is always necessary for distributed training.*

Gang scheduling prevents partial-allocation deadlock for a rigid synchronous group, but the required group need not equal the job’s maximum requested size. Asynchronous workers may progress independently, while elastic training can begin with its minimum viable gang and resize at supported boundaries. Independent hyperparameter trials can also run separately. The scheduler gains flexibility by admitting the actual unit of progress, while preserving atomic allocation wherever synchronous dependencies require it. Changes to worker count or synchronization policy still require convergence validation.

**Pitfall**: *Setting static quotas based on peak demand.*

Organizations commonly set team quotas to handle worst-case demand, reasoning that teams need guaranteed access during crunch periods. If Team A’s peak demand is 500 GPUs and Team B’s is 300 GPUs, the cluster needs 800 GPUs with static quotas. In practice, peaks rarely coincide. A 600-GPU cluster can accommodate either peak when the other team’s simultaneous demand leaves enough capacity, but it cannot guarantee both peaks concurrently. Hierarchical fair-share with borrowing uses that statistical opportunity only when the declared guarantees fit physical capacity and borrowed work can yield within the reclamation contract. In this example, sizing static quotas to the sum of the peaks provisions 800 GPUs where a 600-GPU shared cluster with borrowing covers either peak. The extra 200, a quarter of the larger cluster, are guarantees that sit unused whenever the peaks do not coincide.

**Fallacy**: *Spot instances are always cheaper for training.*

Actual savings depend on the billed overhead each interruption adds, not on the scenario’s 60 to 90 percent discount band. Consider 15-minute checkpoint overhead to slow storage and 30-minute restart overhead to reacquire capacity and reload, far longer than a restart on a cluster’s own file system, totaling 45 minutes of billed nonproductive time per event. If each event follows 2 hours of retained training progress, the nominal 65 percent discount becomes about 52 percent savings under the fixed-allocation model. This illustration assumes a successful save and no replay; a real interruption budget must also include discarded progress when the save cannot finish. The scheduler must separately account for unbilled capacity waits when evaluating deadlines. Sufficient billed overhead can erase the discount, regardless of the advertised rate.

**Pitfall**: *Ignoring topology when scheduling distributed training.*

Schedulers that ignore connectivity can place communication-intensive groups across paths with less available bandwidth or more contention. On a fleet with NVLink confined to each node, spreading a tensor-parallel group across nodes moves its traffic onto the inter-node fabric; other hardware can expose larger NVLink domains. Crossing a spine switch does not itself prove congestion, so the placement decision must compare effective path capacity and competing traffic. A measured throughput penalty accumulates over a long run and may exceed the delay of waiting for a more suitable allocation. The scheduler must weigh that gain against queue delay and fragmentation.

**Pitfall**: *Using elastic training to avoid gang-scheduling decisions.*

Elastic training complements gang scheduling by moving between viable execution groups. With a fixed 8-way tensor-parallel layout, each group still requires eight workers; changing the number of data-parallel replicas does not relax that requirement. A runtime that changes the tensor or pipeline layout must also redistribute state and establish a new viable group. The scheduler must honor the supported resize granularity, atomic admission requirements, and placement budget for each configuration. Elasticity adds feasible choices; it does not make an incomplete group productive or an arbitrary placement efficient.

**Pitfall**: *Treating high utilization as proof of cluster health.*

A high allocated-GPU percentage does not establish that the cluster has enough headroom to admit new work promptly, because waiting time climbs steeply as offered load nears capacity ([[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/02 Distributed ML/08 Fleet Orchestration/V2-08.13 Debugging Cluster Utilization#^fig-utilization-paradox|figure 16]]). That queueing model does not turn a GPU-busy percentage into a prediction for a gang-scheduled fleet. Real headroom targets depend on arrival bursts, job sizes, placement constraints, and the queue-delay or serving-latency objective. Measuring those outcomes instead of applying a universal utilization threshold keeps platform teams from optimizations that improve a dashboard metric while delaying the work the fleet exists to complete.

1.  Why is targeting near $100\%$ GPU cluster utilization an operational anti-pattern for an interactive AI research and development organization?

    1.  GPU hardware components melt if utilization exceeds $90\%$ for more than 5 consecutive minutes.
    2.  Modern deep learning frameworks automatically downgrade model accuracy when cluster utilization exceeds $85\%$.
    3.  Operating above $95\%$ utilization causes cloud providers to double their hourly billing rates without warning.
    4.  Near saturation, queueing delays can rise sharply and delay experimental feedback; allocation utilization alone does not establish a safe headroom target for the fleet.
