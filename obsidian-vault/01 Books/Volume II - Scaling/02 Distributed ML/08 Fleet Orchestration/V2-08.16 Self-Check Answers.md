1.  **In the Kalos language-model cluster trace, most jobs requested a single GPU yet consumed a tiny share of GPU time, while jobs of 256 or more GPUs were a few percent of submissions and consumed nearly all GPU time. What does this distribution imply for judging a scheduling policy?**

    1.  Judge the policy by job count alone, because most users submit small jobs.
    2.  Judge the policy by GPU-hours alone, because large jobs hold nearly all of the capacity.
    3.  Judge the policy under both views, because small jobs set most users’ waits while a few large gangs hold most of the capacity.
    4.  Treat the distribution as an artifact of one cluster, because production fleets have uniform job sizes.

    *Answer*: The correct answer is C. Counted by jobs, the cluster is a stream of small experiments whose users feel queue delay; counted by GPU-hours, it is a few large gangs that decide packing, reservation, and preemption. A policy tuned to either view alone harms the other. Job count alone ignores where the capacity goes, GPU-hours alone ignores most users’ experience, and the trace shows this mix is the normal production case rather than an artifact.

    *Learning Objective*: Interpret a production job-size distribution under both job-count and GPU-time views.

2.  **A 10,000-GPU cluster raises utilization from 50 percent to 80 percent with no new hardware, holding useful work per active GPU-hour constant. Why does the gain count as 3,000 productive GPU-equivalents but replace 6,000 raw GPUs, and when does it become a cash saving?**

    *Answer*: The productive gain is 10,000 times 0.30, or 3,000 fully utilized GPU-equivalents of useful work. Procurement asks how much hardware that gain replaces: new GPUs run under the old policy would also reach only 50 percent utilization, so delivering 3,000 productive equivalents would take 6,000 raw GPUs. The recovered capacity becomes cash only when it avoids a planned purchase or reduces billed resource time; otherwise it is more useful work from the same investment.

    *Learning Objective*: Distinguish productive GPU-equivalents from raw hardware replaced when valuing a utilization improvement.

3.  **True or False: Whether a job must be scheduled as a rigid gang follows from whether it is labeled an ML training job or an HPC job.**

    *Answer*: False. Rigidity is a property of the execution graph, not of the workload’s field. Rigid synchronous jobs in either domain need a fixed set of co-scheduled ranks, while malleable jobs can progress across a declared range of resource counts. The scheduler must model each job’s minimum viable gang and permitted resize points.

    *Learning Objective*: Classify jobs as rigid or malleable by workload semantics rather than by domain label.

1.  **A scheduler control plane separates admission control, placement, and reconciliation. State what each decides, and explain why keeping them separate helps an operator diagnose a job that has not started.**

    *Answer*: Admission decides whether a job may enter the runnable set under quota, priority, gang, and safety constraints. Placement binds an admitted job to a feasible set of nodes given accelerator capability, topology, power, storage, and network capacity. Reconciliation compares desired allocations with observed node and job state and starts, retries, evicts, or fences work until they agree. Separating them distinguishes an admission denial (for example, quota) from a placement failure (no feasible nodes) and from a reconciliation problem (allocation granted but not realized on the nodes).

    *Learning Objective*: Explain the roles of admission, placement, and reconciliation in a cluster scheduler.

2.  **A network partition cuts the scheduler off from four nodes running a training job, and their heartbeats time out. What must hold before the scheduler reassigns those nodes’ GPUs to another job?**

    1.  Nothing beyond the timeout, because a missed heartbeat proves the old processes have stopped.
    2.  The old allocation must be revoked with confirmed cleanup or fenced, for example by rejecting stale allocation generations, so the old work cannot keep using or writing through those GPUs.
    3.  The partition must heal first, because no recovery may begin while nodes are unreachable.
    4.  The new job must run at higher priority, because priority resolves conflicting ownership.

    *Answer*: The correct answer is B. A timeout can trigger recovery, but it does not prove the old execution has stopped; reassigning without revocation or fencing risks two owners of the same devices when the partition heals. Waiting for the partition to heal is one policy choice, not a requirement, and priority orders requests without preventing duplicate ownership.

    *Learning Objective*: Identify the ownership guarantees required before reusing resources after a network partition.

3.  **True or False: A hypothetical per-device annual hardware-fault probability of 0.001 is sufficient to conclude that all-cause interruptions will remain rare during multi-week jobs on a 10,000-GPU fleet.**

    *Answer*: False. That hypothetical probability covers only the stated device-fault event. Job interruptions also depend on worker count, runtime faults, links, hosts, and shared power or storage domains. A fleet interruption model must include those causes and their dependencies; a device-level annual figure does not establish a measured all-cause job failure rate.

    *Learning Objective*: Evaluate the mathematical relationship between individual component reliability and aggregate fleet failure frequency in large GPU clusters.

1.  **A one-hour job waits nine hours in the queue and then runs for its isolated runtime of one hour. Its \_\_\_\_, the ratio of its queueing-plus-running time to its isolated runtime, is 10, even if its team eventually receives its nominal GPU share.**

    *Answer*: slowdown. Slowdown divides the time from submission to completion by the isolated runtime, here (9 + 1) / 1 = 10. It measures the delay a job experiences, which resource-share parity cannot reveal.

    *Learning Objective*: Define job slowdown as an outcome measure of the delay a job experiences.

2.  **Two teams receive equal dominant shares of the fleet over a month, yet one team’s jobs routinely wait days. Which measurements expose this unfairness, and why does share parity miss it?**

    *Answer*: Share parity measures allocated resources, not the delay users experience before obtaining results. The scheduler should report the distribution of slowdown by user and job class and the age of the oldest pending feasible job: slowdown exposes unequal delay, and maximum feasible wait exposes starvation risk. A policy can be fair in GPU-hours while unfair in the time users wait.

    *Learning Objective*: Distinguish resource-share fairness from completion-delay fairness and name the metrics for each.

3.  **Based on the Pollaczek-Khinchine queue model $\frac{W_q}{1/\mu} = \frac{\rho}{1-\rho} \cdot \frac{1+C_s^2}{2}$, under this illustrative single-server approximation, how does a queue with heavy-tailed job durations ($C_s = 3$) operating at $\rho = 0.8$ utilization compare in expected waiting time ($W_q$) to a queue with the same mean service time and exponential durations ($C_s = 1$) at the same utilization?**

    1.  The ML cluster experiences a $2\times$ increase in expected wait time ($8\times$ vs. $4\times$ the average job duration).
    2.  The ML cluster experiences a $5\times$ increase in expected wait time ($20\times$ vs. $4\times$ the average job duration).
    3.  The ML cluster experiences a $9\times$ increase in expected wait time ($36\times$ vs. $4\times$ the average job duration).
    4.  The ML cluster experiences no change in expected wait time because utilization $\rho$ dominates the Pollaczek-Khinchine denominator.

    *Answer*: The correct answer is B. For $\rho = 0.8$, the utilization multiplier $\frac{\rho}{1-\rho} = \frac{0.8}{0.2} = 4$. For an exponential distribution ($C_s = 1$), the variance term is $\frac{1+1^2}{2} = 1$, yielding $W_q = 4 \times (1/\mu)$. For an ML cluster with heavy-tailed durations ($C_s = 3$), the variance term is $\frac{1+3^2}{2} = 5$, yielding $W_q = 4 \times 5 = 20 \times (1/\mu)$, which represents a $5\times$ increase in expected wait time. The assertion of a $2\times$ increase mistakenly scales linearly with $C_s$ rather than quadratic variance. The $9\times$ multiplier erroneously squares $C_s$ without averaging with the baseline offset. The claim of no change ignores service-time variability. This single-server model illustrates queue pressure; a gang-scheduled fleet requires its own measured arrival, placement, and service behavior.

    *Learning Objective*: Calculate the expected queue waiting time multiplier for heavy-tailed ML workloads compared to uniform workloads using the Pollaczek-Khinchine formula.

1.  **True or False: Under Dominant Resource Fairness (DRF), if Team A’s workload requires $4\text{ GPUs}$ and $8\text{ CPU cores}$ per worker while Team B’s workload requires $1\text{ GPU}$ and $64\text{ CPU cores}$ per worker on a cluster with $100\text{ GPUs}$ and $1000\text{ CPU cores}$, DRF equalizes each team’s share of their dominant resource (GPUs for Team A, CPUs for Team B).**

    *Answer*: True. Dominant Resource Fairness (DRF) multi-resource allocation identifies each tenant’s dominant resource (the resource type for which the tenant requests the largest fraction of total cluster capacity) and equalizes the dominant shares across all active tenants, ensuring multi-resource equity without allowing single-resource monopolies.

    *Learning Objective*: Apply Dominant Resource Fairness (DRF) principles to multi-resource ML allocations.

2.  **When multi-GPU jobs leave unallocated accelerators spread across nodes that cannot be combined to satisfy pending multi-GPU requests due to intra-node interconnect requirements, the cluster suffers from \_\_\_\_.**

    *Answer*: fragmentation (or resource fragmentation). In ML scheduling, fragmentation refers to stranded, non-contiguous capacity across nodes that individually satisfy no pending job, despite the aggregate free GPU count being theoretically sufficient.

    *Learning Objective*: Identify the primary operational inefficiency caused by multi-dimensional bin-packing constraints in GPU clusters.

3.  **In a 1,024-GPU cluster receiving two 1,024-GPU synchronous training jobs, what condition causes a hold-and-wait deadlock under a non-gang scheduler, and how does gang scheduling eliminate it?**

    1.  Non-gang schedulers allocate partial subsets (e.g., 512 GPUs to each job) where neither job can progress through synchronous AllReduce collectives, whereas gang scheduling enforces atomic all-or-nothing allocation so unallocated jobs hold zero resources.
    2.  Non-gang schedulers force jobs to time-slice GPU compute cycles, causing NCCL timeouts during AllReduce, whereas gang scheduling disables CUDA preemption entirely.
    3.  Non-gang schedulers assign GPUs across multiple racks, creating switch contention, whereas gang scheduling restricts all 1,024 GPUs to a single physical server chassis.
    4.  Non-gang schedulers allow lower-priority jobs to preempt higher-priority jobs, whereas gang scheduling locks node allocations until all jobs in the queue complete.

    *Answer*: The correct answer is A. In synchronous distributed training, the marginal utility of a partial allocation is zero because every worker must participate in every AllReduce collective. If a non-gang scheduler grants 512 GPUs to Job 1 and 512 GPUs to Job 2, both jobs stall waiting for the remaining GPUs, burning idle power at 0% useful progress. Gang scheduling eliminates the Coffman hold-and-wait condition by mandating atomic all-or-nothing allocation: a job is either granted its full requirement or holds nothing in the queue. The time-slicing distractor confuses multi-tasking with resource allocation. The single-chassis distractor states a physical impossibility for 1,024 GPUs. The lock-queue distractor misrepresents priority preemption.

    *Learning Objective*: Analyze how gang scheduling prevents distributed deadlocks in synchronous multi-node training.

4.  **Describe how priority inversion occurs when a high-priority distributed training job requires accelerators held by a low-priority job, and explain why simply setting higher priority levels does not resolve the blockage.**

    *Answer*: Priority inversion occurs when a high-priority job is blocked waiting for accelerators held by a low-priority job that cannot complete its exit path because intermediate-priority jobs are saturating auxiliary resources (such as host CPU, storage I/O, or network bandwidth needed to write a checkpoint). Simply increasing priority levels fails because the blockage is transitive: the high-priority job depends on the low-priority job exiting, which in turn depends on the low-priority job receiving sufficient auxiliary bandwidth (holistic preemption) to finish checkpointing and release the accelerators.

    *Learning Objective*: Explain how priority inversion manifests in multi-tenant ML clusters and why strict priority levels alone fail to prevent it.

5.  **How does heterogeneous gang scheduling differ from traditional homogeneous gang scheduling in the context of reinforcement learning from human feedback (RLHF) workflows?**

    *Answer*: Traditional gang scheduling allocates identical worker nodes executing symmetric bulk-synchronous parallel graphs. In contrast, heterogeneous gang scheduling atomically co-allocates asymmetric roles within a single logical job, such as memory-optimized nodes with high HBM for inference rollout generation alongside compute-dense nodes for policy gradient updates. The co-allocation requires viable resource bundles and sufficient data-transfer capacity across roles. Measured production and consumption rates reveal imbalance; continuous or perfectly balanced routing is not guaranteed.

    *Learning Objective*: Compare traditional homogeneous gang scheduling with heterogeneous gang scheduling in multi-role pipelines such as RLHF.

1.  **What is the fundamental difference in control philosophy between Slurm and Kubernetes when managing distributed ML training workloads?**

    1.  Slurm relies on continuous reconciliation loops against desired state stored in etcd, whereas Kubernetes executes imperative one-time allocation scripts via a centralized daemon.
    2.  Slurm uses peer-to-peer gossip protocols to schedule tasks across nodes, whereas Kubernetes enforces hierarchical master-slave token ring passing.
    3.  Slurm employs an imperative allocation model where users request explicit physical resources guaranteed at launch, whereas Kubernetes uses a declarative model where controllers continuously reconcile observed cluster state toward declared desired state.
    4.  Slurm schedules containerized microservices with dynamic horizontal auto-scaling, whereas Kubernetes only supports uncontainerized bare-metal MPI processes.

    *Answer*: The correct answer is C. Slurm originates from HPC batch systems and uses an imperative model (sbatch requests explicit resources like `--gres=gpu:8 --nodes=4`), providing authoritative upfront allocation. Kubernetes uses a declarative model where controllers continuously observe actual state and execute reconciliation loops to converge toward desired state (e.g., Pods, Deployments). The option swapping Slurm and Kubernetes mechanisms reverses their core architectures. The gossip protocol alternative invents a non-existent scheduling model for Slurm. The containerization claim reverses the platforms’ primary native workload types.

    *Learning Objective*: Compare the core architectural control mechanisms of Slurm and Kubernetes for ML training workflows.

2.  **Kubernetes’ default scheduler places a 64-pod training job one pod at a time, and the cluster has room for only 60 of the pods. How does a PodGroup with a minimum membership of 64 change the outcome?**

    1.  The scheduler binds the first 60 pods immediately and starts training with a smaller communication group, adding the last 4 pods as capacity frees.
    2.  The scheduler tentatively reserves placements but binds no pod until all 64 members are placed, releasing every reservation if the attempt fails or times out.
    3.  The scheduler preempts every lower-priority pod in the cluster until 64 GPUs are free, regardless of priority-class settings.
    4.  The PodGroup guarantees that all 64 containers start at the same instant, so the framework no longer needs to establish its communication group.

    *Answer*: The correct answer is B. A PodGroup turns admission into a group decision. The scheduler reserves placements as it finds them but binds none until the minimum membership is placed, and it releases the reservations on failure or timeout, so 60 pods never hold GPUs while waiting for 4 more. Binding 60 pods recreates the partial allocation that strands capacity, and preemption follows priority-class policy rather than group admission. Group admission also does not make launches simultaneous; the framework must still form its communication group.

    *Learning Objective*: Explain how PodGroup minimum membership supplies the group admission that a rigid training job needs on Kubernetes.

3.  **In the illustrative fair-share priority model ($\text{Priority}_{\text{effective}} = \text{Priority}_{\text{base}} \times \frac{F_{\text{target}}}{F_{\text{actual}} + \epsilon}$), what role does the usage decay half-life play, and what is the trade-off between setting a short versus a long half-life?**

    *Answer*: The half-life determines how quickly the scheduler discounts past GPU consumption in $F_{\text{actual}}$, allowing heavy users to gradually restore their priority. A short half-life (e.g., 1-2 days) rapidly restores priority but can cause oscillatory usage where teams cycle between hogging and starvation; a long half-life (e.g., 2-4 weeks) stabilizes priority order but penalizes researchers long after a major burst project has completed.

    *Learning Objective*: Explain how usage decay affects priority in the illustrative fair-share model.

4.  **Why do high-performance distributed training jobs in Kubernetes typically enable `hostNetwork: true` and leverage the NVIDIA Network Operator instead of relying on default Container Network Interface (CNI) overlay networks?**

    *Answer*: Host networking changes which network namespace a pod uses and can avoid an overlay path. It does not allocate an RDMA device or grant access to its device files. Device plugins and operators must arrange the required devices, drivers, permissions, and GPU-to-NIC mapping; the fabric must also support the intended transport. Validate bandwidth and latency for that configured path rather than inferring RDMA from hostNetwork alone.

    *Learning Objective*: Explain the separate network-namespace, device-access, and transport requirements of high-performance training in Kubernetes.

5.  **True or False: Kueue replaces the native Kubernetes `kube-scheduler` binary entirely to implement custom gang scheduling algorithms, whereas Volcano acts purely as an admission controller layered on top of the default scheduler.**

    *Answer*: False. Kueue governs admission and quota reservation while delegating pod placement to a scheduler. Its all-or-nothing mechanisms can track workload readiness and return quota when an admitted workload cannot become ready within its configured contract. Volcano provides batch and gang scheduling and may coexist with the default scheduler. Admission, placement, and readiness are separate controls.

    *Learning Objective*: Differentiate between scheduler replacement (Volcano) and queue admission control (Kueue) in the Kubernetes batch ecosystem.

6.  \*\*Order the dependencies in this illustrative launch path for a Kubernetes training job configured with a compatible training operator and Volcano group admission:

2.  **A persistent degraded switch path lowers measured end-to-end training throughput by 30 percent. A checkpoint-and-migration pause would take 10 minutes, healthy replacement capacity is available, and days of training remain. What is the appropriate orchestration response?**

    1.  Continue running the job indefinitely because a 10-minute checkpoint-and-reschedule migration would cost more compute than a 30% slowdown over a multi-week run.
    2.  Rely on the GPU driver to disable NVLink and route all tensor-parallel traffic through host memory over PCIe.

Rail alignment reduces avoidable cross-rail traffic, but concurrent jobs, oversubscribed links, and skew can still contend for bandwidth.

1.  **When an elastic scheduler resizes a distributed training job from 64 GPUs to 128 GPUs, what trade-off exists between maintaining a constant global batch size versus scaling batch size proportionally with worker count?**

    1.  Constant global batch size halves the effective examples per worker per optimizer update, removing one source of optimization change without guaranteeing identical convergence; proportional batch scaling preserves that per-worker count but changes optimization and requires a validated learning-rate policy.
    2.  Constant global batch size requires restarting the model weights from epoch zero, whereas proportional batch size allows continuous gradient descent without learning rate adjustment.
    3.  Constant global batch size disables NVLink communication across workers, whereas proportional batch size enables ring AllReduce.
    4.  Constant global batch size doubles the learning rate automatically via PyTorch hooks, whereas proportional batch size keeps the learning rate fixed to prevent divergence.

With constant global batch, doubling worker count halves the effective per-worker batch, which includes gradient accumulation rather than necessarily requiring smaller microbatches. Shrinking instead raises the examples each worker must contribute, potentially increasing memory demand or accumulation time. Preserving global batch controls one source of optimization change but does not prove identical convergence or hardware efficiency. 2.  **An elastic job running on 64 GPUs at 100 steps/minute can scale to 128 GPUs, reaching 180 steps/minute. If resizing triggers a dynamic rendezvous and checkpoint-sync transition pause of 60 seconds (0 steps during pause), what is the break-even duration the job must run after the pause at 128 GPUs to recover the lost progress in total steps completed?**

    1.  At least 15 seconds.
    2.  At least 30 seconds.
    3.  At least 45 seconds.
    4.  At least 75 seconds.

    *Answer*: The correct answer is D. In the 60-second (1.0 minute) pause, the 64-GPU baseline would have completed $100 \text{ steps/min} \times 1.0 \text{ min} = 100 \text{ steps}$. At 128 GPUs, the job produces $180 \text{ steps/min}$, which is an incremental gain of $\Delta = 180 - 100 = 80 \text{ steps/min}$. To recover the 100 lost steps, the job must run at 128 GPUs for $t = 100 / 80 = 1.25 \text{ minutes} = 75 \text{ seconds}$. At 75 seconds the runs tie; a net gain requires running longer. The shorter choices do not recover the 100 lost steps.

    *Learning Objective*: Calculate the break-even duration required for an elastic scaling resize to deliver positive net training throughput.

3.  **What three parameters define the operational contract between an elastic scheduler and an elastic training framework (such as TorchElastic), and why is each necessary?**

    *Answer*: The contract specifies the elastic range and valid allocation increments, the measured transition cost including any checkpoint, restart, redistribution, and rendezvous, and the scaling-efficiency curve for supported configurations. The range identifies viable execution groups; the transition cost determines how long a new allocation must persist to repay disruption; the curve supports comparing marginal progress with the value of allocating those devices elsewhere. Batch and quality policies must also remain valid under the chosen resize.

    *Learning Objective*: Explain the three core parameters of the contract between an elastic scheduler and a training framework.

4.  **True or False: Under a supported resize and checkpoint contract, an elastic scheduler can reclaim capacity by shrinking a training job to a smaller viable configuration without terminating the logical job, although worker processes may restart.**

    *Answer*: True. Elasticity adds supported allocation increments between the declared bounds rather than permitting arbitrary or continuous worker counts. A planned shrink can save state, restart or redistribute workers, and rebuild the group at reduced throughput. It still pays transition overhead; preserving recent progress depends on a valid save, while a forced interruption can require replay from the last durable checkpoint.

    *Learning Objective*: Compare binary preemption with elastic worker downsizing under sudden cluster contention.

5.  **A worker encounters an unhandled CUDA fault during a TorchElastic training run. Explain the dependencies that must hold before useful training resumes, and why recovery cannot require every surviving worker to finish a clean step boundary.**

    *Answer*: Failure detection triggers worker-group recovery; the scheduler must preserve exclusive resource ownership and provide or authorize a viable allocation, while elastic agents stop and restart the worker group as required by the runtime. Restarted workers re-rendezvous, rebuild ranks and process groups, and restore valid durable training state before resuming. Detection, allocation, and cleanup can overlap rather than follow one universal serial order. A fault can strand peers inside a collective, so a clean step boundary is not guaranteed; recovery may replay work after the last valid checkpoint.

    *Learning Objective*: Explain the allocation, membership, and durable-state dependencies of elastic recovery after an unexpected worker failure.

1.  **What is a preemption cascade in a shared GPU cluster, and which control stops it at the first level?**

    1.  A cascade is a burst of hardware failures in one rack; spare-capacity reservation stops it.
    2.  A cascade is a high-priority admission that evicts medium-priority jobs, each of which then evicts lower-priority jobs to reschedule itself; requiring evicted jobs to requeue without preempting anyone stops it after the first level.
    3.  A cascade is a queue of jobs waiting behind a large gang; backfill scheduling stops it.
    4.  A cascade is repeated checkpointing by one job; lengthening its checkpoint interval stops it.

    *Answer*: The correct answer is B. If an urgent job displaces a handful of jobs and each of those displaces as many again on rescheduling, the interruptions multiply with each level, each paying the preemption overhead and adding checkpoint reload traffic at the same moment. A rule that evicted jobs requeue without preempting caps the damage at the first level, and a preemption budget limits how often any one job can be displaced. Failure bursts, queueing behind a gang, and checkpoint frequency are different problems.

    *Learning Objective*: Explain how preemption cascades multiply interruption cost and which controls bound them.

2.  **A training job moves from on-demand to spot capacity at the chapter’s scenario discount. According to the effective-cost relation, in which effective cost equals the spot price times billed allocation time divided by retained productive time, what determines whether the discount survives?**

    1.  Only the advertised discount, because interruptions are free once a provider sends a notice.
    2.  The ratio of billed allocation time to retained productive time, which grows with checkpoint overhead, restart time, and work replayed after each interruption.
    3.  The number of GPUs in the job, because larger jobs always receive deeper spot discounts.
    4.  The provider’s capacity-placement score, which predicts how many interruptions the job will see per day.

    *Answer*: The correct answer is B. Effective cost is the spot price scaled by billed time over retained progress. Checkpointing, recovery, and discarded work all add billed time without adding retained progress, so frequent or expensive interruptions can erase the discount. A notice gives an opportunity to save state but does not make the interruption free, and a placement score estimates whether a request will succeed, not how often the job will be interrupted.

    *Learning Objective*: Apply the spot effective-cost relation to decide whether a discount survives interruption overhead.

3.  **An organization runs steady 24/7 inference, urgent retraining with tight deadlines, and a large hyperparameter sweep. Which assignment of these workloads to reserved, on-demand, and spot capacity matches each workload’s deadline and recovery contract?**

    1.  Inference on spot, retraining on reserved, sweep on on-demand.
    2.  Inference on reserved, retraining on on-demand, sweep on spot.
    3.  All three on spot, because the discount outweighs any interruption risk.
    4.  Inference on on-demand, retraining on spot, sweep on reserved.

    *Answer*: The correct answer is B. Reserved capacity suits predictable continuous load such as steady inference. On-demand capacity avoids long-term commitment for urgent work that cannot tolerate reclamation, at full price and with admission and startup delay. Spot capacity suits restart-tolerant work such as independent sweep trials. Placing inference or deadline-bound retraining on reclaimable capacity, or reserving capacity for a bursty sweep, mismatches the tier to the workload’s contract.

    *Learning Objective*: Map workloads to reserved, on-demand, and spot capacity tiers by deadline and restart tolerance.

4.  **This chapter treats lifecycle total cost of ownership (TCO) as an input to the scheduler rather than as a scheduling mechanism. What does the scheduler consume from TCO analysis and cost accounting, and how does it use those inputs?**

    *Answer*: It consumes per-resource opportunity costs and policy constraints: the marginal price of each capacity tier (owned, reserved, on-demand, or interruptible), the reservation a project may claim, the interruption risk it may accept, and the deadline it must meet. It uses them only to rank feasible scheduling actions such as waiting, preempting lower-priority work, falling back to on-demand capacity, or shrinking elastically. Building the TCO model, attributing costs, and chargeback belong to the fleet’s accounting and economics processes, not to the orchestrator.

    *Learning Objective*: Explain how lifecycle cost analysis reaches the scheduler as price signals and constraints rather than as a scheduling mechanism.

5.  **A low-priority 64-GPU fine-tuning run is forcibly preempted. What makes up the preemption overhead for that event, and why does a per-job preemption budget matter more for this run than for a high-priority job?**

    *Answer*: The overhead is the work discarded since the last durable checkpoint plus the relaunch, checkpoint reload, and warmup needed before the job is productive again, charged across every GPU of the allocation. A low-priority run sits at the bottom of every preemption cascade, so without a budget it can be struck many times a day and lose hours of allocation time; a budget caps how often it can be displaced, at the cost of delaying some higher-priority admissions.

    *Learning Objective*: Decompose the preemption overhead and explain why preemption budgets protect low-priority work.

6.  **True or False: Because each VM instance in a cloud provider has an independent lifecycle, placing worker nodes of a 64-node distributed training job across different instance types within the same Availability Zone guarantees that preemption events will remain uncorrelated and statistically independent.**

    *Answer*: False. Cloud capacity pressures and spot revocations are driven by macroeconomic cluster demand spikes and provider-level resource reclamation within a datacenter or Availability Zone. Preemption events for high-demand GPU types (e.g., A100/H100) are heavily correlated within an AZ; when capacity becomes scarce, providers reclaim multiple instances simultaneously, invalidating assumptions of independent failure.

    *Learning Objective*: Evaluate the assumption of statistical independence in cloud spot instance preemption events.

1.  **How does the Tiresias scheduler solve the problem of inaccurate and inflated user runtime estimates in GPU cluster scheduling?**

    1.  It uses two-dimensional least attained service based on observed spatial (GPU count) and temporal (elapsed execution time) resource attainment, prioritizing jobs that have consumed less cumulative compute without asking users for duration estimates.
    2.  It requires users to pay monetary deposits that are forfeited if their job runtime deviates by more than $10\%$ from their initial estimate.
    3.  It parses Python ASTs at submission time to statically calculate the exact loop iteration count and FLOP requirements of every training script.
    4.  It forces all jobs to run for exactly 60 minutes before automatically killing and requeueing them.

    *Answer*: The correct answer is A. Tiresias eliminates the game-theoretic distortion of user runtime estimates by adopting duration-agnostic scheduling. It implements a two-dimensional least-attained-service policy that tracks both spatial (GPU count) and temporal (time elapsed) resources consumed. Jobs with low attained service are given higher priority, naturally prioritizing short exploratory jobs over long pretraining runs without requiring users to predict runtimes. Financial penalties, AST-based static runtime calculation, and fixed 60-minute time limits are incorrect.

    *Learning Objective*: Explain how Tiresias eliminates reliance on user runtime estimates using Least Attained Service (LAS) scheduling.

2.  **In the Pollux scheduling architecture, how is the** Goodput\*\* metric ($G_j$) defined to co-optimize cluster resource allocation and job-level training dynamics?\*\*

    1.  Goodput is the total number of raw GPU clock cycles executed divided by the cluster’s electricity consumption in kilowatt-hours.
    2.  Goodput is the ratio of network packets transmitted over InfiniBand to the number of retransmissions caused by packet drops.
    3.  Goodput is training throughput in examples per second multiplied by statistical efficiency, the progress per example relative to a reference batch size.
    4.  Goodput is the percentage of time GPUs spend running CUDA kernels compared to CPU data loading time.

    *Answer*: The correct answer is C. Pollux combines examples processed per second under an allocation and batch size with the relative optimization progress each example contributes at that batch size. Increasing batch size can raise system throughput while lowering statistical efficiency, so more examples per second do not necessarily mean faster convergence. Allocation changes also pay a transition cost, and the selected policy must be validated at the same target quality. The other choices describe energy efficiency, network reliability, and hardware activity.

    *Learning Objective*: Apply the Pollux Goodput optimization metric to balance cluster-wide resource allocation and job-level batch size.

3.  **What mechanisms does Gandiva use to achieve fine-grained, low-overhead time-slicing and job migration across GPUs?**

    *Answer*: Gandiva uses iteration boundaries as preemption points after temporary activations have been released. Weights and optimizer state remain and may still be expensive to serialize or transfer. Profiling iteration and transition costs lets the scheduler choose when time-slicing or migration is worthwhile; the boundary reduces transient state without making switching free or eliminating all lost progress.

    *Learning Objective*: Describe the core mechanisms used by Gandiva to achieve low-latency scheduling without wasting training progress.

4.  **The custom ML scheduler Themis optimizes for \_\_\_\_ fairness, ensuring that each job completes in a time comparable to what it would achieve in a dedicated cluster share, thereby preventing gaming and multi-resource allocation monopolies.**

    *Answer*: finish-time (or finish-time fairness, finish time). Themis designs an auction-based, finish-time fairness metric that balances resource allocation based on how much a scheduler’s decision affects the actual completion time of competing jobs.

    *Learning Objective*: Identify the fairness concept implemented by Themis to prevent users from gaming cluster resource allocators.

5.  **Under a two-dimensional attained-service policy with service bins of less than 1, 1 to 10, 10 to 100, and more than 100 GPU-hours, order these running jobs from highest to lowest scheduling priority: (1) 64 GPUs for 2 hours, (2) 1 GPU for 30 minutes, (3) 8 GPUs for 1 hour, (4) 16 GPUs for 4 hours.**

    *Answer*: The correct order is: (2) 1 GPU for 30 minutes, (3) 8 GPUs for 1 hour, (4) 16 GPUs for 4 hours, (1) 64 GPUs for 2 hours. Attained service multiplies GPU count by elapsed time, giving 0.5, 8, 64, and 128 GPU-hours, which fall in four successive bins, and lower-service bins run first. Ranking by elapsed time alone would place the 64-GPU job ahead of the 16-GPU job and miss that it has already consumed twice the GPU-time. Under heavy-tailed durations, low attained service predicts a short job, and a starvation guard can still promote a job that has waited too long.

    *Learning Objective*: Apply two-dimensional attained service to rank jobs by scheduling priority and explain why GPU count must enter the ranking.

1.  **Why does the fleet orchestrator schedule a 175B-parameter serving replica as an eight-GPU group rather than as eight independent GPUs?**

    1.  Kubernetes cannot address more than one GPU per pod unless the pod is marked as a gang.
    2.  The replica’s weights are sharded across a tensor-parallel group, so it can serve only when every member is placed; a partial placement delivers no serving capacity at all.
    3.  Eight independent GPUs would each need a full copy of the weights, which improves fault tolerance but wastes memory.
    4.  Grouping GPUs lets the orchestrator skip readiness checks for the replica.

    *Answer*: The correct answer is B. A model of this size does not fit on one accelerator, so its replica is a tensor-parallel execution group that can accept requests only when every shard is resident. The orchestrator must therefore place the group atomically, just as it places a training gang. Pod-level GPU limits, full weight copies, and readiness checks are not the reason for the grouping.

    *Learning Objective*: Explain why serving replicas for large models are indivisible scheduling units in the shared fleet.

2.  **In a multi-tenant inference environment hosting multiple smaller models on shared GPUs, what is the primary architectural trade-off between NVIDIA Multi-Process Service (MPS) and Multi-Instance GPU (MIG)?**

    1.  MIG is only supported on consumer GeForce cards, whereas MPS requires enterprise Grace Hopper superchips.
    2.  MPS provides sub-nanosecond hardware memory encryption, whereas MIG only supports unencrypted cleartext transfers.
    3.  MPS requires dedicated physical PCIe switches per tenant, whereas MIG operates over standard Ethernet cables.
    4.  MPS permits concurrent clients sharing execution and memory-bandwidth resources, while MIG partitions device compute and memory resources more strongly at the cost of fixed profiles and placement constraints.

    *Answer*: The correct answer is D. Modern MPS clients have separate virtual address spaces but share execution, memory bandwidth, capacity, and parts of the device fault domain. MIG provides hardware resource partitions and stronger performance and fault containment, with fixed profiles. Neither guarantees immunity to all device, host, or platform failures, and a client OOM is not itself proof that the entire MPS server crashes.

    *Learning Objective*: Compare GPU resource sharing mechanisms (MIG, MPS, Time-slicing) along isolation, memory protection, and efficiency dimensions.

3.  **How can an organization exploit predictable diurnal serving demand, and what readiness and reclamation contract is necessary to protect serving SLOs?**

    *Answer*: Lend surplus serving capacity to opportunistic training during forecast low demand, while preserving already-ready headroom for bursts faster than reclamation. Begin transfers before predicted demand rises, allowing for the measured complete path through training checkpoint or shrink, cleanup, serving launch, weight loading, and warmup. Priority establishes which workload yields; it does not make released GPUs immediately ready or establish a universal overnight demand reduction. Size the reserve against burst demand, forecast error, and measured readiness delay.

    *Learning Objective*: Design an operational policy for harvesting diurnal idle inference GPU capacity for batch training.

4.  **Why can a shared fleet not move GPUs between training and serving in response to second-by-second traffic bursts, and how does the orchestrator use the diurnal traffic curve instead?**

    *Answer*: The full path can include training checkpoint or shrink, allocation release and cleanup, inference launch, weight loading, and warmup, with any overlap measured for the runtime. When it takes minutes, it cannot cover bursts that exhaust ready headroom in seconds. The orchestrator therefore starts transfers ahead of forecast demand and retains already-ready reserve for faster changes; the required lead and reserve depend on the workload, startup measurements, and forecast error.

    *Learning Objective*: Analyze how mode-switching cost determines the timescale on which the training-serving boundary can move.

5.  **True or False: If two serving models fit together within an 80 GB GPU’s memory under MPS, co-locating them is guaranteed to halve the number of GPUs the fleet needs without affecting latency.**

    *Answer*: False. Memory fit is only the first admission condition. Co-located models under MPS share execution units and memory bandwidth, so two compute-bound models contend for the same SMs and each loses throughput, while differing isolated bottlenecks make a pair a candidate for complementary placement rather than prove compatibility. The size of either penalty must be measured for the actual pair. Consolidation is justified only after load testing shows that each co-resident pair still meets its throughput and tail-latency objectives with the required headroom.

    *Learning Objective*: Evaluate the interference conditions that must hold before GPU sharing reduces fleet size.

6.  **Instead of statically partitioning a serving cluster sized for peak traffic and a separate training cluster, an orchestrator that lends idle overnight serving GPUs to training and reclaims them before the morning ramp practices dynamic \_\_\_\_.**

    *Answer*: The correct answer is sharing (dynamic sharing). It unifies the serving and training pools, recovering idle serving capacity at the cost of mode-switching time that must be planned around the diurnal forecast.

    *Learning Objective*: Identify the policy that replaces static training-serving partitioning in a shared fleet.

1.  **In a multi-tenant cluster, how does decoupling ‘guaranteed quota’ from ‘limit (burst) quota’ prevent stranded idle capacity while protecting team allocations?**

    *Answer*: A guaranteed quota defines baseline access under an explicit reclaim-time contract; a burst limit permits borrowing otherwise idle devices above that baseline. When the owning team needs its guarantee, borrowed work must shrink or be preempted within that contract. Concurrent guarantees must fit reclaimable physical capacity, and checkpoint or shutdown delays mean access is not necessarily instantaneous.

    *Learning Objective*: Explain the architectural difference between guaranteed quota and limit quota in shared GPU clusters.

2.  **What is ‘quota hoarding’ in shared GPU clusters, and what automated scheduler policies prevent researchers from engaging in it?**

    *Answer*: Quota hoarding retains idle physical allocations to avoid waiting again when work becomes ready. Low activity should trigger investigation against step progress, runtime health, dependencies, and the workload’s timeout and return contract; legitimate input or communication waits can look idle. Eligible capacity can be lent under bounded reclamation, and leases permit reuse only after enforced revocation and cleanup. Charging holding time exposes opportunity cost, while revising a longer-term guarantee requires a separate demand review.

    *Learning Objective*: Analyze the organizational anti-pattern of quota hoarding and describe governance mechanisms to eliminate it.

3.  **Team A in Department X submits a new 16-GPU job. Which entitlement, feasibility, fairness, and ownership checks must its scheduler enforce before granting the allocation, and why is checking free GPU count alone insufficient?**

    *Answer*: Check department and team caps, distinguish guaranteed from borrowed capacity, and establish any required reclamation contract. Candidate bundles must satisfy accelerator capability, every requested resource dimension, gang membership, and topology before fairness chooses among runnable alternatives. Commit the allocation against current authoritative state, enforce its ownership at the resources, and update accounting. Free GPU count alone does not prove a viable group, timely return of borrowed capacity, or absence of conflicting ownership; implementations can interleave checks while preserving these dependencies.

    *Learning Objective*: Explain how hierarchical entitlements and feasible resource bundles constrain fair admission in a multi-tenant fleet.

1.  **A cluster dashboard reports $85\%$ GPU utilization, yet researchers see slow progress. Which distinction between utilization tiers explains the contradiction?**

    1.  Allocated capacity can be high while active utilization is low because jobs wait on input or communication, and productive utilization can be lower still because of restarts and replayed work.
    2.  The dashboard counts only CPU time, so GPU activity is invisible to it.
    3.  High utilization always means the cluster is healthy, so the researchers’ perception must be wrong.
    4.  Productive utilization always exceeds allocated utilization, so the dashboard understates the cluster’s progress.

    *Answer*: The correct answer is A. Allocated capacity counts devices held by complete allocations, active utilization counts hardware activity under a stated counter, and productive utilization counts retained progress at the job’s fault-free rate. Each tier counts no more than the one above it, so an 85 percent allocation figure can hide idle kernels and discarded work. High utilization does not establish health, and productive capacity cannot exceed what is allocated.

    *Learning Objective*: Distinguish allocated, active, and productive utilization when diagnosing a cluster.

2.  **An administrator observes that average GPU compute utilization across a 1,000-GPU cluster is only $55\%$, yet users experience multi-day queue wait times for 64-GPU jobs. Which diagnostic finding points to the primary root cause?**

    1.  Demand exceeds the cluster’s capacity, so the queue can shrink only after more GPUs are purchased.
    2.  Severe resource fragmentation and strict gang scheduling constraints prevent 64-GPU contiguous blocks from forming, while short jobs hold scattered GPUs across nodes without filling them.
    3.  At 55 percent load, the queueing factor ${\rho_{\text{serv}}}/(1-{\rho_{\text{serv}}})$ alone already makes multi-day waits unavoidable.
    4.  Input-pipeline stalls lower SM activity on running jobs, and low SM activity is what keeps the scheduler from admitting the 64-GPU jobs.

    *Answer*: The correct answer is B. Moderate average utilization can coexist with long waits when small jobs hold one or two GPUs on many nodes. Hundreds of GPUs sit idle in aggregate, but no 64-GPU gang can assemble within the placement constraints. A capacity shortfall would not leave hundreds of GPUs idle while jobs wait, so adding hardware treats a placement-feasibility problem as a demand problem. At a load of 0.55 the factor ${\rho_{\text{serv}}}/(1-{\rho_{\text{serv}}})$ is only about 1.2, far from the steep part of the curve. Admission depends on whether a complete allocation is feasible, not on the SM activity of jobs already running.

    *Learning Objective*: Analyze the root causes of high queue wait times coexisting with moderate average cluster utilization.

3.  **During a multi-GPU training job, GPU SM utilization oscillates between 10 percent and 95 percent, host CPUs are busy, and disk wait is high. What hypothesis should the operator test next before choosing a remedy?**

    1.  A zombie allocation; the low phases show that the job has stopped making progress, so it should be terminated.
    2.  A communication bottleneck; the ranks are waiting on collectives, so the job should be moved under a single leaf switch.
    3.  The input pipeline may be limiting progress; align GPU idle intervals with batch-readiness waits, then measure whether decoding, storage, or transfer is the limiting stage.
    4.  KV-cache memory fragmentation; the allocator cannot fit the next batch even though HBM occupancy shows free capacity.

    *Answer*: The correct answer is C. Busy host CPUs and high disk wait point toward an input-pipeline investigation, but they do not establish its cause. Confirm that GPU idle intervals coincide with waits for the next batch, then separate storage, decoding or augmentation, and host-to-device transfer costs before choosing a remedy. A zombie allocation makes no further progress, whereas this job keeps returning to 95 percent activity. Collective waits do not explain busy host CPUs and disk wait, and communication kernels can keep SM activity high. KV-cache fragmentation limits how many requests a serving replica admits; it does not describe a training job’s input supply.

    *Learning Objective*: Use aligned runtime and host telemetry to test an input-pipeline bottleneck hypothesis before selecting a remedy.

4.  **What is a ‘zombie allocation’ (or ghost job) in a GPU cluster, and how can automated health monitors detect and remediate it?**

    *Answer*: A zombie allocation remains assigned after useful execution has stopped, for example when failure propagation leaves ranks blocked or orphaned processes retain device state. Low SM activity or retained memory alone cannot establish that diagnosis because legitimate input and communication waits can look similar. Correlate scheduler state with step progress, runtime health, process evidence, and the timeout contract; then enforce termination or node remediation and confirm old execution has stopped before releasing or reassigning the devices.

    *Learning Objective*: Formulate a diagnostic procedure to identify and clean up zombie GPU allocations.

5.  **True or False: In a ring AllReduce across 32 nodes, if 31 nodes show $95\%$ GPU utilization and 1 node shows $10\%$ utilization, the node with $10\%$ utilization is the only healthy node while the 31 nodes are encountering network hardware faults.**

    *Answer*: False. Utilization alone cannot identify which node is healthy: high activity may be useful compute or waiting inside communication kernels, and low activity may reflect input starvation or another dependency. Correlate per-rank compute and collective timelines, link health, and input waits to identify the rank or path delaying the group before assigning a hardware diagnosis.

    *Learning Objective*: Evaluate telemetry signals to pinpoint straggler nodes in multi-node AllReduce rings.

6.  **A dashboard shows 120 free GPUs, yet a 64-GPU gang waits for days because the free devices sit one or two per node across racks that its topology constraint cannot combine. A \_\_\_\_ index, which counts free GPUs that cannot be allocated because of topology constraints, makes this stranded capacity visible where aggregate utilization hides it.**

    *Answer*: fragmentation. The fragmentation index separates idle capacity the scheduler could use from idle capacity that no pending request can fit, so an operator can tell a placement-feasibility problem from a demand shortfall before adding hardware or changing priorities.

    *Learning Objective*: Identify the dashboard metric that exposes stranded, unallocatable capacity and distinguish it from aggregate utilization.

1.  **Why is targeting near $100\%$ GPU cluster utilization an operational anti-pattern for an interactive AI research and development organization?**

    1.  GPU hardware components melt if utilization exceeds $90\%$ for more than 5 consecutive minutes.
    2.  Modern deep learning frameworks automatically downgrade model accuracy when cluster utilization exceeds $85\%$.
    3.  Operating above $95\%$ utilization causes cloud providers to double their hourly billing rates without warning.
    4.  Near saturation, queueing delays can rise sharply and delay experimental feedback; allocation utilization alone does not establish a safe headroom target for the fleet.

    *Answer*: The correct answer is D. Simple queue models contain a $1/(1-\rho)$ delay term as offered load approaches service capacity. This explains why headroom matters, but allocated-GPU utilization is not that model’s load parameter. Actual targets depend on arrivals, job sizes, gang placement, and delay objectives; no universal 80–85 percent threshold predicts multi-day waits.

    *Learning Objective*: Evaluate why operating a research GPU cluster at near 100% utilization is counterproductive to organizational velocity.

2.  **Explain the fallacy that ‘more sophisticated scheduling algorithms always improve utilization,’ and describe what operators should audit before replacing their cluster scheduler.**

    *Answer*: Low cluster utilization and long wait times are more frequently caused by policy misconfigurations than algorithmic limitations. Before replacing or upgrading a scheduler, operators should audit: (1) Resource request inflation (users requesting 8 GPUs when 1 is needed); (2) Unconfigured gang scheduling timeouts; (3) Mismatched hardware partitions (e.g., jobs requiring A100s when A10s suffice); and (4) Stale fair-share decay parameters that fail to balance contention.

    *Learning Objective*: Explain why policy misconfigurations rather than scheduling algorithm limitations are often the root cause of poor cluster efficiency.

3.  **True or False: Gang scheduling is strictly mandatory for every distributed ML workload, including asynchronous parameter-server training, hyperparameter sweeps, and elastic fine-tuning jobs.**

    *Answer*: False. The required atomic group follows the execution dependencies, not the workload label or maximum request. Independent trials or asynchronous workers may progress separately, while elastic synchronous jobs still require their minimum viable tensor-, pipeline-, or collective groups and supported resize boundaries. Elasticity permits changing between complete feasible configurations; it does not make an incomplete synchronous group productive. Changes to synchronization or worker count still require quality validation.

    *Learning Objective*: Evaluate when gang scheduling constraints can be relaxed in distributed machine learning workloads.
