# V2-08.02 The Scheduling Problem

[[Home|Home]] · [[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/Volume 2 Overview|Volume Overview]] · [[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/02 Distributed ML/08 Fleet Orchestration/V2-08 Fleet Orchestration|V2-08 Fleet Orchestration]]

^sec-fleet-orchestration-introduction

Consider two research teams sharing a 1,000-GPU cluster: Team A submits a 512-GPU job that will run for a month, while Team B submits hundreds of 8-GPU experiments that each run for an hour. If the scheduler continually admits whichever jobs fit, Team B’s small experiments can indefinitely block Team A’s large training run. The scheduling problem is to resolve this conflict among throughput, fairness, and cluster utilization.

Production traces show that this mix is the normal case rather than a contrived one. In the Kalos cluster that Shanghai AI Laboratory used for language-model development in 2023, 86 percent of 19,907 GPU jobs requested a single GPU, yet together they consumed 0.1 percent of the cluster’s GPU time. Jobs of 256 GPUs or more made up 4 percent of submissions and consumed 96 percent of GPU time ([[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/02 Distributed ML/08 Fleet Orchestration/V2-08.17 Source References#^ref-hu2024characterization|Hu et al. 2024]]), two distributions that [[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/02 Distributed ML/08 Fleet Orchestration/V2-08.02 The Scheduling Problem#^fig-acme-job-size|figure 1]] plots side by side. Counted by jobs, the cluster is a stream of small experiments; counted by GPU-hours, it is a few percent of jobs that hold nearly all the accelerators. A scheduler must serve both views at once, which is why fairness, packing, and preemption policies cannot be judged by either count alone.

Mechanisms for synchronizing workers efficiently (**[[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/02 Distributed ML/05 Collective Communication/V2-05 Collective Communication#^sec-collective-communication|Collective Communication]]**), partitioning computation across them (**[[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/02 Distributed ML/06 Distributed Training/V2-06 Distributed Training#^sec-distributed-training-systems|Distributed Training]]**), and recovering from hardware failures (**[[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/02 Distributed ML/07 Fault Tolerance/V2-07 Fault Tolerance#^sec-fault-tolerance-reliability|Fault Tolerance]]**) solve the single-job scaling problem. Each of them assumed that the job already held its accelerators. Fault tolerance assumed more than that. Its recovery paths drew on a spare pool deep enough to replace a drained node and on spot capacity whose loss the job could absorb. Neither exists unless something outside the job grants it, holds it in reserve, or takes it back. That something is orchestration, the policy that decides which jobs run, where they run, when one is preempted for another, and how competing demands share the fleet. An allocation decision must hold across unreliable nodes, balance competing fleet objectives, and satisfy each job’s feasibility constraints. Only then can platform control planes, topology-aware placement, elastic and cost-aware policies, workload-aware ML schedulers, and serving, multi-tenant, and diagnostic practices turn raw capacity into useful work. Training and serving draw on the same fleet, so the capacity envelope this chapter allocates is the one both depend on; how a serving system spends its share, request by request, is the subject of **[[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/03 The Fleet in Production/10 Serving at Scale/V2-10 Serving at Scale#^sec-inference-scale|Serving at Scale]]**.

In the fleet stack (shown in the margin), orchestration occupies the Reliability & Control row of the distribution band, but the infrastructure band beneath it constrains its decisions. The scheduler must account for GPU heterogeneity, NVLink topology, rack power limits, and network bisection bandwidth. When scheduling goes wrong, debugging can require examining all four bands to identify whether the root cause lies in infrastructure (hardware constraints), distribution (algorithm limitations), serving (workload mismatch), or governance (policy misconfiguration). Orchestration connects physical infrastructure to distributed algorithms, translating resource requests into placements that respect both hardware topology and organizational policy.

Translating those requests into placements grows harder as the fleet and its flagship workloads scale. In a 10,000-GPU cluster, a single pretraining run of a GPT-3-class 175B-parameter model ([[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/02 Distributed ML/08 Fleet Orchestration/V2-08.17 Source References#^ref-brown2020language|Brown et al. 2020]]), the running instance of Archetype A (GPT-4/Llama-3) throughout this book, can hold a 1,024-GPU allocation for weeks. Because the scaling laws of **[[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/00 Introduction/01 Introduction/V2-01.04 AI Scaling Laws#^sec-vol2-intro-ai-scaling-laws-a043|AI Scaling Laws]]** reward larger models trained on more tokens, each new generation of such runs asks for a larger gang held for longer, so the scheduler’s hardest requests keep growing. When enough jobs of that class are queued that together they ask for more GPUs than the cluster holds, alongside thousands of smaller experiments and inference workloads competing for the same pool, naive policies break down. A first-come-first-served policy without backfilling (running smaller jobs in the gaps while a large job waits) or preemption can let large jobs occupy the cluster for weeks, delaying smaller experiments that researchers need to iterate quickly. A fair-share policy that divides capacity without respecting each job’s minimum resource bundle can leave many partial allocations and no runnable large job.

↩︎ Archetype A and its 175B-parameter running instance are established in **[[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/00 Introduction/01 Introduction/V2-01.07 The Fleet Stack and the Lighthouse Archetypes#^sec-introduction-archetype-gpt4llama3-b02f|Archetype A (GPT-4/Llama-3)]]**.

The economic stakes make these decisions consequential at every scale. A continuously provisioned 10,000-GPU cluster, valued at the chapter’s cloud-equivalent rate of \$2/GPU-hour, represents \$480,000 per day in capacity, whether the GPUs are computing useful work or sitting idle while jobs wait in a queue. If scheduling inefficiencies leave 30 percent of GPUs idle, that translates to \$144,000 per day in unused capacity value, or about \$52.6M annually. This share counts only the idle capacity that better scheduling can recover, the gap between a poorly scheduled fleet at 50 percent utilization and a well-scheduled one at 80 percent. The poorly scheduled fleet idles more of its GPUs than this, and [[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/02 Distributed ML/08 Fleet Orchestration/V2-08.09 Cost Optimization#^sec-fleet-orchestration-cost-optimization|section 8.8]] prices that full idle share.

Holding useful work per active GPU-hour constant, improving utilization from 50 percent to 80 percent adds 3,000 fully active GPU-equivalents of productive capacity without purchasing additional hardware. That count measures the gain in useful work. Procurement planning asks a different question, namely how much hardware the gain replaces. For that comparison the answer is the gain divided by the 50 percent baseline utilization, or 6,000 raw GPUs, because new GPUs run under the same scheduling policy would also reach only 50 percent utilization. Recovered capacity becomes cash savings only when it avoids purchases or billed resource time. Scheduling can therefore increase the useful work delivered by an existing investment, even when the fleet’s immediate expenditure remains unchanged.

Workload semantics, rather than an ML-versus-high-performance computing (HPC) boundary, determine scheduling flexibility. Rigid synchronous jobs in both domains require a fixed set of co-scheduled ranks, whereas malleable jobs can make progress across a declared range of resource counts. Gang scheduling[[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/02 Distributed ML/08 Fleet Orchestration/V2-08.18 Footnotes#^fn1|¹]] is the central allocation constraint for a rigid training group: a job configured to require 1,024 GPUs cannot complete its distributed iterations with only half of them. Malleable data-parallel training can instead vary its replica count at explicit reconfiguration boundaries, but tensor- and pipeline-parallel groups usually retain a rigid base allocation. The scheduler must therefore model each job’s minimum viable gang, the smallest set of co-scheduled devices on which it can make progress, and its permitted resize points rather than infer rigidity from the workload’s field.

If gang scheduling determines which resources must be available together, topology awareness via placement groups[[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/02 Distributed ML/08 Fleet Orchestration/V2-08.18 Footnotes#^fn2|²]] determines how efficiently they can communicate. Tensor-parallel groups benefit from placement within a high-bandwidth NVLink domain; a placement across slower links must still meet the job’s communication budget. Two allocations of the same size can therefore deliver very different useful throughput.

GPU heterogeneity is the third dimension. Many clusters contain mixtures of A100 and H100 accelerators with different compute throughput and interconnect bandwidth. Their training-throughput ratio depends on the workload, numerical precision, and communication path, so the scheduler needs measured performance and the applicable allocation cost. Memory capacity adds a hard admission test once pools differ in it. The common A100 and H100 variants both carry 80 GB of high-bandwidth memory (HBM), but smaller-memory variants of the same accelerator and older generations often share the fleet. A job whose working set fits only the larger devices cannot run on the rest at any priority. The debugging example in [[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/02 Distributed ML/08 Fleet Orchestration/V2-08.13 Debugging Cluster Utilization#^sec-fleet-orchestration-debugging|section 8.12]] returns to exactly such a mixed fleet. The scheduler must match workloads to appropriate hardware based on actual requirements, not user preferences, while maintaining high utilization across heterogeneous pools. When users request specific GPU types out of habit rather than necessity, the mismatch between requested and required resources can leave entire hardware pools idle while queues overflow on preferred hardware.

Job durations are also uncertain: a training run may converge early and complete in days rather than weeks, hardware failures may abort jobs and return resources at unpredictable times, and hyperparameter searches may terminate unpromising configurations early. In both ML and scientific computing, fixed-step jobs with stable iteration times permit better release-time estimates than jobs whose stopping conditions or execution rates vary. The scheduler must therefore distinguish an estimated completion time from an enforced runtime limit when deciding whether another job can safely use a temporary gap in capacity.

The interaction between these challenges creates combinatorial complexity. A scheduler must simultaneously handle gang scheduling constraints (atomic allocation), heterogeneous resources (capability matching), topology requirements (NVLink locality for tensor parallelism), priority and fairness policies (organizational equity), preemption decisions (which running jobs to interrupt), and cost optimization (spot vs. on-demand placement). Each dimension constrains the others. A topology-optimal placement may violate fairness policies, a cost-optimal spot placement may violate reliability requirements, and a fairness-driven allocation may fragment resources in ways that prevent gang scheduling. The scheduler therefore does more than pack jobs onto GPUs. It must maintain a consistent, economically useful view of a failing, heterogeneous fleet. At that scale, placement stops being only an optimization problem and becomes a distributed systems problem of keeping state coherent across thousands of machines.

1.  In the Kalos language-model cluster trace, most jobs requested a single GPU yet consumed a tiny share of GPU time, while jobs of 256 or more GPUs were a few percent of submissions and consumed nearly all GPU time. What does this distribution imply for judging a scheduling policy?

    1.  Judge the policy by job count alone, because most users submit small jobs.
    2.  Judge the policy by GPU-hours alone, because large jobs hold nearly all of the capacity.
    3.  Judge the policy under both views, because small jobs set most users’ waits while a few large gangs hold most of the capacity.
    4.  Treat the distribution as an artifact of one cluster, because production fleets have uniform job sizes.
