# V2-08.03 Distributed Scheduling Complexity

[[Home|Home]] · [[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/Volume 2 Overview|Volume Overview]] · [[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/02 Distributed ML/08 Fleet Orchestration/V2-08 Fleet Orchestration|V2-08 Fleet Orchestration]]

^sec-fleet-orchestration-distributed-scheduling

A single-machine scheduler works inside one operating-system boundary, where three properties hold closely enough to design around: it observes resource state directly and consistently, its allocation decisions take effect atomically, and failures are nearly binary (the machine is up or it is down). Device faults and delayed cleanup still occur, but they do not hide behind a network. A cluster scheduler surrenders all three properties. It coordinates independent nodes through delayed messages and uncertain observations, so an authoritative allocation decision does not establish that every node has received or completed it. Several distributed systems problems emerge from that gap.

Partial failures pose the first challenge. A node can fail between allocation and job start, creating a gap between the scheduler’s decision and its effect. The scheduler may successfully allocate 32 GPUs across 4 nodes, only to have one node fail before the job launches. The remaining 24 GPUs sit idle while the scheduler detects the failure, updates its state, and re-plans the allocation. Meanwhile, other jobs that could have used those 24 GPUs have already been placed elsewhere, potentially on suboptimal hardware. Before the first training step, recovery must repair allocation and launch state even though no new model progress exists to restore. Once execution begins, resource recovery must also coordinate with the framework’s checkpoint and membership mechanisms.

Network partitions create a second problem. The scheduler may lose connectivity to a subset of nodes while those nodes continue operating normally. From the scheduler’s perspective, their status is uncertain; from the nodes’ perspective, jobs may still be running and producing useful work. Reallocating their GPUs risks double allocation if the partition heals while the old work still runs; waiting for reconnection leaves possibly healthy capacity idle. The partition’s duration cannot be known in advance, so any fixed timeout is a guess about network behavior that may prove wrong. A timeout can trigger recovery, but it does not prove that the old execution has stopped. Reusing a GPU therefore requires confirmed cleanup or an enforced revocation of the old allocation. Restarting a job on reachable replacement nodes also requires fencing stale attempts from shared outputs. One common form of fencing advances the allocation’s generation number, so that node agents and storage reject any work carrying an older one. The recovery policy can choose how long to wait for reconnection, but each choice must preserve exclusive ownership where the workload requires it.

↩︎ Epoch fencing and leases during job recovery are established in **[[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/02 Distributed ML/07 Fault Tolerance/V2-07.10 Failure Detection and Recovery#^sec-fault-tolerance-reliability-reliability-recovery-procedures-002d|Recovery procedures]]**.

State inconsistency emerges as a third challenge that compounds the first two. Resource state may differ between the scheduler’s view and reality on individual nodes. A GPU the scheduler believes is free may still be cleaning up from a previous job: flushing caches, deallocating device memory, running a zombie process from a failed container, or completing a CUDA driver reset after an error. Conversely, a GPU marked as “in use” may have been freed by a job that terminated between heartbeat intervals. The scheduler therefore operates on a model of cluster state that is always slightly stale. The degree of staleness varies across nodes with heartbeat frequency, network latency, and the complexity of cleanup operations.

Ordering without global time presents the fourth issue. Allocation messages issued before and after a partition can arrive out of order. Logical timestamps, counters that order events without a shared clock, can describe an order, but do not by themselves prevent conflicting ownership. The control plane must serialize conflicting allocation decisions, and the resource manager must reject stale allocation generations before acting on them. Otherwise, an old launch request can revive work after a newer allocation has taken ownership.

These four challenges echo the CAP theorem of **[[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/00 Introduction/01 Introduction/V2-01.05 Constraints of Scale#^sec-introduction-cap-theorem-reality-f3b0|The CAP theorem reality]]**, under which a partitioned system cannot guarantee both consistency and availability. CAP is a theorem about distributed data systems, not a literal proof about every scheduler, but the analogy draws attention to which allocation decisions remain safe when nodes cannot communicate. Slurm, an HPC workload manager, and Kubernetes, a container orchestration platform, both need authoritative allocation records and mechanisms that reconcile those records with execution. Optimistic concurrency permits schedulers to plan against potentially stale snapshots, then validate proposed changes before committing them. Rejecting a conflicting proposal costs scheduling work; accepting conflicting ownership would violate a different, essential invariant.

The control-plane tension becomes concrete when the scheduler interacts with the ML framework’s own distributed coordination plane during a partition. A scheduler may decide that a subset of nodes is unavailable while workers on those nodes are still blocked inside a collective operation. That creates a brief window of conflicting intent: the scheduler wants to recover the allocation, but the training job has not yet converged on a new membership view. Elastic launch mechanisms such as TorchElastic reduce this ambiguity through rendezvous, the step at which workers agree on group membership and ranks. They use heartbeats and re-rendezvous so the framework can either reform a viable worker group or terminate cleanly, which gives the scheduler a clearer recovery signal than an opaque collective timeout ([[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/02 Distributed ML/08 Fleet Orchestration/V2-08.17 Source References#^ref-pytorchElasticDocs|PyTorch Contributors 2026b]], [[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/02 Distributed ML/08 Fleet Orchestration/V2-08.17 Source References#^ref-pytorchRendezvousDocs|2026a]]). This layered coordination, with scheduler-level policy for coarse allocation and framework-level membership for workers, is the characteristic architecture of production distributed training clusters.

### Admission, Placement, and Reconciliation

^sec-fleet-orchestration-control-plane

When a single undifferentiated queue mixes quota checks, hardware binding, and crash recovery, operators cannot tell whether a pending job is blocked by team quota, fragmented topology, or a dead node whose lease, a time-limited ownership grant that lapses unless renewed, has not yet expired. A stale retry can also assign the same accelerators to two jobs at once. A scheduler control plane avoids this ambiguity by separating three decisions. **Admission control** decides whether a job may enter the runnable set under quota, priority, gang, and safety constraints. **Placement** binds an admitted job to a feasible set of nodes while accounting for accelerator capability, topology, power, storage, and network capacity. **Reconciliation** compares desired allocations with observed node and job state, then starts, retries, evicts, or fences work until the two agree. Keeping these stages explicit makes it possible to distinguish admission denial from placement failure, while versioned decisions and resource-level enforcement prevent stale observations from authorizing competing owners.

Control-plane architectures distribute these decisions differently ([[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/02 Distributed ML/08 Fleet Orchestration/V2-08.17 Source References#^ref-hindman2011|Hindman et al. 2011]]; [[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/02 Distributed ML/08 Fleet Orchestration/V2-08.17 Source References#^ref-burns2016|Burns et al. 2016]]). A centralized scheduler serializes admission and placement against one authoritative state, which simplifies conflict handling but concentrates scheduling latency and failure impact. A two-level design offers resources to framework schedulers, gaining policy specialization at the cost of delayed or rejected offers. A shared-state design lets several schedulers plan concurrently against a common state and resolves conflicts through version checks or optimistic concurrency. In every design, reconciliation closes the loop. Node agents must enforce allocation generations and lease validity so that an old decision cannot revive after a newer placement has taken ownership.

Feasibility also extends beyond free accelerator count. Power-aware admission rejects or delays a placement whose worst-case draw would exceed a rack or facility envelope; relying on average draw risks correlated throttling when colocated jobs enter compute-heavy phases together. I/O-aware placement treats dataset-read and checkpoint-write bandwidth as reservable capacity, so a gang that fits in GPU memory does not start where concurrent input pipelines or checkpoint bursts will saturate the same storage path. These constraints can deliberately leave accelerators idle. The idle capacity is the cost of preserving the power and data-movement budgets required for the admitted jobs to make progress.

### Failure as a Scheduling Input

^sec-fleet-orchestration-failure-rates

A scheduler that allocates 10,000 GPUs as if every granted node will stay healthy until the job finishes will see a multi-thousand-GPU training gang stall every few hours waiting for replacement capacity that was already handed out to other workloads. As **[[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/02 Distributed ML/07 Fault Tolerance/V2-07.03 The Mathematics of Inevitable Failure#^sec-fault-tolerance-reliability-reliability-mathematics-inevitable-failure-93ef|The Mathematics of Inevitable Failure]]** established, the fleet failure rate is the per-device rate multiplied by the population (**[[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/02 Distributed ML/07 Fault Tolerance/V2-07.03 The Mathematics of Inevitable Failure#^eq-system-mtbf|equation 7.4]]**), so a cluster of ten thousand GPUs loses a device every few hours (**[[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/02 Distributed ML/07 Fault Tolerance/V2-07.03 The Mathematics of Inevitable Failure#^tbl-cluster-mtbf-scaling|Cluster MTBF Scaling]]**). Where fault tolerance turns that failure rate into checkpoints, detection, and elastic recovery for a running job, the scheduler must plan for failure in every allocation decision. The scheduler uses two failure rates, one for each decision. It sizes spare capacity and replacement paths from the GPU-only count of devices it must replace. Its estimate of how often a running job is interrupted, by contrast, uses the shorter full-system interval of **[[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/02 Distributed ML/07 Fault Tolerance/V2-07.03 The Mathematics of Inevitable Failure#^sec-fault-tolerance-reliability-reliability-worked-example-cluster-mtbf-calculation-9255|Cluster MTBF calculation]]**, which adds PCIe and network failure domains, because those faults also stop a synchronous job even when no GPU needs replacing.

The scheduler cannot treat the cluster as a static resource pool where resources, once allocated, remain available until voluntarily released. It must anticipate loss of allocated resources during execution and provide replacement capacity or rescheduling paths. The response depends on the fault and the validity of the surviving state. A transient disruption may permit recovery in place if the execution state remains usable; a persistent hardware failure requires replacement resources. Even a short disruption can force checkpoint recovery if the framework cannot safely continue ([[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/02 Distributed ML/08 Fleet Orchestration/V2-08.17 Source References#^ref-tiwari2015|Tiwari et al. 2015]]).

When a node heartbeat is missed, the scheduler faces three recovery policies with different delay and capacity costs. It can wait to see whether the heartbeat resumes, though this delays recovery if the machine has permanently crashed. It can instead begin recovery promptly on replacement resources, provided stale execution is fenced before conflicting work resumes. An intermediate approach marks the node as suspect and prepositions replacement capacity while checking its status; this reduces launch delay if replacement becomes necessary, but holds capacity that could otherwise serve pending work. Whichever policy it picks, reuse of the node’s GPUs still waits on the fencing that network partitions require. Historical failure and recovery measurements set how long to wait and how much replacement capacity to hold.

The recovery-time decomposition of fault tolerance (**[[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/02 Distributed ML/07 Fault Tolerance/V2-07.10 Failure Detection and Recovery#^eq-recovery-time|equation 7.10]]**) shows where scheduler policy intervenes most effectively. Of its detection, restart, load, and warmup terms, the scheduler owns most of ${T_{\text{restart}}}$, the time to allocate replacement resources and launch processes, and that is the term its spare-capacity policy shortens. Waiting longer on a missed heartbeat lengthens ${T_{\text{detect}}}$ in exchange for avoiding a needless restart, so the wait is worth extending only while a transient fault remains more likely than a permanent one.

↪ The same decomposition in scheduler-facing phases (detect, reschedule, reload, and replay of work since the last checkpoint) is developed in **[[05 Reference/Volume II - Scaling/V2 Reliability Foundations#^sec-appdx-reliability-foundations-recovery-anatomy|The anatomy of recovery time]]**.

These requirements connect the scheduler directly to checkpoint and recovery infrastructure. Scheduling decisions about spare capacity, preemption policies, and elastic training support determine how quickly the system recovers from statistically expected failures. A scheduler that reserves spare capacity or supports elastic recovery can restore throughput much faster than one that requires every failed job to wait for a full fresh allocation and reload. The reserve fraction follows from the measured failure rate and recovery time of the fleet’s own workloads. Once the control plane can maintain coherent ownership across admission, placement, reconciliation, and node failures, it still needs an explicit policy objective to decide which competing jobs receive capacity when demand exceeds what the fleet can run at once.

**Self-Check: Question**

1.  A scheduler control plane separates admission control, placement, and reconciliation. State what each decides, and explain why keeping them separate helps an operator diagnose a job that has not started.

2.  A network partition cuts the scheduler off from four nodes running a training job, and their heartbeats time out. What must hold before the scheduler reassigns those nodes’ GPUs to another job?

    1.  Nothing beyond the timeout, because a missed heartbeat proves the old processes have stopped.
    2.  The old allocation must be revoked with confirmed cleanup or fenced, for example by rejecting stale allocation generations, so the old work cannot keep using or writing through those GPUs.
    3.  The partition must heal first, because no recovery may begin while nodes are unreachable.
    4.  The new job must run at higher priority, because priority resolves conflicting ownership.
