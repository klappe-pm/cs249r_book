# V2-08.05 Core Allocation Constraints

[[Home|Home]] · [[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/Volume 2 Overview|Volume Overview]] · [[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/02 Distributed ML/08 Fleet Orchestration/V2-08 Fleet Orchestration|V2-08 Fleet Orchestration]]

^sec-fleet-orchestration-core-allocation

Awarding the 64-GPU fine-tuning run its fair share of the cluster delivers zero progress if those GPUs sit on nodes with exhausted host memory, lack the interconnect its parallel layout requires, or arrive only 32 at a time. A scheduling objective can choose only among allocations that a job can actually run. Fairness must be computed across every resource a tenant consumes, a request must fit every dimension of the node that receives it, a rigid group must arrive whole, and the resources each job holds must not block one another. Each of these constraints narrows the feasible set before any objective selects within it.

### Dominant-Resource Fairness Allocation

^sec-fleet-orchestration-drf-worked-allocation

Equalizing GPU counts alone fails when one tenant’s jobs saturate host CPU and memory while another’s saturate the network fabric, leaving free GPUs unusable behind exhausted auxiliary resources. Dominant-resource fairness (DRF) makes a multi-resource entitlement comparable by normalizing each tenant’s allocation against fleet capacity and taking its largest share ([[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/02 Distributed ML/08 Fleet Orchestration/V2-08.17 Source References#^ref-ghodsi2011|Ghodsi et al. 2011]]). Consider the deliberately small cluster in [[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/02 Distributed ML/08 Fleet Orchestration/V2-08.05 Core Allocation Constraints#^tbl-fleet-orchestration-drf-allocation|table 1]]: 8 GPUs, 32 CPU cores, 256 GB of memory, and 80 Gb/s of network capacity. Tenant A’s replica requires 1 GPU, 4 cores, 32 GB, and 10 Gb/s; Tenant B’s distributed worker requires 2 GPUs, 2 cores, 16 GB, and 20 Gb/s.

| **Tenant** | **Allocation** | **GPU share** | **CPU share** | **Memory share** | **Network share** | **Dominant share** |
|:-----------|:---------------|:-------------:|:-------------:|:----------------:|:-----------------:|:------------------:|
| A          | 4 replicas     |  $4/8=0.50$   | $16/32=0.50$  |  $128/256=0.50$  |   $40/80=0.50$    |       $0.50$       |
| B          | 2 workers      |  $4/8=0.50$   | $4/32=0.125$  |  $32/256=0.125$  |   $40/80=0.50$    |       $0.50$       |

Table 1: **Worked DRF Allocation**: Progressive filling gives Tenant A four one-GPU replicas and Tenant B a pair of two-GPU workers. Both tenants reach a dominant share of 0.50 even though their CPU and memory allocations differ. GPUs and network are jointly saturated; CPU and memory remain available but cannot increase either allocation without exceeding a saturated resource.

^tbl-fleet-orchestration-drf-allocation

For tenant ${i}$, define its dominant share as ${d}_i=\max_{r}({a_{i,r}}/{C_{r}})$, where ${a_{i,r}}$ is its allocation of resource ${r}$ and ${C_{r}}$ is fleet capacity for that resource. The calculation equalizes ${d}_i$, not every resource share. One A replica raises A’s dominant share by $1/8$; one B worker raises B’s by $2/8$. Progressive filling, which repeatedly gives one more bundle to the tenant with the smaller dominant share until a resource saturates, therefore grants roughly two A replicas for each B worker until both dominant shares reach $0.50$. This is a fair policy target, but it is not yet a feasible placement. If B’s two workers form one four-GPU gang, the four GPUs must become available atomically. If tensor parallelism requires one NVLink domain, four free GPUs scattered across nodes do not satisfy the allocation. If the job requires 80 GB HBM accelerators, four incompatible GPUs do not count at all. A production scheduler therefore applies capability, gang, and topology constraints to the feasible set, then uses DRF to choose fairly among allocations that can actually run. The order is not a convenience. Feasibility comes before fairness (principle [[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/02 Distributed ML/V2 Distributed ML Principles#^pri-feasibility-before-fairness|15]]) because a fair share of accelerators on which the job cannot run delivers no progress to the tenant who holds it. When no equal-share placement exists, the scheduler should report the constraint causing the deviation rather than silently presenting an infeasible entitlement as service delivered.

**Lighthouse 8.1: Lighthouse archetypes: Placement changes fairness**

The same resource share has different meaning across the three lighthouse workloads introduced in **[[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/00 Introduction/01 Introduction/V2-01.07 The Fleet Stack and the Lighthouse Archetypes#^sec-vol2-introduction-archetypes|Three systems archetypes]]**:

- **Archetype A (GPT-4/Llama-3)** needs a rigid, topology-compatible gang. Fractional allocations below its minimum parallel configuration deliver no progress, so fairness must include maximum feasible wait for a contiguous placement rather than GPU-hours alone.
- **Archetype B (DLRM at Scale)** couples accelerator memory to sparse-routing bandwidth. A nominal GPU allocation is infeasible when embedding shards or network capacity cannot follow it, so network and memory must enter the dominant share and placement must protect the serving-latency envelope.
- **Archetype C (Federated MobileNet)** schedules participation rather than data-center GPUs. Eligibility filters for power, connectivity, and device capability can systematically exclude parts of the population, so fairness must be audited through participation and outcome rates across device and user groups, not equal device counts in a round.

The common rule is to define the feasible bundle first and assess fairness over useful progress second. Treating every accelerator or client as interchangeable would conceal the constraint that makes each archetype difficult.

### Bin Packing

^sec-fleet-orchestration-bin-packing

Even when a cluster has dozens of free GPUs in total, a pending 8-GPU job will sit blocked if previous placements scattered those free GPUs in 1- and 2-GPU remnants across partially occupied nodes. Fitting jobs of varying sizes into fixed-capacity nodes without stranding capacity is an instance of **bin packing**.[[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/02 Distributed ML/08 Fleet Orchestration/V2-08.18 Footnotes#^fn3|³]] Because general bin packing is NP-hard, no known algorithm finds optimal solutions in polynomial time. Practical heuristics such as first-fit decreasing and best-fit decreasing offer tractable choices, but their performance depends on job sizes and placement constraints. How much usable capacity a heuristic recovers in a heterogeneous ML fleet has to be measured against that fleet’s job mix, because the classical guarantees cover only one-dimensional packing.

Consider a 64-node cluster with 8 GPUs per node, totaling 512 GPUs. If every job requires 6 GPUs within one node, only one such job fits per node, leaving 2 GPUs unused and reducing effective capacity for this workload to 75 percent. The remaining 2 GPUs per node cannot be combined across nodes into another feasible six-GPU allocation, although smaller compatible jobs could use them. With a mix of 1-GPU, 3-GPU, and 7-GPU jobs, fragmentation depends on the placement and the pending requests; total free capacity can exceed a waiting job’s demand while no individual node has enough space. [[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/02 Distributed ML/08 Fleet Orchestration/V2-08.05 Core Allocation Constraints#^fig-fragmentation-problem|Figure 3]] maps this stranded capacity across an $8{\times}8$ allocation grid. Although 19 slots (30 percent of cluster capacity, dashed white cells) sit idle across nodes 0 through 7, active allocations (J1 through J15) leave every node partially occupied, which blocks a pending 8-GPU job that requires an entire unfragmented node.

ML placement is a multi-dimensional packing problem. Jobs require GPUs together with CPU cores for data preprocessing, host memory for data loading and augmentation pipelines, local SSD storage for dataset caching, and network bandwidth for gradient synchronization. A job requesting 4 GPUs, 32 CPU cores, and 256 GB of RAM may not fit on a node that has 4 free GPUs but only 16 free CPU cores because other jobs have consumed the host CPU for preprocessing. The scheduler must simultaneously satisfy all resource dimensions, and the job fits only if every dimension has sufficient capacity on the selected node. A bottleneck in any one dimension can therefore strand capacity in the others.

Locality constraints further restrict placement beyond simple resource availability. A training job may require tensor-parallel groups to remain within NVLink-connected nodes when its communication budget cannot tolerate the lower per-endpoint bandwidth of the external fabric (450 GB/s per direction intra-node vs. 50 GB/s per port inter-node). On this topology, where NVLink does not connect the nodes, a job requesting “4 GPUs with NVLink connectivity” cannot use 2 GPUs from node A and 2 from node B, even if all 4 GPUs are individually available and the total capacity is sufficient. These topology constraints turn resource counts into a placement problem where which specific resources are available matters as much as how many. A capacity-feasible packing must also pass the job’s locality constraints.

Schedulers address fragmentation through several complementary strategies, each targeting a different aspect of the problem. The first, backfill, lets smaller jobs run in the gaps while a large job waits for contiguous resources; [[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/02 Distributed ML/08 Fleet Orchestration/V2-08.05 Core Allocation Constraints#^sec-fleet-orchestration-gang-scheduling|section 8.4.3]] develops it together with the reservation it must protect and the runtime estimates it depends on. A second strategy, periodic **defragmentation**, migrates or preempts low-priority jobs to consolidate free resources into contiguous blocks, analogous to memory compaction in operating systems. The scheduler identifies nodes where partial allocations leave stranded resources, preempts the jobs occupying those resources, and re-schedules them on nodes where they can be packed more efficiently. The cost includes state transfer and restart, plus replay of work not captured by a valid checkpoint. A graceful checkpoint can preserve recent progress, but still takes time and storage bandwidth. The scheduler must weigh these costs against the benefit of starting large jobs sooner, and low-demand periods reduce the number of competing jobs affected by the move.

A third strategy, **over-subscription**, admits more demand than a capacity dimension can sustain simultaneously, but only where the resource is divisible and the scheduler can bound interference. Average activity of a GPU’s streaming multiprocessors (SMs), its compute units, does not create fractional whole GPUs. A job that reserves device memory, requires an exclusive CUDA context, or belongs to a rigid gang still occupies its assigned device while kernels are idle. Statistical multiplexing can be safe for explicitly shareable CPU, network, storage, or partitioned GPU capacity when tenants have measured, weakly correlated peaks and enforceable limits. It is unsafe to convert 70 percent average GPU utilization into a 1.4$\times$ device overcommit ratio. Whole-device capacity should instead be recovered through preemption, elastic resizing, or an isolation mechanism such as Multi-Instance GPU (MIG) hardware partitioning or NVIDIA’s Multi-Process Service (MPS), which lets several processes’ kernels share one device. Either mechanism helps only if the scheduler models its memory, compute, and fault-sharing contract explicitly. Both reappear with the serving workloads they suit best in [[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/02 Distributed ML/08 Fleet Orchestration/V2-08.11 Serving Capacity in the Shared Fleet#^sec-fleet-orchestration-resource-isolation|section 8.10.1]].

### Gang Scheduling

^sec-fleet-orchestration-gang-scheduling

Partial allocation of rigid multi-GPU jobs creates a distributed scheduling failure mode in which two jobs each hold half the resources they need and block each other indefinitely while every allocated GPU sits idle. In the left panel of [[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/02 Distributed ML/08 Fleet Orchestration/V2-08.05 Core Allocation Constraints#^fig-gang-scheduling-deadlock|figure 4]], jobs J1 and J2 each hold 4 of the 8 GPUs they require, and the whole pool stalls. Atomic gang scheduling (right panel) instead grants J1 all 8 GPUs as one allocation while J2 waits without holding any, which prevents this allocation deadlock.

![[90 Assets/Volume II/08 Fleet Orchestration/Gang Deadlock 4c1498ff42.svg]]

Figure 4: **The Distributed Deadlock (Hold and Wait)**: In a non-gang scheduler (left), job J1 acquires half the cluster and waits for the rest, while job J2 acquires the other half and waits for J1 to release its GPUs. Neither can proceed, and 100 percent of the cluster sits idle. Gang scheduling (right) solves this by mandating atomic allocation: a job is either granted its entire ${N}$-GPU requirement simultaneously or it waits in the queue without holding any resources.

^fig-gang-scheduling-deadlock

**Definition 8.1: Gang scheduling**

***Gang scheduling*** is the ML cluster scheduling policy that atomically allocates the resources required by a job’s minimum viable execution group, so a rigid group either receives its complete allocation or waits without retaining a partial one.

1.  **Significance**: A rigid synchronous job configured for 1,024 GPUs but holding only 512 GPUs completes zero training steps, not half as many: each collective stalls until every rank in its communication group arrives, so the held GPUs consume power and capital at zero useful output. Without atomic allocation, two such jobs can each acquire half the cluster and deadlock, both at zero progress while consuming the full cluster’s operating cost.
2.  **Distinction**: Unlike a malleable service or parallel job that can make progress across a resource range, rigid synchronous training has step-function utility: the minimum viable gang or nothing. The distinction is a property of the execution graph, not of whether the workload is labeled ML, cloud, or HPC.
3.  **Common pitfall**: A frequent misconception is that priorities or quotas alone solve this. Atomicity must extend to every co-scheduled resource the job needs to make progress (accelerators, high-bandwidth interconnect domains, network capacity for collectives); enforcing it at GPU granularity alone recreates the deadlock one resource layer down.

↩︎ Ring and tree AllReduce schedules, each of which completes only after every rank’s contribution has been reduced, are established in **[[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/02 Distributed ML/05 Collective Communication/V2-05.07 AllReduce Algorithms and Topologies#^sec-communication-collective-operations-collective-operations-allreduce-algorithms-7545|AllReduce Algorithms and Topologies]]**.

The partial-allocation pattern is a hold-and-wait deadlock,[[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/02 Distributed ML/08 Fleet Orchestration/V2-08.18 Footnotes#^fn4|⁴]] and its price is the whole allocation for as long as the cycle persists. At the scale of the chapter’s reference workload, two 1,024-GPU gangs that have each acquired 900 GPUs from a pool too small to complete either one burn about \$1,800 per hour apiece. The limiting case is two jobs that each request the entire cluster, the example OpenAI used to motivate group admission (war story [8.1](https://mlsysbook.ai/vol2/08_fleet_orchestration/08_fleet_orchestration.html#ws-fleet-orchestration-openai-gang-scheduling)). On the 10,000-GPU cluster, a deadlock that strands every device wastes \$20,000 per hour at the chapter’s rate while producing no progress for either job. Measured against the stake computed at the start of the chapter, raising utilization from 50 percent to 80 percent recovers about \$144,000 per day in equivalent capacity value. About 7 hours of such a deadlock therefore erase a full day of that gain. Gang scheduling does not create the gain, but it protects it. For a rigid job ${J}$ requiring ${N}$ GPUs, the scheduler must grant all ${N}$ GPUs atomically or leave the job queued without a partial allocation. This removes hold-and-wait for the resources covered by that allocation; it does not prevent later deadlocks in application code or auxiliary resources.

Naive gang scheduling is wasteful in predictable ways. Atomic allocation means the waiting job holds nothing, yet a large gang will never assemble if every freed GPU goes to a smaller job. The scheduler therefore places a **reservation** for the job at the head of the queue, draining capacity toward it by declining to start new work on GPUs that the gang will need. If the cluster has 900 free GPUs and that job requests 1,024, the reservation keeps the 900 GPUs idle until 124 more become available, potentially wasting thousands of GPU-hours. Backfill scheduling addresses this by identifying jobs in the queue that can fit within the 900 available GPUs without delaying the large job’s expected start time ([[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/02 Distributed ML/08 Fleet Orchestration/V2-08.05 Core Allocation Constraints#^fig-fleet-orchestration-backfill-reservation|figure 5]]), an idea popularized in the ANL/IBM SP scheduling system ([[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/02 Distributed ML/08 Fleet Orchestration/V2-08.17 Source References#^ref-lifka1995anlibm|Lifka 1995]]). A 128-GPU job with an estimated runtime of 2 hours is a backfill candidate if the 124 additional GPUs needed by the large job will remain unavailable for at least 2 hours. Protecting the reservation requires an enforced runtime bound or a preemption contract that releases the backfill allocation in time, including any checkpoint and cleanup delay. The estimate identifies an opportunity; the release contract makes the reservation enforceable.

![[90 Assets/Volume II/08 Fleet Orchestration/Backfill Reservation 90a2cc6266.svg]]

Figure 5: **Backfill under a Reservation**: The head-of-queue gang holds a reservation that begins when the running jobs release their GPUs. Until then, the free GPUs form a backfill window. A short job that ends before the reservation start runs in that window, while a job whose runtime bound would carry it past the start is held, because admitting it would delay the gang. The schematic carries no measured values.

^fig-fleet-orchestration-backfill-reservation

The accuracy of runtime estimates determines backfill effectiveness, and this creates a game-theoretic challenge. If users overestimate runtimes, fewer jobs appear to fit a backfill window. If they underestimate, the scheduler may need to preempt a backfilled job to honor the reservation, paying recovery costs and losing unsaved progress. A policy that kills jobs at their declared limit encourages conservative estimates. Tiresias ([[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/02 Distributed ML/08 Fleet Orchestration/V2-08.17 Source References#^ref-gu2019tiresias|Gu et al. 2019]]), discussed in [[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/02 Distributed ML/08 Fleet Orchestration/V2-08.10 Custom ML Schedulers#^sec-fleet-orchestration-tiresias|section 8.9.1]], avoids duration estimates when setting priority by using observed resource consumption. That choice addresses ordering under uncertainty, while reservation protection still needs an enforced runtime bound or release contract that says when a backfilled job will give its capacity back.

### Deadlock Prevention and Detection

^sec-fleet-orchestration-deadlock

Gang scheduling eliminates the hold-and-wait condition for the atomically allocated bundle, but it does not immunize the cluster against all forms of resource contention. Later requests for auxiliary resources can still create a cycle of dependencies. Priority rules can also prevent a resource holder from making progress without creating a cycle. Distinguishing these cases matters because breaking a deadlock and providing a starved job with enough resources to exit require different interventions.

Priority inversion occurs when a high-priority job waits on a low-priority resource holder whose progress other work delays. Consider the 64-GPU fine-tuning run of the 175B-parameter model, submitted at high priority. The scheduler has reserved 60 available GPUs, but the remaining 4 are held by a low-priority data processing job. Normally, the scheduler would preempt the low-priority job to satisfy the high-priority request. However, if a stream of medium-priority development jobs saturates the cluster’s CPU or network bandwidth, starving the low-priority job of the resources it needs to checkpoint and exit, the low-priority job stalls. Its graceful release path cannot complete. The high-priority job waits for the low-priority job, which is effectively blocked by the medium-priority jobs. The 60-GPU idle block persists until the exit path receives resources or an enforced termination policy reclaims the remaining GPUs, potentially losing uncheckpointed work.

![[90 Assets/Volume II/08 Fleet Orchestration/Fleet Orchestration Dependency Cascade a02b7897d7.svg]]

*Wait-for chain of three job nodes. An arrow labeled waits on runs from the high-priority job to the low-priority job holding GPUs, and an arrow labeled starved by runs from the low-priority job to the medium-priority jobs that consume its checkpoint exit path.*

*Priority inversion: the high-priority job waits on a low-priority holder that medium-priority work starves.*

**Definition 8.2: Priority inversion**

***Priority inversion*** is an ML cluster scheduling pathology in which a high-priority task is forced to wait for a lower-priority task to release a shared resource.

1.  **Significance**: It ties the progress of a high-priority job, and the accelerators reserved for it, to the progress of the lowest-priority job in the wait-for chain. In ML clusters, this typically occurs when a low-priority job holding GPUs is starved of auxiliary resources (for example, bandwidth for checkpointing), preventing it from finishing and releasing the accelerators needed by high-priority workloads.
2.  **Distinction**: Unlike standard queuing (where tasks wait their turn), priority inversion involves an active blockage: the high-priority task is ready to run but is transitively dependent on a task that the scheduler does not prioritize.
3.  **Common pitfall**: A frequent misconception is that strict priority levels solve this. Raising the waiting job’s priority does not supply the CPU or bandwidth that its lower-priority resource holder needs to exit; the preemption policy must protect that release path.

Prevention and detection address different parts of this problem. Atomic allocation prevents hold-and-wait for the resources included in the gang, while reserving checkpoint bandwidth allows a preempted job to complete its exit path. Timeouts instead bound how long the system waits before investigating or reclaiming an allocation; they do not prevent deadlock states from arising. Monitoring can trigger recovery when progress stops, but safe reclamation still requires enforcing the new ownership decision.

Detection, unlike exact placement search, is computationally cheap. Before feasibility filtering, a cluster with 10,000 GPUs and 500 pending jobs can have more than $10^{15}$ candidate allocations, which puts exhaustive allocation search out of reach. Detection instead works on a **wait-for graph**, a directed graph with one vertex per job and an edge from job A to job B whenever A waits for a resource that B holds. A cycle identifies a potential deadlock. It establishes deadlock when every edge is an unavoidable blocking dependency and no alternative resource allocation can let a member proceed. Finding a cycle in an already constructed graph takes time linear in its vertices and edges. The difficulty lies in construction. Resource state changes continuously across a distributed control plane, so the scheduler rarely holds a complete, current graph, and a cycle detected on stale state may already have resolved while a real one goes unseen.

Large-scale schedulers therefore supplement wait-for detection with mechanisms that preserve liveness under incomplete state. A **lease-based resource** gives every allocation a time-to-live; expiry authorizes reclamation only when the resource manager enforces revocation and cleanup before reuse. **Progress monitoring** checks advancement through signals such as completed-step counters, using GPU utilization and heartbeats as supporting observations rather than proof of useful work. When the scheduler must preempt a lower-priority job, **holistic preemption** reserves the CPU, network, and storage bandwidth needed for that job to checkpoint and exit cleanly; otherwise, the victim can be too starved to release the accelerators. False failure suspicions can cost useful work, but must not bypass the ownership checks required for safe recovery.

### Heterogeneous Gang Scheduling

^sec-fleet-orchestration-heterogeneous-gang

In preference-training pipelines such as reinforcement learning from human feedback (RLHF), allocating the policy-training GPUs while the rollout-generation or reward-scoring workers sit queued stalls the workflow just as completely as a missing AllReduce rank. Homogeneous gang scheduling allocates ${N}$ identical workers executing the same compute graph. Heterogeneous workflows instead combine trainers, evaluators, and inference-style workers that need different GPU counts, memory footprints, and lifetimes, yet still must run together as a single job.

↩︎ The models that alignment training co-locates, and their unequal resource demands, are established in **[[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/02 Distributed ML/06 Distributed Training/V2-06.08 Multi-Model Training for Alignment and RLHF#^sec-distributed-training-rlhf|Multi-Model Training for Alignment and RLHF]]**.

**Systems Perspective 8.1: Routing over synchrony**

Heterogeneous gang scheduling extends atomic allocation to the roles that must coexist for a workflow to progress. A viable group might pair high-HBM nodes for memory-bound generation with compute-oriented nodes for policy updates. The orchestration problem then shifts from packing a homogeneous ring toward routing data. Rather than reducing gradients among identical workers, the job streams rollouts from generation workers to the workers that compute policy updates, so placement must also support the data exchanged between those roles. Rollout and training stages may exchange work in batches or through a buffered pipeline; their measured production and consumption rates determine where queues grow or accelerators wait. The scheduler needs a minimum viable resource bundle and a supported scaling policy for each role, rather than an assumption that all workers are interchangeable or perfectly balanced.

Homogeneous and heterogeneous jobs share an allocation requirement: the scheduler must assemble a viable group before expecting progress. Backfill can use capacity while that group waits, provided runtime estimates or preemption contracts protect the reservation. Enforcing these policies requires a control plane that represents ownership and reconciles allocations after failures.
