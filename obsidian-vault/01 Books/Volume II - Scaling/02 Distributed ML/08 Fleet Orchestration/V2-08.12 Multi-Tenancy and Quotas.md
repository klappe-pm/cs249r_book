# V2-08.12 Multi-Tenancy and Quotas

[[Home|Home]] · [[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/Volume 2 Overview|Volume Overview]] · [[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/02 Distributed ML/08 Fleet Orchestration/V2-08 Fleet Orchestration|V2-08 Fleet Orchestration]]

^sec-fleet-orchestration-multi-tenancy

When the computer vision team hoards 500 GPUs for a week while the natural language processing team sits blocked trying to push a critical bug fix to production, shared clusters devolve into a tragedy of the commons. Teams with the most aggressive job submission rates, the largest jobs, or the most persistent resubmission scripts consume disproportionate resources, while teams with more modest or intermittent needs find the cluster perpetually occupied. The result is organizational friction and underinvestment in slower-moving but potentially higher-value projects whose teams cannot compete for capacity on submission volume alone. A static serving reservation faces the same pressure from the other direction, since a training team that sees idle serving devices will want them.

Multi-tenancy and quotas turn organizational priorities into rules for resource allocation, borrowing, and reclamation that the scheduler can enforce. A quota defines an entitlement or a limit. Whether that entitlement becomes prompt, productive access depends on borrowing that lends idle guarantees to other teams and on reclamation that returns them within a bounded time, both developed below. Accounting that separates the capacity a team holds from the capacity it uses well belongs with utilization debugging in [[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/02 Distributed ML/08 Fleet Orchestration/V2-08.13 Debugging Cluster Utilization#^sec-fleet-orchestration-resource-accounting|section 8.12.1]].

### Hierarchical Fair-Share

^sec-fleet-orchestration-fair-share-multi

Assigning fixed GPU quotas at the namespace or project level (for example, 500 GPUs to Team A, 300 to Team B, and 200 to Team C) gives each team a clear entitlement, but leads to systematic underutilization whenever workloads fluctuate. If Team A’s 500-GPU allocation sits 50 percent idle during a quiet period while Team B’s queue overflows with urgent work, rigid quotas leave 250 GPUs wasted that Team B is forbidden to use. Allocating smaller static quotas to each team creates the opposite failure: teams are blocked from running legitimate work during peak periods even when the cluster has spare capacity overall.

Hierarchical quotas, which Slurm expresses through its account hierarchy and fair-share accounting ([[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/02 Distributed ML/08 Fleet Orchestration/V2-08.06 Orchestration Paradigms#^eq-fleet-orchestration-fair-share|equation 1]]) and Kueue through ClusterQueues grouped into borrowing cohorts, separate a team’s guaranteed share from its maximum permitted allocation. In [[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/02 Distributed ML/08 Fleet Orchestration/V2-08.12 Multi-Tenancy and Quotas#^eq-fleet-orchestration-quota|equation 4]], ${Q_{\text{team}}}$ denotes a team’s maximum burst cap rather than its guarantee, ${Q_{\text{department}}}$ is the department’s total capacity limit, and ${U_{\text{allocated}}}$ is each sibling team’s current active allocation. Assuming those allocations remain within the department limit, the smaller of the team’s burst cap and the department’s currently unallocated capacity sets the team’s admission ceiling before any reclamation:

$$
{Q_{\text{effective}}}= \min\left({Q_{\text{team}}},\ {Q_{\text{department}}}- \sum_{\text{other teams}} {U_{\text{allocated}}}\right) \tag{4}
$$

^eq-fleet-orchestration-quota

The second term inside the minimum is what lends idle guarantees across sibling teams. If Team A uses only 60 percent of its 500-GPU guarantee, its active allocation ${U_{\text{allocated}}}$ drops below its guarantee and leaves the remaining 200 GPUs in ${Q_{\text{department}}} - \sum_{\text{other teams}} {U_{\text{allocated}}}$ for sibling teams to claim, up to each borrower’s own cap ${Q_{\text{team}}}$. This ceiling bounds what a team may hold at admission, and it encodes neither fair-share weights nor guarantees. When several teams contend for that spare capacity at once, the scheduler still applies its fairness policy, the dominant-resource calculation of [[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/02 Distributed ML/08 Fleet Orchestration/V2-08.05 Core Allocation Constraints#^sec-fleet-orchestration-drf-worked-allocation|section 8.4.1]], to the feasible gangs that fit under each ceiling.

### Burst Capacity and Quota Borrowing

^sec-fleet-orchestration-burst-capacity

Lending turns unused guarantees into burst capacity. Suppose a small team holds a guarantee of 64 GPUs but needs 256 for a weekend-long training run while a sibling team’s guaranteed share sits idle. The small team may borrow idle whole devices up to its maximum quota. Those devices remain part of physical cluster capacity rather than becoming virtual capacity, and they carry lower preemption priority than owned resources. When the sibling team submits work that needs its guarantee, the scheduler reclaims the borrowed devices by preempting or shrinking the borrower’s opportunistic jobs.

Borrowing resolves that static-quota dilemma only when the lent capacity remains reclaimable within the platform’s response-time objective, safety headroom, and current physical capacity. A team entitled to 500 GPUs can reclaim borrowed devices according to that contract, but the scheduler must not promise an immediate 500-GPU allocation when failures, nonpreemptible reservations, or recovery headroom reduce reclaimable capacity.

Burst capacity therefore raises the maximum a team may hold, while its guarantee stays fixed. The small team above may hold up to 256 GPUs, but the platform promises only its 64-GPU guarantee concurrently with every other team’s. The sum of guarantees that the platform promises to honor concurrently must fit reclaimable physical capacity after nonpreemptible reservations and safety headroom. Teams may advertise higher maximums or fair-share weights, the target shares that set priority in [[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/02 Distributed ML/08 Fleet Orchestration/V2-08.06 Orchestration Paradigms#^eq-fleet-orchestration-fair-share|equation 1]], because borrowed jobs yield when guaranteed demand returns, but those values are not guarantees.

Reclamation couples quota policy to the checkpoint infrastructure. The admission controller separates guaranteed jobs from opportunistic jobs. Guaranteed jobs run on the team’s owned quota and face preemption only from higher-priority work. Opportunistic jobs run on borrowed capacity, yield first, and must checkpoint often enough that reclamation wastes little work, since every forced reclaim pays the preemption overhead of [[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/02 Distributed ML/08 Fleet Orchestration/V2-08.09 Cost Optimization#^sec-fleet-orchestration-preemption-cascades|section 8.8.1]]. Elastic jobs soften reclamation further through the graceful preemption of [[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/02 Distributed ML/08 Fleet Orchestration/V2-08.08 Elastic Scheduling#^sec-fleet-orchestration-elastic-scheduler-integration|section 8.7.2]], shrinking one replica at a time toward their minimum viable allocation rather than terminating.

One policy for quota borrowing uses four tiers. Production serving (Priority 0) holds capacity protected from routine preemption. Production training (Priority 1) yields only under a defined serving-failover policy. Interactive and debug sessions (Priority 2) receive short execution limits to preserve iteration speed without indefinite resource occupation. Batch and research tasks (Priority 3) use surplus capacity and yield under their reclamation contract. These numbers label this example’s tiers; they are not Kubernetes priority values, where larger integers indicate higher priority. The hierarchy establishes precedence, but deadline protection still requires feasible capacity and bounded reclamation time, while lower-priority work needs an explicit starvation policy.

As the over-subscription discussion in [[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/02 Distributed ML/08 Fleet Orchestration/V2-08.05 Core Allocation Constraints#^sec-fleet-orchestration-bin-packing|section 8.4.2]] showed, a partly idle device is still a whole allocated device, so the safe burst limit follows from reclaimability rather than average SM utilization. Fractional sharing is a separate mechanism that requires an explicit MIG, MPS, or time-slicing contract. The scheduler must measure how quickly borrowed jobs can checkpoint, shrink, or terminate, and it must reserve the I/O and network bandwidth required for that transition. Only then can it bound the amount of opportunistic capacity that is safe to lend while still honoring the guarantee’s response-time objective. Until reclamation completes, a borrowed job that still runs beside the owner’s returning work is a noisy neighbor on the shared network and storage paths ([[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/02 Distributed ML/08 Fleet Orchestration/V2-08.11 Serving Capacity in the Shared Fleet#^sec-fleet-orchestration-resource-isolation|section 8.10.1]]). Isolation and the reclaim deadline must together bound how much the borrower can slow the owner.

### Security and Namespace Isolation

^sec-fleet-orchestration-security-isolation

Teams that share a fleet must also be kept from observing or altering one another’s model weights, training data, and inference traffic. A namespace provides only an administrative boundary that host, device, network, and storage controls must enforce. Network policy must cover the collective paths an admitted gang actually uses, and host networking moves the enforcement point off the pod network, so each path needs its own verification.

↩︎ Fabric-level isolation, through virtual lanes and traffic classes that keep one job’s bursts from stretching another job’s collectives, is established in **[[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/01 The Fleet/03 Network Fabrics/V2-03.09 Network Virtualization#^sec-network-fabrics-qos|Traffic isolation and quality of service]]**.

Across teams, the isolation spectrum of [[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/02 Distributed ML/08 Fleet Orchestration/V2-08.11 Serving Capacity in the Shared Fleet#^tbl-gpu-virtualization-isolation-spectrum|table 7]] becomes a trust-boundary choice, because colocated workloads now belong to owners with different exposure to one another. Side-channel attacks on shared GPUs are a documented vulnerability; by monitoring contention on shared caches or memory controllers, a malicious tenant can infer the architecture or even data properties of a co-resident model. In sensitive environments, MIG or dedicated devices can strengthen device-resource isolation, but neither creates a complete external-tenant security boundary by itself. A multi-tenant platform combines device partitioning with VM or container isolation, configuration of the input-output memory management unit (IOMMU) that limits which host memory a device can reach, driver and runtime hardening, authentication and authorization, and network and storage controls. Single-organization workloads with a narrower trust boundary can use MPS or time-slicing when weaker fault and performance isolation is acceptable.

↪ The attack surface of shared accelerators, and which sharing mechanisms leave co-resident tenants observable to one another, is developed in **[[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/04 The Responsible Fleet/13 Security & Privacy/V2-13.07 Fleet-Internal Trust Boundaries#^sec-security-privacy-shared-accelerators|Shared accelerators and caches]]**.

### Quota Governance and Organizational Dynamics

^sec-fleet-orchestration-quota-governance

Even when a scheduler enforces quotas accurately, a guarantee set six months ago can leave 60 percent of a team’s reserved GPUs underused after its flagship model ships while another team’s urgent launch queue backs up. While the scheduler manages immediate resource assignments, **quota governance** revises the entitlements and limits those assignments must honor. Periodic **quota review cycles** compare observed use, forecast demand, and the cost of meeting each team’s capacity promise. A team using only 40 percent of a 128-GPU guarantee does not necessarily block other teams if unused devices can be borrowed. The governance question is whether the guarantee remains justified and whether its reclamation contract lets the fleet use spare capacity productively between demand peaks.

The central tension in quota management is the **hoarding problem**, in which teams retain idle reservations to avoid queuing again when work becomes ready. A workflow that pauses policy training while awaiting human annotations or a training run delayed by unfinished tokenization can leave its reserved accelerators unused, reflecting dependencies in particular workflows rather than an unavoidable idle phase of all training. Utilization-based reclamation can release idle physical allocations to borrowers, provided the platform preserves any promised return time; revising the team’s longer-term guarantee instead requires comparing expected demand with observed use. Clear return contracts reduce the incentive to hold idle devices without making low activity alone grounds for breaking a capacity promise.

Quota accounting should remain close to the scheduling decision. Every admitted job needs an owner, project, workload class, and preemption contract so the scheduler can apply quota and recovery policy consistently, and Kubernetes labels and Slurm accounts carry this scheduling identity. GPU-hours charged against a reservation, borrowed share, or preemption class then make the opportunity cost of hoarding visible and let the control plane reclaim idle guarantees.

**Example 8.1: Quota hoarding**

**Scenario**: A research team reserves a static block of 500 GPUs for an upcoming model refresh.

**Diagnosis**: Upstream data delays postpone training, but the team retains the reservation to ensure future availability. The GPUs sit idle for 3 weeks, leaving \$504,000 of capacity value unused while other teams’ queues back up.

**Systems lesson**: An idle reservation should trigger a review of lending eligibility and future demand. The orchestrator can admit opportunistic borrowers under a bounded reclamation contract without changing the owner’s guarantee. Reducing that guarantee is a separate governance decision; lending owned capacity does not turn it into cloud spot capacity.

^exmp-fleet-orchestration-quota-hoarding

A “use it or lose it” reclamation policy addresses the most visible symptom of quota hoarding. Lending idle capacity to another team, however, requires the system to distinguish borrowed capacity that is preemptible on short notice from guaranteed reservations protected by SLOs. Defining borrowing, preemption, and guaranteed tiers as first-class policy primitives is the core multi-tenancy design decision.

**Checkpoint 8.4: Multi-tenancy design decisions**

Consider a 2,000-GPU cluster shared between a research team (60 percent allocation) and a production team (40 percent allocation):

**Idle reclamation**: decide whether the production team can borrow the research team’s allocation while half of it sits idle, and describe the preemption behavior when the research team submits a large job.

**Priority classes**: configure priority classes for a production inference workload that needs 100 GPUs with guaranteed latency SLOs and a research training job that can tolerate preemption.

**Sharing contract**: explain why 65 percent average SM utilization does not justify whole-device overcommitment, then identify which measured resource and isolation constraints would make statistical multiplexing safe.

Even with isolation, quotas, and dynamic sharing in place, a cluster can report low utilization beside a full queue. Top-level dashboards rarely explain why pending jobs cannot claim the free devices. Locating that loss requires systematic utilization debugging across hardware pools, scheduler constraints, and user request habits.
