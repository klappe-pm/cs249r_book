# V2-08.04 Scheduling Objectives and Their Conflicts

[[Home|Home]] · [[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/Volume 2 Overview|Volume Overview]] · [[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/02 Distributed ML/08 Fleet Orchestration/V2-08 Fleet Orchestration|V2-08 Fleet Orchestration]]

^sec-fleet-orchestration-objectives

Giving a large training gang an unbroken allocation for weeks amortizes launch and data-movement overhead, but leaves short interactive debugging jobs waiting hours in the queue. Preempting the large gang so short jobs start immediately burns cluster capacity on checkpoint saves and restarts. Every scheduling policy trades four competing objectives against one another: throughput, fairness, latency, and cost efficiency. Throughput benefits from allocations that sustain useful work and amortize launch, interruption, and data-movement overhead; a large allocation helps only while the job scales effectively. Maintaining fairness demands an equitable distribution of resources across competing tenants to prevent a single dominant project from monopolizing the fleet. Dominant resource fairness ([[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/02 Distributed ML/08 Fleet Orchestration/V2-08.17 Source References#^ref-ghodsi2011|Ghodsi et al. 2011]]) formalizes this balance by equalizing each tenant’s largest normalized share across GPUs, CPU, and memory. Reducing queue latency helps short, interactive jobs complete promptly, but aggressive preemption can repeatedly interrupt long-running training. Pursuing cost efficiency introduces different costs for different policies: spot capacity risks interruption, off-peak scheduling delays admission, and dense packing can increase interference. Each policy must be evaluated against useful work completed and the delays it imposes.

Fairness requires an outcome metric rather than an input quota rule alone. For job ${j}$, **slowdown** ${S}_j=({W}_j+{T}_j)/{T}_j$ compares its queueing-plus-running time with its isolated runtime ${T}_j$, assuming the running time is unchanged by sharing. A one-hour job that waits nine hours has ${S}_j=10$, even if its team eventually receives its nominal GPU share. When contention or resizing changes execution time, the numerator must instead use the observed queueing-plus-running duration while the denominator retains the isolated baseline. A scheduler should report the distribution of slowdown by user and job class, together with the age of the oldest pending feasible job. Dominant-share parity detects unequal resource allocation, slowdown exposes unequal delay, and the maximum feasible wait exposes starvation risk. These metrics separate a policy that is fair in allocated GPU-hours from one that is fair in the time users wait to obtain useful results.

Each of the four objectives, pursued alone, schedules the same pool differently and gives up something the others protect ([[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/02 Distributed ML/08 Fleet Orchestration/V2-08.04 Scheduling Objectives and Their Conflicts#^fig-fleet-orchestration-scheduling-tradeoffs|figure 2]]). Favoring a large unbroken allocation can delay short experiments, while repeatedly admitting short jobs can prevent a large gang from assembling. These conflicts depend on the workload and available capacity; improving one objective does not necessarily worsen every other objective.

Consider a 64-GPU fine-tuning run of the same 175B-parameter model, a far smaller allocation than its 1,024-GPU pretraining run. If it sustains high utilization for days, an uninterrupted allocation amortizes launch overhead across a long execution. Admitting it may nevertheless require draining 64 suitably placed GPUs, delaying smaller experiments. Conversely, repeatedly filling available gaps with short jobs can prevent the large job from assembling its required group. Preemption or resizing can recover flexibility only if the job supports the transition and its cost is acceptable. The policy must therefore compare useful throughput with completion delay for both job classes. In scheduler terminology, those choices often appear as quality-of-service classes: job-priority tiers that decide which objective wins when throughput, latency, fairness, and cost collide.

Queueing adds a further conflict between utilization and latency, because a fleet run close to full load leaves arriving jobs little spare capacity. In a single-server queue with random arrivals, mean waiting time, expressed as a multiple of the mean job duration, is the product of a load factor ${\rho_{\text{serv}}}/(1-{\rho_{\text{serv}}})$ and a variability factor $(1+{c_{s}}^2)/2$. Here ${\rho_{\text{serv}}}$ is offered load relative to service capacity, and ${c_{s}}$ is the coefficient of variation of job duration. The load factor stays small through moderate load and grows without bound as load approaches capacity, so waiting time bends sharply upward near saturation instead of rising in proportion to utilization. The variability factor scales the whole curve. A queue that mixes minute-long debugging jobs with week-long training runs waits several times longer at the same load than a queue of similar-length jobs. Each added point of utilization therefore costs more waiting time in an ML fleet than in a uniform one. Notebook [8.1](https://mlsysbook.ai/vol2/08_fleet_orchestration/08_fleet_orchestration.html#nbk-fleet-orchestration-queuing-theory-gpu-clusters) states the relation formally and prices both factors.

**Napkin Math 8.1: The queuing theory of GPU clusters**

To isolate the effect of runtime variability, consider an M/G/1 queue with first-come-first-served service. Kendall notation names the queue by its arrival process, service-time distribution, and server count: M for memoryless (Poisson) arrivals, G for a general service-time distribution, and 1 for a single server. Treating a fleet as one server means modeling its aggregate capacity as a single resource that works through one job at a time, with each job’s service time equal to its GPU-hours divided by the fleet’s GPU count. That mapping ignores gang packing and parallel admission, so it isolates the variability effect rather than predicting a real fleet’s wait. Jobs arrive according to a Poisson process $({\lambda_{\text{arr}}})$, and independent service times follow a general distribution $({G})$ with mean $1/{\mu}$ and finite standard deviation ${\sigma}$. When offered load is below service capacity, the Pollaczek-Khinchine formula gives the steady-state expected waiting time in the queue $({W_{q}})$:

$$
\frac{{W_{q}}}{1/{\mu}} = \frac{{\rho_{\text{serv}}}}{1-{\rho_{\text{serv}}}} \cdot \frac{1+{c_{s}}^2}{2}
$$

Here, ${\rho_{\text{serv}}} = {\lambda_{\text{arr}}}/{\mu}$ represents offered load relative to the model’s service capacity, $1/{\mu}$ is the average job duration, and ${c_{s}}= {\sigma}{\mu}$ is the **coefficient of variation** of job duration. The right-hand side is therefore the waiting time expressed as a multiple of the average job duration; a fleet’s measured GPU-busy percentage is not automatically this model’s load.

An exponential service-time distribution provides a reference case, yielding ${c_{s}}\approx 1$. A mixed ML queue can have much greater variability when short debugging jobs measured in minutes share capacity with training runs measured in weeks. The representative ${c_{s}}=3$ calculation in this callout captures that variability without claiming a universal fleet constant. The impact on wait times is multiplicative. For the scheduling argument here, the single-server multiplier is sufficient; **[[05 Reference/Volume II - Scaling/V2 Inference Foundations#^sec-appdx-inference-scale-queuing-theory-batched-inference-bbf0|Queuing theory for batched inference]]** develops an explicit single-server surrogate for batched serving and explains why multi-server, finite-buffer M/G/c/K systems require additional modeling.

At 80 percent utilization $({\rho_{\text{serv}}} = 0.8)$: with an exponential workload $({c_{s}}= 1)$, ${W_{q}}=$ 4 $\times$ the average job duration. With an illustrative high-variability workload $({c_{s}}= 3)$, ${W_{q}}=$ 20 $\times$ the average job duration. In this M/G/1 model, service-time variability multiplies waiting time at the same offered load. The example does not prescribe a universal GPU-utilization threshold or model gang placement directly. It explains why capacity planning needs the service-time distribution and queue-delay objective as well as average load.
