# V2-08.10 Custom ML Schedulers

[[Home|Home]] · [[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/Volume 2 Overview|Volume Overview]] · [[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/02 Distributed ML/08 Fleet Orchestration/V2-08 Fleet Orchestration|V2-08 Fleet Orchestration]]

^sec-fleet-orchestration-custom-schedulers

A short experiment waiting behind a long training run can suffer even when every running job has an efficient placement. Improving that outcome requires a policy for deciding which job receives the next available resources. Custom ML schedulers use different signals to make that decision: consumed service can prioritize jobs without runtime estimates, iteration boundaries can make preemption cheaper, normalized finish time can expose unfair delay, and measured goodput can guide joint resource and batch-size changes. Only the last of these requires a model of learning progress; the others improve completion time, utilization, or fairness without predicting convergence.

### Duration-Agnostic Scheduling (Tiresias)

^sec-fleet-orchestration-tiresias

When a researcher submits an exploratory training run without knowing whether it will fail in ten minutes or run for a week, scheduling policies that depend on upfront duration estimates ([[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/02 Distributed ML/08 Fleet Orchestration/V2-08.05 Core Allocation Constraints#^sec-fleet-orchestration-gang-scheduling|section 8.4.3]]) either mispack the queue or penalize inaccurate guesses. **Tiresias** ([[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/02 Distributed ML/08 Fleet Orchestration/V2-08.17 Source References#^ref-gu2019tiresias|Gu et al. 2019]]) eliminates the requirement for duration estimates entirely. It uses a two-dimensional attained service[[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/02 Distributed ML/08 Fleet Orchestration/V2-08.18 Footnotes#^fn11|¹¹]] scheduler that makes priority decisions based on what a job has already consumed, not what it claims it will consume. Jobs accumulate “service” based on GPU-time consumed, with priority decreasing as service increases. The two dimensions are time (how long the job has been running) and resources (how many GPUs the job uses), capturing both elapsed duration and resource intensity.

A discretized version groups jobs into service bins (illustratively, less than 1 GPU-hour, 1 to 10 GPU-hours, 10 to 100 GPU-hours, greater than 100 GPU-hours), giving lower-service bins higher priority. When job durations are heavy-tailed, a job that has consumed little service is likely to be short, so favoring low-service jobs approximates shortest-remaining-processing-time ordering without any runtime estimate. New jobs can receive service before long-running jobs, but their eventual duration remains unknown, and a high-priority gang still needs a feasible allocation. Tiresias also promotes jobs that have waited too long, trading some average-completion-time benefit for protection against starvation. The priority rule therefore replaces runtime prediction with an observable signal and an explicit waiting-time policy; it does not guarantee that every short job starts promptly.

The Tiresias evaluation reports lower average job completion time (JCT) on its testbed and trace-driven workloads ([[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/02 Distributed ML/08 Fleet Orchestration/V2-08.17 Source References#^ref-gu2019tiresias|Gu et al. 2019]]). The result depends on job arrivals, sizes, and the comparison policy. Prioritizing low-service jobs can delay long-running jobs, so average completion time must be evaluated alongside starvation prevention and the delay experienced by each job class.

### Iteration-Aware Scheduling (Gandiva)

^sec-fleet-orchestration-gandiva

Preempting a job in the middle of a training step either discards the partial step or requires saving gigabytes of temporary forward-pass activations alongside the model weights. **Gandiva** ([[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/02 Distributed ML/08 Fleet Orchestration/V2-08.17 Source References#^ref-xiao2018gandiva|Xiao et al. 2018]]) avoids that mid-step state transfer by exploiting repetitive training iterations to profile jobs and time-slice GPUs at iteration boundaries. To give a GPU to another job, Gandiva suspends the running job at the end of a minibatch, copies its live GPU memory to host memory, and resumes another job on the device; switching back reverses the copy. Several jobs can therefore take turns on one GPU, and the same suspend-and-resume path lets Gandiva migrate a job to better-placed devices or pack compatible jobs onto one GPU when their combined memory fits. The boundary is the cheap place to switch because temporary activations have been released, so far less state must move than in the middle of a step. Weights, optimizer state, and runtime allocations remain, so each switch still costs a transfer proportional to that persistent state, and a job whose persistent state nearly fills the device cannot share it. The scheduler must measure memory demand, interference, and transition cost before admitting additional jobs.

Gandiva also implements **grow-shrink elasticity**, applying the allocation mechanism of [[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/02 Distributed ML/08 Fleet Orchestration/V2-08.08 Elastic Scheduling#^sec-fleet-orchestration-elastic-training|section 8.7]] to jobs that explicitly permit resizing. It expands eligible jobs into idle capacity and shrinks them when demand returns, using profiles to estimate which jobs benefit. The published implementation limits growth to one server and waits through an idle period before expanding. Its contribution is the connection between measured job behavior and resource control, rather than a claim that arbitrary training jobs can resize cheaply.

### Finish-Time Fairness (Themis)

^sec-fleet-orchestration-themis

Equal GPU-time shares can produce unequal completion delays when workloads respond differently to placement. **Themis** ([[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/02 Distributed ML/08 Fleet Orchestration/V2-08.17 Source References#^ref-mahajan2020themis|Mahajan et al. 2020]]) measures fairness in normalized finish time. Each application, which can contain related training jobs, compares its completion time in the shared cluster with its completion time on an isolated $1/{N}$ share of the cluster, where ${N}$ is the number of applications contending for it. The ratio has the same form as the slowdown ${S}_j$ defined with the scheduling objectives, except in its denominator. Themis divides by the completion time on a fair share of the cluster rather than by the isolated runtime on the job’s own allocation, so a ratio of one means the application finished no later than its fair share would have allowed.

Themis targets the worst finish-time fairness ratio across applications. Its auction rounds offer available resources to the applications farthest from fair completion, while allowing placement efficiency to influence which specific GPUs each one receives within a round. With two applications whose current estimates are 1.2 and 2.0, the second is projected to finish twice as late as its fair share would allow, so it enters the round ahead of the first. The first application’s ratio rises while it waits, and once it overtakes the second it moves to the front of later rounds. The signal is relative completion delay, not proximity to completion or GPU-hours already consumed.

Within a round, contending applications bid on candidate GPU subsets by reporting the finish-time fairness ratio they would reach on each subset. Because a communication-heavy job finishes much faster on a compact subset than on a scattered one while a communication-light job sees little difference, these bids expose placement sensitivity rather than monetary willingness to pay. The arbiter can then assign compact subsets to the lagging applications whose finish-time ratios gain the most from locality. This auction trades short-term allocation efficiency against the long-term fairness objective, making the quality of completion-time and placement estimates part of the scheduler’s operating assumptions.

### Adaptive Resource Allocation (Pollux)

^sec-fleet-orchestration-pollux

Adding GPUs to a running job by increasing its global batch size raises throughput in examples per second. Past the critical batch size, however, each example contributes less optimization progress, so the extra GPUs can slow convergence rather than speed it up. **Pollux** ([[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/02 Distributed ML/08 Fleet Orchestration/V2-08.17 Source References#^ref-qiao2021pollux|Qiao et al. 2021]]) addresses this coupling by extending resource adaptation to training hyperparameters. Where Gandiva changes only device allocations, Pollux jointly considers allocation, batch size, and learning-rate scaling because a larger batch can use more devices efficiently while changing the amount of work needed to converge. The preferred configuration can therefore change with both cluster demand and the stage of training.

Pollux uses statistical goodput to connect system throughput, measured in training examples per second, with statistical efficiency, which measures progress per example relative to a reference batch size. The word goodput already carries two other meanings. The run-level goodput of **[[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/00 Introduction/01 Introduction/V2-01.06 The C³ Taxonomy and Foundations of Scale#^sec-vol2-introduction-fleet-law|The Fleet Law]]** discounts work lost to failures and restarts, and Google’s ML productivity goodput multiplies program, runtime, and scheduling factors. Statistical goodput instead discounts each example by the optimization progress it contributes at the current batch size, and **[[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/03 The Fleet in Production/09 Fleet Efficiency/V2-09.03 ML Goodput#^sec-performance-engineering-goodput-family|One word, several measures]]** sets the three side by side. Statistical efficiency falls as the batch grows past the critical batch size of **[[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/02 Distributed ML/06 Distributed Training/V2-06.05 Scaling Efficiency and Convergence#^sec-distributed-training-systems-systems-critical-batch-size-parallelism-hurt-4961|Critical batch size and diminishing returns]]**, which is why more GPUs at a larger batch can raise throughput while lowering goodput. Pollux estimates how allocation and batch changes affect both quantities, then uses those estimates to guide resource sharing across jobs. Additional GPUs are valuable only when their throughput gain survives the corresponding change in statistical efficiency and the cost of changing the allocation.

The Pollux evaluation reports average job completion times 37 to 50 percent lower than the state-of-the-art deep learning schedulers it was compared against, which do not make the same combined adjustment of resources and batch size ([[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/02 Distributed ML/08 Fleet Orchestration/V2-08.17 Source References#^ref-qiao2021pollux|Qiao et al. 2021]]). The number belongs to that testbed and trace. Its durable lesson is the coupling: an allocation can increase samples per second while reducing progress per sample. The scheduler must estimate both effects and validate completion time at the same target quality, rather than transferring a reported improvement to a different workload.

### Scheduler Comparison Framework

^sec-fleet-orchestration-scheduler-comparison

Each research scheduler pursues a different objective, and each objective requires its own observations and controls ([[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/02 Distributed ML/08 Fleet Orchestration/V2-08.10 Custom ML Schedulers#^tbl-fleet-orchestration-scheduler-comparison|table 5]]). Consumed service can be obtained from resource accounting, while iteration timing and learning-progress estimates require closer runtime integration. The choice therefore turns on what the platform can observe and control, not on a ranking of scheduler sophistication.

| **Scheduler** | **Key insight**                       | **Scheduling signal**  | **Optimization target**  | **Integration requirement**                    |
|:--------------|:--------------------------------------|:-----------------------|:-------------------------|:-----------------------------------------------|
| **Tiresias**  | Attained service informs priority     | GPU-hours consumed     | Minimize average JCT     | Resource accounting and queue reprioritization |
| **Gandiva**   | Iteration boundaries enable sharing   | Iteration timing       | Improve utilization      | Runtime profiling and coordinated time slicing |
| **Themis**    | Completion delay defines fairness     | Normalized finish time | Improve worst fairness   | Finish-time estimates and allocation auctions  |
| **Pollux**    | Batch size and allocation are coupled | Goodput                | Maximize cluster goodput | Runtime resizing and batch-size adaptation     |

Table 5: **Custom ML Scheduler Comparison**: A taxonomy of research schedulers based on the specific workload characteristic they exploit. Tiresias uses attained-service accounting, Gandiva profiles iterations, Themis estimates relative finish times and placement effects, and Pollux models goodput under allocation and batch-size changes.

^tbl-fleet-orchestration-scheduler-comparison

The common lesson is to match the policy signal to the scheduling objective. Attained service addresses runtime uncertainty and can work from accounting records. Iteration boundaries lower the cost of interrupting work but need reliable timing profiles. Normalized finish time measures unequal delay, and goodput couples resource allocation to learning progress, which requires the training loop to accept allocation and batch-size changes. Each added control creates a failure mode of its own: inaccurate profiles can increase interference, and an inaccurate learning-progress model can choose allocations that fail to improve convergence. A platform should therefore test the chosen policy against queue delay, starvation risk, and measured recovery cost before adopting it.

### From Research to Production

^sec-fleet-orchestration-research-to-production

A production cluster containing V100s alongside A100s and H100s, with both InfiniBand and Ethernet paths, gives the same GPU count very different performance across placements, while live jobs crash, hang, or exceed declared memory needs. Because a research scheduler’s evaluation establishes results on a specific testbed, workload trace, and set of runtime controls, a production fleet must test whether its own hardware and failure conditions preserve those gains. Deploying a policy therefore requires integrating its decisions with admission, failure recovery, and the existing orchestrator, then measuring its benefit under the fleet’s actual workload.

Consider applying adaptive allocation to our 175B-parameter model training run. The runtime must first expose viable resize configurations and their costs. A checkpoint-and-restart path can require persisting and restoring large optimizer state, while other state-transfer mechanisms impose different costs. Repeated resizing can consume the expected throughput gain before the job benefits from its new allocation. The policy should therefore compare predicted additional progress over the expected allocation duration with the measured transition cost, retaining a stable placement when resizing does not repay that cost. The decision follows the workload and cluster demand, not a universal training phase.

The production decision is whether a specialized policy improves marginal allocation enough to justify its operational burden, judged by the measures this chapter has already built: queue delay and slowdown, useful GPU-hours, preemption overhead, and placement efficiency. Group admission settles whether a gang can start at all; the research schedulers answer the next question, which feasible group to admit, and each imposes different requirements on the runtime. Selecting a policy therefore means matching its objective and assumptions to the fleet rather than choosing a single scheduler as universally best.

**Checkpoint 8.3: Custom scheduler design space**

Consider the trade-offs between the four research schedulers:

**Estimate-free scheduling**: identify the information Tiresias sacrifices by eliminating runtime estimates, and describe when that sacrifice hurts scheduling quality.

**Time-slicing limits**: identify workloads for which Gandiva’s iteration-boundary time-slicing fails, and explain why.

**Job starvation**: explain how waiting changes an application’s finish-time fairness ratio in Themis, and why this differs from favoring jobs that are nearly complete.

**Goodput-model risk**: describe the failure mode when Pollux’s goodput model is wrong.

The training policies just compared balance completion time, fairness, utilization, and learning progress. Serving adds a different deadline: capacity must be ready before an arriving request exhausts its latency budget. That requirement makes provisioning delay and burst headroom central to how the fleet allocates capacity to serving.
