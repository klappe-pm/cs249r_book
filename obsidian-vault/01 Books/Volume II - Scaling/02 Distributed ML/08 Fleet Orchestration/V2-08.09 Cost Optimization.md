# V2-08.09 Cost Optimization

[[Home|Home]] · [[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/Volume 2 Overview|Volume Overview]] · [[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/02 Distributed ML/08 Fleet Orchestration/V2-08 Fleet Orchestration|V2-08 Fleet Orchestration]]

^sec-fleet-orchestration-cost-optimization

An idle accelerator in an enterprise fleet incurs operational costs every hour whether computing or blocked in a queue, turning placement and preemption decisions into direct financial trade-offs. The orchestrator translates this utilization-to-dollars relationship from [[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/02 Distributed ML/08 Fleet Orchestration/V2-08.02 The Scheduling Problem#^sec-fleet-orchestration-introduction|section 8.1]] into an online scheduling signal, balancing resource prices, reservation commitments, interruption risk, checkpoint cost, and job deadlines to decide whether to admit, delay, preempt, or resize work. Capital planning, portfolio return, cost attribution, and lifecycle total cost of ownership are larger, slower decisions developed in **[[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/03 The Fleet in Production/12 ML Operations at Scale/V2-12.10 Fleet Economics and Utilization#^sec-ops-scale-fleet-economics|Fleet Economics and Utilization]]**; the scheduler consumes their outcomes as prices and constraints.

At a fixed utilization, idle capacity grows linearly with fleet size, so the dollar cost of a scheduling inefficiency grows with every procurement ([[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/02 Distributed ML/08 Fleet Orchestration/V2-08.09 Cost Optimization#^fig-cost-idle-gpus|figure 13]]). At 10,000 GPUs operating at 50 percent utilization, annual waste reaches \$87.6M under the chapter’s \$2/GPU-hour accounting scenario. Published measurements show that such underutilization occurs in production. Microsoft’s Philly cluster study ([[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/02 Distributed ML/08 Fleet Orchestration/V2-08.17 Source References#^ref-jeon2019analysis|Jeon et al. 2019]]) measured mean GPU utilization of approximately 52 percent. The 30 to 70 percent shaded band in the figure is an illustrative operating range for the cost model, not a universal empirical claim about all production clusters. Moving from 50 percent to 80 percent utilization, the improvement priced in the chapter opening, recovers over \$52M annually in equivalent capacity value. That is the same 3,000 GPU-equivalents the opening counted as the recoverable 30 percent idle share, and it becomes cash savings only when it avoids billed usage or a planned purchase.

![[90 Assets/Volume II/08 Fleet Orchestration/Cell 22 Output 1 5f4910a4eb.png]]

Figure 13: **The Cost of Idle GPUs**: Annual wasted cost as a function of cluster size for different GPU utilization levels, assuming \$2/GPU-hour (cloud equivalent). The shaded region between 30 percent and 70 percent is an illustrative operating range for the scenario analysis. At 10,000 GPUs with 50 percent utilization, the model yields \$87.6M in annual wasted capacity. ^fig-cost-idle-gpus

Recovering that idle capacity requires pricing each scheduling lever against the disruption it causes. Because reclaiming GPUs from running jobs is the most direct way to admit higher-value work, the cost of a single preemption and the cascades it can trigger come first. Discounted spot capacity then lowers the hourly price of restart-tolerant training, diversification and checkpoint policies keep correlated reclamations from erasing that discount, and capacity-reservation tiers combine with workload cost policies to route jobs across reserved, on-demand, and interruptible pools.

### Preemption Cost and Cascades

^sec-fleet-orchestration-preemption-cascades

![[90 Assets/Volume II/08 Fleet Orchestration/Fleet Orchestration Preempt Cascade 0c8c7420cc.svg]]

*Two-level tree. A red square for the admitted job evicts two jobs, labeled k, and each of those evicts two more, labeled k squared. A dashed line labeled requeue cap crosses the tree between the two levels.*

*Each eviction can evict again, so one admission reaches ${k} + {k}^2$ jobs.*

When a high-priority job enters a saturated cluster, the scheduler must decide which running workloads to terminate to free up resources. This decision is rarely isolated. In tightly packed clusters, evicting a medium-priority job to accommodate a high-priority request often triggers a **preemption cascade**, where the evicted job immediately attempts to reschedule itself by displacing lower-priority workloads. The disruption multiplies with each level. If the urgent job displaces ${k}$ medium-priority jobs and each of those, on rescheduling, displaces ${k}$ lower-priority jobs, one admission interrupts ${k}+ {k}^2$ jobs after two levels. Every one of them pays the preemption overhead quantified below and adds a checkpoint reload to the storage system at the same moment. Two dampening controls cap that growth. A rule that evicted jobs requeue without preempting anyone themselves stops the chain at the first level, so one admission costs at most ${k}$ interruptions. A **preemption budget** limits how often any single job, or what fraction of capacity, may be displaced in a time window, which protects the jobs at the bottom of the priority order that every chain would otherwise reach.

The engineering cost of preemption extends beyond scheduling latency. The **preemption overhead** depends on the exit path. A successful graceful checkpoint adds the time to save and restart while preserving completed progress; a forced stop can instead discard work since the last durable checkpoint. Consider such a victim, a forced interruption of the 64-GPU fine-tuning run for our 175B parameter model. The scenario assumes lost work equivalent to 15 minutes of execution. The restart then repeats the cold-restart steps of the recovery budget in **[[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/02 Distributed ML/07 Fault Tolerance/V2-07.10 Failure Detection and Recovery#^sec-fault-tolerance-reliability-reliability-recovery-procedures-002d|Recovery procedures]]**, without its detection step because the scheduler itself stopped the job. These steps take 3 minutes to relaunch, 24.5 seconds to reload the 2.45 TB checkpoint with its optimizer state, and 2 minutes of warmup as data pipelines refill and just-in-time (JIT) compilation and runtime initialization complete. The calculation prices replay and restart across the same allocation; it does not also charge for a successful final save.

Reconstructing the distributed data pipeline also belongs in the preemption contract. The checkpoint must preserve the sampling and loader state needed to resume the intended data order, alongside model and optimizer state. If the restart path omits that state, it can repeat or skip samples and lose exact replay of the training trajectory. A different shuffle order does not by itself prove statistical corruption, but the recovery policy must specify which changes are acceptable. The scheduler should therefore measure restoration and pipeline warmup as part of recovery cost, rather than assume that reloading model weights alone makes the job productive.

The resulting 20.4-minute overhead combines discarded execution, its larger part, with relaunch, reload, and warmup time; it is not a measurement of continuous kernel activity after eviction. Spread over the run, it belongs to the Fleet Law’s coordination term ${T_{\text{sync}}}({N})$ (**[[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/00 Introduction/01 Introduction/V2-01.06 The C³ Taxonomy and Foundations of Scale#^eq-fleet-law|equation 1.3]]**), which counts scheduling decisions and failure recovery alongside barrier waits, so frequent preemption lengthens the average step exactly as frequent failure does. At \$2/GPU-hour, the scenario values each event at \$44 in allocation time. A low-priority run sits at the bottom of every cascade, so without a budget it can be struck many times a day. At the scenario’s 12 forced preemptions per day, it loses 4.1 hours of equivalent allocation time, or 261 GPU-hours for this 64-GPU run. A per-job budget bounds that loss directly. When a budget is exhausted, the scheduler must consider waiting, another placement, or a defined emergency exception. The trade-off is reduced recovery churn against delayed admission, measured under the workload’s deadlines.

### Spot Instances and Preemptible VMs

^sec-fleet-orchestration-spot-instances

Renting on-demand cloud GPUs for a multi-week training run protects the job from provider reclamation, but pays full price even when spare GPUs sit idle in the provider’s data center. Renting interruptible capacity cuts the hourly rate by 60 to 90 percent, but lets the provider reclaim those GPUs on best-effort notice. Cloud providers sell this spare capacity through spot instances[[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/02 Distributed ML/08 Fleet Orchestration/V2-08.18 Footnotes#^fn10|¹⁰]] (AWS) or **preemptible VMs** (GCP), where actual discounts depend on the resource and market. The reference interruption windows range from up to 30 seconds for GCP preemptible shutdown to 2 minutes for AWS stop or termination notices, with best-effort delivery rather than a guaranteed recovery interval. At the scenario rates of \$0.70/GPU-hour instead of \$2/GPU-hour, the worked comparison below shows how savings can exceed half the on-demand cost after accounting for its assumed interruption overhead.

The viability of spot instances for ML training depends on two factors that determine whether the discount translates into actual savings. The first is the cost of each interruption (checkpoint overhead plus restart time plus lost work since the last checkpoint). The second is the frequency of interruption (which varies by instance type, region, time of day, and overall cloud demand). [[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/02 Distributed ML/08 Fleet Orchestration/V2-08.09 Cost Optimization#^eq-fleet-orchestration-spot-cost|Equation 3]] gives the **effective cost** of spot training:

$$
{C_{\text{effective}}}= {C_{\text{spot}}}\times \frac{{T_{\text{total}}}}{{T_{\text{productive}}}} \tag{3}
$$

^eq-fleet-orchestration-spot-cost

where ${T_{\text{total}}}$ is billed allocation time, including checkpointing, recovery, and work later discarded after an interruption, and ${T_{\text{productive}}}$ is the equivalent training time represented by retained progress. The ratio assumes a fixed allocation size and price, with unchanged useful throughput while training; variable allocations require summing the cost of each allocation interval. Waiting for replacement capacity extends completion time but contributes to billed time only while resources remain allocated. When checkpointing and interruption losses consume a small fraction of billed time, effective cost stays near the spot price. Frequent interruptions, expensive recovery, or substantial replay can instead erase the discount.

Spot savings and fault tolerance infrastructure are coupled. Fault tolerance mechanisms (frequent asynchronous checkpointing, fast checkpoint loading, elastic recovery) make spot capacity usable by lowering the cost of each interruption. Elastic training can resume a logical job with fewer workers after reclamation, provided the remaining allocation is viable, although the framework may still restart worker processes and restore checkpointed state, so elasticity reduces recovery cost rather than eliminating it. The same infrastructure therefore earns a double return. It protects against hardware losses, and it enables the deliberate use of interruptible capacity. Both benefits require measurement. The scheduler combines observed interruption rates, recovery costs, and retained training progress with resource prices to estimate whether spot savings repay the additional recovery infrastructure. The same evidence supports valuing protection against hardware failures; neither benefit follows from the discount or checkpoint mechanism alone.

### Spot Instance Strategies for ML Training

^sec-fleet-orchestration-spot-strategies

Spot capacity pays off only when an interruption rarely removes the whole job at once, so schedulers that use it diversify where capacity comes from instead of simply retrying on failure. Availability zone diversification reduces the probability of simultaneous fleet-wide reclamation. Cloud providers typically manage spot pools independently per availability zone (AZ). If a training job requires 1,024 GPUs, allocating them as a single block in one zone exposes the job to a complete stop if that specific zone’s spot pool is reclaimed. Diversifying independent jobs or restart destinations across zones reduces exposure to one exhausted pool. Splitting a single synchronous gang across zones also changes its communication paths, so the scheduler must include cross-zone bandwidth, latency, and transfer costs before accepting that placement. The scheduler still must model correlation carefully: independent per-instance interruptions make large simultaneous losses unlikely, but pool-level or AZ-level reclamations can remove hundreds of GPUs at once and must be treated as elastic-shrink or failover events rather than ordinary isolated failures.

Instance type diversification expands the set of capacity pools a job can use, though availability across those pools can remain correlated. A job that requests only one instance type cannot use spare capacity in another pool. Accepting alternatives therefore requires a compatibility list that includes memory capacity, runtime support, communication paths, and measured throughput. An instance with more memory can still be an unsuitable substitute if its runtime support, communication paths, or throughput differ. The scheduler compares each feasible alternative’s price and recovery cost per unit of retained progress; mixing devices within a synchronous gang also requires accounting for unequal worker speeds.

Spot capacity signals can guide placement but do not necessarily predict interruption frequency. For example, the AWS Spot Placement Score estimates the current likelihood that a capacity request will succeed in a region or availability zone; it explicitly does not predict interruption risk. A scheduler can use this signal to choose where to request capacity, but estimating interruptions per day requires separate historical measurements for the instance type and region. If the resulting workload-specific recovery model shows that interruption overhead erases the discount, the scheduler should migrate the job to on-demand capacity or a different region.

Checkpoint frequency optimization for spot instances extends the reliability logic. The Young-Daly formula developed in **[[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/02 Distributed ML/07 Fault Tolerance/V2-07.09 Checkpointing and Progress Preservation#^sec-fault-tolerance-young-daly|The Young-Daly checkpoint interval]]** models losses from unpredicted interruptions, whether caused by hardware failure or reclamation without a usable warning. Provider notices offer an additional opportunity to save state, using the reference windows of 2 minutes on AWS and up to 30 seconds on GCP preemptible VMs. A **two-tier checkpointing** approach combines periodic checkpoints, with an interval chosen for the workload’s interruption model, and a best-effort checkpoint triggered by a notice. The notice-triggered checkpoint reduces lost work only if it becomes durable before reclamation; otherwise, the job restores its last durable state, whether produced by a periodic, asynchronous, or incremental checkpoint, and replays subsequent work. That contingency planning matters more when spot interruptions are correlated across a pool rather than isolated to single machines.

**Systems Perspective 8.3: Correlated spot risk**

**Context**: Spot capacity is cheap because it is reclaimable. That risk is not independent across instances: cloud regions, instance types, product launches, and deadline cycles can create correlated demand.

**Failure mode**: A fleet that concentrates all interruptible training in one region and one instance type can lose a large fraction of workers at once, even if each individual worker appears restartable.

**Consequence**: Checkpointing handles isolated interruptions; it does not solve fleet-wide preemption when every retry targets the same exhausted pool.

**Systems insight**: A robust scheduler diversifies compatible restart destinations, monitors interruption risk, and plans an on-demand fallback when spot capacity cannot meet the job’s deadline or cost target. That fallback still depends on available capacity and the time needed to restore state.

### Capacity Reservation Strategies

^sec-fleet-orchestration-capacity-reservation

Committing to long-term reserved capacity for 100 percent of peak demand leaves expensive GPUs idle whenever traffic dips, while relying solely on spot or on-demand pools risks capacity shortages during product launches and sudden reclamations during deadline crunches. To avoid paying for idle reservations or starving urgent workloads, organizations that rely on cloud infrastructure (or that price on-premises capacity in economic terms) combine three tiers of compute capacity. Each tier trades cost and commitment against availability and interruption risk ([[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/02 Distributed ML/08 Fleet Orchestration/V2-08.09 Cost Optimization#^tbl-cloud-capacity-reservation-tiers|table 4]]).

| **Capacity tier**      | **Cost and commitment**                                                       | **Availability and interruption risk**                                       | **Best-fit workloads**                                                                                                                       |
|:-----------------------|:------------------------------------------------------------------------------|:-----------------------------------------------------------------------------|:---------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------|
| **Reserved capacity**  | Capacity reservation; price discounts require a separate purchasing contract. | Capacity assurance within the reservation terms; startup still takes time.   | Predictable continuous workloads: 24/7 inference, scheduled training, CI and regression testing, jobs under service-level agreements (SLAs). |
| **On-demand capacity** | Full price with no long-term commitment.                                      | Admission depends on available capacity; provisioning and startup add delay. | Urgent retraining, interactive debugging, and short-lived jobs that do not justify spot management.                                          |
| **Spot capacity**      | Discounted interruptible capacity.                                            | Availability varies with cloud demand, and workers can be reclaimed.         | Restart-tolerant jobs: hyperparameter sweeps, ablations, checkpointed pretraining, and exploratory research.                                 |

Table 4: **Cloud Capacity Reservation Tiers**: Capacity tiers trade cost and commitment against availability guarantees and interruption risk, giving the scheduler a price ladder for routing workloads by urgency and restart tolerance.

^tbl-cloud-capacity-reservation-tiers

The constructed portfolio in [[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/02 Distributed ML/08 Fleet Orchestration/V2-08.09 Cost Optimization#^fig-fleet-orchestration-capacity-reservation|figure 14]] divides capacity into a reserved tier covering the steady 60 percent baseline, an on-demand band between 60 and 80 percent, and an interruptible spot tier serving peaks up to 92 percent. An organization with 60 percent steady-state utilization and 40 percent burst demand might reserve capacity for 60 percent of peak need, use on-demand for bursts whose deadlines tolerate admission and startup delay, and use spot for restart-tolerant work. These shares illustrate a policy rather than establish an optimal portfolio. The scheduler must match each workload’s deadline and recovery contract to the capacity tier. Reserved capacity can support the steady serving load or a training job’s base allocation, while spot can support elastic replicas or independent experiments that tolerate reclamation. A peak in urgent demand cannot automatically be assigned to spot merely because it lies above the reserved band.

![[90 Assets/Volume II/08 Fleet Orchestration/Capacity Reservation 64580c13df.svg]]

Figure 14: **Capacity Reservation Tiers and Portfolio Optimization**: This constructed portfolio combines reserved physical capacity, on-demand acquisitions, and interruptible workers. Reserved capacity covers the steady 60 percent base, on-demand the next 20 percent, and spot the peak; these shares and the demand curve are scenario assumptions. A discount commitment alone does not reserve capacity, and on-demand admission is not instantaneous. Schedulers map steady-state baseline demand to reserved capacity, diurnal spikes to on-demand instances, and restart-tolerant training to discounted spot pools. Hatching marks reserved capacity that is held while demand sits below it.

**Systems Perspective 8.4: Spot vs. on-demand training**

Consider training a 70B parameter model, a smaller workload than the chapter’s 175B reference, on 512 GPUs for 14 days. The scenario fixes the event budget at 196 interruptions over the run and treats each as graceful, so no progress is replayed.

**On-Demand**: 512 GPUs $\times$ 336 hours $\times$ \$2/GPU-hour = \$344,064

**Spot** (65 percent discount, 5 percent checkpoint overhead, 14 interruptions per day of useful training with 30 minutes restart):

- Base cost: 512 GPUs $\times$ 336 hours $\times$ \$0.70/GPU-hour = \$120,422
- Checkpoint overhead: $5\%$ $\times$ 336 hours = 16.8 hours
- Restart overhead: 196 interruptions $\times$ 0.5 hours = 98 hours
- Total time: 336 hours + 16.8 hours + 98 hours = 450.8 hours
- Total cost: 512 GPUs $\times$ 450.8 hours $\times$ \$0.70/GPU-hour = \$161,567

**Savings**: \$182,497 (53.0 percent), at the cost of approximately 34.2 percent longer wall-clock time.

These savings assume fault tolerance infrastructure is already in place. Without checkpointing, a single interruption forces restart from scratch, potentially wasting days of compute and negating all savings.

### Cost-Aware Scheduling Policies

^sec-fleet-orchestration-cost-scheduling

Once a fleet spans reserved, on-demand, and spot tiers, a newly submitted job can often launch immediately on reserved A100 GPUs or wait two hours for spot H100 instances that, under illustrative assumptions, deliver twice the throughput at half the execution cost. Deciding whether those savings repay the two-hour delay and interruption risk turns the purchasing tiers into an online scheduling decision. Alongside completion time, utilization, and fairness, a cost-aware orchestrator evaluates three recurring trade-offs: waiting versus running immediately, preempting lower-priority work on reserved capacity (such as a hyperparameter sweep) versus routing a high-priority training run to more expensive on-demand instances, and replacing reclaimed spot instances with on-demand nodes versus shrinking elastically. Each choice requires the costs of waiting, recovery, and displaced work as well as the price of the new allocation.

These decisions require the scheduler to model both the time-value of computation (the organizational cost of a 2-hour delay in researcher productivity, product launch schedules, or competitive position) and the financial cost of different resource allocations. Delaying a production model retrain by 2 hours might violate a service level agreement (SLA), while the same delay to an exploratory experiment can postpone a research decision or block dependent work. A workload class’s delay tolerance is therefore a policy input grounded in its dependencies and deadlines, not an assumption that exploratory work has no time cost.

Production systems can define **cost policies** per workload class that encode these trade-offs as configuration rather than requiring per-decision human judgment. One policy places steady inference demand on reserved capacity and maintains ready replicas to meet its latency target; the reservation alone does not guarantee application availability or latency. Large training runs can combine a reserved minimum viable allocation with interruptible elastic expansion, requesting on-demand replacements when a deadline justifies the cost and capacity is available. Exploratory workloads with flexible deadlines can use spot capacity while accepting interruptions and variable completion times. These assignments express the organization’s priorities rather than a universal mapping from workload type to purchasing tier.

The scheduler does not implement the organization’s financial accounting system. It consumes the resulting price and policy signals: the marginal price of each capacity tier, the reservation a project may claim, the interruption risk it may accept, and the deadline it must meet. That accounting system reports or bills each team for its usage (showback or chargeback) and attributes shared costs; the scheduler uses its outputs only to rank feasible scheduling actions. Lifecycle total cost of ownership (TCO) reaches the scheduler the same way, as per-resource opportunity costs and constraints that record whether capacity is owned, reserved, on demand, or interruptible, rather than as a scheduling mechanism.

**Checkpoint 8.2: Cost-aware scheduling trade-offs**

Before moving to ML-specific schedulers, review how the cost-optimization mechanisms interact:

**Spot break-even**: identify the additional inputs needed to compute a break-even checkpoint interval for the 512-GPU, 14-day training job of the spot comparison above (65 percent spot discount, 14 interruptions per day, 30-minute restart overhead), including checkpoint duration, the interruption-rate clock, and lost-progress assumptions.

**Double return**: explain why fault tolerance infrastructure has a “double return” for reliability and cost savings, and identify the minimum fault tolerance investment needed to make spot instances viable.

**Reservation sizing**: starting from the constructed portfolio that reserves 60 percent of peak, identify the demand history, reservation terms, unit prices, and unused-capacity costs needed to decide whether raising the reserved share to 70 percent reduces total spending.

Spot pricing and reservation strategy reduce the cost of capacity, but they do not determine which competing job should receive it. Runtime uncertainty, unequal completion delays, and the cost of interrupting an iteration remain even when prices are known. Resolving them requires signals from the workload itself. A scheduler that tracks learning progress can optimize time-to-accuracy, the wall-clock time until a run reaches a target validation metric, rather than execution speed alone.
