# V2-08.13 Debugging Cluster Utilization

[[Home|Home]] · [[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/Volume 2 Overview|Volume Overview]] · [[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/02 Distributed ML/08 Fleet Orchestration/V2-08 Fleet Orchestration|V2-08 Fleet Orchestration]]

^sec-fleet-orchestration-debugging

A cluster dashboard shows average GPU utilization stuck at 60 percent, yet developers complain about three-day queue times for small debugging jobs. Hundreds of GPUs sit idle, but no jobs are starting. Explaining the gap requires tracing the interactions among hardware topology, scheduler policies, and user behavior.

Part of the paradox is the queueing arithmetic of notebook [8.1](https://mlsysbook.ai/vol2/08_fleet_orchestration/08_fleet_orchestration.html#nbk-fleet-orchestration-queuing-theory-gpu-clusters). The ${\rho_{\text{serv}}}/(1-{\rho_{\text{serv}}})$ factor grows without bound as offered load approaches service capacity, so mean wait rises gently through moderate load and then steeply above 80 percent for small, mixed, and large workloads alike ([[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/02 Distributed ML/08 Fleet Orchestration/V2-08.13 Debugging Cluster Utilization#^fig-utilization-paradox|figure 16]], plotted on a logarithmic scale). The three curves differ only in mean job duration, as their labels show, and all use the notebook’s exponential reference case (${c_{s}}= 1$), so they are the gentlest version of the effect. A mixed ML queue with the notebook’s representative ${c_{s}}= 3$ multiplies every curve by the larger variability factor, shifting each one upward without changing where it bends. The curves also describe offered service load rather than allocated or kernel-active GPU percentages, and their 75 percent headroom marker is an illustrative choice rather than a universal cluster threshold. Queueing explains why a busy fleet waits. A fleet whose queue is full while devices sit idle is waiting for a different reason, and finding it requires looking past the dashboard.

![[90 Assets/Volume II/08 Fleet Orchestration/Cell 35 Output 1 9ec082a311.png]]

Figure 16: **The Utilization Paradox**: As cluster utilization approaches saturation (100 percent), job wait times increase hyperbolically and diverge near saturation, not linearly. The shaded region above 80 percent and the dashed 75 percent target illustrate headroom choices in this toy queue model, not measured GPU-utilization thresholds. Real queue delay also depends on job sizes, gang constraints, and arrival variability.

### Allocated, Active, and Productive Utilization

^sec-fleet-orchestration-resource-accounting

The 64 GPUs that sat idle for a weekend because a researcher forgot to cancel a reservation represent invisible waste in a multi-tenant cloud fleet. In a dedicated cluster, such waste is at least visible to the team that owns the hardware. In a shared environment, the cost is hidden and compounds across thousands of jobs. Effective governance requires moving beyond a single utilization number to a tiered accounting model that distinguishes between what was allocated, what was used, and what was useful.

Three tiers of measurement expose this gap between allocated hardware and actual progress. Allocated capacity counts resources held by complete allocations, so devices assigned to a gang that still waits for its remaining members do not count. A partial gang can run no step, so those held devices are a scheduling loss below the allocated tier rather than allocated capacity. Active utilization measures hardware activity under a stated counter definition, which can include communication kernels or work later replayed. Productive utilization requires a workload-level measure of retained training progress or serving work that meets its objective, normalized to the job’s own fault-free step rate. For example, a team allocated 500 GPUs might average 350 active-device equivalents (70 percent activity) while delivering 280 device-equivalents of useful work at its fault-free rate (56 percent of its allocation). These are time-averaged equivalents, not necessarily distinct sets of active and productive devices. The gap motivates profiling; it does not identify its cause or imply that necessary communication and recovery can all be eliminated. The allocated and productive tiers also serve as the boundaries of two goodput factors that **[[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/03 The Fleet in Production/09 Fleet Efficiency/V2-09.03 ML Goodput#^sec-performance-engineering-goodput-boundaries|Accounting boundaries]]** measures. Scheduling goodput is the share of offered capacity held by complete allocations, and runtime goodput is the share of allocated time that becomes retained progress.

*Allocated, active, and productive capacity in GPU equivalents, each tier counting less than the one above.*

This data feeds the utilization dashboard, the cluster operator’s primary instrument for fleet health. This view must synthesize per-team utilization against quotas, queue depth by priority class, and the **fragmentation index**, a measure of free GPUs that cannot be allocated due to topology constraints. It must also track preemption rates, spot interruptions, checkpoint traffic, and the recovery overhead they induce. These measurements let the scheduler distinguish idle capacity it can reclaim from allocated capacity whose apparent idleness reflects communication, I/O, or a rigid gang dependency.

Automated anomaly detection turns these metrics into investigation triggers. A sudden spike in a team’s consumption to 3$\times$ its historical norm might reflect a runaway submission script or an intentional experiment. A growing queue can indicate insufficient capacity, but it can also reflect quota limits, fragmented placements, or a stalled dependency. A sudden power drop can accompany a shared storage stall, though job completions and changes in workload mix can produce a similar signal. Operators must correlate these alerts with admission decisions and job progress before changing capacity or policy.

**Systems Perspective 8.5: The three utilization metrics**

Each tier narrows the investigation. Low allocated utilization calls for checking demand, quotas, device health, and placement feasibility. Low active utilization on allocated devices calls for tracing input supply, communication waits, and runtime execution. Low productive utilization despite busy kernels calls for comparing retained progress with replay, synchronization, and other overhead. The metric selects the next evidence to collect; it does not by itself select the remedy.

### Policy-Infrastructure Mismatch

^sec-fleet-orchestration-policy-infrastructure-mismatch

When a cluster adds a new generation of high-memory GPUs alongside older pools, job templates written for the newest hardware can leave the older pools sitting idle while dozens of jobs queue behind the newest partition. Diagnosing this **policy-infrastructure mismatch** requires comparing scheduler state with physical capability and inspecting job requests against each hardware pool. A full queue and idle accelerators can coexist when the scheduler is honoring constraints that the top-level dashboard has aggregated away. The cluster at the center of example [[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/02 Distributed ML/08 Fleet Orchestration/V2-08.13 Debugging Cluster Utilization#^exmp-fleet-orchestration-debugging-low-gpu-utilization|8.2]] runs the heterogeneous hardware in [[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/02 Distributed ML/08 Fleet Orchestration/V2-08.13 Debugging Cluster Utilization#^tbl-fleet-orchestration-debug-inventory|table 8]], accumulated across three procurement cycles.

|:---------------|:---------:|:-----------:|:---------------------:|:------------------------:|
| **A100-80 GB** | 400 GPUs  | 80 GB HBM2e |   NVLink (600 GB/s)   | 50 nodes $\times$ 8 GPUs |
| **A100-40 GB** | 424 GPUs  | 40 GB HBM2e |   NVLink (600 GB/s)   | 53 nodes $\times$ 8 GPUs |
| **V100-32 GB** | 200 GPUs  | 32 GB HBM2  | PCIe Gen3 (15.8 GB/s) | 50 nodes $\times$ 4 GPUs |

**Example 8.2: Debugging low GPU utilization**

**Scenario**: A 1,024-GPU cluster reports 60 percent average utilization despite over 50 pending jobs in queue.

**Diagnosis**: Over-specification (users copying A100-80 GB templates for 35 GB memory jobs), pool fragmentation, and legacy V100 avoidance leave A100-40 GB and V100 nodes idle while large jobs wait for A100-80 GB allocations. The scheduler correctly enforced policies written for a homogeneous fleet that no longer matched the heterogeneous infrastructure.

**Systems lesson**: Surface-level diagnosis often mistakes policy-infrastructure mismatches for scheduling algorithm bugs. Updating scheduler policies to support automatic capability matching, dynamic memory profiling, and backfilling raises cluster utilization to 84 percent, recovering \$7M in annual compute capacity. [[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/02 Distributed ML/08 Fleet Orchestration/V2-08.13 Debugging Cluster Utilization#^tbl-fleet-orchestration-debug-results|Table 9]] breaks that gain out pool by pool, using the allocated and active tiers of [[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/02 Distributed ML/08 Fleet Orchestration/V2-08.13 Debugging Cluster Utilization#^sec-fleet-orchestration-resource-accounting|section 8.12.1]].

| **Pool**         |        **Before**         |         **After**         | **Change** |
|:-----------------|:-------------------------:|:-------------------------:|:----------:|
| **A100-80 GB**   | 95% allocated, 72% active | 89% allocated, 94% active |   +22 pp   |
| **A100-40 GB**   |       64% allocated       |       88% allocated       |   +24 pp   |

Pool rows report allocated utilization, and for the A100-80 GB pool also the active share of its allocated GPUs; the A100-80 GB change is in that active share, the others in allocation. The cluster-wide row is the fleet’s separately observed average utilization, so the pool rows are diagnostic signals that locate the loss rather than terms that add up to the total.

^tbl-fleet-orchestration-debug-results

For this capacity valuation, assume that each active GPU-hour delivers the same retained useful work before and after the policy change, including across hardware pools. Under that simplifying assumption, the observed utilization gain is equivalent to approximately 246 fully active GPUs of productive work ([[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/02 Distributed ML/08 Fleet Orchestration/V2-08.13 Debugging Cluster Utilization#^tbl-fleet-orchestration-debug-results|table 9]]). A heterogeneous production fleet must validate that conversion against workload progress and measured throughput in each pool. As at the start of the chapter, procurement planning compares the gain with the hardware it replaces, here 410 additional raw GPUs, because new devices under the old policies would also run at only 60 percent utilization. At \$2/GPU-hour, that avoided purchase represents approximately \$7M in annual recovered value, achieved through policy changes requiring zero additional hardware investment.

### Common Utilization Anti-Patterns

^sec-fleet-orchestration-anti-patterns

Fixing policy-infrastructure mismatches gets queued jobs onto GPUs, but a dashboard reporting 85 percent allocation still says only how much capacity is assigned, not whether those allocations deliver productive work. Inside allocated capacity, four runtime patterns commonly widen the gap between the allocated, active, and productive tiers, and separating them requires correlating device telemetry with execution timelines.

A zombie job retains an allocation after its useful execution has stopped, so its devices stay in the allocated tier while contributing nothing to the productive one, and they may or may not register as active. In a distributed run training a 175B parameter model, a rank failure can leave a 128-GPU group idle if worker cleanup or failure propagation does not complete. The surviving ranks may block inside a collective that will never finish, or a process left behind by a driver-level fault may resist reaping by the container runtime, so the devices stay allocated until a timeout or node-level remediation clears them. Persistent memory allocation combined with low SM activity is an investigation signal, not proof of a driver fault; valid input or communication waits can look similar. The operator must compare runtime health, step progress, and the workload’s timeout contract, then verify that old workers have stopped before releasing or reassigning their devices.

The data starvation pattern lowers the active tier. It manifests as a high-frequency sawtooth wave in GPU utilization, where the device oscillates between 100 percent load (forward/backward pass) and 0 percent load (waiting for the next batch). While the average utilization might appear acceptable at 60 to 70 percent, the GPU is effectively idling for a third of its life. This occurs when the CPU-bound data pipeline cannot sustain the throughput required by the GPU, a common scenario in computer vision where JPEG decoding and augmentation saturate host CPUs. The data stall ratio of **[[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/01 The Fleet/04 Data Storage/V2-04.05 The Data Pipeline Equation#^eq-data-stall|equation 4.4]]** quantifies the loss as the fraction of each step spent waiting for input. A timeline that aligns the idle intervals with input waits supports the diagnosis; utilization variance alone does not identify the cause. The remedy depends on whether decoding, storage, or transfer limits the input path.

A communication bottleneck can leave ranks waiting for collectives, which lowers the active tier, though communication kernels can also keep SM activity high and mask the wait in that tier. Per-rank gradient traffic depends on gradient precision, sharding, and the parallelism layout, so the ratio of step time to compute-only time (forward plus backward passes in isolation) is a quicker first bound on exposed overhead. If total iteration time exceeds 1.5$\times$ compute-only time, non-compute overhead exceeds 0.5$\times$ compute time, which is material but not dominant. Setting ${T_{\text{sync}}}({N})$ and ${T_{\text{overlap}}}$ to zero in the Fleet Law (**[[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/00 Introduction/01 Introduction/V2-01.06 The C³ Taxonomy and Foundations of Scale#^eq-fleet-law|equation 1.3]]**) leaves ${T_{\text{step}}}({N}) = {T_{\text{compute}}}/{N}+ {T_{\text{comm}}}({N})$, under which communication exceeds per-device computation only when the ratio is greater than 2$\times$. Real steps also contain synchronization waits and input stalls, so profiling must separate communication from those before the ratio is read as a communication diagnosis. Placement can reduce exposed communication when it improves the relevant paths. Gradient accumulation can reduce synchronization frequency only when the runtime defers gradient exchange across accumulation steps.

On the serving side, memory fragmentation can prevent a replica from admitting new requests even when its HBM occupancy suggests free capacity, which lowers the productive tier without necessarily showing in the active one. Its signal is a replica that holds fewer concurrent requests than its occupancy predicts. The KV-cache allocator, rather than the aggregate occupancy percentage, decides whether a new sequence fits in contiguous or paged memory blocks. For the fleet scheduler, the allocator matters through its result, the number of concurrent requests a replica can hold, because that measured capacity sets how many replicas the serving envelope needs.

↪ Paged KV-cache allocation and its effect on replica concurrency are developed in **[[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/03 The Fleet in Production/10 Serving at Scale/V2-10.08 Memory and Decode-Time Management#^sec-inference-scale-pagedattention-3c94|PagedAttention]]**.

The debugging example connects scheduling mechanics to fleet governance. Technical policies (GPU type matching, gang scheduling timeouts, preemption grace periods) interact with organizational policies (team quotas, priority hierarchies, cost allocation) and human behavior (job template reuse, resource request habits, queue submission patterns). Effective fleet orchestration requires attending to all three layers simultaneously, because the most expensive scheduling bug is often not a software defect but a policy that was correct for the old infrastructure and was never updated for the new one.

**Self-Check: Question**

1.  A cluster dashboard reports $85\%$ GPU utilization, yet researchers see slow progress. Which distinction between utilization tiers explains the contradiction?

    1.  Allocated capacity can be high while active utilization is low because jobs wait on input or communication, and productive utilization can be lower still because of restarts and replayed work.
    2.  The dashboard counts only CPU time, so GPU activity is invisible to it.
    3.  High utilization always means the cluster is healthy, so the researchers’ perception must be wrong.
    4.  Productive utilization always exceeds allocated utilization, so the dashboard understates the cluster’s progress.

2.  An administrator observes that average GPU compute utilization across a 1,000-GPU cluster is only $55\%$, yet users experience multi-day queue wait times for 64-GPU jobs. Which diagnostic finding points to the primary root cause?

    1.  Demand exceeds the cluster’s capacity, so the queue can shrink only after more GPUs are purchased.
    2.  Severe resource fragmentation and strict gang scheduling constraints prevent 64-GPU contiguous blocks from forming, while short jobs hold scattered GPUs across nodes without filling them.
    3.  At 55 percent load, the queueing factor ${\rho_{\text{serv}}}/(1-{\rho_{\text{serv}}})$ alone already makes multi-day waits unavoidable.
    4.  Input-pipeline stalls lower SM activity on running jobs, and low SM activity is what keeps the scheduler from admitting the 64-GPU jobs.

3.  During a multi-GPU training job, GPU SM utilization oscillates between 10 percent and 95 percent, host CPUs are busy, and disk wait is high. What hypothesis should the operator test next before choosing a remedy?

    1.  A zombie allocation; the low phases show that the job has stopped making progress, so it should be terminated.
    2.  A communication bottleneck; the ranks are waiting on collectives, so the job should be moved under a single leaf switch.
    3.  The input pipeline may be limiting progress; align GPU idle intervals with batch-readiness waits, then measure whether decoding, storage, or transfer is the limiting stage.
    4.  KV-cache memory fragmentation; the allocator cannot fit the next batch even though HBM occupancy shows free capacity.

4.  What is a ‘zombie allocation’ (or ghost job) in a GPU cluster, and how can automated health monitors detect and remediate it?

5.  True or False: In a ring AllReduce across 32 nodes, if 31 nodes show $95\%$ GPU utilization and 1 node shows $10\%$ utilization, the node with $10\%$ utilization is the only healthy node while the 31 nodes are encountering network hardware faults.

6.  A dashboard shows 120 free GPUs, yet a 64-GPU gang waits for days because the free devices sit one or two per node across racks that its topology constraint cannot combine. A \_\_\_\_ index, which counts free GPUs that cannot be allocated because of topology constraints, makes this stranded capacity visible where aggregate utilization hides it.
