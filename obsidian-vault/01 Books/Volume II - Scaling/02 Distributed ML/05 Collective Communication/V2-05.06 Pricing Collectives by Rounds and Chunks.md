# V2-05.06 Pricing Collectives by Rounds and Chunks

[[Home|Home]] · [[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/Volume 2 Overview|Volume Overview]] · [[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/02 Distributed ML/05 Collective Communication/V2-05 Collective Communication|V2-05 Collective Communication]]

^sec-communication-collective-operations-collective-operations-network-performance-modeling-0d8e

A gradient bucket, the group of gradient tensors reduced in one call, can reach tens of megabytes, large enough to saturate a single network link. Yet when a thousand-GPU ring divides that bucket into a thousand shards, each hop moves only tens of kilobytes and can spend more time starting the transfer than sending its bytes. Pricing a collective therefore requires separating the fixed time to initiate each message from the rate at which bytes move once in transit.

The ${\alpha}$-${\beta}$ model ${T}({n}) = {\alpha}+ {n}/{\beta}$ prices a transfer over a single link (principle [[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/01 The Fleet/V2 Fleet Principles#^pri-alpha-beta-model|5]]), separating a fixed startup cost (${\alpha}$) from a per-byte transfer cost (${n}/{\beta}$) ([[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/02 Distributed ML/05 Collective Communication/V2-05.16 Source References#^ref-hockney1994|Hockney 1994]]). A collective pays that price at every step of its schedule, so the two terms, multiplied by the schedule’s round count and traffic factor (the multiple of the tensor each rank sends), determine algorithm selection across the fleet.

↩︎ The ${\alpha}$-${\beta}$ model for a single link and its latency-dominated and bandwidth-dominated regimes are established in **[[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/01 The Fleet/03 Network Fabrics/V2-03.05 Level 2 Transport and the Performance Model#^sec-network-fabrics-performance-model|The α-β performance model]]**.

↪ Worked message-size regimes and crossover calculations for this model are developed in **[[05 Reference/Volume II - Scaling/V2 Communication Foundations#^sec-appdx-comm-foundations-alpha-beta|The α-β Communication Model]]**.

For collectives, the parameters must describe the effective path. Latency (${\alpha}$) is the startup cost paid at every step of a schedule, so it includes recurring software overhead, PCIe traversal, and network switching time; one-time communicator initialization is a separate setup cost. Because the ranks in a step send concurrently, elapsed time counts one startup per step rather than one per rank. Bandwidth (${\beta}$) is the sustained per-direction transfer rate. The critical message size ${n^*} = {\alpha}\cdot {\beta}$, where the two terms are equal, carries over unchanged. It must, however, be compared against each step’s chunk (${M}/{N}$ in an ${N}$-rank ring) rather than the whole tensor ${M}$. That is why a large bucket can still fall below the inter-node crossovers in [[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/02 Distributed ML/05 Collective Communication/V2-05.06 Pricing Collectives by Rounds and Chunks#^tbl-interconnect-parameters|table 4]]. Below ${n^*}$, a step is latency-bound; above it, bandwidth-bound.

The crossover explains two recurring optimizations. For InfiniBand NDR at ${\alpha}\approx$ 1.5 μs and ${\beta}\approx$ 50 GB/s, the knee sits at ${n^*} \approx$ 75 KB, the value **[[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/01 The Fleet/03 Network Fabrics/V2-03.05 Level 2 Transport and the Performance Model#^sec-network-fabrics-performance-model|The α-β performance model]]** computed for a single transfer. A 4 KB token-routing message is far below ${n^*}$, so the startup cost dominates. If 100 ready messages have compatible destinations and can share one transfer, fusing them into a 400 KB message reduces the modeled cost from approximately $100{\alpha}=$ 150 μs to ${\alpha}+ {n}/{\beta}\approx$ 9.5 μs. That is roughly a 16× improvement before packing costs. More generally, ${N_{\text{msg}}}$ serialized messages of size ${n}/{N_{\text{msg}}}$ can cost up to ${N_{\text{msg}}}\times$ as much as one message of size ${n}$ when ${n}/{N_{\text{msg}}}\ll {n^*}$. Fusion is the first optimization, and it motivates framework gradient buckets and destination-aware token coalescing.

Compression is the second, and the crossover tells where it pays. A 140 GB gradient tensor sits far above ${n^*}$, so shrinking its bytes shrinks the dominant ${n}/{\beta}$ term directly, provided encoding and aggregation costs are counted. Even keeping only the largest 1 percent of values (Top-k sparsification, developed in [[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/02 Distributed ML/05 Collective Communication/V2-05.10 Gradient Compression Under Bandwidth Scarcity#^sec-communication-collective-operations-collective-operations-gradient-compression-7a5c|section 5.9]]) leaves 1.4 GB of values before their indices are added, still far above ${n^*}$, so the saving remains a byte saving. Compressing a 4 KB token-routing message, by contrast, offers little, because its unchanged ${\alpha}$ term already dominates.

Across the typical data center interconnects tabulated in [[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/02 Distributed ML/05 Collective Communication/V2-05.06 Pricing Collectives by Rounds and Chunks#^tbl-interconnect-parameters|table 4]], intra-node NVLink stays latency-bound up to several hundred kilobytes, whereas the inter-node fabrics cross over below roughly 100 KB. A message between those crossover sizes can therefore be latency-bound inside a node but bandwidth-bound once it travels between nodes.

| **Interconnect**                        | **Latency (${\alpha}$)** | **Bandwidth (${\beta}$)** | **Critical size (${n^*}$)** |
|:----------------------------------------|:------------------------:|:-------------------------:|:---------------------------:|
| **NVLink 4.0 (intra-node)**             |          1–2 μs          |         450 GB/s          |           ~0.7 MB           |
| **InfiniBand NDR 400 Gbps**             |          1–3 μs          |    50 GB/s (per port)     |           ~75 KB            |
| **InfiniBand HDR 200 Gbps**             |          2–5 μs          |          25 GB/s          |          ~87.5 KB           |
| **PCIe Gen5 (GPU$\leftrightarrow$CPU)** |          2–5 μs          |          64 GB/s          |           ~224 KB           |
| **Ethernet 100 Gbps (RoCE)**            |         5–10 μs          |         12.5 GB/s         |          ~93.7 KB           |

Table 4: **Interconnect Performance Parameters**: Effective per-message launch latency (${\alpha}$, including the software stack), per-direction link bandwidth (${\beta}$, the one-way deliverable rate that enters ${T}({n}) = {\alpha}+ {n}/{\beta}$; NVLink’s datasheet 900 GB/s is the bidirectional total, so its ${\beta}$ is the 450 GB/s one-way rate), and the computed crossover size ${n^*} = {\alpha}\cdot {\beta}$ at each row’s ${\alpha}$ midpoint (for InfiniBand NDR, at the representative 1.5 μs used throughout this chapter), where communication transitions from latency-bound to bandwidth-bound.

^tbl-interconnect-parameters

The NVLink and NDR rows show the roughly ninefold per-direction cliff of **[[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/01 The Fleet/02 Compute Infrastructure/V2-02.06 The Node#^sec-compute-bandwidth-hierarchy|Bandwidth hierarchy]]**, which **[[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/01 The Fleet/03 Network Fabrics/V2-03.02 The Synchronization Backbone#^sec-network-fabrics-introduction|The Synchronization Backbone]]** traces across a decade of interconnect generations. Even on the lower-crossover inter-node fabric, a bucket far above ${n^*}$ can still travel in chunks below it because a ring divides the bucket among its ${N}$ ranks. Pricing one bucket at two group sizes shows when that happens and what it costs.

**Napkin Math 5.1: The critical message size**

**Problem**: A data-parallel job reduces a 25 MB gradient bucket with Ring AllReduce over InfiniBand NDR, with ${\alpha}=$ 1.5 μs and ${\beta}=$ 50 GB/s. The bucket is hundreds of times larger than the single-transfer knee. Does each step of the ring run in the bandwidth-bound regime on 64 GPUs, and on the 1,024-GPU reference cluster?

**Math**:

1.  **Knee**: ${n^*} = {\alpha}\cdot {\beta}=$ $1.5 \times 10^{-6}$ s $\times$ $5 \times 10^{10}$ B/s $=$ 75 KB.
2.  **Chunk per step**: A ring sends ${M}/{N}$ per step, 390.6 KB on 64 ranks (above the knee) but only 24.4 KB on 1,024 ranks (below it). The ring takes one fewer step than it has ranks in each of its two phases.
3.  **Ring time** $2({N}-1)\left({\alpha}+ \frac{{M}}{{N}{\beta}}\right)$: about 1.2 ms on 64 ranks, of which the byte term is 1.0 ms; about 4.1 ms on 1,024 ranks, where the byte term is still only 1.0 ms.

**Systems insight**: The regime belongs to the chunk, not to the tensor. The byte term barely moves as the group grows, but at 1,024 ranks each step carries a chunk below ${n^*}$, so startup dominates and the same bucket takes several times longer. Large language model (LLM) gradients are bandwidth-bound as tensors, yet a flat ring over a large group can still carry them in latency-bound steps. The levers then shift to larger buckets, fewer rounds, and hierarchy, which is why the algorithms of [[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/02 Distributed ML/05 Collective Communication/V2-05.07 AllReduce Algorithms and Topologies#^sec-communication-collective-operations-collective-operations-allreduce-algorithms-7545|section 5.6]] compete on round count as well as bytes.

For collectives, the two regimes map onto different levers. In the latency-bound regime $({n}< {n^*})$, where fine-grained per-peer token-routing chunks, scalar reductions, and the small chunks of a large-group schedule sit, the levers are fusion, fewer schedule rounds, fewer hops, and a leaner software stack. In the bandwidth-bound regime $({n}\gg {n^*})$, where LLM gradients and optimizer states sit, they are compression, a byte-efficient algorithm, and link aggregation across multiple network interface cards (NICs). A token-routing exchange can fall on either side, because its regime depends on the chunk sent to each peer, which shrinks as the group grows if each rank’s payload stays fixed. Classifying each exchange, and each chunk within it, into one regime is the diagnostic skill the rest of the chapter applies.

**Checkpoint 5.1: Per-chunk pricing diagnostics**

Verify your understanding of per-chunk pricing regimes:

**Chunk regime**: classify each ring step of a 25 MB gradient bucket reduced over 1,024 ranks, on links with ${\alpha}=5\ {\text{µ}}\text{s}$ and ${\beta}=50\ \text{GB/s}$, as latency-bound or bandwidth-bound.

**Software overhead**: determine which type of parallelism benefits more when software overhead (${\alpha}$) is reduced by half, data parallelism or tensor parallelism.

**Chunks under an upgrade**: explain why upgrading links from 100 Gbps to 400 Gbps at constant software latency can move a large ring’s per-step chunks from the bandwidth-bound to the latency-bound side of ${n^*} = {\alpha}\cdot {\beta}$.

**NIC scaling**: assess the True or False claim that, in the bandwidth-bound regime, doubling the number of NICs per node effectively doubles the transmission speed for large tensors.

The pricing model charges a schedule per round and per chunk, but it does not choose the schedule. AllReduce, the most performance-critical primitive, admits several schedules whose round counts and byte volumes differ sharply, and for a large gradient the choice can decide whether gradient synchronization can be hidden at all.

**Self-Check: Question**

1.  A single-transfer model uses effective startup cost $\alpha=1.5\,\mu\text{s}$ and per-direction bandwidth $\beta=50\text{ GB/s}$. Which conclusion follows for a 4 KB transfer, and what must be added before classifying a complete gradient AllReduce?

    1.  The single-transfer knee is 75 KB, so startup dominates the 4 KB transfer. A complete AllReduce additionally requires the schedule’s round count and chunk size, then calibration for its placement.
    2.  The 4 KB transfer is bandwidth-bound because adding ranks makes every individual transfer saturate its port.
    3.  The 75 KB knee is a maximum buffer size, so larger messages cannot be sent over this link.
    4.  The critical message size defines the maximum possible buffer size that can be transferred in a single CUDA stream without host CPU intervention.

2.  Explain why small-message collective timings can exceed a bare-wire prediction by a much larger relative factor than large-message timings. Which measurements are needed before using this difference in a cluster capacity plan?
