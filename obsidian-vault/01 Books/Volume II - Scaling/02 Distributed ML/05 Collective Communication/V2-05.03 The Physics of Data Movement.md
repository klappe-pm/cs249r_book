# V2-05.03 The Physics of Data Movement

[[Home|Home]] · [[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/Volume 2 Overview|Volume Overview]] · [[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/02 Distributed ML/05 Collective Communication/V2-05 Collective Communication|V2-05 Collective Communication]]

^sec-communication-collective-operations-physics-data-movement

Whenever a gradient shard leaves an accelerator, three limits bound its trip before any collective algorithm runs. The speed of light sets a propagation floor[[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/02 Distributed ML/05 Collective Communication/V2-05.17 Footnotes#^fn3|³]] of roughly 5 ${\text{µ}}\text{s}$ per kilometer in fiber. High-frequency signal loss limits how far copper traces can reach before the link must convert to optics (the bandwidth-distance product of **[[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/01 The Fleet/03 Network Fabrics/V2-03.04 Level 1 Wire and Link#^sec-network-fabrics-copper-optics|Reach and medium for copper and optics]]**). Host-kernel networking stacks add tens of microseconds of software copy overhead, unless kernel-bypass transports such as remote direct memory access (RDMA) and GPUDirect RDMA strip per-message initiation down to a few microseconds.

↩︎ Kernel-bypass RDMA, which strips the per-message software overhead, and GPUDirect RDMA, which removes the host-memory bounce, are established in **[[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/01 The Fleet/03 Network Fabrics/V2-03.05 Level 2 Transport and the Performance Model#^sec-network-fabrics-rdma|RDMA and GPUDirect]]**.

These constraints govern different parts of a communication budget. Propagation latency sets a floor for every message regardless of size. Link bandwidth caps throughput once a transfer is large enough to fill the pipe. Protocol overhead adds a fixed per-message cost that penalizes fine-grained exchanges unless a kernel-bypass transport removes it. RDMA’s zero-copy path[[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/02 Distributed ML/05 Collective Communication/V2-05.17 Footnotes#^fn4|⁴]] avoids staging each transfer through host memory and the kernel’s socket buffers, keeping that per-message cost low. Because the physical fabric and its software stack fix these limits, the collective schedule determines how many times the job pays them.

Collective communication also pays an energy price for every bit moved across those links, which the schedule must respect as firmly as latency and bandwidth. Moving data consumes energy across storage arrays, interfaces, serializers, transceivers, and switches. A transfer that starts in local SRAM can stay on-package in high-bandwidth memory (HBM), cross to an intra-node peer GPU, or traverse intermediate switches to a remote node. Each step widens the physical accounting boundary that an energy estimate must include ([[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/02 Distributed ML/05 Collective Communication/V2-05.03 The Physics of Data Movement#^fig-data-movement-hierarchy|figure 1]]). Each boundary activates distinct physical components: on-package links, intra-node crossbars, serializers, optical transceivers, and switch fabrics. Distance alone does not determine energy per bit, and an endpoint-only estimate cannot be compared directly with a complete fabric-path estimate. Locality remains useful because it can avoid transfers and active interfaces; the energy saving must be evaluated under the same workload, utilization, and accounting boundary.

Figure 1: **Data-Movement Accounting Boundaries**: Successively broader paths involve different storage and communication components. The schematic identifies what an energy estimate must include; it assigns no universal distance law or fixed energy multiplier. Compare energy per transferred byte only after specifying endpoints, interfaces, switches, and utilization.

^fig-data-movement-hierarchy

The chapter’s reference gradient buffer of 1 GB stands for one large reduction, far smaller than a full model gradient but large enough that per-byte transfer, not per-message startup, governs its cost; later examples return to it by name. Reducing it around an illustrative 10,000-rank ring sends approximately twice the payload from every rank. At a fabric-path transfer cost of 35 pJ/bit, a representative midpoint of the range typical of switched fabrics rather than a measurement of any particular one, this gives about 5.6 kJ per reduction summed over all the ranks, paid again at every synchronous step. The usable rule is that communication energy is the bytes an algorithm sends multiplied by the energy per bit of every boundary those bytes cross. A byte that leaves the package or the node crosses boundaries an on-chip operand does not, which can raise its energy by orders of magnitude. An algorithm that moves fewer bytes, or a placement that keeps more of them inside the node, therefore saves energy as directly as it saves time.

The share of the step’s total energy that communication represents must be measured at the same accounting boundary as the compute it accompanies. The fabric-path figure charges the serializers, transceivers, and switches between two endpoints. A whole-system estimate that also charges endpoint memory reads, host software, and idle network power assigns each byte crossing the network a much larger cost, so two per-byte figures can be compared only after their boundaries match.

↪ The energy cost of moving a byte across memory, storage, and network boundaries, and why movement can outweigh arithmetic, is developed in **[[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/04 The Responsible Fleet/15 Sustainable AI/V2-15.05 Energy Measurement and Modeling#^sec-sustainable-ai-energy-per-byte-5d87-5d87|Energy per byte]]**.

Across this section, propagation delay, link bandwidth, per-message software overhead, and energy per bit have bounded single transfers in isolation. Which collective operations a training step actually invokes, and how often they pay those limits, depends on how the workload partitions its parameters, activations, and tokens across devices.

1.  On the same fabric, one collective exchanges thousands of 4 KB messages per step while another moves a single 1 GB fused buffer. Which term governs each cost, and which lever addresses it?

    1.  Both are bandwidth-bound, so a faster link shortens both by the same factor.
    2.  The small messages pay per-message latency and protocol overhead on every send, so fusing messages or using a kernel-bypass transport helps; the 1 GB buffer is governed by per-byte transfer, so moving fewer bytes or adding bandwidth helps.
    3.  The small messages are bandwidth-bound because there are many of them, while the 1 GB buffer is latency-bound because a large transfer takes the longest path.
    4.  Both are latency-bound, because the speed of light sets a floor on every message regardless of its size.
