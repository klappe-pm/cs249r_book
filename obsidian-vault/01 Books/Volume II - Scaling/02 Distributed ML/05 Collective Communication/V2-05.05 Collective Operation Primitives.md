# V2-05.05 Collective Operation Primitives

[[Home|Home]] · [[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/Volume 2 Overview|Volume Overview]] · [[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/02 Distributed ML/05 Collective Communication/V2-05 Collective Communication|V2-05 Collective Communication]]

^sec-communication-collective-operations-collective-operations-collective-operation-vocabulary-fdc7

Sending a gradient tensor to a peer does not specify which workers must contribute or where the final result belongs. Collective operations supply that group-level contract, using a vocabulary standardized by MPI and inherited by modern ML communication libraries ([[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/02 Distributed ML/05 Collective Communication/V2-05.16 Source References#^ref-mpiforum2015mpi31|Message Passing Interface Forum 2015]]). The four contracts sketched at the start of the chapter differ in what each rank holds afterward ([[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/02 Distributed ML/05 Collective Communication/V2-05.05 Collective Operation Primitives#^fig-collective-primitives-overview|figure 2]]). AllReduce leaves an identical sum (${\Sigma}$) on every rank. AllGather leaves every rank with the concatenation of all shards. ReduceScatter sums across ranks but leaves each rank one reduced shard (${\Sigma}_i$). AllToAll performs a distributed matrix transpose, routing each rank’s ${j}$-th chunk to rank ${j}$. Two further primitives complete the set and serve as building blocks for these four: Broadcast, in which rank 0 sends its data to all ranks, and Reduce, in which all ranks aggregate into rank 0.

↪ The bandwidth and latency cost terms of AllReduce, AllGather, ReduceScatter, AllToAll, and Broadcast, under ring, tree, and pairwise-exchange algorithms, are developed in **[[05 Reference/Volume II - Scaling/V2 Communication Foundations#^sec-appdx-comm-foundations-collective-complexity|Collective Operation Complexity]]**.

**Definition 5.2: Collective operation**

***Collective operation*** is a distributed communication pattern in which every rank in a process group invokes a matching operation to aggregate, broadcast, or redistribute data. The operation’s contract specifies the output at each rank; it does not require simultaneous entry and does not, by itself, impose a general-purpose barrier before or after the call ([[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/02 Distributed ML/05 Collective Communication/V2-05.16 Source References#^ref-mpiforum2015mpi31|Message Passing Interface Forum 2015]]).

1.  **Significance**: Because the contract names only each rank’s result, the schedule that carries it decides how communication demand spreads as the group grows. A schedule that funnels every contribution through one endpoint makes the communication term ${T_{\text{comm}}}({N})$ of the Fleet Law (**[[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/00 Introduction/01 Introduction/V2-01.06 The C³ Taxonomy and Foundations of Scale#^eq-fleet-law|equation 1.3]]**) grow with ${N}$, while a balanced schedule keeps each rank’s bytes nearly constant; [[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/02 Distributed ML/05 Collective Communication/V2-05.07 AllReduce Algorithms and Topologies#^sec-communication-collective-operations-naive-vs-optimal|section 5.6.1]] quantifies the gap.
2.  **Distinction**: Unlike point-to-point communication, a collective defines one operation over a process group. Ranks may enter at different times. In Broadcast or Reduce, which center on one rank, some algorithms allow a rank to complete before another has entered, whereas an AllReduce result needs every rank’s contribution. Correctness depends on matching the operation sequence and argument contract across ranks, not on barrier semantics.
3.  **Common pitfall**: Treating a non-blocking collective as complete when its initiation call returns. The returned request or stream event represents work in flight. A dependent consumer must wait for completion, and a late rank can extend that dependency even though the collective is not a barrier.

(1) AllReduce: all aggregate and all receive the result. (2) AllGather: all send to all and concatenate. (3) ReduceScatter: all aggregate and results are distributed in shards. (4) AllToAll: every process sends unique data to every other process. The two foundational primitives Broadcast (rank 0 sends to all) and Reduce (all aggregate to rank 0) compose the patterns shown here and are defined in the surrounding prose.

The primitive choice is the first scaling decision because it fixes which ranks must coordinate and which result each must obtain. Once that contract is chosen, the algorithm and placement determine how the resulting traffic crosses the fabric.

### The Six Core Primitives

^sec-collective-communication-six-core-primitives-8a66

Calling a full AllReduce when each rank’s optimizer updates only a $1/{N}$ parameter shard moves twice as many bytes as the step needs, because half the work goes into assembling full tensors that every rank immediately discards. Choosing the right primitive therefore means matching the contract in [[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/02 Distributed ML/05 Collective Communication/V2-05.05 Collective Operation Primitives#^tbl-collective-primitives|table 3]] to the exact slice the next computation consumes.

The table’s rows run from the root-centered patterns (Broadcast and Reduce), through the three in which every rank contributes to and receives part or all of one shared tensor (AllReduce, AllGather, and ReduceScatter), to AllToAll, in which every pair of ranks exchanges distinct data. That order describes routing rather than cost. ReduceScatter and AllGather each move half of AllReduce’s bytes because each is one of the two phases into which AllReduce decomposes.

The cost column previews the algorithms derived in [[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/02 Distributed ML/05 Collective Communication/V2-05.07 AllReduce Algorithms and Topologies#^sec-communication-collective-operations-collective-operations-allreduce-algorithms-7545|section 5.6]], using ${N}$ for the participating rank count, ${M_{\text{coll}}}$ for the full tensor, and ${M}$ for the per-rank AllToAll input payload. For AllReduce, the full tensor has the same byte size as each rank’s input payload, the convention used in the following algorithm models. Two schedules appear there: a ring passes chunks between neighbors, whereas a simple full-message tree reduces up a binary tree and broadcasts back down it, forwarding the whole tensor at each of its $2\log_2 {N}$ steps. For Broadcast and Reduce, the table counts startup rounds and bytes per tree edge separately. Each traverses one tree depth, so the elapsed transfer cost accumulates across its levels. The Tree AllReduce derivation combines those two traversals.

Ring achieves AllReduce’s bandwidth-optimal cost while the simple full-message Tree model trades bandwidth for logarithmic latency ([[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/02 Distributed ML/05 Collective Communication/V2-05.16 Source References#^ref-patarasuk2009|Patarasuk and Yuan 2009]]; [[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/02 Distributed ML/05 Collective Communication/V2-05.16 Source References#^ref-thakur2005|Thakur et al. 2005]]). AllReduce itself also decomposes into ReduceScatter followed by AllGather. The decomposition is operational as well as mathematical, since Ring AllReduce’s first phase is a ReduceScatter and its second phase is an AllGather. ReduceScatter performs arithmetic, so it does not undo an AllGather even though it shrinks buffers where AllGather expands them. Full Zero Redundancy Optimizer (ZeRO) sharding and PyTorch FSDP use the pair the same way, gathering parameter shards with AllGather before a module needs them and reducing gradients with ReduceScatter after backward so each rank retains only its shard for the optimizer step ([[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/02 Distributed ML/05 Collective Communication/V2-05.16 Source References#^ref-rajbhandari2020|Rajbhandari et al. 2020]]). Sharding parameters, gradients, and optimizer state across workers reduces persistent model-state memory roughly by ${N}\times$, while temporary full-parameter buffers and prefetching determine peak memory. That memory saving is developed in **[[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/02 Distributed ML/06 Distributed Training/V2-06.04 Data Parallelism#^sec-distributed-training-systems-systems-memoryefficient-data-parallelism-zero-fsdp-0e69|Memory-efficient data parallelism with ZeRO and FSDP]]**; the communication it adds is priced in [[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/02 Distributed ML/05 Collective Communication/V2-05.05 Collective Operation Primitives#^sec-communication-fsdp-patterns|section 5.4.2]].

The same AllGather/ReduceScatter pair also appears in sequence parallelism, which splits activations along the sequence dimension. There an AllGather reconstructs the activation view needed for a local operation, and a later ReduceScatter redistributes the result along that dimension. A primitive’s semantics stay fixed even when the tensor dimension being sharded changes, so every schedule developed for the pair applies to both uses.

Beyond defining data-movement dependencies, collective operations carry explicit interface and numerical contracts. At the interface level, every rank in a process group must issue collectives in a compatible sequence; mismatched operation orders can deadlock or pair unrelated buffers. Operations also enforce shape and type contracts. AllReduce requires matching element counts and datatypes across all ranks, whereas variable-size exchanges require explicit per-destination counts and displacements. These invariants form the contract between the parallel decomposition and the communication runtime. The model decides which tensor slices each rank owns, and the collective contract guarantees that those slices can be recombined deterministically.

Reduction order introduces a second, numerical contract. Floating-point addition is not associative, so ring, tree, and switch-assisted reductions (in which network switches do the adding, developed in [[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/02 Distributed ML/05 Collective Communication/V2-05.08 Hierarchical Communication#^sec-communication-sharp|section 5.7.3]]) may accumulate the same values in different orders and produce different low-order bits ([[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/02 Distributed ML/05 Collective Communication/V2-05.16 Source References#^ref-goldberg1991|Goldberg 1991]]). A correct AllReduce therefore guarantees the specified reduction over all contributions, not bitwise identity across algorithms, rank counts, or hardware. Reproducibility-sensitive workloads must fix the algorithm and process-group configuration or use a reproducible accumulation scheme; ordinary training should treat small order-dependent differences as numerical behavior while still checking for larger rank disagreement.

AllToAll scales differently from AllReduce with cluster size, and [[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/02 Distributed ML/05 Collective Communication/V2-05.09 AllToAll Exchanges at Scale#^sec-communication-alltoall|section 5.8]] develops the bound that governs it. With the six contracts defined, every row of [[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/02 Distributed ML/05 Collective Communication/V2-05.04 Parallelism Strategies and Their Traffic#^tbl-parallelism-communication-mapping|table 1]] now names a primitive whose output and traffic pattern are precise. The sharded data-parallel row repays closer study, because sharding changes how often those primitives run rather than which ones run.

### FSDP Communication Patterns

^sec-communication-fsdp-patterns

Sharding every parameter, gradient, and optimizer-state tensor across ${N}$ data-parallel ranks saves memory, but it replaces one end-of-step reduction with a stream of collective calls inside the forward and backward passes. Under fully sharded data parallelism ([[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/02 Distributed ML/05 Collective Communication/V2-05.16 Source References#^ref-rajbhandari2020|Rajbhandari et al. 2020]]), each rank holds only $1/{N}$ of a layer’s weights. It must call AllGather before the layer’s forward pass to assemble the full weights. After the backward pass, it calls ReduceScatter to leave each rank the gradient for its own shard, and then frees the temporary full weights.

Trading resident memory for on-demand assembly multiplies the collective call count. With one communication group per layer, a schedule that holds each layer’s gathered weights until its backward pass finishes issues $2{N_L}$ collective calls per step (one forward AllGather and one backward ReduceScatter per layer) where data parallelism can reduce its whole gradient in one AllReduce. To save peak activation memory, full-resharding FSDP instead frees the full weights right after each layer’s forward pass and runs a second AllGather before that layer’s backward pass, raising the count to about $3{N_L}$ calls per step. The byte volume changes far less. Because each AllGather and ReduceScatter over a large group moves nearly ${M_{\text{coll}}}$ bytes per rank across all layers combined, where ${M_{\text{coll}}}$ is the model-sized payload without optimizer state, the $2{N_L}$-call schedule sends roughly the AllReduce’s $2{M_{\text{coll}}}$ per rank and the resharded $3{N_L}$-call schedule sends about $3{M_{\text{coll}}}$ ($1.5\times$) when parameters and gradients travel at the same width. Different parameter and reduction datatypes shift that ratio.

The call count carries its own price, because every call pays a startup cost. An effective startup cost ${\alpha_{\text{eff}}}$ contributes approximately $2{N_L}{\alpha_{\text{eff}}}$ per step before overlap, or $3{N_L}{\alpha_{\text{eff}}}$ with resharding. FSDP is therefore more sensitive to ${\alpha_{\text{eff}}}$ than a single whole-gradient reduction even though its bytes barely change. The counts describe one grouping policy, since FSDP units need not coincide with layers and data parallelism can also split its reduction into several calls to enable overlap. FSDP implementations mitigate the startup cost through prefetching and communication-stream pipelining (issuing collectives on a stream separate from computation), but the relevant ${\alpha_{\text{eff}}}$ must come from a matched collective benchmark rather than a wire-latency specification.

↪ The ZeRO stages and the reshard-after-forward FSDP schedule, with the memory each saves and the communication each adds, are developed in **[[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/02 Distributed ML/06 Distributed Training/V2-06.04 Data Parallelism#^sec-distributed-training-systems-systems-memoryefficient-data-parallelism-zero-fsdp-0e69|Memory-efficient data parallelism with ZeRO and FSDP]]**.

Selecting the right collective algorithm at each invocation requires distinguishing transfer cost from dependency exposure. An FSDP AllGather sits on the critical path, because a layer cannot compute until its full parameters arrive. Its transfer can nevertheless be bandwidth-bound when the parameter tensor is large. Prefetching can hide part of that gather, while a ReduceScatter can overlap with the remaining backward computation. Neither dependency alone selects Ring or Tree; message size, group size, topology, and the available overlap window jointly determine the useful schedule.

The primitives specify what must be communicated, and sharding shows how often a training step invokes them. Choosing a schedule for each invocation first requires a price for every round of an exchange.
