# V2-05.11 Communication-Computation Overlap

[[Home|Home]] · [[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/Volume 2 Overview|Volume Overview]] · [[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/02 Distributed ML/05 Collective Communication/V2-05 Collective Communication|V2-05 Collective Communication]]

^sec-communication-overlap

Even with hierarchical routing and gradient compression, waiting until the entire backward pass finishes before launching AllReduce leaves every GPU idle across the full transfer window before the optimizer can update the weights. **Communication-computation overlap** hides that stall by running transfers concurrently with independent backward-pass computation, attacking the communication time that algorithm selection and compression leave exposed. The time it hides is the overlap term ${T_{\text{overlap}}}$ that the Fleet Law (**[[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/00 Introduction/01 Introduction/V2-01.06 The C³ Taxonomy and Foundations of Scale#^eq-fleet-law|equation 1.3]]**) subtracts from step time. How much of a transfer can be hidden depends on which parts of that transfer occupy the processor and which occupy only the network.

### The LogP Model of What Overlap Can Hide

^sec-communication-collective-operations-collective-operations-logp-model-e45d

The α-β model estimates transfer duration without specifying how much independent computation can proceed during that transfer. Adding the entire estimate to compute time therefore overstates step time when overlap is possible. The **LogP model** ([[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/02 Distributed ML/05 Collective Communication/V2-05.16 Source References#^ref-culler1993|Culler et al. 1993]]) separates network transit from processor involvement with four parameters:

- **${L_{\text{LogGP}}}$ (latency)**: The time for a message to traverse the network. The chapter’s effective startup cost ${\alpha}$ bundles this transit time with the processor overhead defined next.
- **${o}$ (overhead)**: The processor time spent initiating or completing a transfer. During this work, the same execution resource cannot compute, making ${o}$ the nonoverlappable cost. In a distributed training context, dispatch, device-kernel launch, memory registration, and runtime progress can all contribute. The value is an effective parameter of a particular software and hardware path, so it must be measured with the same collective, process group, and topology used by the workload.
- **${g}$ (gap)**: The minimum spacing between consecutive sends or consecutive receives at a processor. It limits endpoint message rate but does not describe every source of link contention in a routed fabric. The overlap analysis that follows needs ${L_{\text{LogGP}}}$ and ${o}$ but not the gap.
- **${N}$ (rank count)**: The number of ranks in the communication group. The original model names this processor count with its final letter, P; this chapter writes it ${N}$ to match its other collective cost formulas.

LogP distinguishes network latency (${L_{\text{LogGP}}}$, which can be hidden) from processor overhead (${o}$, which cannot). Independent computation can overlap the network transit interval, while initiation and completion overhead remain exposed on the execution resource that performs them. The per-byte transfer time of the ${\alpha}$-${\beta}$ model falls on the network side of this split when the network makes progress without the processor.

**Napkin Math 5.8: Hiding communication behind computation**

**Problem**: A training pipeline attempts to overlap gradient AllReduce with the next layer’s backward pass. The backward pass takes 500 μs. Represent the complete transfer by a toy budget with network time 100 μs, processor overhead ${o}=$ 50 μs to initiate, and ${o}=$ 50 μs to receive. Assume both overhead intervals occupy the same execution resource as backward computation, with independent network progress and no contention. Can the communication be hidden under these assumptions?

**Math**:

1.  **Overlappable portion**: Network latency ${L_{\text{LogGP}}}$ = 100 μs (data in flight while GPU computes).
2.  **Non-overlappable portion**: $2{o}$ = 100 μs (GPU busy initiating/receiving).
3.  **Compute available**: 500 μs.
4.  **Hidden**: All 100 μs of ${L_{\text{LogGP}}}$ can overlap with compute.
5.  **Exposed**: The 100 μs of $2{o}$ overhead cannot overlap.

**Result**: Effective time is 100 μs + max(500 μs, 100 μs) = 600 μs. The network latency is hidden, but the processor overhead remains exposed. Applied to every layer of a backward pass, the same split determines which gradient transfers a training step can hide.

**Systems insight**: The α-β model captures the total communication time. The LogP model reveals how much of it can be hidden. When designing pipelined training, optimize for low ${o}$ (kernel bypass, GPUDirect) rather than high ${\beta}$ alone.

The ${\alpha}$-${\beta}$ model estimates transfer duration from latency and bandwidth measurements. LogP adds the processor/network distinction needed to estimate exposed delay in pipelined execution. Choosing a communication schedule therefore requires both a transfer budget and the independent computation available to overlap it.

When messages are large and pipelined across the network, the LogGP model ([[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/02 Distributed ML/05 Collective Communication/V2-05.16 Source References#^ref-alexandrov1995|Alexandrov et al. 1995]]) extends LogP with a gap-per-byte parameter ${G}$, modeling the streaming cost of long messages. These models describe multiprocessor communication with endpoint rate and capacity limits; they are not restricted to one flow on one link. Their compact parameters do not explicitly represent every shared path, oversubscribed cut, or routing hotspot. For congestion-sensitive workloads, particularly AllToAll for MoE, measurements on the target placement must test whether the model captures the active bottleneck.

↪ The LogGP long-message cost model and its gap parameters are developed in **[[05 Reference/Volume II - Scaling/V2 Communication Foundations#^sec-appdx-comm-foundations-loggp|The LogGP model extension]]**.

### Layer-By-Layer Overlap

^sec-communication-layer-overlap

Gradient computation during the backward pass proceeds layer by layer, from the output layer back to the input layer. The gradient for layer $\ell$ is available as soon as that layer’s backward pass completes, even while layers $\ell-1, \ell-2, \ldots$ are still computing. That ordering lets the gradient for layer $\ell$ start communicating immediately while the GPU computes the gradient for layer $\ell-1$. Over a four-layer backward pass, the transfers for layers 4 through 2 run beneath later backward work, and only the transfer for layer 1, which becomes ready last, is left exposed before the optimizer step ([[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/02 Distributed ML/05 Collective Communication/V2-05.11 Communication-Computation Overlap#^fig-comm-compute-overlap|figure 10]]).

In PyTorch’s `DistributedDataParallel`, this overlap is implemented through gradient hooks. Each parameter registers a hook that marks its gradient ready. Once all required gradients in a bucket are ready, the reducer can launch an asynchronous AllReduce, preserving the same collective order across ranks. Meanwhile, the backward pass continues computing gradients for earlier layers. A transfer is hidden only if it completes before its result is needed; queued communication can consume part of that window, and the last bucket’s transfer remains exposed ([[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/02 Distributed ML/05 Collective Communication/V2-05.11 Communication-Computation Overlap#^sec-communication-overlap-limits|section 5.10.4]]).

![[90 Assets/Volume II/05 Collective Communication/Comm Compute Overlap a69c44c098.svg]]

Figure 10: **Communication-Computation Overlap**: The backward pass runs from the last layer to the first, and each layer’s gradient AllReduce launches as soon as that layer finishes, so it runs on the network while the GPU computes the next layer’s gradients. The first layer finishes last and has no computation left to hide behind, so its AllReduce is exposed before the optimizer step. Overlap succeeds only when the nonoverlappable overhead (${o}$) is small, leaving the bulk transfer free to proceed concurrently. Durations are schematic.

^fig-comm-compute-overlap

The effectiveness of this overlap depends on the relative sizes of computation and communication at each layer. For transformer models, the largest gradient tensors belong to the attention and feed-forward weight matrices, which are also the most computationally expensive layers. That computation cannot hide the same layer’s gradient transfer, because the gradient is ready only afterward. It can hide a previously launched transfer; the ordering and cost of the remaining layers determine each gradient’s overlap window.

The overlap strategy interacts with algorithm choice from [[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/02 Distributed ML/05 Collective Communication/V2-05.07 AllReduce Algorithms and Topologies#^sec-communication-collective-operations-collective-operations-allreduce-algorithms-7545|section 5.6]] and [[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/02 Distributed ML/05 Collective Communication/V2-05.08 Hierarchical Communication#^sec-communication-collective-operations-collective-operations-mapping-collectives-topology-3214|section 5.7]]. An isolated-collective crossover need not predict the best training-step schedule, because the dependency schedule determines how much of each candidate’s transfer time is exposed. Overlap with the backward pass can reduce Ring’s exposed latency penalty, but the benefit must be measured with the actual gradient-ready times and runtime progress behavior. The optimizer step still cannot begin until every required reduction completes, so the last transfer, closest to the input, stays exposed along with any backlog.

### Bucket Fusion

^sec-communication-bucket-fusion

The layer-by-layer overlap described in [[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/02 Distributed ML/05 Collective Communication/V2-05.11 Communication-Computation Overlap#^sec-communication-layer-overlap|section 5.10.2]] assumes that each layer’s gradient is communicated as a single message. In reality, a transformer layer contains dozens of individual parameter tensors (query, key, value projection matrices, output projection, layer norm parameters, feed-forward weights, and biases). Launching a separate AllReduce for every individual parameter would create thousands of small-message collectives, each paying the full ${\alpha_{\text{eff}}}$ overhead. To avoid this, DDP uses bucket fusion to group parameters into buckets (often configured around tens of megabytes, with 25 MB a common PyTorch reference point) and launches one AllReduce per bucket. The bucket size represents a trade-off: larger buckets amortize ${\alpha_{\text{eff}}}$ overhead but delay the start of communication (because the bucket cannot be sent until all its parameters have computed gradients). Smaller buckets start communication earlier but pay more ${\alpha_{\text{eff}}}$ overhead.

The optimal bucket size depends on gradient-ready times, tensor sizes, and network characteristics. Smaller buckets can start communication sooner when waiting for more gradients would consume the overlap window. Larger buckets can amortize startup when repeated small transfers dominate. Neither choice follows from the model family alone; the useful measure is the resulting training-step time, including the exposed final transfer. PyTorch’s `bucket_cap_mb` parameter in DDP allows tuning this trade-off.

Bucket membership also determines how early communication can begin. A bucket containing one late gradient must wait even if its other gradients are ready. DDP can use reverse parameter registration order as an approximation to backward readiness and, depending on configuration, rebuild buckets from the observed backward order. Registration order need not match forward execution order, especially in models with branches or reused parameters. Grouping gradients that become ready together helps preserve the overlap window while maintaining a consistent collective sequence across ranks.

### Overlap Limits and Exposed Communication

^sec-communication-overlap-limits

Even well-tuned bucket fusion cannot hide all communication behind backward compute. Three structural limits (processor launch and completion overhead, boundary stalls at the start and end of backpropagation, and the HBM footprint of in-flight buffers) cap how much transfer time can disappear. The LogP model’s overhead parameter ${o}$ represents processor time consumed by initiating or completing transfers; in the simplified LogP budget, initiation and completion consume $2{o}$ per operation on the participating execution resource, so when backward compute intervals are short relative to that overhead, hiding network transit still leaves processor overhead exposed.

The backward pass also has boundary effects. During the first backward computation, no gradient from this pass is ready to communicate. Once the output-side gradient is ready, however, its communication can overlap with the remaining backward work. At the other end, the input-side gradient has no subsequent backward computation to hide its transfer. More layers create a longer overlap window, but depth alone does not guarantee high coverage: if communication takes longer than each compute interval, a backlog remains even in a deep model.

Memory pressure sets the last limit. Buffers used by an in-flight transfer must remain valid while independent computation produces or consumes other tensors. Their overlapping lifetimes can increase peak GPU memory use, although views and buffer reuse can avoid separate copies. For FSDP workloads, prefetching parameters can keep multiple materialized groups resident at once, trading memory savings for communication overlap. The engineering challenge is to hide transfers within the available memory budget, and FSDP’s prefetch and resharding policies are the controls that set this trade-off.

Within those limits, per-layer transfer time determines how much of a collective schedule can hide behind backward computation. Pricing a realistic 32-layer transformer backward pass shows how much communication remains exposed when each layer’s reduction outlasts its compute window.

**Napkin Math 5.9: Overlap budget for a 7B transformer**

**Problem**: A 32-layer transformer model (7B parameters) is trained on 64 GPUs. Assume the data-parallel reducer communicates FP32 gradients, about 875 MB per layer, in 100 MB buckets, four times the common 25 MB default. Each layer’s backward pass takes 15 ms. Assume the cluster achieves 3 ms per bucket under its contention and protocol overheads, so each layer’s gradients take 27 ms, and assume equal layers and the same charge for the final partial bucket. What is the backward-plus-communication time with and without overlap?

**Without overlap (sequential)**:

${T_{\text{sequential}}}= {T_{\text{backward}}}+ {T_{\text{comm}}}({N})$ = 480 ms + 32 $\times$ 27 ms = 1344 ms.

**With overlap (pipelined)**:

Each layer’s AllReduce (27 ms) runs in parallel with the next layer’s backward pass (15 ms). Since 27 ms \> 15 ms, there is 12 ms of exposed communication per layer that cannot be hidden.

Communication runs back to back from the moment the output layer’s gradients are ready. Each later reduction overruns its backward window by the exposed amount, so the backlog grows by that much per layer, and the last reduction adds its full duration after the backward pass ends:

${T_{\text{pipelined}}}= {T_{\text{backward}}}+ {T_{\text{layer comm}}}+ ({N_L} - 1) \times {T_{\text{exposed}}}$ = 879 ms.

**Systems insight**: Overlap reduces backward-plus-communication time by 34.6 percent. The remaining exposed communication comes from the AllReduce being slower than the per-layer backward pass. To reduce the backlog, either lengthen the independent compute window (for example, with a larger batch) or reduce AllReduce time. The final gradient still has an exposed transfer tail, and any change must preserve the intended training workload and quality target. The overlap is most effective when per-layer backward compute exceeds the reduction time (${T_{\text{layer backward}}} > {T_{\text{layer comm}}}$).

The verdict turns on the assumed per-bucket charge. The hierarchical model of notebook [5.4](https://mlsysbook.ai/vol2/05_collective_communication/05_collective_communication.html#nbk-collective-communication-hierarchical-bandwidth-multiplier) prices one uncontended 100 MB bucket on these eight nodes at about 0.85 ms, or 7.6 ms per layer, inside the 15 ms backward window, so at that rate every transfer except the last would hide. An overlap budget is therefore only as good as its achieved per-bucket time, which the calibration of [[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/02 Distributed ML/05 Collective Communication/V2-05.12 Communication Libraries and Runtimes#^sec-communication-nccl-reality|section 5.11.2]] measures rather than assumes.

An overlap budget joins the collective’s transfer cost to the model’s dependency schedule. Realizing that budget requires a runtime whose asynchronous progress and device-memory path preserve the independent work available between gradient production and consumption.
