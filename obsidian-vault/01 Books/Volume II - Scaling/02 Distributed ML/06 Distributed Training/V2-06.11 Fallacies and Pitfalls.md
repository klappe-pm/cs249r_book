# V2-06.11 Fallacies and Pitfalls

[[Home|Home]] · [[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/Volume 2 Overview|Volume Overview]] · [[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/02 Distributed ML/06 Distributed Training/V2-06 Distributed Training|V2-06 Distributed Training]]

^sec-distributed-training-systems-systems-fallacies-pitfalls-e2bc

Even when a framework makes switching from single-node DDP to multi-node execution a one-line configuration change, the underlying physics still governs the step time. In the GPT-2 scale-out of notebook [6.1](https://mlsysbook.ai/vol2/06_distributed_training/06_distributed_training.html#nbk-distributed-training-gpt-2-data-parallel-scaling) and notebook [6.2](https://mlsysbook.ai/vol2/06_distributed_training/06_distributed_training.html#nbk-distributed-training-gpt-2-scale-out), moving the same model from one NVLink node to four Ethernet-connected nodes quadrupled the GPU count but cut parallel efficiency from 99.4 percent to 27.9 percent. The following misconceptions generalize that error, each treating one term of the step-time accounting as if it were the whole budget.

**Fallacy**: *Linear speedup is achievable with sufficient engineering effort.*

Amdahl’s Law limits speedup when a fixed serial component remains exposed. Synchronous gradient aggregation creates an update dependency, but some of its work can overlap backward computation. As [[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/02 Distributed ML/06 Distributed Training/V2-06.05 Scaling Efficiency and Convergence#^sec-distributed-training-systems-systems-distributed-training-efficiency-metrics-9488|section 6.4]] shows, ${\eta_{\text{scaling}}} = 1/(1 + {N}({T_{\text{comm}}}({N}) + {T_{\text{sync}}}({N}) - {T_{\text{overlap}}})/{T_{\text{compute}}})$ depends on the exposed overhead relative to useful compute as ${N}$ changes. A 1,000-GPU allocation therefore does not by itself justify a 1,000$\times$ speedup forecast. Local kernel efficiency, memory feasibility, communication, and convergence must be evaluated against the specified baseline. Linear scaling is a result to demonstrate for an operating range, not a return guaranteed by device count.

**Pitfall**: *Optimizing MFU without considering scaling efficiency.*

A single-node MFU measurement does not predict fleet throughput by itself. For a fixed workload and hardware peak convention, scaling efficiency compares measured distributed throughput with ideal replication of that single-node baseline. Multiplying the baseline MFU by this scaling efficiency gives distributed MFU; multiplying an already measured distributed MFU by the same scaling efficiency counts communication and synchronization losses twice. The 175B reference job makes the difference concrete. A single node running at 45 percent MFU, scaled with the job’s 83 percent modeled scaling efficiency on the 1,024-GPU cluster (TP-8, PP-8, DP-16, configuration D of [[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/02 Distributed ML/06 Distributed Training/V2-06.07 Hybrid Parallelism#^sec-distributed-training-systems-systems-hybrid-parallelism-12a0|section 6.6]], with tensor-parallel and data-parallel communication and the pipeline bubble all charged), delivers about 37 percent MFU across the fleet. A planner who measures that 37 percent on the full cluster and then applies the 83 percent again would budget for about 31 percent and buy capacity the job does not need. Capacity planning must state the device count and measurement window for each metric. Checkpointing, recovery, and replay require additional accounting only when those losses fall outside that window. Useful goodput must count retained model progress rather than repeated work, under the accounting boundaries of **[[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/03 The Fleet in Production/09 Fleet Efficiency/V2-09.03 ML Goodput#^sec-performance-engineering-goodput-boundaries|Accounting boundaries]]**.

**Fallacy**: *Hyperparameters tuned on small clusters transfer directly to large-scale training.*

Engineers tune hyperparameters on 8-GPU workstations then deploy to 256-GPU clusters expecting identical behavior. At scale, convergence behavior changes. The most sensitive hyperparameter is learning rate. As [[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/02 Distributed ML/06 Distributed Training/V2-06.04 Data Parallelism#^sec-distributed-training-systems-systems-data-parallelism-6132|section 6.3]] explains, global batch increases with GPU count when local batch and accumulation depth remain fixed, so the learning-rate schedule must be revalidated. The linear scaling rule of [[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/02 Distributed ML/06 Distributed Training/V2-06.05 Scaling Efficiency and Convergence#^sec-distributed-training-systems-systems-learning-rate-scaling-rules-fa26|section 6.4.5]], which the one-hour ImageNet run established, suggests ${\eta_{\text{new}}} = {\eta_{\text{base}}}\times ({B_{\text{new}}}/{B_{\text{base}}})$, but this relationship holds only within bounds. As models scale, they eventually encounter the critical batch size of [[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/02 Distributed ML/06 Distributed Training/V2-06.05 Scaling Efficiency and Convergence#^sec-distributed-training-systems-systems-critical-batch-size-parallelism-hurt-4961|section 6.4.6]], where adding more data per step yields diminishing returns in convergence.

Beyond the critical batch size, this relationship breaks down in a model-, data-, and optimizer-dependent way. A team that takes a small-cluster learning rate, multiplies it mechanically with GPU count, and jumps directly to a much larger global batch may see lower final quality or slower sample efficiency even though hardware throughput improved. Warmup schedules, weight decay adjustment, layer-wise optimizers such as LARS/LAMB, and careful momentum tuning can recover accuracy in some regimes, but require systematic experimentation at target scale. Organizations that skip these scaling studies waste thousands of GPU-hours on suboptimal runs.

**Pitfall**: *Adding GPUs to data-parallel jobs without modeling communication.*

More GPUs improve time to target only if the gain in processing rate outweighs communication overhead and any additional sample use. With fixed local batch size, adding data-parallel workers increases the global batch; fixing the global batch instead reduces local work and can expose more communication. The critical-batch analysis in [[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/02 Distributed ML/06 Distributed Training/V2-06.05 Scaling Efficiency and Convergence#^sec-distributed-training-systems-systems-critical-batch-size-parallelism-hurt-4961|section 6.4.6]] supplies a workload-dependent statistical guide, not universal GPU-count or model-size thresholds. Compare complete learning curves and cost at the same target quality before committing to a larger allocation.

**Fallacy**: *Memory capacity alone determines the parallelism strategy.*

A model that exceeds device capacity needs a feasible partition, but memory fit does not select the fastest one. Tensor parallelism exchanges partial results within layers, while pipeline parallelism transfers stage-boundary activations and introduces scheduling bubbles. Their relative cost depends on layer shapes, microbatch count, placement, and overlap. Compare complete training-state and activation budgets together with measured time to target; an unmeasured throughput estimate or a weights-only fit cannot settle the choice.

**Pitfall**: *Applying FSDP or ZeRO without measuring efficiency trade-offs.*

Engineers adopt FSDP universally after reading that it “reduces memory and enables larger models.” Sharding trades memory capacity for communication overhead. Full-shard FSDP gathers parameters before layer execution, gathers them again for the backward pass if the forward pass resharded them, and reduces gradient shards during backward. The relevant comparison begins with the complete training-state budget. Under the mixed-precision Adam convention established here, the 7B worked example exceeds single-device capacity before activations, so standard DDP cannot fit that state on an A100-80 GB. A weights-only fit does not establish a feasible training baseline. When the complete state, activations, and workspaces fit without sharding, DDP can avoid the repeated gathers; when they exceed the 80 GB capacity, the comparison must include a feasible alternative such as sharding or offload. FSDP provides value when it enables training at all or when the larger local batch it permits offsets its communication cost. ZeRO-Offload extends capacity through CPU memory but adds transfers of its own. The decision therefore requires measuring peak memory and exposed communication for the same workload and optimizer contract.

**Fallacy**: *Parallelism overhead is roughly constant regardless of model size.*

Engineers benchmark parallelism strategies on convenient small models then apply those conclusions to large-scale training. For dense transformers at fixed tokens per update, computation and gradient bytes both grow roughly proportionally with parameter count, as the earlier scaling analysis established. Larger parameter count alone therefore does not imply a better computation-to-communication ratio. Architecture, sequence length, local batch size, partition degree, and kernel efficiency determine how much useful work each rank performs; gradient representation and process-group membership determine how much it communicates. A larger model can also require a different partition that introduces new layer collectives or pipeline bubbles. Reliable strategy selection holds the workload and target quality explicit, then recomputes memory fit, local compute time, communication volume, and the available overlap window at the target scale. A small-model benchmark becomes transferable evidence only when those governing terms remain comparable.

**Pitfall**: *Treating scaling efficiency as a property of the hardware alone.*

A vendor benchmark might report 85 percent scaling efficiency at 1,024 GPUs, and procurement teams then plan capacity as if that number were a cluster constant. Scaling efficiency is a joint property of four things: the workload’s communication-to-computation ratio, the parallelism strategy (data, pipeline, tensor, hybrid), the network topology (fat-tree vs. torus vs. dragonfly), and the software stack (NCCL version, kernel fusion, overlap quality). Reference numbers from dense large-language-model benchmarks transfer poorly to other workloads and fabrics. They may overstate scaling for graph neural networks with irregular communication and for embedding-heavy recommendation models, whose AllToAll embedding exchanges and many small random lookups load the fabric differently from dense gradient AllReduce ([[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/02 Distributed ML/06 Distributed Training/V2-06.06 Model Parallelism#^sec-distributed-training-systems-systems-parameter-servers-embedding-sharding-8821|section 6.5.6]]). They also say nothing about a commodity-Ethernet placement like the GPT-2 scale-out, which lost most of its parallel efficiency on leaving the node. Capacity planning should use measured scaling on the target workload, not a vendor hero benchmark.

**Fallacy**: *Gradient accumulation is free.*

Gradient accumulation can reduce synchronization from every microstep to once per window of ${k}$ microsteps, eliminating $({k}-1)/{k}$ of those synchronization events when the implementation defers reduction. The saving has costs. A resident gradient buffer remains live throughout the accumulation window; for the 7B model, the FP16 buffer occupies 14 GB. Retaining graphs across microsteps or increasing the local batch size can add activation pressure. Accumulating eight microsteps makes updates 8$\times$ less frequent per processed microstep, but wall-clock spacing also depends on the communication saved. Larger effective batches can change convergence, and narrow accumulation formats can lose small contributions or overflow. The dtype, loss normalization, and reduction schedule must therefore be validated at the intended batch size. Accumulation trades communication frequency against update spacing, memory residency, and numerical stability.

**Self-Check: Question**

1.  A team achieves 50 percent Model Flops Utilization (MFU) on a single node, scales to 1,024 GPUs, and measures a scaling efficiency $(\eta_{\text{scaling}})$ of 50 percent on their communication-heavy workload. What is the cluster’s realized model-FLOP rate during distributed execution as a fraction of peak hardware compute?

    1.  25 percent of peak compute (delivering 256 GPU-equivalents of model computation)
    2.  50 percent of peak compute (delivering 512 GPU-equivalents of useful work)
    3.  100 percent of peak compute (delivering 1,024 GPU-equivalents of useful work)
    4.  12.5 percent of peak compute (delivering 128 GPU-equivalents of useful work)

2.  A procurement team plans capacity for a graph neural network workload using a vendor benchmark that reports 85 percent scaling efficiency at 1,024 GPUs for large-language-model training. Explain why this figure cannot be treated as a property of the cluster and what the team should measure instead.

3.  Why is gradient accumulation NOT a cost-free substitute for true physical scale-out even though it reduces gradient AllReduce communication frequency by a factor of $k$?

    1.  It is mathematically incompatible with the Adam optimizer
    2.  It keeps a live gradient buffer resident, spaces optimizer updates by $k$ microsteps, and requires appropriate accumulation precision and normalization
    3.  It requires dedicated InfiniBand links between CPU and GPU for each microstep
    4.  It forces batch normalization layers to synchronize across all nodes on every forward pass

4.  The largest global batch size beyond which adding more samples per step yields diminishing reductions in the number of steps needed to reach target quality is called the \_\_\_\_.

5.  True or False: If a 7B-parameter model fits comfortably in single-GPU memory with high compute utilization under standard DDP, universally enabling ZeRO-3 / FSDP full sharding will always increase training throughput by reducing per-GPU memory consumption.
