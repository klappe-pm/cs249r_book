# V2-06.15 Footnotes

[[Home|Home]] · [[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/Volume 2 Overview|Volume Overview]] · [[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/02 Distributed ML/06 Distributed Training/V2-06 Distributed Training|V2-06 Distributed Training]]

^footnotes

<sup>1</sup> **DistBelief**: Google’s DistBelief (2012) was an early framework for training neural networks across thousands of machines, but its parameter server architecture created bandwidth bottlenecks at central nodes. This limitation drove the shift to decentralized AllReduce patterns in successors such as Horovod and PyTorch DistributedDataParallel (DDP), where each data-parallel worker sends and receives about $2({N}-1)/{N}$ times the gradient size per step rather than concentrating traffic at a single server ([[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/02 Distributed ML/06 Distributed Training/V2-06.14 Source References#^ref-dean2012large|Dean et al. 2012]]; [[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/02 Distributed ML/06 Distributed Training/V2-06.14 Source References#^ref-sergeev2018horovod|Sergeev and Balso 2018]]; [[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/02 Distributed ML/06 Distributed Training/V2-06.14 Source References#^ref-li2020pytorch|Li et al. 2020]]; [[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/02 Distributed ML/06 Distributed Training/V2-06.14 Source References#^ref-patarasuk2009|Patarasuk and Yuan 2009]]).

^fn1

<sup>2</sup> **NVLink**: NVIDIA’s point-to-point GPU interconnect delivers 300 GB/s–450 GB/s per-direction bandwidth, roughly 12×–18× InfiniBand HDR per port. This bandwidth gap is why tensor parallelism, which requires AllReduce on every layer, favors the fast NVLink domain, while pipeline and data parallelism can tolerate slower links when their traffic budgets fit. NVLink Switch systems can extend that fast domain beyond one server.

^fn2

<sup>3</sup> **ZeRO (Zero Redundancy Optimizer)**: Introduced by Microsoft researchers in 2019 and released in the DeepSpeed library ([[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/02 Distributed ML/06 Distributed Training/V2-06.14 Source References#^ref-rasley2020|Rasley et al. 2020]]), ZeRO’s stages progressively partition optimizer states, gradients, and parameters. FSDP draws on the fully sharded approach associated with ZeRO’s deepest stage; the shared partitioning principle does not imply identical implementations or communication schedules.

^fn3

<sup>4</sup> **GPipe**: Published by Google in 2019, GPipe introduced synchronous microbatch pipelining that trained a 557M-parameter AmoebaNet across 8 Tensor Processing Units (TPUs) with near-linear scaling. Its schedule keeps every microbatch’s activations live until the backward pass, the cost that later schedules reduce.

^fn4

<sup>5</sup> **1F1B (one-forward-one-backward) scheduling**: Unlike GPipe (which processes all forward passes before any backward passes), 1F1B interleaves them. For equal-sized microbatch activations and a non-interleaved schedule, peak retained activations per stage fall from $\mathcal{O}({m})$ under GPipe to at most $\mathcal{O}({N_{\text{PP}}})$ under 1F1B, where ${m}$ is the microbatch count and ${N_{\text{PP}}}$ the pipeline-stage count. Summed across stages, the corresponding bounds are $\mathcal{O}({m}{N_{\text{PP}}})$ and $\mathcal{O}({N_{\text{PP}}}^2)$. This memory reclamation is what enables the large microbatch counts $({m}\gg {N_{\text{PP}}})$ required to keep the pipeline bubble small.

^fn5

<sup>6</sup> **Megatron-LM**: NVIDIA’s 2019 framework that trained an 8.3B-parameter transformer (24× BERT-Large and 5.5× GPT-2 at the time) with only two AllReduce operations per transformer block in the forward pass (one after attention, one after the multilayer perceptron (MLP)). The run reached 76 percent scaling efficiency across 512 GPUs, and its partitioning remains influential in large-scale transformer training.

^fn6
