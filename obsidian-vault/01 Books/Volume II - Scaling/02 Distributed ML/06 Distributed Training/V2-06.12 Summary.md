# V2-06.12 Summary

[[Home|Home]] · [[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/Volume 2 Overview|Volume Overview]] · [[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/02 Distributed ML/06 Distributed Training/V2-06 Distributed Training|V2-06 Distributed Training]]

^sec-distributed-training-systems-summary

Scaling laws keep pushing frontier training budgets past what one machine can hold or finish in time, and distributed training is how the fleet absorbs that growth. It confronts the scaling wall, the workload-dependent operating point beyond which communication and coordination can consume the compute time saved by adding accelerators. A feasible design must preserve the intended optimization update while satisfying model-state, activation, batch-size, and interconnect constraints. Hardware throughput alone is insufficient; the comparison must also account for time to the target model quality.

Each partition relocates cost along the C³ axes. Data parallelism divides compute across replicas and pays in communication, the gradient aggregation whose exposed share set the scaling efficiency of the 175B communication example. Tensor parallelism divides work inside a layer and pays in communication on the critical path, which confines it to a fast domain. Pipeline parallelism divides layer depth and pays mostly in coordination, the fill and drain bubble that only more microbatches, better balance, or a smarter schedule can shrink. The 3D parallelism cube in [[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/02 Distributed ML/06 Distributed Training/V2-06.03 The Parallelism Design Space#^fig-3d-parallelism-cube|figure 2]] unites these trade-offs in one coordinate space (${N_{\text{DP}}} \times {N_{\text{PP}}} \times {N_{\text{TP}}}$). A feasible point must satisfy peak memory and global-batch constraints as well as communication and coordination budgets.

Sharding and model partitioning make different parts of the training state fit, while synchronization and precision policies determine whether the resulting updates remain useful. Hybrid layouts compose these choices within the bandwidth hierarchy. The alignment workload extends the same reasoning to multiple models and alternating generation and update phases, where peak memory depends on which state is live together.

The chapter’s strategies are complementary dimensions of one design space rather than alternatives. Systems such as Megatron-LM map tensor, pipeline, data, and expert exchanges to the communication domains that can carry them, and that matching is what delays the communication-bound regime as the fleet grows. The workload archetypes weight those dimensions differently. Archetype A (GPT-4/Llama-3) combines dense-model partitioning with state sharding and, at long contexts, sequence or context partitioning. Archetype B (DLRM at Scale) shards sparse embedding state and pays in lookup traffic rather than layer collectives. Archetype C (Federated MobileNet) moves the coordination problem onto unreliable edge devices. In each case the limiting constraint, not the archetype label, selects the configuration.

**Key Takeaways: Parallelism relocates overhead**

- **Splitting work moves the bottleneck**: Distributed training does not make the underlying computation smaller; it converts memory pressure into communication volume, synchronization delay, or idle pipeline time (principle [[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/02 Distributed ML/V2 Distributed ML Principles#^pri-scaling-efficiency|12]]). The winning strategy is the split that sends overhead to the least constrained part of the fleet.
- **The slowest worker sets synchronous step time**: Under bulk-synchronous updates every step waits for its last required contribution, so a rare per-worker delay becomes a near-certain per-step delay at thousands of workers. That wait preserves the single-device global-batch update, which also needs matching sampling, valid-item denominators, and optimizer state; stale and asynchronous protocols trade that wait for staleness, which can cost extra steps to the same target quality.
- **Throughput must serve convergence**: Larger global batches can improve hardware efficiency while yielding diminishing progress per sample. Compare time to the same target quality, and treat the critical-batch estimate as a workload-dependent guide for selecting batch size and update schedules.
- **Sharding buys memory with messages**: ZeRO and FSDP partition optimizer state, gradients, and parameters (principle [[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/01 The Fleet/V2 Fleet Principles#^pri-memory-capacity-gap|2]]), reducing replicated storage. Peak memory must still include activations, temporary gathers, and workspaces; exposed ReduceScatter and AllGather costs determine whether the capacity gain also improves throughput.
- **Tensor parallelism needs a fast domain**: Splitting operations inside layers creates frequent communication on the dependency path. Place those groups on links with sufficient bandwidth and low latency; the relevant boundary is the communication domain, which may span nodes, rather than the server enclosure.
- **Pipeline parallelism trades bytes for bubbles**: Layer staging reduces communication frequency, but fill and drain slots leave accelerators idle (principle [[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/02 Distributed ML/V2 Distributed ML Principles#^pri-distributed-step-time|10]]). Microbatching with ${m}\gg {N_{\text{PP}}}$ is the mechanism that turns model depth into throughput rather than pipeline slack.
- **Hybrid layouts are joint decisions**: Combine only the partitioning and sharding strategies needed to satisfy the workload’s constraints (principle [[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/02 Distributed ML/V2 Distributed ML Principles#^pri-conservation-overhead|14]]). Model divisibility, batch size, peak memory, communication groups, and failure domains must form one feasible configuration; improving one axis can expose a limit on another.

^callout-takeaways--6-12-7395ab36

The displacement of overhead (principle [[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/02 Distributed ML/V2 Distributed ML Principles#^pri-conservation-overhead|14]]) makes a feasible partition the beginning of the design problem. A memory-saving split is useful only if its communication and schedule still meet the run’s time-to-quality budget. Every mechanism in this chapter shrinks the per-device compute term ${T_{\text{compute}}}/{N}$ or the per-device training state, and pays for it in ${T_{\text{comm}}}({N})$, in ${T_{\text{sync}}}({N})$ through barriers, stragglers, and pipeline bubbles, or in lost overlap. In C³ terms, no partition removes the overhead of scale; it only relocates that overhead among compute, communication, and coordination. The engineering judgment is therefore to compare complete configurations, including their exposed dependencies, rather than optimize each parallelism axis independently.

**What’s Next: From logic to resilience**

The fleet now has its traffic patterns (communication) and its logic (parallelism). Every configuration in this chapter, however, assumed that every rank delivers its contribution on time and with correct data. A collective is not a barrier, but no dependent consumer can proceed until every contribution arrives, so one crashed node, failed link, or GPU that slows down without failing stalls the whole synchronous group. A contribution corrupted in transit is reduced into every replica’s update. Across thousands of accelerators such events stop being rare. **[[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/02 Distributed ML/07 Fault Tolerance/V2-07 Fault Tolerance#^sec-fault-tolerance-reliability|Fault Tolerance]]** begins by computing how often they arrive, then builds the checkpointing, recovery, and elastic training that keep the computation making progress on imperfect hardware.
