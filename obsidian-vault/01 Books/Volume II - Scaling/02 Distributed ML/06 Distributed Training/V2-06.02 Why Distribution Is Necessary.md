# V2-06.02 Why Distribution Is Necessary

[[Home|Home]] · [[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/Volume 2 Overview|Volume Overview]] · [[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/02 Distributed ML/06 Distributed Training/V2-06 Distributed Training|V2-06 Distributed Training]]

^sec-distributed-training-systems-systems-multimachine-scaling-fundamentals-ff96

A GPT-3-class 175B-parameter reference model cannot load onto a single accelerator, because its FP16 weights alone exceed the memory of the device that must hold them. The empirical scaling law (principle [[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/02 Distributed ML/V2 Distributed ML Principles#^pri-universal-scaling|9]]) keeps pushing frontier models further past what one machine can store or finish in calendar time. The accelerator hierarchy, network fabric, and storage pipeline (**[[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/01 The Fleet/04 Data Storage/V2-04 Data Storage#^sec-data-storage|Data Storage]]**) form the physical foundation of the fleet, and **[[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/02 Distributed ML/05 Collective Communication/V2-05 Collective Communication#^sec-collective-communication|Collective Communication]]** priced the collective operations that move state across it. The remaining challenge is deciding how to partition the training job: which intermediate results and gradients must cross that fabric, and when workers must synchronize, while preserving the optimization process a single device would have followed.

↩︎ The power-law relationship between quality and compute, data, and parameters, and the compute-optimal allocation it implies, are established in **[[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/00 Introduction/01 Introduction/V2-01.04 AI Scaling Laws#^sec-vol2-intro-ai-scaling-laws-a043|AI Scaling Laws]]**.

In the fleet stack (**[[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/00 Introduction/01 Introduction/V2-01.03 From Single Node to Fleet#^fig-fleet-stack|The Fleet Stack]]**), distributed training occupies the Distributed Execution row of the Distribution band, the logic that partitions the *mathematical* workload across the *physical* fleet. Data, tensor, pipeline, and hybrid parallelism create different traffic patterns for the interconnect to carry, and selecting among them requires evaluating the reference workload against finite high-bandwidth memory (HBM) capacity, compute throughput, and input bandwidth.

Three distinct limits push a training workload past a single accelerator. Memory exhaustion hits first when model parameters, optimizer states, and activation storage exceed single-device capacity. For full mixed-precision training with Adaptive Moment Estimation (Adam), the convention of 16 bytes per parameter (FP16 weights and gradients, an FP32 master weight, and two FP32 Adam moments) can exceed an 80 GB accelerator at roughly 5 billion parameters before activations. Larger 10–20B models therefore need sharding, offload to a slower memory tier, or another memory-saving approach ([[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/02 Distributed ML/06 Distributed Training/V2-06.14 Source References#^ref-rajbhandari2020|Rajbhandari et al. 2020]]). The capacity limit alone does not choose among them.

↪ Accelerator HBM capacity, the ceiling these per-parameter budgets are measured against, is developed in **[[05 Reference/Volume II - Scaling/V2 Single-Machine Foundations#^sec-appdx-dam-taxonomy-memory-hierarchy|Accelerator Memory Hierarchy]]**.

Even when a model fits in memory, unacceptable training duration can still force distribution because a single device may require weeks or months to converge, turning wall-clock time itself into a systems constraint. GPT-3’s 175B-parameter training run used a cluster of V100 GPUs ([[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/02 Distributed ML/06 Distributed Training/V2-06.14 Source References#^ref-brown2020language|Brown et al. 2020]]), illustrating why the calendar, alongside HBM capacity, drives scale-out.

Input demand at dataset scale adds a third pressure. Dataset bytes govern storage capacity, whereas the training schedule governs the required delivery rate. As **[[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/01 The Fleet/04 Data Storage/V2-04.05 The Data Pipeline Equation#^sec-storage-pipeline-equation|The Data Pipeline Equation]]** showed, either can motivate distributed data services, although a large dataset alone does not require distributing model computation. Together, capacity, calendar, and input delivery define the constraints a training configuration must satisfy.

The first signal, memory exhaustion, has a long history, traced in [[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/02 Distributed ML/06 Distributed Training/V2-06.02 Why Distribution Is Necessary#^fig-memory-wall-trend|figure 1]]. Over the plotted span, single-accelerator memory grew about 15× from the K80 to the B200, while the memory needed to train a state-of-the-art model grew by roughly 4 orders of magnitude. Among the plotted models, the curves cross in 2020 at GPT-3, whose training state exceeded the largest accelerator then available by about 35×. GPT-3 marks where the gap passed an order of magnitude, and every later plotted frontier model also sits far above the capacity curve. For those models, spreading state beyond one device’s memory is a precondition for training at all rather than a way to train faster.

Figure 1: **The Memory Wall**: Accelerator memory capacity plotted against the memory a state-of-the-art model needs to train. Capacity climbs steadily while demand climbs faster, and the widening gap is why weights, gradients, and optimizer state must be split across devices rather than merely compressed. Training memory assumes 16 bytes per parameter for mixed-precision Adam state; parameter counts are from Epoch AI ([[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/02 Distributed ML/06 Distributed Training/V2-06.14 Source References#^ref-epochai2026notable|2026]]) and capacities are vendor specifications.

Whichever signal motivates distribution, the system must state which optimization update it implements. The synchronous baseline is developed first. In it, the 1,024 GPUs of the volume’s Archetype A (GPT-4/Llama-3) reference cluster combine their contributions before advancing to the next parameter version. The archetype names a class of workload, and the running instance of it in this chapter is a GPT-3-class 175B-parameter dense transformer. Matching the global batch, loss normalization, and optimizer state preserves the corresponding single-device update mathematically. Later synchronization models deliberately relax that update ordering and must be evaluated under their own convergence assumptions.

↩︎ The three systems archetypes and their limiting constraints are established in **[[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/00 Introduction/01 Introduction/V2-01.07 The Fleet Stack and the Lighthouse Archetypes#^sec-vol2-introduction-archetypes|Three systems archetypes]]**.

**Definition 6.1: Distributed training**

***Distributed training*** partitions the optimization loop across multiple compute nodes, distributing data or model computation and coordinating contributions according to a specified update protocol.

1.  **Significance**: Partitioning training state across devices can meet a memory budget that exceeds one accelerator. GPT-3 (175B parameters) requires approximately 350 GB in BF16 (FP16 also stores 2 bytes per value, so the chapter’s byte counts hold for either format), more than 4× the 80 GB capacity of a single H100. If weights remain entirely in HBM, their aggregate capacity lower bound is 5 H100s. Adding BF16 gradients plus FP32 Adam master weights and moments gives about 2.8 TB, or at least 35 H100s before activations. These lower bounds do not establish a runnable placement; offload changes the capacity calculation, and the time budget can require more accelerators than memory alone.
2.  **Distinction**: CPU threads can share a coherent address space, while accelerators generally hold separate training-state allocations even within one node. Distributed training extends coordination across those devices and node boundaries through collectives or point-to-point transfers governed by the chosen update protocol.
3.  **Common pitfall**: A frequent misconception is that distributed training scales linearly with node count. In practice, communication overhead grows with cluster size and the serial fraction of each step (Amdahl’s Law): a fixed serial fraction of the single-device baseline bounds strong-scaling speedup.

Distribution expands three costs that determine whether a partition is viable. Communication overhead includes gradient aggregation through AllReduce, which returns the same reduced tensor to each participant, or rank. For ${P}$ parameters across ${N}$ devices, a ring AllReduce, in which each worker passes gradient chunks only to its neighbor in a logical ring, sends approximately $2({N}-1)/{N}\times {M_{\text{grad}}}$ bytes per worker per step. Here the gradient payload ${M_{\text{grad}}}$ is the ${P}$ gradient values times their storage width. The ring volume counts one worker’s traffic rather than total fleet traffic.

↩︎ The ring schedule and the derivation of its $2({N}-1)/{N}$ volume are established in **[[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/02 Distributed ML/05 Collective Communication/V2-05 Collective Communication#^sec-collective-communication|Collective Communication]]**.

Fault tolerance becomes more demanding as the number of failure sources grows. Under independent constant-rate failures, 1,024 GPUs that each have a mean time to failure (MTTF) of 50,000 hours produce a GPU failure about every 49 hours, the cluster’s mean time between failures (MTBF). Whether each event interrupts training depends on recovery and redundancy; **[[05 Reference/Volume II - Scaling/V2 Reliability Foundations#^sec-appdx-reliability-foundations-mtbf-cascade|The MTBF cascade]]** develops the assumptions.

Algorithmic stability adds a separate constraint when the partition changes global batch size or update ordering. Learning-rate scaling and warmup are candidate responses to large-batch behavior ([[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/02 Distributed ML/06 Distributed Training/V2-06.14 Source References#^ref-goyal2017accurate|Goyal et al. 2017]]), not requirements unique to multiple machines. A viable design must keep all three costs within budget.

Communication and coordination costs enter every step through the Fleet Law (principle [[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/02 Distributed ML/V2 Distributed ML Principles#^pri-distributed-step-time|10]]), which decomposes the per-step time:

$$
{T_{\text{step}}}({N}) = \frac{{T_{\text{compute}}}}{{N}} + {T_{\text{comm}}}({N}) + {T_{\text{sync}}}({N}) - {T_{\text{overlap}}}
$$

Each step takes the single-device compute time divided among the devices, plus the time spent moving data, plus the time spent waiting on coordination such as barriers and stragglers, minus whatever part of that time stays hidden behind computation.

↩︎ The Fleet Law and its compute, communication, and coordination terms are established in **[[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/00 Introduction/01 Introduction/V2-01.06 The C³ Taxonomy and Foundations of Scale#^sec-vol2-introduction-fleet-law|The Fleet Law]]**.

The **communication-computation ratio**, ${\rho}= {T_{\text{comm}}}({N})/({T_{\text{compute}}}/{N})$, compares communication time with ideally partitioned compute time for fixed global work. It is the time consequence of communication intensity ${\text{CI}}$ (**[[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/00 Introduction/01 Introduction/V2-01.03 From Single Node to Fleet#^eq-ci-ratio|equation 1.1]]**), the bytes moved per operation of the workload. When transfers run at link bandwidth ${\beta}$ and each device computes at sustained rate ${R_{\text{peak}}}{\eta_{\text{hw}}}$, ${\rho}\approx {\text{CI}} \times {R_{\text{peak}}}{\eta_{\text{hw}}}/{\beta}$, so the same workload becomes more communication-bound on faster accelerators or slower links. This ratio identifies the pressure to overlap communication; the complete step time also depends on how much exchange is hidden and how much coordination remains exposed.

↩︎ The ${\alpha}$-${\beta}$ message model, with startup latency ${\alpha}$ and sustained bandwidth ${\beta}$, is established in **[[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/01 The Fleet/03 Network Fabrics/V2-03.05 Level 2 Transport and the Performance Model#^sec-network-fabrics-performance-model|The α-β performance model]]**.

↪ The communication-computation ratio, evaluated for representative data-parallel and point-to-point transfers and related to communication intensity, is developed in **[[05 Reference/Volume II - Scaling/V2 Fleet Foundations#^sec-appdx-fleet-foundations-comm-compute-ratio|The communication-computation ratio]]**.

Two regimes follow from this ratio. When ${T_{\text{compute}}}/{N}\gg {T_{\text{comm}}}({N})$, the cluster is compute-bound, and communication is small relative to useful computation, provided coordination and input delivery do not introduce another limit. Large-batch training of a dense convolutional model such as ResNet commonly sits here. When ${\rho}\gtrsim 1$, ${T_{\text{comm}}}({N})$ matches or exceeds ${T_{\text{compute}}}/{N}$ and the cluster is communication-bound. Insufficient overlap then leaves substantial time waiting for gradients or activations. Large language models (LLMs) with billions of parameters to synchronize frequently land in this regime. Deep Learning Recommendation Model (DLRM)-style workloads also load the network through their embedding exchanges, but memory capacity and lookup rate often limit them first ([[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/02 Distributed ML/06 Distributed Training/V2-06.06 Model Parallelism#^sec-distributed-training-systems-systems-parameter-servers-embedding-sharding-8821|section 6.5.6]]). These are typical operating points rather than fixed labels. Batch size, partitioning, and placement can move the same workload across the boundary, so the ratio must be measured for the configuration at hand. Where a workload lands also depends on how its training loop is cut across devices, the choice each parallelism strategy makes.

↪ The optimizations that help, and the ones that are wasted, for a compute-bound, communication-bound, or coordination-bound fleet are collected for reference in **[[05 Reference/Volume II - Scaling/V2 The C³ Taxonomy#^sec-appdx-c3-taxonomy-bottleneck-table|The bottleneck diagnostic table]]**.

**Self-Check: Question**

1.  A research team is training a 3B-parameter transformer that fits comfortably in one H100 GPU’s 80 GB of memory, but projected single-GPU training would take 18 months to reach target loss. The dataset is 3 TB and fits on local NVMe storage. Which signal makes distributed training necessary in this scenario?

    1.  Dataset exceeding GPU HBM capacity, which automatically mandates tensor parallelism regardless of execution time
    2.  The presence of NVLink inside the node, which makes multi-machine scaling mandatory by default
    3.  Unacceptable wall-clock training duration, even though single-device memory capacity and local storage are sufficient
    4.  Fault tolerance limitations, because single-GPU training cannot survive hardware restarts

2.  A team doubles a data-parallel cluster from 64 to 128 GPUs and observes that total step time drops by only 12 percent even though per-worker compute time halves. Using the distributed step-time equation $T_{\text{step}}(N) = T_{\text{compute}}/N + T_{\text{comm}}(N) + T_{\text{sync}}(N) - T_{\text{overlap}}$, explain what the observation establishes and what measurement would distinguish possible causes.
