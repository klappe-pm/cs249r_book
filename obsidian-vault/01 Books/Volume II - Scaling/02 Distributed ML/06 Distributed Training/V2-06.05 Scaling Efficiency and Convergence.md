# V2-06.05 Scaling Efficiency and Convergence

[[Home|Home]] · [[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/Volume 2 Overview|Volume Overview]] · [[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/02 Distributed ML/06 Distributed Training/V2-06 Distributed Training|V2-06 Distributed Training]]

^sec-distributed-training-systems-systems-distributed-training-efficiency-metrics-9488

Data parallelism and ZeRO sharding show how communication and synchronization enter every training step, yet raw step throughput alone cannot judge a distributed run. When doubling the number of GPUs yields only 1.5$\times$ speedup for the same work, scaling efficiency is 75 percent, and if the larger global batch also requires more optimizer updates to reach the same loss, wall-clock time to quality degrades further. Evaluating a distributed configuration therefore requires separating two questions: how efficiently added devices execute each step, and how batch growth or stale updates affect the number of steps to convergence. The hardware scaling metrics below apply to every parallelism strategy; the convergence analysis that follows concerns data-parallel batch growth and synchronization slack, establishing a batch-size constraint that tensor and pipeline configurations must also respect when combined with data parallelism.

### The Mathematics of Scaling Efficiency

^sec-distributed-training-scaling-math

That first question is answered by scaling efficiency, the fleet-level ratio introduced with the Fleet Law in **[[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/00 Introduction/01 Introduction/V2-01.06 The C³ Taxonomy and Foundations of Scale#^sec-vol2-introduction-fleet-law|The Fleet Law]]** and reported as parallel efficiency in the GPT-2 scaling notebooks. If a model trains in time ${T_1}$ on one device, ideal (linear) scaling would train it in time ${T_1}/{N}$ on ${N}$ devices; communication overhead, pipeline bubbles, and load imbalance widen the gap between that ideal and the realized time ${T_N}$. [[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/02 Distributed ML/06 Distributed Training/V2-06.05 Scaling Efficiency and Convergence#^eq-distributed-training-scaling-efficiency|Equation 1]] formalizes the ratio:

$$
{\eta_{\text{scaling}}} = \frac{{T_1}}{{N}\times {T_N}} \tag{1}
$$

^eq-distributed-training-scaling-efficiency

where ${T_N}$ is the training time on ${N}$ devices. Measured per step of the same work, ${T_1}$ is the quantity the Fleet Law writes as ${T_{\text{compute}}}$, the single-device compute time that its compute term divides by ${N}$. An efficiency of 1.0 means perfect linear scaling; an efficiency of 0.5 means the run achieves only half the ideal speedup.

**Definition 6.4: Scaling efficiency**

***Scaling efficiency*** $({\eta_{\text{scaling}}})$ is the ratio of actual training throughput to ideal linear throughput when increasing the number of ML compute devices (${N}$).

1.  **Significance**: The ratio in [[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/02 Distributed ML/06 Distributed Training/V2-06.05 Scaling Efficiency and Convergence#^eq-distributed-training-scaling-efficiency|equation 1]] measures added devices against the specified baseline. At 0.50 efficiency, a 10,000-GPU run delivers the throughput that 5,000 perfectly scaled devices would, so half of the accelerator-hours buy no progress relative to ideal scaling. The equivalence is to an idealized baseline. A real 5,000-GPU run would have its own, typically higher, efficiency, and the loss is spread across every device as waiting rather than concentrated in idle ones.
2.  **Distinction**: Unlike single-node efficiency, which captures local bottlenecks like ${\text{BW}}$, scaling efficiency measures how performance changes with device count. Communication time $({T_{\text{comm}}}({N}))$ and synchronization contribute, but changing local kernel shapes or input delivery can also affect the ratio.
3.  **Common pitfall**: A frequent misconception is that scaling efficiency is constant. In reality, it depends on both device count and problem size. For a fixed model, the communication-to-compute ratio typically worsens as ${N}$ increases (Amdahl’s Law). At a fixed ${N}$, a smaller model or local batch leaves less computation per device to hide startup latency and coordination, so its efficiency falls sooner.

The Fleet Law leads to the scaling efficiency metric for fixed global work. Substituting its step time (principle [[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/02 Distributed ML/V2 Distributed ML Principles#^pri-distributed-step-time|10]]) for ${T_N}$ in [[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/02 Distributed ML/06 Distributed Training/V2-06.05 Scaling Efficiency and Convergence#^eq-distributed-training-scaling-efficiency|equation 1]], with ${T_{\text{compute}}}$ standing for ${T_1}$ per step as noted there, gives [[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/02 Distributed ML/06 Distributed Training/V2-06.05 Scaling Efficiency and Convergence#^eq-distributed-training-scaling-efficiency-fleet|equation 2]]:

$$
{\eta_{\text{scaling}}} = \frac{{T_{\text{compute}}}}{{N}\times {T_{\text{step}}}({N})} = \frac{1}{1 + \frac{{N}({T_{\text{comm}}}({N}) + {T_{\text{sync}}}({N}) - {T_{\text{overlap}}})}{{T_{\text{compute}}}}} \tag{2}
$$

^eq-distributed-training-scaling-efficiency-fleet

This form states the scaling efficiency bound (principle [[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/02 Distributed ML/V2 Distributed ML Principles#^pri-scaling-efficiency|12]]), under which perfect linear scaling $({\eta_{\text{scaling}}} = 1.0)$ is a *theoretical limit*, not a *practical target*. Real systems fall short of it by an amount that depends on the configuration and grows with ${N}$. The 175B data-parallel example below reaches only 15 percent under its stated assumptions, and the hybrid layouts later in the chapter, priced on the reference training step rather than on this counterfactual, still stay below 1.0. The gap between ${\eta_{\text{scaling}}} = 1.0$ and the achieved efficiency is the price of distributed execution, paid in communication and coordination.

The equation also exposes the **scaling wall**. As ${N}$ increases, the compute term $({T_{\text{compute}}}/{N})$ shrinks, but the communication and synchronization terms can remain constant or grow. A ring AllReduce’s bandwidth term, for example, stays near its bound however many workers join (principle [[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/02 Distributed ML/V2 Distributed ML Principles#^pri-allreduce-bandwidth-bound|11]]). Eventually, the denominator is dominated by overhead, driving efficiency toward zero. The equation holds the global work fixed, which is strong scaling, and that regime reaches the wall sooner. Under weak scaling each device keeps its local batch, so its compute time per step stays level against the same ring term and the wall arrives later, while the growing global batch runs toward the statistical limit of [[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/02 Distributed ML/06 Distributed Training/V2-06.05 Scaling Efficiency and Convergence#^sec-distributed-training-systems-systems-critical-batch-size-parallelism-hurt-4961|section 6.4.6]].

For data-parallel training of the 175B reference model, the per-step communication cost is dominated by the AllReduce of 350 GB of gradients, and how much of that exchange hides behind computation decides the efficiency. The scaling efficiency depends on computation per communicated byte. At fixed tokens per update, increasing parameter count raises both transformer computation and gradient bytes roughly proportionally, so model size alone does not guarantee better scaling. Larger batches raise computation per optimizer step without changing gradient size. Large batches, however, can harm convergence and so require learning-rate tuning and warmup schedules.

Network bandwidth enters directly because doubling InfiniBand bandwidth halves the bandwidth term, but efficiency changes according to the full step-time denominator rather than in direct proportion. The network fabric is therefore a first-order determinant of cluster productivity, not a secondary concern. Every point of scaling efficiency it recovers is returned across the much larger accelerator investment, which is why fabric spending is judged by the efficiency it buys rather than by its own price.

These factors interact, so efficiency cannot be inferred from model size or GPU count alone. The following worked model fixes the parameter count, tokens per update, accelerator count, effective bandwidth, local compute efficiency before communication, and overlap before calculating one efficiency point. Changing any of those inputs requires recomputing the full step-time model.

**Napkin Math 6.5: Scaling efficiency for a 175B model**

**Setup**: A communication-only model for training a 175B model on a DGX H100 cluster with 400 Gb/s InfiniBand per GPU. Pure replicated data parallelism is a counterfactual baseline here: the calculation isolates gradient exchange but does not establish that the full training state fits on each device.

- **Compute per step** (assuming batch size 2M tokens, 6 FLOPs per parameter per token): ${O_{\text{step}}} = 6 \times 175 \times 10^9 \times 2 \times 10^6 \approx 2.1 \times 10^{18}$ FLOPs

- **Per-GPU compute time** on 1,024 GPUs, each at 1,979 TFLOP/s peak in the 8-bit floating-point (FP8) format developed in [[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/02 Distributed ML/06 Distributed Training/V2-06.05 Scaling Efficiency and Convergence#^sec-distributed-training-fp8|section 6.4.8]] (50 percent local compute efficiency before communication). The general matrix multiplications (GEMMs) run in FP8 while the gradients below still cross the wire in FP16, because the collective’s data type is chosen independently of the arithmetic’s: ${T_{\text{compute}}}/{N} \approx 2.1$ seconds

- **AllReduce time** (for 350 GB of gradients using ring-AllReduce with overlap): ${T_{\text{comm,exposed}}}({N}) \approx 11.9$ seconds (raw ring transfer takes $2 \times (N-1)/N \times 350 / 50$, about 14 seconds; the schedule could hide 75 percent of that transfer, but the available compute window caps hidden communication at 15 percent of raw transfer time)

- **Scaling efficiency**: ${\eta_{\text{scaling}}} = {T_{\text{compute,local}}}/({T_{\text{compute,local}}}+ {T_{\text{comm,exposed}}})$, which is [[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/02 Distributed ML/06 Distributed Training/V2-06.05 Scaling Efficiency and Convergence#^eq-distributed-training-scaling-efficiency-fleet|equation 2]] with the per-device compute window ${T_{\text{compute,local}}}= {T_{\text{compute}}}/{N}$ and the exposed term standing for ${T_{\text{comm}}}({N}) + {T_{\text{sync}}}({N}) - {T_{\text{overlap}}}$. Here the numerator is 2.1 s and the complete denominator is 14 s, giving 15 percent. The exposed communication term already subtracts the hidden portion from the raw transfer time and includes collective synchronization; subtracting overlap again would double-count the same saving.

**Systems insight**: The low efficiency (15 percent) shows why naive data parallelism at this scale is insufficient. It is one operating point, fixed by 2M per update, 1,024 GPUs, and 15 percent overlap, not a universal threshold. Another cluster size requires recomputing per-device compute time, collective cost, overlap, and topology. A hierarchical reduction, which first reduces inside each node over NVLink so that each InfiniBand port carries only a fraction of the gradient, shrinks the exposed term, and so does a longer compute window per step. [[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/02 Distributed ML/06 Distributed Training/V2-06.07 Hybrid Parallelism#^sec-distributed-training-systems-systems-hybrid-parallelism-12a0|Section 6.6]] prices both on the reference training step, where memory rather than communication rules out pure data parallelism.

↩︎ The compute window that bounds how much of a collective can hide is established in **[[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/02 Distributed ML/05 Collective Communication/V2-05.11 Communication-Computation Overlap#^sec-communication-overlap-limits|Overlap limits and exposed communication]]**.

#### Parallelism-Infrastructure Interaction

^sec-distributed-training-parallelism-infrastructure

The scaling efficiency of a run is set by the *interaction* between the model’s communication requirements and the infrastructure’s bandwidth hierarchy, not by the *model architecture alone*. Making a large model both fit and run efficiently means mapping each parallelism dimension onto the bandwidth tier that can carry its traffic. [[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/02 Distributed ML/06 Distributed Training/V2-06.07 Hybrid Parallelism#^sec-distributed-training-systems-systems-hybrid-parallelism-12a0|Section 6.6]] works through four concrete configurations, priced on the reference training step, once tensor and pipeline parallelism have been developed.

The collective algorithm adds its own dependence on message size. Tree-based AllReduce reaches every rank in $\mathcal{O}(\log {N})$ steps and suits small, latency-bound messages, whereas large gradient buffers favor the bandwidth-optimal ring; message size, topology, and contention decide between them.

↩︎ The message size at which ring AllReduce overtakes tree AllReduce, derived from the latency and bandwidth terms of each algorithm, is established in **[[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/02 Distributed ML/05 Collective Communication/V2-05.07 AllReduce Algorithms and Topologies#^sec-communication-collective-operations-algorithm-crossover|The algorithm crossover point]]**.

Even pre-frontier transformers carry gradient buffers large enough to demand substantial link bandwidth from that ring. BERT-Base (110M parameters) requires approximately 440 MB of gradient synchronization per iteration in FP32, while BERT-Large (340M parameters) requires approximately 1.4 GB. Because a ring AllReduce across 64 GPUs moves nearly twice the gradient bytes through each GPU’s link, finishing BERT-Large synchronization within 50 ms demands roughly 54 GB/s of sustained per-GPU bandwidth, and BERT-Base about a third of that. At 175B parameters, meeting the step budget requires combining 3D parallelism, gradient accumulation, and overlap rather than relying on a single flat collective, and whatever communication remains unhidden acts as the unscalable residual that Amdahl’s Law prices next.

### Scaling Physics and Amdahl’s Law with Communication

^sec-distributed-training-systems-systems-physics-scaling-amdahls-law-communication-4d7f

Even a small unscalable cost in a single-device step eventually dominates distributed execution, because adding ${N}$ devices shrinks the parallelizable compute share (${T_1} / {N}$) while leaving the fixed residual untouched. An Amdahl-style model separates that scalable work from the fixed residual fraction ${r}$ of the single-device baseline (**[[05 Reference/Volume II - Scaling/V2 Fleet Foundations#^sec-appdx-fleet-foundations-amdahls-fleet|Amdahl's Law at fleet scale]]**). In units of the single-device step, a distributed step then takes the scalable share divided by the device count plus the whole residual, and speedup is the reciprocal of that time.

That baseline fraction is not the synchronization share a profiler reports for a distributed step (the distributed Amdahl pitfall of **[[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/00 Introduction/01 Introduction/V2-01.06 The C³ Taxonomy and Foundations of Scale#^sec-vol2-introduction-fleet-law|The Fleet Law]]**), because the compute share has been divided by ${N}$ while the residual has not. A residual of 1 percent of the single-device step occupies about 84 percent of each step on 512 GPUs. Reading the observed share as ${r}$ would therefore overstate the residual many times over and predict a far lower speedup ceiling than the model implies.

Plotted on log-log axes up to 512 GPUs, the same fixed-residual model shows how much speedup each residual fraction gives up ([[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/02 Distributed ML/06 Distributed Training/V2-06.05 Scaling Efficiency and Convergence#^fig-scaling-tax|figure 9]]), complementing the scaling efficiency bound (principle [[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/02 Distributed ML/V2 Distributed ML Principles#^pri-scaling-efficiency|12]]). In this illustrative model, a small baseline fraction (${r}= 0.1\%$) stays closer to ideal scaling than a larger fraction (${r}= 1\%$). A residual equal to a tenth of baseline time (${r}= 0.10$) limits asymptotic speedup to $1/{r}= 10\times$. Actual collective and coordination costs can change with worker count, so predicting a training run requires the full step-time accounting of [[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/02 Distributed ML/06 Distributed Training/V2-06.05 Scaling Efficiency and Convergence#^eq-distributed-training-scaling-efficiency-fleet|equation 2]].

Published benchmark runs show the same shortfall in practice. MLPerf Training times complete runs to a fixed quality target, and several submitters report the same system at several sizes within one round ([[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/02 Distributed ML/06 Distributed Training/V2-06.14 Source References#^ref-mattson2020mlperf|Mattson et al. 2020]]; [[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/02 Distributed ML/06 Distributed Training/V2-06.14 Source References#^ref-mlcommons2025training|MLCommons 2025]]). Each of 5 such series for the GPT-3 and Llama 3.1 405B benchmarks falls below the ideal line drawn through its smallest configuration ([[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/02 Distributed ML/06 Distributed Training/V2-06.05 Scaling Efficiency and Convergence#^fig-mlperf-training-scaling|figure 10]]). On NVIDIA’s Eos cluster, GPT-3 trained in about 58 minutes on 512 H100 GPUs and 3.9 minutes on 10,752. A 21× increase in accelerators therefore cut the time 14.9×, a scaling efficiency ([[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/02 Distributed ML/06 Distributed Training/V2-06.05 Scaling Efficiency and Convergence#^eq-distributed-training-scaling-efficiency|equation 1]], measured relative to the smallest configuration rather than one device) of 71 percent. Across the series, the largest configuration keeps 71–84 percent of ideal. Two effects compound in these numbers. Communication and coordination grow with worker count, and the larger configurations also train with larger global batches, which can require more samples to reach the target ([[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/02 Distributed ML/06 Distributed Training/V2-06.05 Scaling Efficiency and Convergence#^sec-distributed-training-systems-systems-critical-batch-size-parallelism-hurt-4961|section 6.4.6]]). Predicting a run means pricing each term of [[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/02 Distributed ML/06 Distributed Training/V2-06.05 Scaling Efficiency and Convergence#^eq-distributed-training-scaling-efficiency-fleet|equation 2]] for the configuration at hand. For a large-message ring AllReduce, the communication term’s bandwidth contribution is $\frac{2({N}-1)}{{N}} \frac{{D_{\text{vol}}}}{{\beta}}$. This is the fleet counterpart of the single-machine iron law’s data-movement ratio ${D_{\text{vol}}}/{\text{BW}}$. Here ${D_{\text{vol}}}$ is the payload being reduced (the gradient bytes ${M_{\text{grad}}}$ under data parallelism, or a shard of model state under sharding), and ${\beta}$ is sustained per-link bandwidth. The expression omits per-message startup latency, and a wait it already counts must not be charged again under synchronization.

↪ The single-machine iron law and its data-movement term are developed in **[[05 Reference/Volume II - Scaling/V2 Single-Machine Foundations#^sec-appdx-dam-taxonomy-iron-law-mapping|Iron Law Mapping]]**.

This communication overhead costs energy as well as wall-clock time, and the energy cost of communication grows with the physical distance between devices. At the scale of 10,000 GPUs, moving data is itself a power load. A full H100 NVLink envelope of roughly 9 PB/s at 7.5 pJ/bit represents about 540 kW before cooling and switch overhead. The smaller NDR InfiniBand envelope (500 TB/s at 35 pJ/bit) still represents about 140 kW. The per-boundary accounting behind these per-bit costs is developed in **[[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/02 Distributed ML/05 Collective Communication/V2-05.03 The Physics of Data Movement#^sec-communication-collective-operations-physics-data-movement|The Physics of Data Movement]]**. Overlap hides communication time but not its energy, so avoiding unnecessary movement is the direct way to reduce the power term.

As the device count grows for a fixed workload, the balance can move from a linear scaling regime toward a communication-bound regime. The transition has no universal GPU count, because it depends on compute per device, message sizes, topology, and overlap. Coordination overhead becomes consequential when its exposed time is no longer small relative to useful computation. Measurements at the intended workload and placement must establish the efficiency curve.

The efficiency metrics directly influence the choice of parallelism strategy. Data parallelism works well in the linear scaling regime but becomes communication bound at scale. The model-parallel strategies developed after this section replace part of that gradient traffic with other costs, which the same efficiency equation prices once each mechanism is in place.

Hardware efficiency metrics govern throughput, yet convergence theory determines whether distributed training reaches the same solution quality as single-device training. Parallelism affects optimization convergence in four ways: convergence rate changes with batch size, stale updates can weaken that rate, learning-rate schedules must be revalidated when the global batch changes, and adding workers yields diminishing returns beyond the critical batch size.

### Convergence Rate for Synchronous Data Parallel SGD

^sec-distributed-training-systems-systems-convergence-rate-synchronous-data-parallel-sgd-2ed5

Averaging gradients across ${N}$ workers processes ${N}$ times as many examples per step, but only reduces the number of steps to convergence when gradient noise, rather than loss curvature, is what holds back a larger step size. Formally, let ${\mathcal{L}}$ be lower bounded by ${\mathcal{L}_{\inf}}$ and have ${L_s}$-Lipschitz gradients, so the smoothness constant limits how sharply the gradient can change as parameters move. Each worker computes gradients from ${B_{\text{local}}}$ independent samples. At update ${t}$, the aggregated estimator ${\mathbf{g}}_t$, the average of the ${N}$ workers’ local gradients, is unbiased, and its conditional variance satisfies $\mathbb{E}[\|{\mathbf{g}}_t-\nabla{\mathcal{L}}({\theta}_t)\|^2\mid{\theta}_t]\leq{\sigma}^2/({N} {B_{\text{local}}})$. When averaging reduces that noise, synchronous SGD makes steady progress toward parameters where the gradient is small. These assumptions bound progress toward a small gradient norm, not convergence to a global minimizer or a particular validation accuracy.

**Theorem 6.1: Convergence rate for distributed SGD**

For the update ${\theta}_{t+1}={\theta}_t-{\eta}{\mathbf{g}}_t$ with constant step size $0<{\eta}\leq 1/{L_s}$, after ${T}$ synchronized updates,

$$
\frac{1}{{T}}\sum_{t=0}^{{T}-1}\mathbb{E}\!\left[\|\nabla{\mathcal{L}}({\theta}_t)\|^2\right] \leq \underbrace{\frac{2\bigl({\mathcal{L}}({\theta}_0)-{\mathcal{L}_{\inf}}\bigr)}{{\eta} {T}}}_{\text{optimization term}} + \underbrace{\frac{{\eta} {L_s}{\sigma}^2}{{N} {B_{\text{local}}}}}_{\text{stochastic-variance term}}.
$$

Choosing ${\eta}=\min\!\left\{1/{L_s},\sqrt{2({\mathcal{L}}({\theta}_0)-{\mathcal{L}_{\inf}}){N} {B_{\text{local}}}/({L_s}{\sigma}^2{T})}\right\}$ balances the two terms. Before the smoothness cap becomes active, the stochastic contribution scales as $\mathcal{O}(1/\sqrt{{N} {B_{\text{local}}} {T}})$.

The theorem isolates the benefit that follows from the assumptions. With equal local batch size ${B_{\text{local}}}$, synchronous data-parallel SGD computes the same minibatch estimator as one worker using batch size ${N}\times {B_{\text{local}}}$ for that update, and the estimator variance decreases as $1/({N}\times {B_{\text{local}}})$. That product appears only in the stochastic-variance term of the bound, so at a fixed step size a larger batch shrinks that term and leaves the optimization term alone. The reduction in updates comes through the step size. A smaller variance term lets the balanced step size grow, and the updates the bound requires fall in inverse proportion to the batch. Once the step size sits at the smoothness cap, the optimization term sets the update count, and added samples no longer reduce it in proportion. The critical batch size marks that diminishing return. How far added samples reduce those updates is the statistical efficiency of distributed training, separate from hardware efficiency. Because the theorem assumes perfect synchronization (BSP), convergence guarantees degrade as staleness increases whenever workers proceed at different rates or apply stale gradients.

### Staleness Impact Across Synchronization Models

^sec-distributed-training-systems-systems-staleness-impact-bsp-vs-ssp-vs-asp-e153

When a slow worker applies a gradient computed on an older parameter version, the update pushes the model along a direction that the current parameters may have already outgrown. The staleness parameter ${\tau_{\text{stale}}}$ counts how many updates occur between computing and applying a gradient, tying the barrier savings of BSP, SSP, and ASP ([[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/02 Distributed ML/06 Distributed Training/V2-06.04 Data Parallelism#^sec-distributed-training-systems-systems-synchronization-models-396f|section 6.3.1.5]]) to solution quality ([[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/02 Distributed ML/06 Distributed Training/V2-06.14 Source References#^ref-ho2013more|Ho et al. 2013]]; [[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/02 Distributed ML/06 Distributed Training/V2-06.14 Source References#^ref-dutta2018slow|Dutta et al. 2018]]). Because the damage from stale gradients depends on the objective, step-size schedule, worker count, and delay distribution, no single additive penalty applies across all runs.

**Definition 6.5: Gradient staleness**

***Gradient staleness*** (${\tau_{\text{stale}}}$) is the number of parameter updates that occur between the time a gradient is computed and the time it is applied to the global model state.

1.  **Significance**: It represents the synchronization error in distributed optimization. Increasing ${\tau_{\text{stale}}}$ can improve throughput by reducing barrier waits $({T_{\text{sync}}}({N}))$, but it typically degrades the rate of convergence, requiring more optimizer steps, and therefore more FLOPs, to reach the same accuracy.
2.  **Distinction**: Unlike network latency, which is a physical delay, staleness is an algorithmic offset that arises from the choice of synchronization protocol (e.g., ASP, SSP).
3.  **Common pitfall**: A frequent misconception is that staleness is “always bad.” In reality, it is a throughput-convergence trade-off: for some large-scale workloads, allowing bounded staleness is one practical way to keep thousands of GPUs in use.

The convergence behavior differs across these models in ways that directly affect training cost and solution quality. In BSP (${\tau_{\text{stale}}}=0$), all workers compute on the same parameter version and aggregate before updating. Under the assumptions of theorem [6.1](https://mlsysbook.ai/vol2/06_distributed_training/06_distributed_training.html#thrm-distributed-training-convergence-rate-distributed-sgd), the synchronized estimator inherits the ${N}\times {B_{\text{local}}}$ minibatch guarantee.

SSP (${\tau_{\text{stale}}}\leq {s}$) allows workers to proceed up to ${s}$ iterations ahead of the slowest worker. Ho et al. ([[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/02 Distributed ML/06 Distributed Training/V2-06.14 Source References#^ref-ho2013more|2013]]) bound the average regret of their SSP update sequence at $\mathcal{O}(\sqrt{({s}+1){N}/{T}})$ for ${N}$ workers and ${T}$ updates. Average regret is the per-update gap between the loss its iterates incur and the loss of the best fixed parameters chosen in hindsight. The bound assumes convex Lipschitz component functions, a bounded domain, and a decreasing step size. It makes the staleness dependence explicit, since the bound grows with ${s}$. It measures a different quantity from the gradient-norm bound of theorem [6.1](https://mlsysbook.ai/vol2/06_distributed_training/06_distributed_training.html#thrm-distributed-training-convergence-rate-distributed-sgd), which covers nonconvex objectives, so the two rates cannot be compared directly.

Asynchronous SGD imposes no protocol-wide finite delay bound. Removing the barrier can improve update throughput, but a convergence rate requires additional assumptions about realized delays, the objective, and the step-size schedule. Dutta et al. ([[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/02 Distributed ML/06 Distributed Training/V2-06.14 Source References#^ref-dutta2018slow|2018]]) analyze this as an error-runtime trade-off in which worker speed and gradient staleness jointly determine whether accepting a delayed update helps. Asynchronous execution alone therefore sets no fixed delay penalty and does not by itself forfeit the ${N}$-worker variance reduction; both depend on those assumptions. The protocol fixes each model’s delay control, but the strength of its convergence guarantee depends on further assumptions ([[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/02 Distributed ML/06 Distributed Training/V2-06.05 Scaling Efficiency and Convergence#^tbl-convergence-comparison|table 4]]).

### Learning Rate Scaling Rules

^sec-distributed-training-systems-systems-learning-rate-scaling-rules-fa26

Even under zero-staleness BSP (${\tau_{\text{stale}}}=0$ in [[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/02 Distributed ML/06 Distributed Training/V2-06.05 Scaling Efficiency and Convergence#^tbl-convergence-comparison|table 4]]), scaling out across ${N}$ workers changes the optimization trajectory whenever the global batch grows with the cluster. Multiplying the global batch by ${k}$ cuts the number of optimizer updates per epoch by the same factor, so keeping the single-GPU learning rate leaves the model under-trained, while jumping straight to a ${k}$-fold larger step size can destabilize early epochs. Whenever data parallelism changes the effective batch size, the learning-rate schedule must be revalidated against the target quality; two scaling rules provide starting points for that adjustment.

The linear scaling rule ([[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/02 Distributed ML/06 Distributed Training/V2-06.14 Source References#^ref-goyal2017accurate|Goyal et al. 2017]]) proposes that when batch size is multiplied by ${k}$, learning rate also increases by ${k}$:

$$
{\eta_{\text{new}}} = {k}\cdot {\eta_{\text{base}}}
$$

The rule rests on an approximation that compares one large-batch step at learning rate ${k}{\eta}$ with ${k}$ consecutive small-batch steps at learning rate ${\eta}$. At batch size ${k}\times {B}$, independent gradient contributions reduce estimator variance by factor ${k}$, but lower variance alone does not establish stability. The approximation also requires gradients to change little over the corresponding ${k}$ small-batch steps, because only then do those steps add up to nearly the same update as the one large step. Goyal et al. ([[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/02 Distributed ML/06 Distributed Training/V2-06.14 Source References#^ref-goyal2017accurate|2017]]) validated the recipe for ResNet-50/ImageNet at global batch size 8,192 with gradual warmup. A warmup period increases the learning rate from ${\eta_{\text{base}}}$ to ${k}\cdot {\eta_{\text{base}}}$ over the first ${W}$ iterations to reduce early instability; the schedule still requires validation:

$$
{\eta}_t = {\eta_{\text{base}}}+ \frac{{t}}{{W}}({k}\cdot {\eta_{\text{base}}}- {\eta_{\text{base}}}) \quad \text{for } {t}< {W}
$$

The rule and the warmup were introduced together, and the run that introduced them shows why the scaled learning rate needs the warmup. A learning-rate schedule tuned for an eight-GPU run need not reach the same accuracy at 256 GPUs once the global batch grows with the worker count.

**War Story 6.1: The one-hour ImageNet run (2017)**

**Context**: Facebook AI Research set out to train ResNet-50 on ImageNet in one hour using 256 GPUs, pushing data parallelism far beyond the batch sizes that earlier practice treated as safe ([[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/02 Distributed ML/06 Distributed Training/V2-06.14 Source References#^ref-goyal2017accurate|Goyal et al. 2017]]).

**Mechanism**: Scaling the global batch to 8,192 cut the number of optimizer updates per epoch by the same factor. Keeping the small-batch learning rate left too few effective updates to reach the target accuracy, while scaling the learning rate up by the same factor destabilized optimization in the first epochs, when the loss surface changes fastest.

**Impact**: Without the gradual warmup recipe, the large-batch runs reported higher training and validation error than the small-batch baseline. Higher hardware throughput alone did not preserve the quality target.

**Fix**: The team introduced the linear learning-rate scaling rule paired with a 5-epoch gradual warmup schedule, stabilizing optimization at batch size 8,192.

**Systems lesson**: Distributed training demands more than parallel hardware alone. Scaling changes the optimization regime, so the cluster, communication schedule, batch size, and learning-rate schedule must be tuned as one system.

The square root scaling rule is an alternative learning-rate heuristic to test when increasing batch size:

$$
{\eta_{\text{new}}} = \sqrt{{k}} \cdot {\eta_{\text{base}}}
$$

This rule increases the learning rate more slowly than linear scaling. Its suitability depends on the optimizer, schedule, and training regime; exceeding a critical-batch estimate does not make it mandatory or guarantee convergence. The learning rate must be validated against the same target quality.

Layer-wise adaptive rate scaling (LARS) and layer-wise adaptive moments based optimizer (LAMB) provide related large-batch approaches for ImageNet and BERT-style pretraining ([[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/02 Distributed ML/06 Distributed Training/V2-06.14 Source References#^ref-you2017large|You et al. 2017]]; [[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/02 Distributed ML/06 Distributed Training/V2-06.14 Source References#^ref-you2020large|You et al. 2020]]). LARS ([[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/02 Distributed ML/06 Distributed Training/V2-06.14 Source References#^ref-you2017large|You et al. 2017]]) scales each layer’s update relative to its weight norm. The simplified LARS expression below illustrates the ratio of weight norm to gradient norm with weight decay. LAMB ([[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/02 Distributed ML/06 Distributed Training/V2-06.14 Source References#^ref-you2020large|You et al. 2020]]) applies a related layer-wise adaptation to an Adam-based update rather than using this raw-gradient denominator:

$$
{\eta}_\ell = {\eta_{\text{global}}}\cdot \frac{\|{\mathbf{w}}_\ell\|}{\|{\mathbf{g}}_\ell\| + {\lambda_{\text{reg}}}\|{\mathbf{w}}_\ell\|}
$$

Here ${\lambda_{\text{reg}}}$ is the weight-decay coefficient in the illustrated LARS expression. Layer-wise scaling controls update magnitude relative to parameter magnitude, but does not guarantee convergence for arbitrary batches. Its systems role is to extend the range of validated batch sizes over which added data-parallel workers can reduce time to quality.

### Critical Batch Size and Diminishing Returns

^sec-distributed-training-systems-systems-critical-batch-size-parallelism-hurt-4961

Increasing the global batch eventually yields diminishing reductions in the optimization steps needed to reach a fixed target quality. The critical batch size ${B^{*}}$ marks the transition point beyond which increasing batch size yields diminishing returns in convergence per sample seen.

**Definition 6.6: Critical batch size**

***Critical batch size*** $({B^{*}})$ is a workload-dependent crossover batch size beyond which further batch growth yields diminishing reductions in optimization steps per added sample ([[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/02 Distributed ML/06 Distributed Training/V2-06.14 Source References#^ref-mccandlish2018empirical|McCandlish et al. 2018]]; [[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/02 Distributed ML/06 Distributed Training/V2-06.14 Source References#^ref-Shallue2019measuring|Shallue et al. 2019]]).

1.  **Significance**: It characterizes statistical scaling, separately from hardware throughput. In the gradient-noise model, batches well below ${B^{*}}$ can reduce step count approximately in proportion to batch growth; above it, additional samples buy progressively smaller step-count reductions.
2.  **Distinction**: Unlike the memory-limited batch size (determined by memory capacity and activation footprint), the critical batch size depends on the model, data, optimizer, training phase, and target quality.
3.  **Common pitfall**: A frequent misconception is that training can be sped up indefinitely by adding GPUs. In reality, ${B^{*}}$ is a guide to diminishing statistical returns, not a hard physical ceiling or a universal optimum in wall-clock time.

The gradient-noise-scale proxy for the critical batch size can be estimated as:

$$
{B^{*}}\approx \frac{\text{tr}({\Sigma})}{\|\nabla {\mathcal{L}}({\theta})\|^2}
$$

where $\text{tr}({\Sigma})$ is the trace of the gradient covariance matrix (total gradient variance) and $\|\nabla {\mathcal{L}}({\theta})\|^2$ is the squared gradient norm (signal strength). Intuitively, ${B^{*}}$ is the batch size at which averaging reduces gradient variance to the level of the true gradient magnitude.

Published large-batch regimes illustrate the scale rather than providing fixed thresholds. On ImageNet, Goyal et al. ([[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/02 Distributed ML/06 Distributed Training/V2-06.14 Source References#^ref-goyal2017accurate|2017]]) stabilized ResNet-50 at global batch 8,192 with warmup, and LARS extends ImageNet large-batch training further in some settings ([[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/02 Distributed ML/06 Distributed Training/V2-06.14 Source References#^ref-you2017large|You et al. 2017]]). For BERT-Large pretraining, LAMB reports training at 32K–64K batch sizes with layer-wise adaptive updates ([[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/02 Distributed ML/06 Distributed Training/V2-06.14 Source References#^ref-you2020large|You et al. 2020]]). Across other domains, McCandlish et al. ([[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/02 Distributed ML/06 Distributed Training/V2-06.14 Source References#^ref-mccandlish2018empirical|2018]]) show useful batch-size limits ranging from tens of thousands in ImageNet-like settings to millions in reinforcement-learning workloads, so new frontier-language-model thresholds should be measured rather than copied.

The gradient-noise model separates statistical efficiency from hardware efficiency. Well below ${B^{*}}$, where gradient noise rather than loss curvature holds back the step size, increasing batch size can reduce optimization steps almost proportionally while preserving similar total sample use. Near ${B^{*}}$, the trade-off between fewer updates and more samples becomes material. Above it, curvature caps the step size, so step count approaches a lower limit while sample use continues to increase. Neither maximum samples per second nor minimum training time necessarily occurs at ${B^{*}}$: those objectives also depend on device utilization, communication, and the validated learning-rate schedule.

On log-log axes, the gradient-noise trade-off appears as a divergence between optimization step count and total sample consumption ([[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/02 Distributed ML/06 Distributed Training/V2-06.05 Scaling Efficiency and Convergence#^fig-critical-batch-size|figure 11]]). In the normalized model plotted there, steps relative to their minimum equal one plus the critical batch divided by the batch, and samples relative to their minimum equal one plus the batch divided by the critical batch. Below the critical threshold (${B}/{B^{*}}< 1$), increasing batch size yields near-linear reductions in optimization steps with negligible sample waste. Past the crossover point at ${B}/{B^{*}}= 1$ (where both normalized costs equal 2), the step count curve asymptotically flattens toward 1 while total examples processed climb steeply along the $1 + {B}/{B^{*}}$ trajectory.

![[90 Assets/Volume II/06 Distributed Training/Cell 28 Output 1 94f2ff4d51.png]]

Figure 11: **Statistical Cost of Larger Batches**: A normalized illustration of the gradient-noise trade-off model, with batch size divided by the fitted critical batch. Optimization steps approach a lower limit as batches grow, while total examples processed increase. The crossover is not a hardware-throughput optimum or a measured threshold for a particular model ([[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/02 Distributed ML/06 Distributed Training/V2-06.14 Source References#^ref-mccandlish2018empirical|McCandlish et al. 2018]]).

^fig-critical-batch-size

Dividing the critical batch by the per-worker batch ${B_{\text{local}}}$ estimates where worker growth leaves the near-proportional statistical scaling regime. With one local batch per update and its size held fixed, the crossover occurs around ${N}\approx {B^{*}}/{B_{\text{local}}}$ workers. Past that point, additional workers mainly raise sample throughput that the optimizer cannot convert into proportionally fewer steps. Gradient accumulation increases each worker’s contribution per update and lowers the corresponding worker count. Shrinking ${B_{\text{local}}}$ moves the crossover outward but leaves less computation per worker to hide communication, coupling statistical and hardware efficiency.

That crossover informs a cost-time trade-off rather than setting a mandatory worker count. Larger batches may still reduce time to target even as they consume more examples. That benefit must be established with measured step time and a validated learning-rate schedule at the same target quality. Changing synchronization or compression also changes the optimization process, so its communication savings must be weighed against any additional steps required.

### Communication Cost Versus Convergence Speed

^sec-distributed-training-systems-systems-tradeoff-communication-cost-vs--convergence-speed-cb7c-a7eb1a04

Communication savings matter only through the resulting time and cost to reach target model quality. Scaling a transformer language model from 8 to 64 workers, with a per-worker batch of ${B_{\text{local}}}= 32$, shows how shorter steps and extra iterations to the same target combine into that cost.

**Napkin Math 6.6: Scaling from 8 to 64 workers**

**Setup**: This illustrative comparison assumes the iteration counts needed to reach one fixed target quality. They are scenario inputs, not measured convergence results or predictions from GPU count. The baseline is 100K iterations on single GPU with ${B_{\text{local}}}=$ 32.

**8 workers (BSP)**

- Effective batch size: ${B}=$ 8 $\times$ 32 = 256
- Learning rate: ${\eta}=$ 8 $\times {\eta_{\text{base}}}$ (illustrative candidate with warmup; validate for the workload)
- Expected iterations: 100K $/$ 8 = 12.5K iterations
- Assumed iterations to the same target: 12.8K iterations (98 percent efficiency)
- Communication overhead: 15 percent (NVLink intra-node)
- Wall-clock speedup: 100K $\times$ 1 $/$ (12.8K $\times$ 1.15×) = 6.8×

**64 workers (BSP)**

- Effective batch size: ${B}=$ 64 $\times$ 32 = 2,048
- Learning rate (${N}=$ 64 workers): ${\eta}= {N}\times {\eta_{\text{base}}}$ is the scenario’s assumed candidate schedule, not a prescription determined by ${B^{*}}$ alone
- Assuming ${B^{*}}\approx$ 4,000 (below critical): the useful-worker bound is ${B^{*}}/{B_{\text{local}}}\approx$ 4,000 $/$ 32 $\approx$ 125 workers, so 64 workers stay inside the near-proportional regime; this estimate does not validate a learning-rate schedule
- Expected iterations: 100K $/$ 64 = 1.56K iterations
- Assumed iterations to the same target: 1.72K iterations (91 percent efficiency)
- Communication overhead: 45 percent (InfiniBand inter-node, 8 nodes)
- Wall-clock speedup: 100K $\times$ 1 $/$ (1.72K $\times$ 1.45×) = 40.1×

**SSP workers**: 64 workers (${s}=$ 4)

- Same effective batch size: ${B}=$ 2,048
- Illustrative learning-rate reduction, not a staleness theorem: ${\eta'}= {\eta_{\text{BSP}}}/ \sqrt{1 + {s}}$ with ${s}=$ 4, giving ${\eta'}\approx$ 0.45 $\times {\eta_{\text{BSP}}}$
- Expected iterations: Higher due to staleness penalty
- Assumed iterations to the same target: 2.1K iterations (74 percent efficiency)
- Communication overhead: 25 percent (reduced synchronization)
- Wall-clock speedup: 100K $\times$ 1 $/$ (2.1K $\times$ 1.25×) = 38.1×

The 64-GPU BSP configuration achieves 40× speedup despite only 91 percent sample efficiency because the communication overhead (45 percent) is offset by the larger worker count. SSP at 64 workers comes close in wall-clock time with lower per-iteration communication (25 percent), but the extra iterations its stale updates require hold it to 38.1× against BSP’s 40.1×. In this scenario it therefore finishes slightly later and costs slightly more than 64-worker BSP.

- 8: 12.8K iters $\times$ 0.46 s/iter $\times$ 8/3600 $\times$ \$2/GPU-hour = \$26
- 64 BSP: 1.72K iters $\times$ 0.58 s/iter $\times$ 64/3600 $\times$ \$2/GPU-hour = \$35
- 64 SSP: 2.1K iters $\times$ 0.50 s/iter $\times$ 64/3600 $\times$ \$2/GPU-hour = \$37

Despite higher parallelism, 64-GPU training costs more per run due to communication overhead and reduced sample efficiency. The 8-GPU configuration is more cost-efficient, but its 6.8× speedup against the 64-GPU run’s 40.1× leaves it several times slower in wall-clock time. The choice depends on whether minimizing cost or minimizing time-to-result is the priority.

Moving a job along this cost-time frontier requires shrinking each message, synchronizing less often, or relaxing the global reduction topology. Gradient compression shrinks the payload directly by transmitting quantized, sparsified, or low-rank approximations. Examples include sign-based updates ([[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/02 Distributed ML/06 Distributed Training/V2-06.14 Source References#^ref-bernstein2018signsgd|Bernstein et al. 2018]]), Deep Gradient Compression ([[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/02 Distributed ML/06 Distributed Training/V2-06.14 Source References#^ref-lin2018deep|Lin et al. 2018]]), PowerSGD ([[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/02 Distributed ML/06 Distributed Training/V2-06.14 Source References#^ref-vogels2020powersgd|Vogels et al. 2019]]), quantized SGD with convergence analysis ([[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/02 Distributed ML/06 Distributed Training/V2-06.14 Source References#^ref-alistarh2017qsgd|Alistarh et al. 2017]]), and empirical sparse gradient dropping for neural machine translation ([[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/02 Distributed ML/06 Distributed Training/V2-06.14 Source References#^ref-aji2017|Aji and Heafield 2017]]).

↩︎ Quantized, sparsified, and low-rank gradient compression, the error feedback that keeps them convergent, and the criteria for when they pay, are established in **[[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/02 Distributed ML/05 Collective Communication/V2-05.10 Gradient Compression Under Bandwidth Scarcity#^sec-communication-collective-operations-collective-operations-gradient-compression-7a5c|Gradient Compression Under Bandwidth Scarcity]]**.

Instead of shrinking each payload, Local SGD lowers synchronization frequency by letting workers perform ${H_{\text{local}}}$ local updates before averaging, which cuts collective calls per local update by a factor of ${H_{\text{local}}}$. Results for smooth, strongly convex objectives can recover synchronous SGD’s asymptotic rate under specified learning-rate and local-update-interval conditions ([[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/02 Distributed ML/06 Distributed Training/V2-06.14 Source References#^ref-stich2019local|Stich 2019]]), so the synchronization interval remains part of the convergence contract rather than an unrestricted communication saving.

Where Local SGD keeps a global AllReduce at wider intervals, Decentralized SGD replaces the global collective with sparse neighbor exchanges along a communication graph. Immediate bandwidth demand drops, at the cost of taking additional rounds to spread information across the group. Graph connectivity, link service time, and the optimization effect of delayed mixing determine whether that trade-off helps a geographically distributed workload.

Choosing among these three levers depends on which bottleneck dominates and how many extra iterations the altered update costs. Each candidate must be judged by measured time to the same target quality. A narrower numeric format offers one more way to shrink the payload without changing the collective schedule, provided the collective transmits the narrower values.

### FP8 for Distributed Training

^sec-distributed-training-fp8

Running matrix multiplications in 8-bit floating point (FP8) accelerates local compute, which can make a distributed job *more* communication-bound if gradients still cross the network in 16-bit precision. Compute precision and communication precision are separate choices: casting GEMM operands to half as many bytes reduces operand storage and local memory traffic, but it halves a collective payload only when the communication path explicitly transmits that narrower representation.

↩︎ The throughput that narrower precisions buy on current accelerators is established in **[[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/01 The Fleet/02 Compute Infrastructure/V2-02.09 Accelerator Selection for Fleet Workloads#^sec-compute-infrastructure-precision-throughput-tradeoffs-49a5|Precision and throughput trade-offs]]**.

FP8 matters to distributed training only after four dtypes are distinguished: the stored parameter dtype, the GEMM operand dtype, the accumulator or master-state dtype, and the collective wire dtype. Traditional mixed-precision training commonly retains FP32 optimizer or master state while using FP16/BF16 operands. FP8-capable accelerators add E4M3 (four exponent bits and three mantissa bits) and E5M2 (five exponent bits and two mantissa bits) operand formats optimized for different tensor distributions ([[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/02 Distributed ML/06 Distributed Training/V2-06.14 Source References#^ref-micikevicius2022fp8|Micikevicius et al. 2022]]).

The two FP8 formats trade precision against range. E4M3 keeps an extra mantissa bit for scaled *weights and activations*, which cluster within a predictable range, while E5M2 keeps an extra exponent bit for scaled *gradients*, which can span many orders of magnitude during backpropagation. Because tensor magnitudes drift during training, each tensor is multiplied before conversion by a scale, commonly derived from its recent maximum absolute value, a policy called *dynamic scaling*. Neither the format nor the scaling policy guarantees that every value survives conversion, so training quality must be validated for each workload.

↪ Per-parameter training memory under each of these formats is collected for reference in **[[05 Reference/Volume II - Scaling/V2 Single-Machine Foundations#^sec-appdx-dam-taxonomy-training-memory|Numeric Precision and Training Memory]]**.

An FP8 training contract pairs FP32 master or optimizer state and FP8 GEMM operands with higher-precision accumulation, and it can approach FP16 training quality on suitable workloads ([[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/02 Distributed ML/06 Distributed Training/V2-06.14 Source References#^ref-micikevicius2022fp8|Micikevicius et al. 2022]]). FP8 halves bytes only for tensors actually stored or transmitted in FP8, so a collective that reduces FP16 gradients still moves FP16 bytes even when every preceding GEMM ran in FP8. FP8 therefore earns its place in distributed training when lower-precision compute shortens the local critical path, or when an explicit communication cast shrinks payloads while the accumulation and scaling policy protects convergence. It changes representation, not the parallelization axis. Compression, local updates, and narrower formats all make each data-parallel step cheaper, yet none of them partitions an individual layer or its activations; that remaining capacity constraint motivates model parallelism.
