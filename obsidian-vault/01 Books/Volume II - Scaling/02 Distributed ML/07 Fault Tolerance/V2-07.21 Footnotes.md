# V2-07.21 Footnotes

[[Home|Home]] · [[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/Volume 2 Overview|Volume Overview]] · [[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/02 Distributed ML/07 Fault Tolerance/V2-07 Fault Tolerance|V2-07 Fault Tolerance]]

^footnotes

<sup>1</sup> **Failure rate (${\lambda}$)**: Expressed in FITs (Failures In Time), where 1 FIT equals one failure per billion device-hours. A data center GPU with a 50,000-hour mean time to failure has $\text{FIT} = 20{,}000$, corresponding to ${\lambda_{\text{hour}}} = 2.0 \times 10^{-5}$ failures/hour. That seems negligible for one device but becomes dominant at fleet scale. A cluster with 10,000 GPUs accumulates 200,000,000 FITs from GPUs alone, translating to an expected GPU failure every 5 hours.

^fn1

<sup>2</sup> **Poisson process**: A statistical model for events occurring independently at a constant average rate. In this model, event counts follow a Poisson distribution and waiting times follow an exponential distribution, giving the survival function ${R_{\text{single}}}({t}) = e^{-{\lambda} {t}}$. With independent identical component hazards, the probability of at least one failure in a 10,000-component cluster is $1 - e^{-{N}{\lambda} {t}}$. The aggregate hazard ${N}{\lambda}$ scales linearly with component count; the failure probability is nonlinear in both ${N}$ and ${t}$, approaching ${N}{\lambda} {t}$ only when that product is small.

^fn2

<sup>3</sup> **GPU MTTF variation**: This chapter uses 50,000 hours as a planning constant for current data center GPUs (A100 and H100 class), not as a guarantee for every deployed GPU. Published fleet studies from Google TPUv4 pods and Meta GPU clusters report that field reliability depends on topology, cooling, workload, and operational practice ([[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/02 Distributed ML/07 Fault Tolerance/V2-07.20 Source References#^ref-zu2024tpuv4|Zu et al. 2024]]; [[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/02 Distributed ML/07 Fault Tolerance/V2-07.20 Source References#^ref-kokolis2025|Kokolis et al. 2025]]; [[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/02 Distributed ML/07 Fault Tolerance/V2-07.20 Source References#^ref-dubey2024llama|Dubey et al. 2024]]). The planning constant therefore anchors the arithmetic, while operators should substitute measured fleet rates when they have them.

^fn3

<sup>4</sup> **Single-event upset (SEU)**: From particle physics, where “upset” denotes a nondestructive state change. Cosmic rays and alpha particles from chip packaging can flip bits in memory and logic; the observed rate depends on altitude, process technology, packaging materials, shielding, and workload ([[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/02 Distributed ML/07 Fault Tolerance/V2-07.20 Source References#^ref-ziegler1996|Ziegler et al. 1996]]; [[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/02 Distributed ML/07 Fault Tolerance/V2-07.20 Source References#^ref-baumann2005|Baumann 2005]]). ECC memory corrects single-bit errors but cannot handle every multi-bit upset in the same word, leaving a residual silent-corruption risk that compounds across the terabytes of state in large-scale ML training.

^fn4

<sup>5</sup> **Byzantine-resilient ML**: Named after Lamport’s 1982 “Byzantine Generals Problem,” these algorithms (Krum, trimmed mean, and signSGD, which aggregates only the signs of gradient coordinates by majority vote) tolerate a bounded fraction of corrupted workers, with the exact bound depending on the rule and its assumptions. Krum’s original analysis requires $2{f} + 2 < {n}$ for ${f}$ Byzantine workers among ${n}$, while coordinate-wise median and trimmed mean rest on different robustness assumptions, so the tolerated fraction depends on the aggregation rule, dimensionality, and statistical model. For ${n}$ workers, Krum requires pairwise distance computations over worker gradients, so overhead grows quadratically with worker count and directly competes with the throughput gains of data parallelism ([[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/02 Distributed ML/07 Fault Tolerance/V2-07.20 Source References#^ref-blanchard2017machine|Blanchard et al. 2017]]).

^fn5

<sup>6</sup> **IEEE 754**: The IEEE 754 floating-point standard defines the binary32 format with 1 sign bit, 8 exponent bits, and 23 fraction bits ([[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/02 Distributed ML/07 Fault Tolerance/V2-07.20 Source References#^ref-ieee-754-2019-260483f5|IEEE Standards Association 2019]]). Because bit positions differ so much in consequence, resilience mechanisms often prioritize the sign and exponent bits rather than treating all bits as equally important.

^fn6

<sup>7</sup> **Electromigration**: Gradual displacement of metal atoms in conductors by electron momentum transfer, first characterized in early electromigration studies and later modeled in Black’s reliability equation ([[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/02 Distributed ML/07 Fault Tolerance/V2-07.20 Source References#^ref-black1969electromigration|Black 1969]]). Mean time to failure decreases with higher current density and higher temperature, so sustained high-power accelerator workloads make thermal management a direct determinant of fleet lifespan.

^fn7

<sup>8</sup> **Burn-in testing**: A controlled screening procedure stresses components before deployment to expose early-life defects. Temperature, voltage, duration, and acceptance criteria depend on the component and its qualified test procedure; a generic semiconductor screening recipe is not an operating specification for an assembled accelerator. Screening reduces the defective population entering service but does not certify every later workload or fault mode.

^fn8

<sup>9</sup> **Hamming codes (1950)**: Richard Hamming developed error-correcting codes at Bell Labs after relay computers repeatedly detected errors in his unattended weekend jobs and abandoned them rather than repairing them. A Hamming code uses parity checks to locate a single-bit error with $\mathcal{O}(\log {n})$ check-bit overhead; adding an overall parity bit yields the extended single-error-correcting, double-error-detecting form. Memory ECC implementations vary in code and coverage, so the system must account for both corrected errors and residual faults outside that coverage.

^fn9

<sup>10</sup> **CRC (cyclic redundancy check)**: Polynomial checksum family introduced by Peterson and Brown ([[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/02 Distributed ML/07 Fault Tolerance/V2-07.20 Source References#^ref-peterson1961|1961]]). CRC coverage depends on the polynomial, frame length, and error model; it is best understood as a low-cost detection layer rather than a universal guarantee. In distributed ML training, checksums or hashes can validate gradient payloads exchanged during collectives; without a verification layer, a corrupted gradient packet can silently poison the parameter update for every worker in the collective.

^fn10

<sup>11</sup> **Pipeline deadlock**: In pipelined training, a deadlock can form when stage 1 waits for a free buffer to send activations to stage 2 while stage 2 waits for stage 1 to accept its backward gradients. Neither stage can proceed, and because every other stage depends on them, the whole job stalls with no error raised, which a collective timeout eventually converts into a detectable failure.

^fn11

<sup>12</sup> **Young-Daly formula**: Young ([[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/02 Distributed ML/07 Fault Tolerance/V2-07.20 Source References#^ref-young1974|1974]]) derived the first-order optimal checkpoint interval, and Daly ([[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/02 Distributed ML/07 Fault Tolerance/V2-07.20 Source References#^ref-daly2006|2006]]) independently refined it with tighter second-order bounds.

^fn12

<sup>13</sup> **Adam optimizer state**: Adam’s per-parameter first-moment (${m}$) and second-moment (${v}$) estimates are two FP32 values, 8 of the 14 bytes per parameter in the resumable checkpoint; with the FP32 master weights, optimizer-side state accounts for all but the 2 bytes of BF16 weights. For a 175B parameter model, the two moments alone reach 1.4 TB, which typically dominates checkpoint size and recovery time, making optimizer state the primary bottleneck in checkpoint I/O design.

^fn13

<sup>14</sup> **Two-phase commit (2PC)**: Formalized by Gray ([[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/02 Distributed ML/07 Fault Tolerance/V2-07.20 Source References#^ref-gray1978|1978]]), 2PC ensures all participants either commit or abort atomically. A timeout can trigger coordinator recovery, but it cannot by itself authorize unilateral rollback.

^fn14

<sup>15</sup> **Sharded checkpointing**: Each worker saves only its local partition rather than gathering state to a single writer, parallelizing I/O across all nodes. The trade-off is that recovery requires every shard to be available and consistent, making the checkpoint protocol more complex. Losing one storage device invalidates the generation only if a required shard cannot be recovered from another copy or sufficient storage redundancy.

^fn15

<sup>16</sup> **NCCL timeout**: NVIDIA’s collective communication library exposes transport and RAS timeout controls such as `NCCL_IB_TIMEOUT` and `NCCL_RAS_TIMEOUT_FACTOR` ([[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/02 Distributed ML/07 Fault Tolerance/V2-07.20 Source References#^ref-nvidiaNcclUserGuide|NVIDIA 2026]]). Higher-level launch and distributed-training frameworks can add their own restart and operation-timeout behavior ([[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/02 Distributed ML/07 Fault Tolerance/V2-07.20 Source References#^ref-pytorchElasticDocs|PyTorch Contributors 2026c]]). Aggressive timeout settings accelerate failure detection but risk false positives during legitimate long collective operations on large models, forcing a trade-off between detection latency and training stability that has no universal optimum.

^fn16

<sup>17</sup> **Rendezvous**: From French “rendez-vous” (present yourselves), this coordination protocol lets restarted workers discover one another and establish a new group before training resumes. Recovery waits for the membership policy to be satisfied, so slow startup, delayed communication, and missing workers can extend this phase. Heterogeneity affects those delays but does not by itself imply a proportional latency law.

^fn17

<sup>18</sup> **RecSys freshness**: Freshness makes recovery delay part of the model-quality budget. Its importance depends on how quickly useful user and item signals change and how strongly predictions depend on them. Comparing quality at different state ages establishes the permitted staleness; that measured limit determines how much checkpoint and replication overhead is justified.

^fn18

<sup>19</sup> **KV cache**: Stores key and value projections for all previous tokens across all attention layers, with a per-token size derived in **[[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/03 The Fleet in Production/10 Serving at Scale/V2-10.08 Memory and Decode-Time Management#^sec-inference-scale-kv-cache-wall|The KV cache wall and memory capacity]]**. For a Llama-family 70B model at its 128K context in BF16, the KV cache is about 43 GB per conversation. Reconstruction can exceed serving latency service level agreements (SLAs) even when a replacement replica is already available.

^fn19

<sup>20</sup> **Heisenbug**: A pun on Heisenberg’s uncertainty principle, popularized in Gray ([[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/02 Distributed ML/07 Fault Tolerance/V2-07.20 Source References#^ref-gray1986computers|1986]])’s analysis of why production systems fail. Instrumentation perturbs timing, so observing the bug changes it. In distributed ML training, inserting gradient logging alters collective timing and can mask the very race that caused the silent corruption.

^fn20

<sup>21</sup> **NaN propagation**: Many IEEE 754 arithmetic operations propagate a NaN operand, so an invalid gradient can contaminate dependent updates without stopping the process. The affected scope depends on the computation’s data dependencies. Zero divided by zero and the logarithm of a negative real value produce NaNs; division of a nonzero finite value by zero or floating-point overflow can instead produce infinity, which may cause a later invalid operation. Checking only for a process crash therefore misses numerical failures.

^fn21

<sup>22</sup> **Mixed-precision numerical issues**: FP16’s exponent range is far narrower than FP32’s or BF16’s, which is why mixed-precision training scales the loss before backpropagation and dynamic scaling skips nonfinite updates. The formats and the mixed-precision recipe are recapped in **[[05 Reference/Volume II - Scaling/V2 Single-Machine Foundations#^sec-appdx-dam-taxonomy-training-memory|Numeric Precision and Training Memory]]**.

^fn22
