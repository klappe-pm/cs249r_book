# V2-07.08 The Cost of Failure

[[Home|Home]] · [[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/Volume 2 Overview|Volume Overview]] · [[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/02 Distributed ML/07 Fault Tolerance/V2-07 Fault Tolerance|V2-07 Fault Tolerance]]

^sec-fault-tolerance-reliability-reliability-economic-framework-fault-tolerance-investment-e032

Losing an uncheckpointed day on a 10,000-GPU foundation-model run burns hundreds of thousands of dollars in accelerator time, whereas losing an hour on a small vision job costs a few hundred dollars and a failed update in an online recommender costs live revenue rather than batch rework. Hardware, silent corruption, and software together set how often a fleet fails, but the work a failure destroys determines what each incident costs. A defense is worth buying only where it removes more of that expected loss than it adds in standing overhead.

### Failure Cost by Workload

^sec-fault-tolerance-reliability-reliability-modeltype-diversity-failure-impact-8ec0

Three workload properties set the price of a training interruption: the burn rate of the cluster running the job, the footprint of state that must be saved and reloaded, and how sensitive model quality is to lost or reordered updates. Those three factors separate multi-terabyte foundation models and continuous recommenders from smaller vision, speech, and scientific workloads ([[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/02 Distributed ML/07 Fault Tolerance/V2-07.08 The Cost of Failure#^tbl-failure-impact-by-model|table 5]]).

| **Model type**                  | **Typical training duration** | **Checkpoint size** |      **State sensitivity**      |       **Failure cost**        |
|:--------------------------------|:-----------------------------:|:-------------------:|:-------------------------------:|:-----------------------------:|
| **Archetype A (GPT-4/Llama-3)** |        Weeks to months        |   2.45 TB (175B)    |  High (position in data order)  | \$0.5M compute per 24 hr loss |
| **Vision (ViT-Large)**          |           1–3 days            |       1.2 GB        |   Medium (augmentation state)   |     \$10–50K per day loss     |
| **Archetype B (DLRM at Scale)** |          Continuous           | 10 TB+ (embeddings) | Very High (embedding freshness) |    Revenue impact per hour    |
| **Speech (Whisper-scale)**      |           3–7 days            |       5–10 GB       |             Medium              |    \$50–200K per day loss     |
| **Scientific (AlphaFold)**      |         Days to weeks         |      10–50 GB       |    High (exploration state)     |        Research delay         |

Table 5: **Failure Impact by Model Type**: The cost of training failures varies dramatically by model type, driven by training duration, checkpoint overhead, and the value of accumulated training state. The Archetype A cost is one lost day on the chapter’s 10,000-GPU cluster; the vision, speech, and scientific rows give illustrative ranges rather than measured values. These differences demand model-specific fault tolerance strategies.

^tbl-failure-impact-by-model

Large language model training illustrates how a costly allocation magnifies each hour of lost progress. The chapter’s 10,000-GPU cluster at approximately \$2/GPU-hour incurs \$0.5M in compute costs per day. A job checkpointed only once a day can lose up to a full day of progress to one failure, which costs \$0.5M in wasted compute plus schedule delay. At the checkpoint interval derived in [[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/02 Distributed ML/07 Fault Tolerance/V2-07.09 Checkpointing and Progress Preservation#^sec-fault-tolerance-young-daly|section 7.8.1]], the same failure costs minutes of rework instead of a day. The checkpoint overhead is correspondingly large. At the 14-byte-per-parameter resumable format that the checkpointing analysis uses (BF16 weights, FP32 master weights, and two FP32 Adaptive Moment Estimation (Adam) moments), the 175B-parameter running example writes 2.45 TB at every save.

### Investing in Protection

^sec-fault-tolerance-investing-protection

Every GPU cycle, network byte, and storage drive dedicated to checkpointing, standby nodes, or verification is a resource that is not training the model, yet skimping on protection leaves a multi-million-dollar run exposed to day-long rollbacks. Rational investment in fault tolerance therefore weighs what a failure destroys against what the defense costs to run.

When a failure strikes, its bill extends well beyond the GPU-hours burned on steps that must be replayed. Repeating lost work delays the delivery of the trained model, ties up accelerators that could have run other jobs, and pulls engineers away from research to diagnose faults and nurse manual restarts. On the other side of the cost account, prevention carries a continuous overhead even when the cluster is healthy. Checkpoint storage scales with model size and save frequency, and checkpoint writes compete for memory and network bandwidth. Hot spares and automated recovery controllers consume standing infrastructure, and fault-tolerant coordination adds software complexity that must be maintained and debugged.

Optimal investment balances these two curves against cluster scale. For a small job on a few GPUs where hardware failures are weeks apart, infrequent checkpoints and manual restarts are cheaper than standing recovery infrastructure. On thousands of GPUs where failures arrive several times a day, frequent checkpoints, automated failover, and elastic resizing pay back their overhead many times over. The checkpoint interval derived in [[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/02 Distributed ML/07 Fault Tolerance/V2-07.09 Checkpointing and Progress Preservation#^sec-fault-tolerance-young-daly|section 7.8.1]] applies this exact marginal balance to a single decision variable, choosing the cadence where the cost of one more save equals the expected rework it prevents.

[[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/02 Distributed ML/07 Fault Tolerance/V2-07.08 The Cost of Failure#^eq-ft-total-cost|Equation 5]] presents a simplified economic model for expected cost per training run:

$$
{C_{\text{total}}}= {C_{\text{compute}}}+ \mathbb{E}[{N_{\text{failures}}}] \times {C_{\text{per-failure}}}+ {C_{\text{ft}}} \tag{5}
$$

^eq-ft-total-cost

where ${C_{\text{compute}}}$ is the base compute cost, $\mathbb{E}[{N_{\text{failures}}}]$ is the expected number of failures during training, ${C_{\text{per-failure}}}$ is the cost per failure, and ${C_{\text{ft}}}$ is the cost of fault tolerance mechanisms. The cost per failure includes wasted compute plus overhead.

[[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/02 Distributed ML/07 Fault Tolerance/V2-07.08 The Cost of Failure#^eq-ft-investment-criterion|Equation 6]] formalizes when fault tolerance investment is justified:

$$
\frac{\partial {C_{\text{ft}}}}{\partial {x}} < -\frac{\partial (\mathbb{E}[{N_{\text{failures}}}] \times {C_{\text{per-failure}}})}{\partial {x}} \tag{6}
$$

^eq-ft-investment-criterion

where ${x}$ is the amount of protection bought, a design variable such as checkpoint frequency or the number of hot spares, and the base compute cost is held fixed. The negative derivative on the right is the marginal reduction in expected failure cost. Additional protection reduces total expected cost while this reduction exceeds its marginal cost; an interior optimum equates the two when the cost functions have a suitable minimum.

One investment worked through the criterion shows what it decides. Let ${x}$ be a hot-spare node held idle beside the chapter’s 10,000-GPU cluster, so that a failed node’s replacement no longer waits for the scheduler to find capacity. Its marginal cost is 8 GPUs idling all day, about \$384 per day. Its marginal benefit is the recovery time it removes from each failure while every other GPU waits. At the cascade’s 6.2 failures/day, each minute removed from every recovery is worth about \$2,100 per day, so the spare pays for itself once it shortens each recovery by about 11 seconds, provided it is refilled before the next failure arrives some 3.9 hours later. The same node beside an eight-GPU job, whose GPU-only MTBF in [[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/02 Distributed ML/07 Fault Tolerance/V2-07.03 The Mathematics of Inevitable Failure#^tbl-cluster-mtbf-scaling|table 1]] is 6,250 hours (260 days), would sit idle far longer than it could ever save. Scale reverses the verdict on the same hardware. That reversal reflects the first of three rules of failure at scale.

**Systems Perspective 7.2: Three rules of failure at scale**

1.  *At scale, failures are continuous, not exceptional.* A 10,000-GPU cluster experiences a failure every 3.9 hours on average. Systems must be designed expecting failure as normal operation.
2.  *Checkpoint intervals have an optimum.* The Young-Daly interval (developed in [[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/02 Distributed ML/07 Fault Tolerance/V2-07.09 Checkpointing and Progress Preservation#^sec-fault-tolerance-young-daly|section 7.8.1]]) sets checkpoint frequency from write time and system MTBF under its stated assumptions, and it shrinks only with the square root of a falling MTBF.
3.  *Training and serving have fundamentally different fault tolerance requirements.* A long training job can budget minutes for recovery while preserving accumulated progress; an interactive serving request may have only milliseconds left before its deadline. Those budgets motivate checkpoint recovery for training and ready alternate capacity for serving.

These three rules organize the rest of the chapter. Rules 1 and 2 govern long-running training jobs that must protect accumulated progress, rule 3 governs low-latency serving fleets that must protect live requests, and observability and fault injection verify that both recovery paths work under fire. Training resilience begins with the durable state that every restart or resize falls back on.

**Self-Check: Question**

1.  The expected cost of a training run is modeled as base compute, plus expected failures times the cost per failure, plus the cost of fault-tolerance mechanisms. Under this model, when does one more unit of fault-tolerance investment lower the total expected cost?

    1.  Whenever it reduces the expected number of failures, regardless of what it costs.
    2.  Whenever the cluster is large enough to fail several times per day.
    3.  While the reduction in expected failure cost it buys exceeds its own marginal cost.
    4.  Whenever total fault-tolerance spending stays below the base compute cost of the run.

2.  A 175B-parameter language model trains on 10,000 GPUs at about \$2 per GPU-hour, and a ViT-Large model trains for two days on a much smaller allocation. Both checkpoint once a day. Why can one failure cost the language model run roughly \$0.5M of compute but the vision run only tens of thousands of dollars?

    1.  Each GPU in the language model run fails more often, so every failure destroys more work.
    2.  Lost progress is priced by what the allocation costs per hour and how much state must be recovered, so up to a day of rework on a 10,000-GPU allocation costs about \$0.5M while a smaller allocation loses far less per day.
    3.  Vision checkpoints are larger than language model checkpoints, which shortens vision recovery.
    4.  Vision runs carry no state beyond their weights, so they can resume from any data position at no cost.

3.  On the 10,000-GPU cluster, about 6.2 failures per day each stall every GPU during recovery, at about \$2 per GPU-hour. An idle eight-GPU hot-spare node costs about \$384 per day. Estimate how much recovery time the spare must save per failure to pay for itself, and state the condition attached to that estimate.

4.  True or False: A hot-spare node that pays for itself beside a 10,000-GPU training cluster is also a sound investment beside an eight-GPU job, because the node and its daily cost are identical.
