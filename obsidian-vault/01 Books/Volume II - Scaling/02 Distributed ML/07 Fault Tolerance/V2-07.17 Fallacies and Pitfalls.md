# V2-07.17 Fallacies and Pitfalls

[[Home|Home]] · [[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/Volume 2 Overview|Volume Overview]] · [[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/02 Distributed ML/07 Fault Tolerance/V2-07 Fault Tolerance|V2-07 Fault Tolerance]]

^sec-fault-tolerance-reliability-reliability-fallacies-pitfalls-74b8

An infrastructure team might spend weeks hardening their storage layer against disk failures, only to have their entire training run destroyed by a subtle software bug in their PyTorch distributed backend. Fault tolerance for distributed ML systems involves counterintuitive mathematics and subtle trade-offs where conventional data center wisdom often fails. Recognizing the fallacies and pitfalls below prevents engineers from optimizing for the wrong failure modes.

**Fallacy**: *Hardware redundancy is enough to keep a training run safe.*

This intuition comes from traditional systems, where disk failures, power outages, and network partitions dominate the incident mix. Hardware did cause most of the interruptions that the Llama 3 run recorded ([[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/02 Distributed ML/07 Fault Tolerance/V2-07.04 Failure Taxonomy#^fig-llama3-interruptions|figure 2]]), yet long-lived, stateful ML jobs also expose failure paths that hardware redundancy cannot cover. A malformed checkpoint path can make recovery impossible, a mismatched collective library can hang all ranks, an undersized shared-memory limit can crash data loaders, and a stale feature schema can silently corrupt training. Their relative frequency depends on the deployment, but even an infrequent software or configuration fault can destroy the same multi-day job that hardware redundancy was meant to protect. Investing heavily in hardware redundancy while neglecting software robustness (input validation, gradual rollouts, configuration management) leaves many important failure modes unaddressed, which is why the most reliable ML systems treat software bugs as inevitable and design defensively.

**Pitfall**: *Setting checkpoint interval by intuition.*

Organizations commonly set checkpoint intervals based on “feels right”: “every hour seems reasonable” or “every 1,000 steps.” The Young-Daly formula reveals these intuitions are often wrong. For the 1,000-GPU cluster of [[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/02 Distributed ML/07 Fault Tolerance/V2-07.03 The Mathematics of Inevitable Failure#^tbl-cluster-mtbf-scaling|table 1]], whose GPU-only MTBF is 50 hours, with a checkpoint write time of 5 minutes:

$$
T^\ast_{\text{ckpt}} = \sqrt{2 \times 5 \times 3000} = \sqrt{30000} \approx 173 \text{ minutes}
$$

The intuitive “every hour” checkpoints nearly three times more often than the expected-waste optimum. It reduces potential lost work, but the added write overhead outweighs that benefit under this model. A tighter RPO can still justify the shorter interval. The same hourly policy was too infrequent in the Young-Daly notebook, and both verdicts follow from one rule. The optimum scales as $\sqrt{{T_{\text{write}}} \cdot {\text{MTBF}_{\text{system}}}}$, so an hour is too long when a 24.5 s write meets a full-system MTBF of 3.87 hours, yet too short when a 5-minute write meets a GPU-only MTBF of 50 hours. If checkpoint time increases to 15 minutes (larger model, slower storage), the optimal interval stretches to 300 minutes, further still from the “every 15 minutes” that some teams adopt to “stay safe.” The decision must balance expected waste with the recovery contract rather than treat either interval as universally correct.

**Fallacy**: *Component MTTF values predict individual failure timing.*

Engineers read a GPU MTTF of 50,000 hours and plan around a five-year replacement cadence per device. MTTF is a population mean, not a lifetime assigned to each component. A GPU with MTTF 50,000 hours might fail at 200 hours or last 100,000 hours. Under the chapter’s independent constant-rate model, a fleet of 10,000 GPUs has an aggregate rate of approximately $10{,}000/50{,}000 = 0.2$ failures per hour, or roughly one failure every 5 hours. This planning model helps size recovery capacity and spare pools. It does not make device-health monitoring pointless. Temperature, error history, and recurring anomalies provide condition-specific evidence that the population mean alone lacks.

**Pitfall**: *Using MTBF calculations as if failures were independent.*

The reliability equation ${\text{MTBF}_{\text{system}}} = {\text{MTTF}_{\text{component}}}/{N}$ assumes component failures are statistically independent, while production failures correlate through the shared power, switches, software, and cooling of [[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/02 Distributed ML/07 Fault Tolerance/V2-07.04 Failure Taxonomy#^sec-fault-tolerance-reliability-reliability-correlated-failures-95e8|section 7.3.4]].

Correlated failures change two quantities that the simple MTBF equation hides: incident frequency and blast radius. A cluster with 1,000 independent GPUs each with 50,000-hour MTTF has a first-GPU-failure MTBF of 50 hours. If a shared power, cooling, or software hazard adds an incident rate 10 times larger than that independent first-failure rate, the effective incident MTBF drops to about 5 hours. Separately, a correlated incident may take out 10 GPUs at once, increasing recovery cost even if the independent GPU failure rate itself has not changed. Reliability engineering must identify and mitigate correlation through diversity: different power feeds, different network paths, different software versions in canary deployments.

**Fallacy**: *Every failure should be handled as a full restart.*

Engineers often treat all failures as “node crashed, restart from checkpoint,” but different failure modes require different responses. Transient stalls such as network congestion can permit a bounded wait or safe retry when state and the communication group remain valid; a brief fault can still require recovery if it corrupts state or leaves the group unusable. Permanent device loss requires replacement or a feasible resize, together with restoration of the missing state. Silent corruption requires a trusted recovery point, which may precede the latest checkpoint and therefore motivates checkpoint history retention. Persistent resource exhaustion (OOM, memory fragmentation) needs reconfiguration before restart; otherwise the job can crash again immediately. As [[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/02 Distributed ML/07 Fault Tolerance/V2-07.04 Failure Taxonomy#^sec-fault-tolerance-reliability-reliability-failure-taxonomy-e78c|section 7.3]] details, misdiagnosing failure type wastes compute. Restarting for a harmless stall discards useful work, while continuing from corrupted state can contaminate later updates.

**Pitfall**: *Assuming checkpoints are consistent without validating state.*

Modern frameworks checkpoint transparently, creating the illusion of automatic consistency. In practice, distributed checkpoints require cross-node coordination that can fail subtly across multiple dimensions. Rank desynchronization occurs when workers record different training steps, such as rank 0 snapshotting iteration 1000 while rank 1 captures iteration 1001. Similarly, transient storage failures can cause partial writes that leave behind truncated shards, sharded optimizer state lag leaves optimizer states misaligned with model weights when captured asynchronously, and in-flight gradients from incomplete AllReduce operations can produce ambiguous reduction boundaries. Production systems must therefore implement checkpoint validation: verify all shards exist, verify iteration numbers match, and verify optimizer state matches model state. Without that validation, organizations that discover corrupted checkpoints during recovery from a failure have no recourse except restarting from a potentially much earlier checkpoint.

**Fallacy**: *Overhead budgets are fixed fractions of training time.*

Capacity plans often carry line items such as “pipeline bubble: 10 percent, checkpoint overhead: 5 percent, failure recovery: 3 percent,” and such figures are easy to read as physical laws. They are engineering targets, not constants. Pipeline bubble overhead depends on the number of microbatches and the interleaved schedule, as its bubble fraction shows (**[[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/02 Distributed ML/06 Distributed Training/V2-06.06 Model Parallelism#^sec-distributed-training-systems-systems-pipeline-parallelism-8748|Pipeline parallelism]]**). Elastic recovery can reduce waiting for replacement capacity while still restarting workers and restoring state. Checkpoint overhead is a function of model size, storage bandwidth, and whether checkpointing is synchronous or asynchronous. Treating these numbers as fixed percentages leads to passive acceptance of avoidable inefficiency, because each one is set by a design choice.

**Pitfall**: *Using elastic training as a substitute for checkpointing.*

Elastic training adjusts parallelism degree when workers fail, but resizing still requires a consistent recovery state. Reducing the active worker count requires redistributing model shards, optimizer states, and data assignments, often through checkpoint reload. Below some minimum viable size, training becomes infeasible because the model no longer fits or the batch policy cannot be maintained; the job must wait for sufficient capacity before restoring. If a failure was caused by a software bug triggered by specific data, the bug persists in remaining workers. Elastic training is complementary to checkpointing, not a replacement. Resizing alone does not justify a lower checkpoint frequency, because the recovery contract, failure rate, and checkpoint cost still determine that choice.

**Fallacy**: *Silent data corruption is negligible because hardware has ECC.*

GPUs and memory systems include extensive error correction (ECC, CRC, parity), creating the intuition that silent data corruption is negligible. Large-scale CPU SDC and memory-error studies show that silent corruptions and memory faults still occur at fleet scale and can escape ordinary error reporting ([[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/02 Distributed ML/07 Fault Tolerance/V2-07.20 Source References#^ref-dixit2021silent|Dixit et al. 2021]]; [[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/02 Distributed ML/07 Fault Tolerance/V2-07.20 Source References#^ref-sridharan2015|Sridharan et al. 2015]]). ECC and link CRCs remain necessary layers, but they protect stored and transmitted bits rather than arithmetic, so they are not an end-to-end correctness proof for a training run. A 10,000-GPU cluster executes enough arithmetic that even rare per-device corruption becomes an operating condition, which is why it needs the check-and-verify defenses of [[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/02 Distributed ML/07 Fault Tolerance/V2-07.06 Check-And-Verify Defenses Against Silent Data Corruption#^sec-ft-sdc-verification|section 7.5]] in addition to hardware protection.

**Pitfall**: *Adding GPUs without accounting for communication and recovery overhead.*

Capacity plans typically account for Amdahl’s Law and AllReduce overhead but treat reliability as a fixed background condition. At extreme scale, each additional GPU increases the aggregate failure rate, which inflates the expected recovery and replay time per job. There exists a cluster size beyond which the time lost to failures and recovery exceeds the time saved by additional parallelism, and wall-clock training time increases rather than decreasing with ${N_{\text{GPU}}}$. This is the reliability version of diminishing returns, and it applies on top of the Amdahl ceiling, so the two effects must be modeled together. Whole-run useful goodput must count only retained progress and include recovery time in the denominator, and its reliability penalty generally grows with ${N_{\text{GPU}}}$ under the independent-failure model. **[[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/03 The Fleet in Production/09 Fleet Efficiency/V2-09.03 ML Goodput#^sec-performance-engineering-goodput-factors|The three goodput factors]]** composes that runtime loss with the scheduling and program losses without counting any of them twice.

**Self-Check: Question**

1.  An engineering team operates a 1,000-GPU training cluster with a system MTBF of 50 hours ($3{,}000\text{ minutes}$) and a checkpoint write time of 5 minutes. The team intuitively chooses to checkpoint ‘every 15 minutes to stay safe.’ According to the Young-Daly formula, what is the mathematically optimal interval, and what is the consequence of the team’s intuitive choice?

    1.  Optimal interval is 15 minutes; the intuitive choice is mathematically perfect.
    2.  The first-order optimum is approximately 173 minutes (~2.9 hours); writing every 15 minutes adds more checkpoint overhead than it saves in expected rework under this model.
    3.  Optimal interval is 50 hours; checkpointing every 15 minutes causes disk sectors to wear out in days.
    4.  Optimal interval is 1 minute; checkpointing every 15 minutes risks losing 100% of the training dataset.

2.  Explain what a GPU-only independent-failure estimate, $\mathrm{MTBF}_{\mathrm{system}}=\mathrm{MTBF}_{\mathrm{component}}/N$, omits about shared failure domains and their recovery consequences.

3.  Why does adding more GPUs ($N$) to a distributed training job eventually encounter a ‘reliability ceiling’ where total wall-clock training time increases rather than decreases?

    1.  Because GPUs consume more electrical power than data center transformers can supply.
    2.  Because floating-point numbers lose precision when divided across more than 1,000 workers.
    3.  Because the Python interpreter cannot spawn more than 512 concurrent threads.
    4.  Because aggregate cluster failure rate scales linearly ($N\lambda$), shrinking system MTBF until the cumulative time lost to failure detection, job restarts, checkpoint loading, and rework exceeds the marginal compute speedup of additional parallelism.

4.  Because modern accelerator hardware incorporates hardware ECC on High Bandwidth Memory and CRC on interconnects, application-level gradient checksums and loss validation checks are redundant and unnecessary in 10,000-GPU clusters.
