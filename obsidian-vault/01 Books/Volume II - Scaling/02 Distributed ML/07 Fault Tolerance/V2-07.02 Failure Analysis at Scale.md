# V2-07.02 Failure Analysis at Scale

[[Home|Home]] · [[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/Volume 2 Overview|Volume Overview]] · [[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/02 Distributed ML/07 Fault Tolerance/V2-07 Fault Tolerance|V2-07 Fault Tolerance]]

^sec-fault-tolerance-reliability-reliability-failure-analysis-scale-6b4b

Imagine a 10,000-GPU cluster midway through a three-month training run for a new foundation model. The parallel training plan and the communication layer beneath it have done their job, and thousands of devices exchange gradients through AllReduce, AllGather, and AllToAll as if they were one machine. Then the arithmetic of scale catches up. A GPU fails every few hours, and if the system cannot absorb that ordinary physical event, synchronized training halts while compute worth about \$0.5M a day sits idle. **Fault tolerance** is the system property that lets distributed execution continue making useful progress when components fail, degrade, or disappear. In the fleet stack of **[[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/00 Introduction/01 Introduction/V2-01.03 From Single Node to Fleet#^fig-fleet-stack|The Fleet Stack]]**, fault tolerance occupies the Reliability & Control row of the Distribution band, where it protects the progress that parallelism and communication produce.

That fragility is the direct consequence of successful synchronization. Every collective in **[[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/02 Distributed ML/05 Collective Communication/V2-05 Collective Communication#^sec-collective-communication|Collective Communication]]** assumed that all ranks in its process group arrive, and each dependent consumer waits for every contribution. One stalled device can therefore stop progress in its synchronous training group, even while unrelated jobs continue elsewhere in the fleet. Fault-tolerant ML systems preserve progress by detecting failure, checkpointing recoverable state, restarting or elastically reshaping jobs, and treating hardware churn as normal execution rather than an exceptional path. That reliability envelope covers both halves of the fleet’s work. Training needs recovery that preserves weeks of accumulated progress, while serving needs redundancy, failover, and graceful degradation that preserve a latency contract, and both draw their spare capacity from a shared fleet pool.

↪ How the scheduler holds spare capacity for failed jobs and prepositions replacement nodes while a missed heartbeat is confirmed is developed in **[[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/02 Distributed ML/08 Fleet Orchestration/V2-08.03 Distributed Scheduling Complexity#^sec-fleet-orchestration-failure-rates|Failure as a scheduling input]]**.

Not every failure announces itself. A component can stop outright, slow down while still answering liveness checks, or keep running while it computes wrong values. The quiet cases pair low detection rates with a high blast radius, because the system keeps running on state it should no longer trust. The recovery target here is therefore trustworthy execution: preserving valid training progress and restoring a service after its infrastructure or software fails. A restored model can still make poor predictions on changed inputs even when every component executes correctly. Evaluating that behavioral robustness requires a separate contract for model quality under distribution shifts and adversarial inputs, developed in **[[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/04 The Responsible Fleet/14 Robust AI/V2-14 Robust AI#^sec-robust-ai|Robust AI]]**. The distinction lets us use model-quality signals to diagnose a fault without treating every quality regression as evidence of failed hardware.

Because failures cannot be eliminated at this scale, fault-tolerant systems verify completion rather than assume it, treat failure as a normal code path, exercise recovery continuously, and account for partial failures, in which some components fail while others keep running, a case naive error handling may not anticipate. These techniques draw on decades of distributed systems research, but ML workloads change the economics. Training checkpoints are large but can be sized from the model and optimizer state. Some optimization algorithms also tolerate bounded numerical perturbations without requiring bitwise replay. That tolerance must be established for the training method; stochasticity does not make arbitrary corruption or inconsistent recovery safe. A recovery design can exploit approximate execution only while preserving its declared convergence and quality requirements. Making failure a routine part of operation starts with knowing how often failures arrive, and the mathematics that follows makes the shift from *rare exception* to *routine occurrence* precise.

**Self-Check: Question**

1.  A production cluster operates 10,000 GPUs, where each individual GPU exhibits an independent MTTF of 50,000 hours and an availability of 99.99%. What is the resulting GPU-only cluster MTBF, and what is the probability that all 10,000 GPUs are simultaneously operational at any random instant?

    1.  Cluster MTBF is 50 hours; simultaneous cluster availability is approximately 90.5%.
    2.  Cluster MTBF is 500 hours; simultaneous cluster availability is approximately 99.0%.
    3.  Cluster MTBF is 5.0 hours; simultaneous cluster availability is approximately 36.8%.
    4.  Cluster MTBF is 0.5 hours; simultaneous cluster availability is approximately 1.0%.

2.  Using an illustrative unprotected-memory soft-error rate of 250 FIT/Mbit and 80 GiB of HBM per device, explain why a 1,024-GPU training cluster needs ECC protection.
