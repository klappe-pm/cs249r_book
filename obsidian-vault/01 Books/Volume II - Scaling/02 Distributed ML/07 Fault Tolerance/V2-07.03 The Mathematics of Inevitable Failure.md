# V2-07.03 The Mathematics of Inevitable Failure

[[Home|Home]] · [[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/Volume 2 Overview|Volume Overview]] · [[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/02 Distributed ML/07 Fault Tolerance/V2-07 Fault Tolerance|V2-07 Fault Tolerance]]

^sec-fault-tolerance-reliability-reliability-mathematics-inevitable-failure-93ef

A single data-center GPU can run for years without a hardware fault, yet a synchronous job spanning ten thousand of those same GPUs is interrupted every few hours. System reliability engineering explains why individual device longevity erodes so quickly when thousands of components must stay healthy at the same time ([[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/02 Distributed ML/07 Fault Tolerance/V2-07.20 Source References#^ref-birolini2017|Birolini 2017]]). Each component has a failure rate ${\lambda}$,[[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/02 Distributed ML/07 Fault Tolerance/V2-07.21 Footnotes#^fn1|¹]] measured in failures per unit time. When ${\lambda}$ is constant, the probability that a single component survives without failure until time ${t}$ follows an exponential distribution,[[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/02 Distributed ML/07 Fault Tolerance/V2-07.21 Footnotes#^fn2|²]] as in [[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/02 Distributed ML/07 Fault Tolerance/V2-07.03 The Mathematics of Inevitable Failure#^eq-single-component-reliability|equation 1]]:

$$
{R_{\text{single}}}({t}) = e^{-{\lambda} {t}} \tag{1}
$$

^eq-single-component-reliability

↪ The independence, exponential-lifetime, and Poisson results this failure arithmetic rests on are developed in **[[05 Reference/Volume II - Scaling/V2 Reliability Foundations#^sec-appdx-reliability-foundations-probability-tools|Probability tools for failure analysis]]**.

The expected time to failure of this component equals $1/{\lambda}$. In this book’s convention, mean time to failure (MTTF) names a single component’s expected time to its first failure. MTBF names the repairable system’s expected operating time between successive failures, excluding recovery downtime. Under the constant-rate exponential model used here the two coincide numerically, and the fault-tolerance model uses MTTF for per-component constants and MTBF for composed system rates. Data center GPUs often use planning MTTF values in the tens of thousands of hours, with field behavior depending on operating conditions, cooling effectiveness, manufacturing variation, and workload stress.[[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/02 Distributed ML/07 Fault Tolerance/V2-07.21 Footnotes#^fn3|³]] **[[05 Reference/Volume II - Scaling/V2 Reliability Foundations#^sec-appdx-reliability-foundations-component-rates|Component failure rates]]** catalogs the reference per-component FIT and MTTF values (GPU, high-bandwidth memory (HBM), network interface card (NIC), power supply, PCIe switch, optical cable, ToR switch) that anchor these ${\lambda}$ and FIT figures. A reader can substitute the constant for any device and reproduce the rates used throughout the fault-tolerance analysis.

When multiple independent components operate in a system where any single failure causes system failure, [[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/02 Distributed ML/07 Fault Tolerance/V2-07.03 The Mathematics of Inevitable Failure#^eq-system-reliability-product|equation 2]] formalizes how system reliability becomes the product of individual component reliabilities:

$$
{R_{\text{system}}}({t}) = \prod_{i=1}^{{N}} {{R_{\text{single}}}}_i({t}) = \prod_{i=1}^{{N}} e^{-{\lambda}_i {t}} \tag{2}
$$

^eq-system-reliability-product

For ${N}$ identical components with individual failure rate ${\lambda}$, [[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/02 Distributed ML/07 Fault Tolerance/V2-07.03 The Mathematics of Inevitable Failure#^eq-system-reliability-n-components|equation 3]] gives the identical-component simplification:

$$
{R_{\text{system}}}({t}) = e^{-{N}{\lambda} {t}} \tag{3}
$$

^eq-system-reliability-n-components

The system failure rate becomes ${N}{\lambda}$, and [[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/02 Distributed ML/07 Fault Tolerance/V2-07.03 The Mathematics of Inevitable Failure#^eq-system-mtbf|equation 4]] expresses how the system MTBF scales inversely with component count. This inverse scaling explains why per-device reliability that looks excellent in isolation erodes at cluster scale:

$$
{\text{MTBF}_{\text{system}}} = \frac{1}{{N}{\lambda}} = \frac{{\text{MTTF}_{\text{component}}}}{{N}} \tag{4}
$$

^eq-system-mtbf

The consequence is a gap between failure cadence and job length. At 10,000 GPUs, the GPU-only calculation gives an expected interval of 5 hours, so a training run measured in weeks accumulates dozens of expected failures and one measured in months accumulates hundreds. These are planning averages, not a schedule of incidents; a shorter job can finish without failure even when its expected failure count exceeds one. Once the expected time between failures falls below the length of the job, fault tolerance becomes part of the execution budget for long-running fleet work. Applied to the landmark training runs of **[[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/00 Introduction/01 Introduction/V2-01.02 The Scale Moment#^sec-vol2-introduction-scale-moment|The Scale Moment]]**, whose accelerator counts grew by about four orders of magnitude between 2012 and 2024, the same per-device planning rate puts the largest of them, with more than ten thousand GPUs, below a 10-hour system MTBF. Recovery therefore becomes an active scheduling constraint rather than a reliability feature.

### Composing Component Reliability

^sec-fault-tolerance-reliability-quantitative-analysis

The GPU-only calculation of [[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/02 Distributed ML/07 Fault Tolerance/V2-07.03 The Mathematics of Inevitable Failure#^eq-system-mtbf|equation 4]] supplies the scale intuition; a real training system then composes GPUs, HBM, links, power supplies, storage paths, and redundancy into one failure budget. Component rates are usually quoted in the FIT units introduced with the failure rate, so the 50,000-hour GPU contributes 20,000 FIT to whatever system contains it. The new question is how different component rates compose, and the composition rule depends on the redundancy structure and the independence assumptions. For series systems (for example, a node where all 8 GPUs must work), failure of any component fails the whole. With independent constant-rate failures, the reliabilities multiply as in [[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/02 Distributed ML/07 Fault Tolerance/V2-07.03 The Mathematics of Inevitable Failure#^eq-system-reliability-product|equation 2]], so the rates add even when the components differ:

$$
{\text{MTBF}_{\text{system}}} = \frac{1}{\sum {\lambda}_i}
$$

 Under this additive failure-rate law, the system failure rate grows linearly with component count while system MTBF falls in inverse proportion. Every non-redundant component on the critical path adds its FIT contribution directly to the denominator.

The same product governs instantaneous availability, a different quantity from the survival function ${R_{\text{single}}}({t})$. Availability ${A}$ is the probability that a component is up at a randomly chosen instant, counting repairs, whereas ${R_{\text{single}}}({t})$ is the probability that it has not yet failed by time ${t}$. If each GPU is independently up with probability ${A_{\text{GPU}}}$, all ${N}$ are up together with probability $({A_{\text{GPU}}})^{N}$, which is the reliability gap of **[[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/00 Introduction/01 Introduction/V2-01.05 Constraints of Scale#^eq-reliability-gap|equation 1.2]]**, in which high per-device availability still yields low availability for a job that needs every device. For the chapter’s 10,000-GPU cluster, a per-GPU availability of 99.99 percent (only 52.6 minutes of downtime per year) leaves about a 37 percent chance that every GPU is up at the same instant. This compounding erodes even highly reliable hardware once ${N}$ reaches fleet scale, so software must handle failures automatically.

**Systems Perspective 7.1: Scale transforms failure**

A cluster of 10,000 GPUs turns a per-GPU MTTF of 50,000 hours into a system MTBF of 5 hours. For a repairable system, steady-state availability is ${A}= {\text{MTBF}}/({\text{MTBF}}+{\text{MTTR}})$, where mean time to recovery (MTTR) is the recovery interval. Cutting MTTR from 30 minutes to 5 minutes raises availability from 90.91 percent to 98.36 percent. This availability treats the whole job as one repairable system that resumes after each recovery. It therefore measures a different quantity from the instantaneous probability, computed above from a per-GPU availability, that every GPU is up at once. The stationary two-state model assumes unconstrained repair capacity and complete outage; correlated incidents, repair contention, and degraded operation require direct measurement ([[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/02 Distributed ML/07 Fault Tolerance/V2-07.20 Source References#^ref-birolini2017|Birolini 2017]]). Systems must engineer recovery, not merely improve components.

Engineering faster recovery is one response to that arithmetic; redundancy is the other. For parallel systems providing redundancy, suppose that any surviving component can satisfy the required service. With independent failures and no repair during the observation interval, the system survives until every component has failed:

$$
{R_{\text{system}}}({t}) = 1 - \prod_{i=1}^{{N_{\text{red}}}} (1 - {{R_{\text{single}}}}_i({t}))
$$

Series composition is the training job’s problem, because a synchronous step needs every participant. Parallel composition is the serving fleet’s lever, and it returns when interchangeable replicas protect a latency contract. Both rules, however, describe whatever the model calls a component, and the fault rate assigned to that component depends on which protections sit inside it.

**Lighthouse 7.1: Archetype A (GPT-4/Llama-3): Memory soft-error scale**

Composition makes the distinction between raw faults and job interruptions essential. Memory soft-error rates are quoted per megabit of storage rather than per device, so the exposure grows with memory capacity. As an illustrative raw-bit-error budget, assume 250 FIT per megabit across 1,024 GPUs with 80 GB each, the size of the Archetype A reference cluster. The resulting interval between raw memory errors is roughly 20 seconds. This is not a measured job-failure interval. Error-correcting code (ECC) protection can correct covered error patterns before they affect computation, while detected uncorrectable errors and escaped corruption require different recovery paths. At that raw rate, unprotected memory in the reference cluster would corrupt training state many times per hour, so ECC is a precondition for large-scale training rather than an option. A fleet reliability budget therefore needs the residual job-interrupting and silent-corruption rates under its actual protection scheme; raw bit-error rates alone cannot establish whether memory, logic, or interconnect dominates.

These raw bit-error rates justify ECC, but they are not the interruption rates that set checkpoint cadence; the worked cascade that follows uses device MTTF values that already include the effect of that protection. The cascade also treats its GPU, PCIe, power, and network contributions as separate categories, so a measured whole-node rate that already includes those paths cannot be added to them again.

### Cluster MTBF Calculation

^sec-fault-tolerance-reliability-reliability-worked-example-cluster-mtbf-calculation-9255

Consider a training cluster designed for large language model development, roughly ten times the 1,024-GPU Archetype A reference cluster, with the following specifications:

- 10,000 NVIDIA H100 GPUs
- Individual GPU MTTF: 50,000 hours
- Each GPU connected to host via PCIe (MTTF: 200,000 hours)
- Each node contains 8 GPUs fed by 6 power supply units (MTTF: 100,000 hours each) in 4+2 redundancy, so the node keeps full performance with any 2 of them failed
- Network infrastructure per node (NIC, cables): MTTF 150,000 hours

Each GPU operates within a failure domain that includes the GPU itself, its PCIe connection, and a proportional share of the node’s shared power and network infrastructure. Dividing a node-wide subsystem rate by the 8 GPUs in the node prorates one shared failure source across those GPUs, so that multiplying the per-GPU total by all 10,000 GPUs in the final step counts each node’s shared hardware once per node rather than eight times. Power enters the sum only through supplies whose single failure stops the node, counted as ${n_{\text{psu}}}$ in the power equation. With the node’s supplies in redundant sets, no single unit qualifies, so the power term below is zero in this first-order calculation. The approximation omits the residual risk that additional independent failures exhaust redundancy before repair. A fault upstream of the chassis, such as the loss of the circuits feeding it, can also take down the whole node. That risk belongs to the correlated failures treated later in this chapter. The four subsystem rates follow from their MTTF values:

$$
{\lambda_{\text{GPU}}} = \frac{1}{50{,}000} = 2.0 \times 10^{-5} \text{ failures/hour}
$$

$$
{\lambda_{\text{PCIe}}} = \frac{1}{200{,}000} = 0.5 \times 10^{-5} \text{ failures/hour}
$$

$$
{\lambda_{\text{power/GPU}}} = \frac{{n_{\text{psu}}}}{8} \times \frac{1}{100{,}000} = \frac{0}{8} \times \frac{1}{100{,}000} = 0 \text{ failures/hour}
$$

$$
{\lambda_{\text{network/GPU}}} = \frac{1}{8} \times \frac{1}{150{,}000} = 0.083 \times 10^{-5} \text{ failures/hour}
$$

Because the subsystems sit in series, their rates add to a per-GPU total:

$$
{\lambda_{\text{total/GPU}}} = (2.0 + 0.5 + 0.083) \times 10^{-5} = 2.583 \times 10^{-5} \text{ failures/hour}
$$

Multiplying that total by the cluster size gives the system failure rate, whose reciprocal is the cluster MTBF:

$$
{\lambda_{\text{system}}} = 10{,}000 \times 2.583 \times 10^{-5} = 0.2583 \text{ failures/hour}
$$

$$
{\text{MTBF}_{\text{system}}} = \frac{1}{0.2583} = 3.87 \text{ hours}
$$

Under the model, the cluster experiences a failure every 3.9 hours on average. The expected failure cadence is 6.2 failures/day; a training run lasting one week has an expected count of 43 failures. Actual incident times vary, but the expected loss is large enough that recovery must be part of normal execution planning.

↪ The step-by-step composition of per-component failure rates into a fleet-wide system MTBF, reusable for any node design or cluster size, is developed in **[[05 Reference/Volume II - Scaling/V2 Reliability Foundations#^sec-appdx-reliability-foundations-mtbf-cascade|The MTBF cascade]]**.

The cluster MTBF scaling in [[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/02 Distributed ML/07 Fault Tolerance/V2-07.03 The Mathematics of Inevitable Failure#^tbl-cluster-mtbf-scaling|table 1]] isolates the GPU-only baseline across cluster sizes. The full-system calculation is lower once PCIe and network failure domains are included. The same 10,000-GPU cluster therefore carries three MTBF values in this chapter. Each later calculation names the one it uses: the GPU-only 5 hours of this table, the full-system 3.9 hours of the cascade above, and the two-to-three-hour cadence that somewhat larger production runs report below. The full-system MTBF is the default input; a calculation that uses the GPU-only value says so, usually because it compares cluster sizes on the common basis of [[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/02 Distributed ML/07 Fault Tolerance/V2-07.03 The Mathematics of Inevitable Failure#^tbl-cluster-mtbf-scaling|table 1]], and its failure count is then a lower bound.

Two running scenarios carry the rest of the chapter. The 1,024-GPU Archetype A reference cluster sizes per-node state, restore paths, and the elastic and straggler examples, while the 10,000-GPU cluster of the cascade sets failure cadence and checkpoint intervals.

Table 1: **Cluster MTBF Scaling**: GPU-only mean time between failures falls in inverse proportion to cluster size, transforming failures from rare events to continuous operating conditions at scale. Additional host, power, storage, and network failure domains reduce system-level MTBF further.

^tbl-cluster-mtbf-scaling

Production measurements confirm the $1/{N_{\text{GPU}}}$ trend of [[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/02 Distributed ML/07 Fault Tolerance/V2-07.03 The Mathematics of Inevitable Failure#^tbl-cluster-mtbf-scaling|table 1]] and show that the GPU-only line is optimistic. Plotted on log-log axes, measured MTTF values and fitted projections from Meta’s Research SuperCluster (RSC) fall roughly along the slope of the $50{,}000\,\text{h}/{N_{\text{GPU}}}$ line across more than three orders of magnitude in job size, though less steeply between the largest measured job and the first projection, and sit below it. The gap arises because a job can be interrupted by far more than its GPUs ([[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/02 Distributed ML/07 Fault Tolerance/V2-07.03 The Mathematics of Inevitable Failure#^fig-published-failure-rates|figure 1]]). The Kokolis et al. ([[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/02 Distributed ML/07 Fault Tolerance/V2-07.20 Source References#^ref-kokolis2025|2025]]) study of the RSC reported measured MTTF values for observed RSC job sizes and projected a 1.8-hour MTTF for 16,384-GPU jobs. That projection lands next to the Llama 3 run previewed in **[[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/00 Introduction/01 Introduction/V2-01.02 The Scale Moment#^sec-vol2-introduction-scale-moment|The Scale Moment]]** (war story [7.2](https://mlsysbook.ai/vol2/07_fault_tolerance/07_fault_tolerance.html#ws-fault-tolerance-llama3-failure-cadence) follows its recovery). That run recorded 419 unexpected interruptions across a 54-day snapshot on 16,384 H100 GPUs, which gives an observed MTTF of about 3.1 hours. Unlike the RSC values, that measurement sits close to the GPU-only line at its job size rather than below it.

Together, the measured Llama run and Kokolis projection put 16,384-GPU training on a failure cadence of roughly two to three hours, bracketed by the 1.8-hour projection and the 3.1-hour measurement. This evidence turns the idealized $1/{N_{\text{GPU}}}$ relation into an engineering constraint that determines checkpoint frequency, recovery architecture, and infrastructure investment.
