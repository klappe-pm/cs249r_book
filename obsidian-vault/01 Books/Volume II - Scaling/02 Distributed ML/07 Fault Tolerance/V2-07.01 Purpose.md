*Fleet Machine Learning Systems Stack*

*Fleet stack focus for this chapter.*

*Why does scale transform hardware failure from rare exception to routine condition that systems must absorb continuously?*

A single accelerator can run for years between hardware failures. A thousand accelerators turn that same component risk into failures every couple of days, and ten thousand see failures every few hours, while shared power and network faults can interrupt many workers at once. Individual devices need not become less reliable for the fleet to experience failures routinely. A program on one machine can often stop and restart when a component fails; a distributed training job must also recover agreement among surviving workers about which progress remains valid. Restarting quickly is of little value if the restored state silently changes the computation. Preserving more state reduces the work at risk, but consumes storage bandwidth and memory while the job is healthy. Waiting longer between saves reduces that overhead while increasing the progress a failure can erase. Serving imposes a different consequence: users may abandon a request before recovery completes, and replacing a worker may leave the request’s context unavailable. A response that arrives on time can still be unacceptable if recovery has discarded information needed to produce it. Engineers must therefore bound lost work and recovery delay while preserving the validity of training and the usefulness of service. The goal is continued useful execution despite repeated failures, with the costs and limits of recovery made explicit. In C³ terms, fault tolerance spends compute and communication on coordination so that component failures do not routinely become failures of the entire workload.

**Learning Objectives**

- Calculate mean time between fleet failures from component rates and estimate interruption frequency for large training clusters
- Classify hardware, software, silent-data-corruption, and slow-but-alive failures by detectability, blast radius, and recovery path
- Select checkpoint intervals that balance write overhead against acceptable lost work and recovery delay
- Design distributed checkpointing and elastic recovery plans for multi-terabyte model state across accelerator fleets
- Design serving redundancy, failover, and state-recovery policies under latency budgets and partial failures
- Select graceful degradation strategies that preserve useful service when models, features, regions, or capacity fail
- Evaluate detection timeouts, restart strategies, and fault-injection evidence against a per-failure recovery-time budget
