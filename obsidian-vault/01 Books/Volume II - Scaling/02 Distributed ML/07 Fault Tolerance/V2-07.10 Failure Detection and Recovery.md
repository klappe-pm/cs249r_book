# V2-07.10 Failure Detection and Recovery

[[Home|Home]] · [[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/Volume 2 Overview|Volume Overview]] · [[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/02 Distributed ML/07 Fault Tolerance/V2-07 Fault Tolerance|V2-07 Fault Tolerance]]

^sec-fault-tolerance-reliability-reliability-failure-detection-recovery-0f54

A GPU silently hangs, dropping its utilization to zero while its peer GPUs wait indefinitely at an AllReduce barrier. On the chapter’s 10,000-GPU cluster, every minute the system takes to notice this hung rank and reboot the node costs about \$330 in idle compute. Checkpoints preserve state, but the recovery process itself determines how much compute a failure wastes. In the accounting of the Fleet Law (**[[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/00 Introduction/01 Introduction/V2-01.06 The C³ Taxonomy and Foundations of Scale#^eq-fleet-law|equation 1.3]]**), that waiting belongs to the synchronization term ${T_{\text{sync}}}({N})$. Every surviving rank idles at the barrier until the group is whole again, so detection, restart, and reload add directly to step time averaged over the run. The goodput accounting of **[[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/03 The Fleet in Production/09 Fleet Efficiency/V2-09.03 ML Goodput#^sec-performance-engineering-goodput-boundaries|Accounting boundaries]]** charges the same time to runtime goodput, the share of a job’s held allocation spent on steps a checkpoint retains.

[[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/02 Distributed ML/07 Fault Tolerance/V2-07.10 Failure Detection and Recovery#^eq-recovery-time|Equation 10]] decomposes recovery time into four primary components:

$$
{T_{\text{recovery}}}= {T_{\text{detect}}}+ {T_{\text{restart}}}+ {T_{\text{load}}}+ {T_{\text{warmup}}} \tag{10}
$$

^eq-recovery-time

where ${T_{\text{detect}}}$ is the time between the physical hardware fault and system classification as a failure. The term ${T_{\text{restart}}}$ is the latency required for the job scheduler to allocate resources and launch replacement processes. The term ${T_{\text{load}}}$ is the I/O duration to read checkpoint state from distributed storage into GPU memory. The term ${T_{\text{warmup}}}$ is the interval needed to refill data pipelines, compile just-in-time (JIT) kernels, and stabilize training throughput. Each component presents distinct optimization opportunities, and the dominant term varies by cluster configuration. Understanding this decomposition enables targeted investment in the bottleneck rather than uniform improvement across all components.

### Failure Detection Mechanisms

^sec-fault-tolerance-reliability-reliability-failure-detection-mechanisms-efed

![[90 Assets/Volume II/07 Fault Tolerance/Fault Tolerance Detection Ladder d0779bbee3.svg]]

*A ladder of bars for detection latency on a log scale, ordered by how hard a failure is to see: a process crash within 30 seconds, a GPU hang within 120 seconds, a network partition within 180 seconds, and silent data corruption as the longest bar, labeled hours, whose end continues as a dotted open extension.*

*Detection latency climbs from seconds (crash) to hours (silent corruption).*

To the rest of the cluster, a dead rank and a rank busy flushing a multi-gigabyte checkpoint shard both look silent. Every failure detector must therefore balance two expensive mistakes: setting timeouts too short kills healthy workers during transient network or storage stalls, while setting them too long leaves thousands of peer GPUs idling at a collective barrier. Heartbeat monitoring is the first detection layer and usually sets the floor on ${T_{\text{detect}}}$. Each GPU rank periodically signals liveness to a coordinator, and if no heartbeat arrives within the timeout period ${T_{\text{timeout}}}$, the coordinator declares the worker failed.

For a heartbeat interval of ${H}$ seconds and network-delay standard deviation ${\sigma_d}$ in seconds, [[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/02 Distributed ML/07 Fault Tolerance/V2-07.10 Failure Detection and Recovery#^eq-timeout-calculation|equation 11]] defines the timeout heuristic:

$$
{T_{\text{timeout}}} = {H}+ {k}{\sigma_d} \tag{11}
$$

^eq-timeout-calculation

The first term waits out one full heartbeat interval, because a healthy worker may have just sent its last beat, and the second adds a margin for heartbeats that the network delays longer than usual. Here, ${k}$ is a dimensionless safety multiplier; values from three to five illustrate a tuning range rather than a guaranteed false positive rate. The heuristic treats the normal heartbeat delivery delay as small relative to the heartbeat interval. A deployment with substantial baseline delay or a heavy delay tail must account for those measurements as well as ${\sigma_d}$. Tuning ${T_{\text{timeout}}}$ too tightly causes legitimate checkpoint writes or long collectives to trigger false failure declarations; tuning it too loosely extends the window during which other ranks wait for the missing contribution.

Collective communication timeouts provide a second detection layer. During synchronous training, a collective required by the next step remains an incomplete critical-path dependency if a required rank disappears. AllReduce needs every rank’s contribution before it can produce the reduced result; the progress and failure behavior of other collectives depends on the operation and runtime. Transport and process-group timeouts bound how long the job waits before declaring the operation failed and entering recovery. NCCL[[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/02 Distributed ML/07 Fault Tolerance/V2-07.21 Footnotes#^fn16|¹⁶]] provides configurable transport and reliability, availability, and serviceability (RAS) timeout parameters for this purpose, while higher-level frameworks may impose process-group operation timeouts. These settings are often set conservatively to avoid crashing jobs during legitimate periods of slow communication, which unfortunately extends ${T_{\text{detect}}}$.

Container orchestration health checks provide a third layer. Kubernetes, the container orchestration system, offers liveness probes (verifying that a process still responds and makes progress) and readiness probes (verifying that a process is ready to handle requests). Slurm, the batch scheduler common on HPC-style training clusters, instead runs a periodic node health-check program that can drain a node whose GPUs, links, or file systems fail their tests. Both operate independently of the training framework, catching failures that application-level heartbeats might miss; a liveness probe that tests forward progress rather than mere process existence can catch a process that is alive but deadlocked.

Loss spike detection exposes an anomaly, not its cause. [[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/02 Distributed ML/07 Fault Tolerance/V2-07.10 Failure Detection and Recovery#^fig-training-failure-signatures|Figure 14]] contrasts an idealized healthy loss trend with gradient explosion, a premature plateau, and NaN divergence. Real stochastic training need not decrease monotonically, so a transient rise alone does not establish failure, and nonfinite values need not crash the process. Silent hardware corruption can produce an abrupt loss or gradient-norm spike, but so can a bad batch, an optimizer instability, a data-ordering defect, or a distributed-state mismatch. Robust systems therefore pause before the suspect update commits, preserve the input and worker evidence, and replay the step on known-good hardware. The system drains hardware only after that evidence implicates the device; otherwise, it repairs the data, optimizer, or synchronization path that produced the same symptom.

![[90 Assets/Volume II/07 Fault Tolerance/Cell 26 Output 1 9fcbc3b20d.png]]

Figure 14: **Training Failure Signatures**: Common patterns of model loss over time that indicate underlying system or algorithmic failures. (A) Normal training. (B) Gradient explosion. (C) Loss plateau. (D) NaN divergence, ending where a nonfinite-value check halts the run; without such a check the process can keep running on NaN values rather than crash. Identifying these signatures automatically is essential for rapid recovery in autonomous training pipelines. The curves are synthetic illustrations of each pattern, not logs from a training run.

**Systems Perspective 7.5: Realistic failure detection latencies**

Failure detection includes more than the configured heartbeat timeout when the policy requires verification before recovery. The ranges in [[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/02 Distributed ML/07 Fault Tolerance/V2-07.10 Failure Detection and Recovery#^tbl-fault-tolerance-detection-latencies|table 8]] illustrate planning scenarios, not universal detection guarantees. Aggressive timeouts risk false positives (killing healthy-but-slow workers), while conservative timeouts delay real failure detection. A multi-stage policy can use an initial timeout to trigger investigation and a later threshold to trigger recovery. Its actual latency must be measured under the workload and failure conditions it is intended to handle.

| **Failure type**           | **Planning range** | **Why**                                        |
|:---------------------------|:------------------:|:-----------------------------------------------|
| **Process crash**          |    5–30 seconds    | Heartbeat timeout + verification retries       |
| **GPU hang**               |   30–120 seconds   | Must distinguish from legitimately slow kernel |
| **Network partition**      |   60–180 seconds   | Must distinguish from temporary congestion     |
| **Silent data corruption** |  Minutes to hours  | Requires statistical anomaly detection         |

### Recovery Procedures

^sec-fault-tolerance-reliability-reliability-recovery-procedures-002d

Once a failure is classified, a cold-restart recovery path executes a coordinated sequence to restore consistency. The warm-restart alternative developed next preserves surviving processes when their state can be trusted.

1.  Job termination: A `SIGTERM` is broadcast to all surviving workers. This cold-restart path tears down the affected training group so that no survivor continues using the old membership or partially completed collective state.
2.  Resource reclamation: The scheduler marks the failed node as “draining” to prevent immediate rescheduling and requests a replacement from the spare pool.
3.  Job restart: New containers are launched (from cache if available), and the training binary is re-initialized on all nodes.
4.  Checkpoint loading: Each worker reads the state assigned to it from the distributed filesystem. When membership or parallelism changes, recovery remaps the saved shards to the new partitions.
5.  State synchronization: Ranks handshake to establish a new communicator, the object that names the participating set (for example, through `ncclCommInitRank`), and workers verify they are all at the same training step.
6.  Training resumption: The data loader restores its recorded position and sampling state, and the training loop resumes from the checkpoint step. A batch index alone suffices only when it reconstructs the same logical input stream.

Automatic recovery systems perform these steps without human intervention. Modern training frameworks integrate with cluster managers to automate parts of the sequence. DeepSpeed documents save/load routines for model and ZeRO optimizer checkpoints, which provide the durable state needed after failure ([[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/02 Distributed ML/07 Fault Tolerance/V2-07.20 Source References#^ref-deepspeedCheckpointingDocs|DeepSpeed Developers 2026a]]). PyTorch’s `torchrun` elastic launch documents failure and membership-change behavior through its rendezvous mechanism,[[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/02 Distributed ML/07 Fault Tolerance/V2-07.21 Footnotes#^fn17|¹⁷]] the coordinator-backed protocol by which surviving workers agree on membership and ranks after a restart ([[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/02 Distributed ML/07 Fault Tolerance/V2-07.20 Source References#^ref-pytorchElasticDocs|PyTorch Contributors 2026c]], [[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/02 Distributed ML/07 Fault Tolerance/V2-07.20 Source References#^ref-pytorchRendezvousDocs|2026b]]).

Recovery validation tests whether the restored job behaves consistently with its recorded state. Parameter shapes, dtypes, and shard identities must match the manifest. A short replay or validation workload then checks loss and gradient statistics under the expected numerical tolerance; different batches need not reproduce the last prefailure loss. Immediate divergence is evidence to investigate the checkpoint, data state, and execution configuration, not proof that the snapshot is corrupt. Falling back to an earlier validated generation is appropriate when the newest state cannot be trusted.

Recovery must also prevent the failed ownership epoch from returning. A coordinator grants each worker-group incarnation a monotonically increasing epoch (a membership counter, unrelated to a training epoch over the dataset) and associates membership with a lease, a grant of ownership that expires unless the holder renews it. Every state-changing write carries that epoch as a fencing token. Before replacement writes proceed, the recovery protocol advances the enforced epoch at each protected resource, which rejects writes carrying an older token ([[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/02 Distributed ML/07 Fault Tolerance/V2-07.10 Failure Detection and Recovery#^fig-epoch-fencing|figure 15]]). The old worker then cannot publish a checkpoint or update job progress, even if it resumes after a long pause; rendezvous likewise admits only the current membership. A lease alone is insufficient because expiration changes the coordinator’s ownership decision, not the old worker’s ability to reach storage. Fencing enforces that decision at the resource boundary ([[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/02 Distributed ML/07 Fault Tolerance/V2-07.20 Source References#^ref-kleppmann2017designing|Kleppmann 2016]]).

![[90 Assets/Volume II/07 Fault Tolerance/Epoch Fencing 0df7de5f14.svg]]

Figure 15: **Epoch Fencing during Recovery**: When the old worker’s lease expires, the coordinator starts epoch 8 and advances the epoch the checkpoint store enforces before the replacement begins writing. The replacement’s writes carry token 8 and are accepted; when the old worker resumes from its pause or partition, its write still carries token 7 and the store rejects it. The lease decides ownership, and the fence at the store enforces that decision even against a worker that never learned it lost ownership.

^fig-epoch-fencing

Recovery steps such as a checkpoint read or a rendezvous attempt are often retried when they fail, and a careless retry can repeat a side effect or overload a service that is itself recovering. An operation may be retried automatically only when it is idempotent, meaning that repeating it has the same effect as performing it once, or carries a stable operation identifier that lets the receiver deduplicate repeated effects. Each attempt inherits the caller’s absolute deadline rather than receiving a fresh timeout, and the system caps attempts with a retry budget shared across the request or recovery tree. Exponential backoff, which multiplies the wait by a fixed factor after each failed attempt, and jitter, which randomizes each wait, spread attempts in time, but they do not prevent overload if every layer retries independently. The budget therefore belongs at one retry-owning layer; exhausted budget returns a failure or invokes degradation instead of amplifying the original incident ([[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/02 Distributed ML/07 Fault Tolerance/V2-07.20 Source References#^ref-beyer2016sre|Beyer et al. 2016]]; [[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/02 Distributed ML/07 Fault Tolerance/V2-07.20 Source References#^ref-nygard2007releaseit|Nygard 2007]]).

**Napkin Math 7.3: The recovery time budget**

**Problem**: Consider a 175B parameter model training on 1,024 GPUs. The checkpoint size is approximately 2.45 TB (BF16 weights, FP32 master weights, and Adam moments at 14 bytes per parameter). Estimate the cost of a single failure event.

**Setup**: The recovery budget ${T_{\text{recovery}}}$ has four terms.

- **${T_{\text{detect}}}$**: 60 s (conservative heartbeat timeout with verification retries).
- **${T_{\text{restart}}}$**: 3 min (scheduler queue time + container launch + Python import overhead + NCCL initialization).
- **${T_{\text{load}}}$**: 24.5 s (this is the aggregate-shared-storage read of the full 2.45 TB checkpoint at 100 GB/s aggregate). With sharded local-NVMe reads at 5 GB/s, one GPU’s 2.4 GB shard alone would load in under a second. The GPUs of a node share its drive, however, so the node’s 19.1 GB share takes about 3.8 s. That local read is available only on nodes that survived with their local copy.
- **${T_{\text{warmup}}}$**: 2 min (JIT kernel compilation, data pipeline buffer fill, TCP connection re-establishment).

**Total**: ${T_{\text{recovery}}}= {T_{\text{detect}}}+ {T_{\text{restart}}}+ {T_{\text{load}}}+ {T_{\text{warmup}}}\approx$ 6.4 min per failure event.

**Impact**: For this recovery path, ${T_{\text{recovery}}}$ is the operational MTTR term in the steady-state availability relation (perspective [7.1](https://mlsysbook.ai/vol2/07_fault_tolerance/07_fault_tolerance.html#psp-fault-tolerance-scale-transforms-failure)). It is not the whole cost of a failure. Every failure also discards the work done since the last completed checkpoint, on average half the checkpoint interval ([[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/02 Distributed ML/07 Fault Tolerance/V2-07.09 Checkpointing and Progress Preservation#^fig-checkpoint-recovery-timeline|figure 12]]), and the Young-Daly waste of [[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/02 Distributed ML/07 Fault Tolerance/V2-07.09 Checkpointing and Progress Preservation#^eq-ft-checkpoint-waste|equation 8]] already counts that rework, so the figures below price only the recovery term.

Recovery time optimization matters more as clusters grow. The GPU-only baseline from [[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/02 Distributed ML/07 Fault Tolerance/V2-07.03 The Mathematics of Inevitable Failure#^tbl-cluster-mtbf-scaling|table 1]] compares the two sizes on a common basis and understates both failure counts. On that basis, a 1,024-GPU cluster has MTBF of 49 hours and experiences about 0.49 failures/day, losing about 3 minutes daily, a modest 0.2 percent overhead. However, a 10,000-GPU cluster has GPU-only MTBF of 5 hours and experiences about 4.8 failures/day, losing about 31 minutes daily (2.1 percent overhead). That is equivalent to wasting roughly 5,127 GPU-hours every day (the daily lost minutes multiplied across all 10,000 GPUs). Failures arrive in proportion to the number of GPUs, so when each one costs the same recovery time, recovery overhead grows linearly with cluster size. The optimized checkpoint terms grow only with the square root of cluster size ([[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/02 Distributed ML/07 Fault Tolerance/V2-07.09 Checkpointing and Progress Preservation#^sec-fault-tolerance-young-daly|section 7.8.1]]), so under that fixed recovery time, recovery eventually becomes the larger loss.

### Warm Restart Vs. Cold Restart

^sec-fault-tolerance-reliability-warm-vs-cold-restart

The recovery procedure described in [[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/02 Distributed ML/07 Fault Tolerance/V2-07.10 Failure Detection and Recovery#^sec-fault-tolerance-reliability-reliability-recovery-procedures-002d|section 7.9.2]] is a cold restart: every process in the affected job group exits, and the group reloads state from a valid checkpoint. This simplifies recovery by avoiding dependence on surviving in-memory state, but it can discard useful state. After one GPU fails in the 1,024-GPU reference job, every surviving worker may retain reusable values; an interrupted update or silent corruption, however, can make some of those values unsafe to keep.

A warm restart preserves the state of surviving workers. When a rank fails, surviving ranks detect the failure but do not exit. They enter a waiting state, preserving loaded model weights and optimizer states in GPU memory. The scheduler replaces only the failed node. The new node joins, receives the current state of its partition from a peer replica where one exists, or otherwise loads it from the latest checkpoint, and the communicator is rebuilt. The replacement and survivors must agree on the same committed training step before resuming. A shard restored from an older checkpoint cannot be combined with survivor shards that have advanced beyond it; the runtime must reconstruct the missing current state or roll the group back to a common recovery point. Training resumes only after that consistency check.

When the missing state can be reconstructed at the survivors’ current step, warm restart avoids a full-cluster reload and repeated warmup. For the 1,024 GPUs example, that fast path avoids rereading the full 2.45 TB checkpoint through shared storage, whose cold-path ${T_{\text{load}}}$ is 24.5 s. The replacement still needs state transfer and initialization, and all ranks wait until it is ready. If consistency instead requires a rollback, the recovery budget must include the corresponding survivor-state restoration and lost work.

The trade-off is software complexity. Survivor-preserving warm restarts require specialized application and runtime support for dynamic membership changes without leaking CUDA memory, corrupting shared state, or deadlocking during communicator reconstruction. Common training frameworks restart the worker group or reload a checkpoint rather than preserve survivors ([[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/02 Distributed ML/07 Fault Tolerance/V2-07.11 Elastic Recovery#^sec-fault-tolerance-reliability-reliability-framework-support-elastic-training-8b75|section 7.10.1.2]]). A deployment that implements a warm-restart fast path still needs cold restart as a safety net. The cold-restart range in [[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/02 Distributed ML/07 Fault Tolerance/V2-07.10 Failure Detection and Recovery#^tbl-cold-vs-warm-restart|table 9]] brackets the 6.4 min budget computed above. The warm-restart range is a planning range for the fast path, which applies only when the replacement’s current state can be reconstructed and avoids the full-cluster reload and warmup; its actual duration depends on how quickly the replacement receives its state and the communicator is rebuilt.

| **Aspect**                  | **Cold restart**                 | **Warm restart**                       |
|:----------------------------|:---------------------------------|:---------------------------------------|
| **Recovery time**           | 4–10 minutes (full reload)       | 30–90 seconds (single node reload)     |
| **State guarantee**         | Clean: all state from checkpoint | Assumes surviving state is valid       |
| **Implementation**          | Simple: kill all, reload all     | Complex: dynamic membership management |
| **Failure during recovery** | Retry cold restart               | Fall back to cold restart              |
| **Best for**                | Correlated failures, SDC events  | Single-node failures, GPU errors       |

Table 9: **Cold vs. Warm Restart Trade-Offs**: Cold restart kills every process and reloads from disk; warm restart preserves surviving workers and replaces only the failed node. Warm restart cuts recovery from minutes to tens of seconds for the typical single-node failure but introduces software complexity around dynamic membership management. Mature deployments can run warm restart as the fast path and cold restart as the safety net. Recovery times are illustrative planning ranges, not measurements.

^tbl-cold-vs-warm-restart

### Recovery Automation Pipeline

^sec-fault-tolerance-reliability-recovery-automation-pipeline

At the scale of 10,000+ GPUs, failures occur multiple times per day, so human operators cannot intervene manually on each incident. Recovery must be an autonomous control loop managed by the cluster control plane. Published reports from large training fleets describe multi-stage automation that classifies failures and selects the minimum viable remediation ([[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/02 Distributed ML/07 Fault Tolerance/V2-07.20 Source References#^ref-dubey2024llama|Dubey et al. 2024]]; [[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/02 Distributed ML/07 Fault Tolerance/V2-07.20 Source References#^ref-kokolis2025|Kokolis et al. 2025]]; [[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/02 Distributed ML/07 Fault Tolerance/V2-07.20 Source References#^ref-zu2024tpuv4|Zu et al. 2024]]).

The pipeline operates in four ordered stages:

1.  Health monitoring daemon: Continuously scrapes GPU telemetry (ECC error counters, temperature, fan speed, NVLink status) alongside application metrics like training loss and step throughput, often from a sidecar container, a helper process deployed beside the training container.
2.  Failure classifier: Determines whether a signal indicates a fatal error (for example, NVIDIA Xid error 48: double-bit ECC error), a transient stall (for example, temporary network congestion), or a performance degradation (for example, thermal throttling).
3.  Action selector: Chooses the cheapest response consistent with the affected state and communication group. A process hang on one rank usually requires restarting the whole communication group rather than one container, because every surviving rank still holds a communicator that includes the hung rank. A confirmed hardware fault requires draining the node and drawing a replacement from the spare pool, which is slower and consumes standby capacity. A network partition can justify a bounded wait that preserves in-memory state, but if the wait expires, replacement must fence the old ownership before accepting new writes.
4.  Validation stage: Runs a recorded “canary batch” or validation workload under the expected numerical tolerance. Unexpected loss or gradient behavior triggers investigation of restored state and execution configuration, with fallback to an earlier validated snapshot when the new state cannot be trusted.

This automation removes the human page-diagnose-repair cycle from the recovery path for the common failure types, which shortens mean time to recovery. The classification stage matters most. Treating every failure as a cold restart wastes compute on transient issues, while treating a hardware failure as transient allows corrupted computation to continue. The Llama 3 pretraining run shows what such a pipeline buys at fleet scale.

**War Story 7.2: The training run that failed every few hours (2024)**

**Context**: Meta reports training Llama 3 405B on 16,384 H100 GPUs during a 54-day snapshot of pretraining ([[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/02 Distributed ML/07 Fault Tolerance/V2-07.20 Source References#^ref-dubey2024llama|Dubey et al. 2024]]).

**Mechanism**: The snapshot recorded 419 unexpected interruptions, about one every 3.1 hours, with the root causes shown in [[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/02 Distributed ML/07 Fault Tolerance/V2-07.04 Failure Taxonomy#^fig-llama3-interruptions|figure 2]].

**Impact**: Meta nevertheless reported more than 90 percent effective training time, measured as useful training time divided by elapsed time. Significant manual intervention was required only three times.

**Fix**: Meta reduced job startup and checkpointing time and developed automated diagnosis and recovery tools. Communication traces helped locate stalled collectives and slow workers before restarting useful work.

**Systems lesson**: At fleet scale, reliability is a property of the recovery system rather than of the hardware. Effective training time stayed high because automation, not operators, handled nearly every interruption.

### Stragglers Versus Fail-Stop Failures

^sec-fault-tolerance-reliability-reliability-distinguishing-stragglers-failures-285d

Not every recovery decision starts from a crashed process. When one GPU in a synchronous job slows down without dying, every healthy peer still waits at the AllReduce barrier, forcing the recovery pipeline to decide whether the slow rank is a temporary straggler or an incipient failure (perspective [7.6](https://mlsysbook.ai/vol2/07_fault_tolerance/07_fault_tolerance.html#psp-fault-tolerance-straggler-or-failure)).

**Systems Perspective 7.6: Straggler or failure**

A straggler, the slow rank of a synchronous step (**[[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/02 Distributed ML/06 Distributed Training/V2-06.04 Data Parallelism#^sec-distributed-training-systems-systems-synchronization-models-396f|Synchronization models]]**), bounds the throughput of every peer that waits on it, so one node running 10 percent slow can cost thousands of nodes 10 percent of their capacity. Fault tolerance must decide what that slowness means. A straggler can still produce correct results, so slowness alone establishes neither incorrect values nor a failed process, and its cause is often system jitter from background OS processes, network congestion, or thermal throttling rather than bad hardware. The decision is between mitigation, which keeps the job running and removes the slowdown, and recovery, which treats the rank as failed and pays for a restart.

Under the simplified no-overlap straggler model, the slowest rank sets the step time of the entire cluster because AllReduce cannot complete until every participant has submitted its gradients:

$$
{T_{\text{step,straggler}}}({N}) = \max({T_{\text{rank}_0}}, {T_{\text{rank}_1}}, \dots, {T_{\text{rank}_{N-1}}}) + {T_{\text{comm}}}({N})
$$

Many stragglers are gray failures, the silent degradations named in the failure taxonomy, seen from inside a synchronous job, and none of their common causes raises an explicit error. Thermal throttling reduces clock speed, degrading interconnect cables increase communication latency, OS background processes (memory scrubbing, log rotation) consume CPU cycles, and data loading from a congested network filesystem introduces variable I/O delays. A slow worker can continue meeting a liveness timeout while reducing training throughput, or become slow enough to trigger the same timeout as a failed worker.

Progress measurements are therefore needed alongside liveness checks. A common detector records each rank’s step time, or the time its contribution reaches each collective, and compares it with the median across ranks, so a rank that is persistently slower than its peers stands out even while it answers every heartbeat. It flags a rank once its time exceeds the median by a set margin over several consecutive steps. A tight margin or a short window catches a degrading device sooner but also flags ranks slowed by transient jitter, while a loose margin leaves the whole job running at the slow rank’s pace for longer.

Timeout policy governs where the system draws the line between *mitigation* (redistributing work or replacing the slow node) and *recovery* (checkpoint-restart). Aggressive timeouts treat transient stragglers as fail-stop crashes, causing unnecessary job restarts that waste more compute than the slowdown itself, whereas conservative timeouts leave thousands of healthy GPUs waiting on a degraded node that will never speed up. A slow rank that stays in the job lowers program goodput, the ratio of a step’s ideal execution time to its actual time, on every step, while replacing it spends runtime goodput on the interruption. The goodput accounting of **[[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/03 The Fleet in Production/09 Fleet Efficiency/V2-09.03 ML Goodput#^sec-performance-engineering-goodput-boundaries|Accounting boundaries]]** records these as separate losses. The replacement decision therefore weighs one loss against the other.

Straggler mitigation strategies change either the work assignment or the execution resources. Dynamic load balancing moves work away from slow workers while preserving the intended update, whereas bounded staleness allows delayed gradients under an algorithm that tolerates them; its convergence cost depends on the workload and delay bound. Backup workers replicate selected tasks and accept one result per logical contribution, trading extra GPU cycles for a shorter wait without double-counting gradients. At the infrastructure boundary, proactive replacement uses hardware telemetry, such as rising operating temperatures or growing correctable-ECC error counts, to identify suspect components and replace them when the expected benefit exceeds migration and recovery costs. A simple cost calculation shows why a severe straggler is often cheaper to replace than to tolerate.

**Napkin Math 7.4: The cost of a straggler**

Consider a 1,024-GPU cluster where a normal training iteration takes 1 second. A single GPU enters a thermally throttled state, clocking down to 50 percent speed, and now takes 2 seconds to complete its computation.

Because AllReduce cannot complete until every rank has submitted its gradients, the other 1,023 healthy GPUs sit idle waiting for the straggler.

**Impact**:

- Normal step time: 1 second
- Straggler step time: 2 seconds
- Effective cluster speed: 1 step/2s = 0.5 steps/sec

A single failing device (0.1 percent of the hardware) has reduced the throughput of the entire cluster by 50 percent. At \$2/GPU-hour, this straggler wastes \$1,024/hour in idle compute.

**Systems insight**: Replacement is worthwhile only when the expected time saved exceeds recovery and replay costs. Suppose the slowdown is expected to persist for the next ${K}$ useful updates, whose step times are ${T_{\text{slow}}}$ on the degraded allocation and ${T_{\text{healthy}}}$ after replacement. The simplified break-even condition is then ${K}({T_{\text{slow}}}-{T_{\text{healthy}}}) > {T_{\text{restart}}}+ {T_{\text{replay}}}$. The right side includes waiting for capacity and restoring state. A short-lived slowdown may clear before replacement pays off; false-positive detection and uncertain persistence also affect the decision. MTBF describes failure recurrence, not how long this slowdown will last.

Replacing a slow node while maintaining the original GPU count requires available capacity. An ${N}+1$ redundancy model reserves a hot spare for the isolation and promotion stages of [[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/02 Distributed ML/07 Fault Tolerance/V2-07.06 Check-And-Verify Defenses Against Silent Data Corruption#^fig-sdc-controller|figure 10]], reducing allocation delay. Promotion still requires compatible resources, valid state, and communication-group reconstruction; an idle device alone does not guarantee recovery within seconds. A cold spare adds startup work, while a job without a reserved spare depends on the scheduler finding capacity or on repair. The hot spare buys shorter waiting time with devices that sit idle while the fleet is healthy. Fixed-size checkpoint-restart recovery restores the original resource count. Elastic recovery relaxes that requirement by allowing the job to continue with fewer workers when the remaining allocation can support it.
