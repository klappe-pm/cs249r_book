# V2-07.06 Check-And-Verify Defenses Against Silent Data Corruption

[[Home|Home]] · [[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/Volume 2 Overview|Volume Overview]] · [[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/02 Distributed ML/07 Fault Tolerance/V2-07 Fault Tolerance|V2-07 Fault Tolerance]]

^sec-ft-sdc-verification

Of the hardware faults just classified, the costliest are the ones whose manifestation is silent. A corrupted value that raises no error passes every liveness check the fleet runs, and training keeps building on it. Defending against silent data corruption therefore requires checks that establish a correct result, not merely a live process, and choosing those checks starts with where the corruption comes from and how it surfaces in training.

### The Physics of Silent Corruption

^sec-fault-tolerance-reliability-physics-silent-corruption-5d5d

An accelerator executes deterministic logic gates, yet the silicon carrying those gates operates under narrow physical margins where both random particle strikes and load-dependent defects can flip bits without halting the processor. Single-event upsets (SEUs), the particle strikes introduced with transient failures, create fleet exposure that grows with device count and operating time even when the per-device rate is low. Manufacturing variances can instead leave “marginal” chips that pass initial QA but fail under particular inputs or operating conditions. Voltage and temperature margins matter, including supply disturbances during rapid current changes ($d{i}/d{t}$). Because these margin defects produce repeatable errors under specific workloads while particle strikes arrive at random, fleet reliability requires a probabilistic exposure model even when individual hardware faults are deterministic.

Facebook (now Meta) documented a pervasive SDC issue where a hardware fault caused a valid file to be reported as “size zero” during decompression ([[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/02 Distributed ML/07 Fault Tolerance/V2-07.20 Source References#^ref-dixit2021silent|Dixit et al. 2021]]); the system “worked” without crashing, but data was silently deleted. In ML, the same failure manifests as valid-looking but numerically corrupted gradients. The pipeline trace in [[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/02 Distributed ML/07 Fault Tolerance/V2-07.06 Check-And-Verify Defenses Against Silent Data Corruption#^fig-sdc-example|figure 7]] follows a single ALU or memory bit flip as it corrupts an individual tensor entry, taints gradient reduction during the backward pass, commits into weights, and finally surfaces as degraded quality at evaluation. The comparative timelines beneath contrast this silent latency with a standard detected error. A hardware trap halts within milliseconds to restart cleanly from the last checkpoint, whereas silent corruption commits contaminated state into checkpoints over hours or days before an evaluation alert forces a costly rollback.

Telemetry from production training fleets shows this corruption is routine rather than anomalous. At modern training scales, hardware bit flips occur regularly, and erroneous computations from a single malfunctioning accelerator can propagate into the global model state, as Jeff Dean documented in his MLSys 2024 keynote on silent data corruption ([[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/02 Distributed ML/07 Fault Tolerance/V2-07.20 Source References#^ref-dean2024mlsys|Dean 2024]]) ([[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/02 Distributed ML/07 Fault Tolerance/V2-07.06 Check-And-Verify Defenses Against Silent Data Corruption#^fig-sdc-jeffdean|figure 8]]).

The plot shows the symptom without identifying the damaged bit or circuit. Corrupting a floating-point exponent is one mechanism that can greatly change a tensor value and subsequent updates. Gradient clipping can limit update magnitude, but it does not reconstruct the correct gradient; an anomaly detector must trigger validation or recovery before suspect state is accepted.

### Verification at Fleet Scale

^sec-ft-sdc-verification-fleet-scale

![[90 Assets/Volume II/07 Fault Tolerance/Vol2 Fault Tolerance Margin 001 877cba6a54.svg]]

*One red curve over training steps that rises and flattens toward a dashed certainty line, with a dot marking 63 percent: cumulative silent-corruption probability approaches certainty over a long run.*

*Cumulative SDC probability nears certainty over a long run.*

Even a small per-GPU SDC rate accumulates across repeated collective operations, and the accumulation grows with fleet size. Any single collective is unlikely to contain an SDC event. Under the illustrative per-GPU rate stated in the notebook below, the per-step probability remains small while the cumulative probability grows over a long training run, reaching $1 - e^{-1} \approx 63$ percent once the expected number of events reaches one. On the chapter’s 10,000-GPU cluster, that rate gives one expected event roughly every 100 hours, or about 22 over the three-month run of the chapter opening. The notebook below scales the same estimate to a fleet ten times larger to show where the trend leads, since the expected interval shrinks in proportion to fleet size.

Standard AllReduce reduces the supplied contributions; it does not establish that an *alive* worker computed a *correct* gradient. The fleet therefore needs validation appropriate to its fault model in addition to liveness checks. The robust aggregation rules of [[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/02 Distributed ML/07 Fault Tolerance/V2-07.04 Failure Taxonomy#^sec-fault-tolerance-reliability-reliability-byzantine-failures-8a24|section 7.3.3]], Krum and trimmed mean, tolerate a bounded number of wrong gradients at extra communication and computation cost. Check-and-verify methods instead look for the wrong values, testing data-path invariants, subject to the boundary drawn for transient failures in [[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/02 Distributed ML/07 Fault Tolerance/V2-07.04 Failure Taxonomy#^sec-fault-tolerance-reliability-reliability-transient-failures-57b4|section 7.3.1]]. Digests test byte integrity and agreement within their error-detection limits; they cannot establish that the original computation was correct. Independent computation, an algorithmic invariant, or replay tests that additional claim under a declared fault model. A quick estimate shows how much exposure those checks must cover.

**Napkin Math 7.1: SDC risk accumulation**

**Problem**: Under an illustrative generic SDC-rate assumption, calculate the probability that at least one GPU in a 100,000-GPU fleet experiences an SDC event during a single 2 s training step.

1.  **Fleet size**: 100,000 accelerators.
2.  **Individual risk**: $10^{-6}$ per GPU-hour (an illustrative generic SDC-rate assumption).
3.  **The exposure**: In a 2-second window, the fleet has $100{,}000 \times (2/3600) \approx 56$ “GPU-hours” of exposure.
4.  **The probability**: $\Pr(\text{at least one SDC}) \approx$ 0.0056 percent.

**Systems insight**: Under this illustrative constant-rate assumption, a 100k-GPU fleet expects one SDC event every 18,000 steps (roughly every 10 hours), even though the per-step probability is only 0.0056 percent. A CRC or hash can protect each shard or reduced buffer against corruption in transit or storage, while a redundant reduction can test the arithmetic path independently. Each check moves robustness from a *restart* problem toward a *verification* problem only for the failure mechanism it covers.

Full replication protects a broader computation path at a correspondingly high resource cost. Dual modular redundancy runs every computation twice and compares the results, and triple modular redundancy runs it three times and takes a majority vote. These schemes pay 100 and 200 percent hardware overhead, respectively, to detect or mask a fault within a single control deadline ([[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/02 Distributed ML/07 Fault Tolerance/V2-07.20 Source References#^ref-sheaffer2007|Sheaffer et al. 2007]]; [[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/02 Distributed ML/07 Fault Tolerance/V2-07.20 Source References#^ref-arifeen2020|Arifeen et al. 2020]]). These guarantees depend on the replicas avoiding a shared fault. The price is justified in safety-critical embedded control, such as a vehicle’s onboard computers, where one wrong output can endanger people and the replicated hardware is a small computer. For fleet training, duplicating every step of a run on 10,000 GPUs doubles the compute devoted to those steps, before comparison and coordination costs. When corruption is rare and rollback can recover valid state, selective redundancy can protect useful progress at lower cost. Screening removes suspect devices, statistical checks run continuously, standby pools absorb drained nodes, and periodic or triggered recomputation checks selected execution paths.

Google’s reactive hot-spare protocol shows how those pieces combine in three stages ([[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/02 Distributed ML/07 Fault Tolerance/V2-07.06 Check-And-Verify Defenses Against Silent Data Corruption#^fig-sdc-controller|figure 10]]). When runtime monitors flag an anomalous gradient and replay, which reruns the flagged computation on other hardware and compares the results, implicates a suspect accelerator (G2), the scheduler isolates that device and promotes a standby hot spare (S) into the active ring. It then provisions a replacement spare (S’) while the suspect unit undergoes hardware diagnostics ([[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/02 Distributed ML/07 Fault Tolerance/V2-07.20 Source References#^ref-dean2024mlsys|Dean 2024]]). The fleet pays in advance for one idle device per pool rather than for a duplicate of every computation, and it gives up coverage in exchange. The spare replaces only a device that monitors and replay have flagged, so corruption those checks miss stays in the training state. The protocol shifts the burden of correctness from unverified hardware to system-level auditing. Even so, these hardware checks reduce only the risk of an unreliable physical substrate; they do not protect against faults that the software stack introduces itself, bugs that look like valid computation while corrupting the ML pipeline.

![[90 Assets/Volume II/07 Fault Tolerance/Sdc Controller 2fb2edebe8.svg]]

Figure 10: **Hot-Spare Failover**: An ${N}+1$ pool of four active GPUs and one standby spare. When loss or gradient sanity checks flag a suspect GPU and replay implicates it, the job drains that device and resumes on the spare from the last known-good state; the device goes to repair and a healthy unit refills the spare slot. Google’s data-center examples use this reactive pattern to keep training moving despite hardware faults, in contrast to running every computation on two or three redundant units and comparing results ([[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/02 Distributed ML/07 Fault Tolerance/V2-07.20 Source References#^ref-dean2024mlsys|Dean 2024]]). The standing cost is the idle spare.
