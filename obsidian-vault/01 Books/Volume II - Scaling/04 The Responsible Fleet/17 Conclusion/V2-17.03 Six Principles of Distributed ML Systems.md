# V2-17.03 Six Principles of Distributed ML Systems

[[Home|Home]] · [[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/Volume 2 Overview|Volume Overview]] · [[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/04 The Responsible Fleet/17 Conclusion/V2-17 Conclusion|V2-17 Conclusion]]

^sec-conclusion-six-principles-distributed-ml-systems-746a

When those inherited constraints surface in production, as a training run that stalls every few hours on a failed component or a serving pool whose tail latency spikes across a fan-out query, the failure comes from the fleet’s physical and institutional structure rather than an operator mistake. The six engineering practices introduced with the fleet stack (instrument first, reserve headroom for recovery and tail latency, expect bottlenecks to migrate, treat failure as steady state, compound small efficiency gains, and co-design hardware with algorithms) describe how a fleet engineer responds to those realities, whereas the six principles of distributed ML systems describe what the fleet’s physical and institutional constraints make true, whatever the practice.

The six principles condense the part-level principles stated at the opening of each part: Part I’s fleet principles condense into the first, Part II’s distributed ML principles into the second and third (while its empirical scaling law supplies the demand side of the growth decision in [[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/04 The Responsible Fleet/17 Conclusion/V2-17.04 The Complete Production System#^sec-conclusion-complete-production-system-3885|section 17.3]]), Part III’s fleet-in-production principles into the fourth, and Part IV’s responsible fleet principles into the fifth and sixth. Where this chapter cites one of those part-level principles as “principle” followed by a number, the number refers to its box in the part opener. The six themselves are named in words, and [[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/04 The Responsible Fleet/17 Conclusion/V2-17.03 Six Principles of Distributed ML Systems#^tbl-vol2-principles|table 1]] pairs each with its practice.

Each principle also explains why its paired practice is necessary rather than merely prudent. A fleet that loses training progress to routine interruptions must budget recovery as part of normal operation, which is why treating failure as steady state is a practice. Because a bottleneck migrates whenever one term shrinks, an optimization judged only by the term it shrinks can leave the step no faster, which is why the engineer must expect bottlenecks to migrate and measure the layers they move into.

↩︎ The six engineering practices, from instrument first to co-design hardware with algorithms, are established with the fleet stack in **[[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/00 Introduction/01 Introduction/V2-01.07 The Fleet Stack and the Lighthouse Archetypes#^sec-vol2-introduction-foundational-concepts|The Fleet Stack and the Lighthouse Archetypes]]**.

The Fleet Law, the distributed counterpart of the single-machine iron law, connects these principles to execution costs. It is the step-time equation of **[[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/00 Introduction/01 Introduction/V2-01.06 The C³ Taxonomy and Foundations of Scale#^eq-fleet-law|equation 1.3]]**, unchanged:

$$
{T_{\text{step}}}({N}) = \frac{{T_{\text{compute}}}}{{N}} + {T_{\text{comm}}}({N}) + {T_{\text{sync}}}({N}) - {T_{\text{overlap}}} \tag{1}
$$

^eq-conclusion-fleet-law

Infrastructure sets the per-device rates behind ${T_{\text{compute}}}/{N}$ and the link bandwidths behind ${T_{\text{comm}}}({N})$. In C³ terms, the first term is Compute, ${T_{\text{comm}}}({N})$ is Communication, the time spent moving bytes, and ${T_{\text{sync}}}({N})$ is Coordination, the time spent waiting at barriers and on stragglers, plus each step’s share of run-level costs such as scheduling and recovery. Neither of the last two is guaranteed to shrink as ${N}$ rises. Failure lands in the same terms. Checkpoint pauses, detection timeouts, and restarts add to ${T_{\text{sync}}}({N})$ averaged over the run. Asynchronous checkpointing and warm restart move part of that cost into ${T_{\text{overlap}}}$, the time hidden behind useful compute, and a rollback adds the lost steps back to the work the run must still complete. Serving applies the same accounting to each request, where queueing and routing delay form the coordination share of its latency. Responsibility and sustainability add no new term, but they spend the existing ones. Verification work occupies compute, approval gates add coordination that no replica count removes, and every second of step time costs joules at the fleet’s average power. Meeting those obligations also requires evidence that execution time cannot supply, such as subgroup error rates, privacy accounting, and energy measured over a stated boundary.

↪ The term-by-term decomposition of the Fleet Law is developed in **[[05 Reference/Volume II - Scaling/V2 The C³ Taxonomy#^sec-appdx-c3-taxonomy-component-decomposition|Component decomposition]]**.

Placed on the fleet stack, the six principles follow its four bands ([[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/04 The Responsible Fleet/17 Conclusion/V2-17.03 Six Principles of Distributed ML Systems#^fig-fleet-stack-conclusion|figure 1]]). The Infrastructure band determines physical capability. The Distribution band partitions execution and therefore carries both communication and failure recovery, one principle per row. The Serving band bounds runtime delivery by its contract. The uppermost Governance band has two rows. Its lower row, Assurance, holds security and robustness, and its top row is split into a governance half and a sustainability half. The band’s two principles divide it by obligation rather than strictly by row. Responsibility covers every obligation owed to the people the system affects, so it spans the whole Assurance row and the governance half of the top row, where **[[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/04 The Responsible Fleet/16 Responsible AI/V2-16 Responsible AI#^sec-responsible-ai|Responsible AI]]** develops fairness and accountability. Sustainability covers the resource obligation alone, in the other half of the top row. The arrow that runs the length of the stack marks the scale moment. Rather than acting as a seventh principle, it is the premise on which this book opened, and crossing it is what turns each of the six from a local concern into a fleet-wide constraint.

The first principle is that infrastructure determines capability. Physical infrastructure constrains both feasible configurations and attainable rates. Memory capacity and safe power and cooling envelopes limit how much state and active hardware a configuration can support. Cluster-wide bisection bandwidth instead limits how quickly distributed work can exchange data (principle [[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/01 The Fleet/V2 Fleet Principles#^pri-bisection-bandwidth|6]]); insufficient bandwidth can make a deadline or cost target unattainable without making the computation impossible. Storage sets a further ceiling, because accelerators that the data pipeline cannot feed sit idle regardless of their peak rate (principle [[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/01 The Fleet/V2 Fleet Principles#^pri-io-wall|7]]). These are the memory, network, and energy walls of the scale moment, measured at the scale of the node, the fabric, and the facility. Partitioning, batch size, and communication scheduling determine how those limits bind, and the limits themselves run from chip-level thermal density to cluster-wide bisection bandwidth.

The second principle is that communication can become the binding term at scale ([[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/04 The Responsible Fleet/17 Conclusion/V2-17.10 Source References#^ref-jiang2024megascale|Jiang et al. 2024]]; [[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/04 The Responsible Fleet/17 Conclusion/V2-17.10 Source References#^ref-narayanan2021|Narayanan et al. 2021]]), with its cost set by the bandwidth-latency trade-off (principle [[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/01 The Fleet/V2 Fleet Principles#^pri-alpha-beta-model|5]]) inside the Fleet Law’s ${T_{\text{comm}}}({N})$ term. Communication intensity, the ratio of bytes crossing the network to local FLOPs, indicates network demand relative to arithmetic. Whether that demand dominates also depends on achieved compute and network rates, message sizes, topology, and overlap. Gradient synchronization and straggler effects must therefore be measured on the synchronous step’s critical path rather than inferred from byte volume alone.

↩︎ The communication-intensity ratio is established in **[[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/00 Introduction/01 Introduction/V2-01.03 From Single Node to Fleet#^sec-vol2-introduction-ci-ratio|Communication intensity (the CI ratio)]]**.

Collective algorithms such as Ring AllReduce,[[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/04 The Responsible Fleet/17 Conclusion/V2-17.11 Footnotes#^fn1|¹]] popularized for deep learning by Horovod ([[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/04 The Responsible Fleet/17 Conclusion/V2-17.10 Source References#^ref-sergeev2018horovod|Sergeev and Balso 2018]]), together with gradient compression and overlapping computation with communication, address communication bottlenecks. For dense collective workloads, conventional oversubscribed fabrics often bottleneck, motivating high-bisection fabrics and transport choices matched to ML communication patterns. Recognizing when communication is the active constraint clarifies when algorithmic optimizations will help and when they merely shift work between equally constrained resources. Such a shift is the displacement of overhead (principle [[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/02 Distributed ML/V2 Distributed ML Principles#^pri-conservation-overhead|14]]), in which reducing one term of the step increases another, and it is what makes bottlenecks migrate. Expanding the fabric across thousands of nodes also multiplies the components that a job needs at the same instant, and that multiplication makes failure routine.

↩︎ Ring collectives, communication overlap, and gradient compression are established in **[[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/02 Distributed ML/05 Collective Communication/V2-05 Collective Communication#^sec-collective-communication|Collective Communication]]**.

The third principle is that failure is routine. A job that needs many components at once can encounter interruptions frequently enough that recovery becomes normal operation. The reliability gap in **[[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/00 Introduction/01 Introduction/V2-01.05 Constraints of Scale#^sec-vol2-introduction-reliability-gap|The reliability gap]]** follows from **[[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/00 Introduction/01 Introduction/V2-01.05 Constraints of Scale#^eq-reliability-gap|equation 1.2]]**. Under it, a job that needs every one of its components at once, each independently available with probability ${A_{\text{component}}}$, sees its availability decay exponentially in the number of components it requires. Meta’s experience training Llama 3 on 16,384 GPUs, 16 times the Archetype A (GPT-4/Llama-3) reference cluster used in this chapter’s worked expansion, documented 419 unexpected interruptions over 54 days, averaging one unexpected interruption every 3.1 hours ([[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/04 The Responsible Fleet/17 Conclusion/V2-17.10 Source References#^ref-dubey2024llama|Dubey et al. 2024]]). A failed rank can stall every other rank in its collective. Under full-job checkpoint recovery, the affected job rolls back to its last checkpoint; other jobs in the fleet need not roll back. For a fixed amount of lost wall-clock progress, wasted accelerator-time grows with the size of the affected job, which is why checkpoint cadence and recovery architecture are first-order design dimensions.

Architects must embed failure handling from the beginning. Checkpointing strategies balance write overhead against expected lost work, and the Young-Daly interval (principle [[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/02 Distributed ML/V2 Distributed ML Principles#^pri-young-daly|13]]) estimates that balance under its failure and checkpoint-cost assumptions. Elastic training supports changing cluster membership, while graceful degradation preserves a defined level of service as capacity diminishes. Each mechanism needs a tested recovery contract; its name alone does not establish that progress or service quality survives a failure. Recovery should automate known failure paths and escalate cases whose safe response requires investigation. Recovery also needs capacity. A restarted synchronous job makes progress only when the scheduler can again grant it a gang allocation, all of its accelerators at once and in a feasible topology (principle [[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/02 Distributed ML/V2 Distributed ML Principles#^pri-feasibility-before-fairness|15]]). That is why fleet orchestration keeps spare capacity, preempts lower-priority work, and admits only allocations a job can actually use. Coordination, the third C³ axis, is not confined to this principle. It is paid in recovery and gang scheduling here, in request routing for serving, and in the approval gates of governance.

↩︎ Checkpoint cadence, mean time between failures (MTBF) analysis, and elastic recovery protocols are established in **[[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/02 Distributed ML/07 Fault Tolerance/V2-07 Fault Tolerance#^sec-fault-tolerance-reliability|Fault Tolerance]]**.

Communication and failure together constitute the operational reality of the Distribution band. Deployment carries both into a setting where the unit of work is a user request rather than a training step, which gives the fourth principle, that serving is bounded by its contract. The accounting begins before any request arrives. The ML goodput decomposition (principle [[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/03 The Fleet in Production/V2 Fleet-In-Production Principles#^pri-ml-goodput|16]]) assigns each interval of capacity that the built fleet loses to exactly one of three factors, so that every loss has one owner. Scheduling goodput is the share of capacity held by complete allocations, runtime goodput is the share of that allocated time retained as progress, and program goodput is the fraction of the ideal rate that the retained steps achieve. A serving fleet’s useful capacity is then the work it completes within its latency, availability, and cost targets, not the work its accelerators execute in isolation, which is what **[[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/03 The Fleet in Production/10 Serving at Scale/V2-10.04 SLO Goodput and the Serving Tax#^sec-inference-scale-serving-tax-overhead-distribution-5ef3|SLO Goodput and the Serving Tax]]** measures as service-level objective (SLO) goodput.

Production inference that waits for every worker in a fan-out inherits tail effects ([[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/04 The Responsible Fleet/17 Conclusion/V2-17.10 Source References#^ref-dean2013|Dean and Barroso 2013]]), because completion depends on the slowest required response. A request that touches 100 servers has a 63.4 percent chance that at least one of them responds beyond its 99th-percentile latency when server delays are independent,[[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/04 The Responsible Fleet/17 Conclusion/V2-17.11 Footnotes#^fn2|²]] so a latency that is rare for one server becomes the common case for the request. Routing is where coordination enters the request path. Probing two of ${R}$ replicas and sending the request to the less loaded (principle [[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/03 The Fleet in Production/V2 Fleet-In-Production Principles#^pri-power-two-choices|19]]) sharply shortens the longest queue in the idealized balls-into-bins model, at the cost of only two probes. On live traffic the gain still depends on arrivals, service times, and how fresh the load estimate is, so it narrows the tail rather than bounding every request’s latency.

↩︎ The power of two choices and the queue-length bounds behind it are established in **[[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/03 The Fleet in Production/10 Serving at Scale/V2-10.12 Load Balancing and Request Routing#^sec-inference-scale-power-two-choices-ffdd|The power of two choices]]**.

Inside each replica, the memory wall returns as a serving constraint. Compute (TFLOP/s) can be abundant while memory bandwidth (GB/s) gates small-batch autoregressive decode (principle [[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/03 The Fleet in Production/V2 Fleet-In-Production Principles#^pri-autoregressive-bottleneck|18]]), because weight and KV-cache traffic from high-bandwidth memory (HBM) leaves compute units underused. Batching amortizes each weight read across the requests in the batch, so the binding constraint can shift toward compute, KV traffic, or the latency budget. A replica’s execution envelope must therefore be measured before any fleet-level policy is chosen. The contract also has a lifetime. A heavily used model can spend more on inference than it did on training (principle [[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/03 The Fleet in Production/V2 Fleet-In-Production Principles#^pri-serving-cost-dominance|17]]), so the cost per request, not the one-time training bill, eventually dominates what the model costs to own.

Beyond the data center, the contract takes other forms. On a phone-class device the contract is the device’s own power, thermal, and foreground-latency envelope, and the device enforces it by admitting background work, including local adaptation that updates the model on the device’s own data, only when the envelope has room. That admission policy has a consequence that reaches back into training. Only devices whose policy accepts the work take part in adaptation, so the serving-side contract also decides whose data the shared model learns from (principle [[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/03 The Fleet in Production/V2 Fleet-In-Production Principles#^pri-participation-selects-population|20]]), a bias the fifth principle must then account for. Across a platform of hundreds of models, staged rollouts and per-service reliability budgets enforce the contract release by release, so every release must carry evidence that its targets still hold. Once the fleet can deliver a service, governance determines whether it is allowed to.

↩︎ On-device adaptation within phone-class power and thermal envelopes is established in **[[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/03 The Fleet in Production/11 Edge Intelligence/V2-11.04 Design Constraints#^sec-edge-intelligence-energy-thermal-constraint-analysis-3133|Energy, heat, and background admission]]**.

The fifth principle is that responsibility constrains design. Responsible AI translates risk-management frameworks into engineering constraints ([[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/04 The Responsible Fleet/17 Conclusion/V2-17.10 Source References#^ref-nist2023airmf|Tabassi 2023]]). Safety-specific risks such as reward hacking, distribution shift, and scalable oversight show why those constraints need implementation mechanisms ([[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/04 The Responsible Fleet/17 Conclusion/V2-17.10 Source References#^ref-amodei2016concrete|Amodei et al. 2016]]). Reward hacking occurs when a system games its measured objective, distribution shift when deployment inputs differ from the training data, and scalable oversight is the difficulty of supervising behavior that people cannot check directly. Fairness, transparency, accountability, privacy, and safety are first-class requirements that shape system architecture throughout the ML lifecycle. The part-level principles behind this one span provenance, privacy, robustness, and feedback. A model can be trusted only as far as the verified record of its data, code, and weights reaches (principle [[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/04 The Responsible Fleet/V2 Responsible Fleet Principles#^pri-provenance-bounds-trust|22]]). Released outputs can disclose what a model learned (principle [[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/04 The Responsible Fleet/V2 Responsible Fleet Principles#^pri-information-leakage|23]]). Robustness evaluation and defenses require mechanism-specific resource budgets (principle [[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/04 The Responsible Fleet/V2 Responsible Fleet Principles#^pri-robustness-compute-penalty|24]]). Deployed decisions can shape future training observations (principle [[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/04 The Responsible Fleet/V2 Responsible Fleet Principles#^pri-sociotechnical-feedback|27]]).

↩︎ Reward hacking, scalable oversight, and distribution shift as concrete AI safety problems are established in **[[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/04 The Responsible Fleet/16 Responsible AI/V2-16.09 AI Safety, Robustness, and Generative Controls#^sec-responsible-ai-ai-safety-value-alignment-8c93|AI safety and value alignment]]**.

The fairness impossibility theorem (principle [[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/04 The Responsible Fleet/V2 Responsible Fleet Principles#^pri-fairness-impossibility|26]]) shows why some requirements cannot be satisfied by tuning alone. With unequal base rates and imperfect prediction, a risk score cannot be both calibrated within each group and balanced across groups within each outcome class ([[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/04 The Responsible Fleet/17 Conclusion/V2-17.10 Source References#^ref-kleinberg2016inherent|Kleinberg et al. 2017]]). Calibration requires that, within every group, each score equal the observed rate of the outcome among the people who receive it, and balance requires that people with the same outcome receive the same average score in every group. This is the score-level counterpart of the conflict between equal opportunity and demographic parity in the admissions example of **[[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/04 The Responsible Fleet/16 Responsible AI/V2-16.06 Fairness and Bias Mitigation#^sec-responsible-ai-fairness-criteria-conflict|Why the fairness criteria conflict]]**. The deployment therefore needs justified criteria, evidence that they hold, and an owner authorized to respond when they fail. Monitoring exposes supported disparities but cannot choose the criterion or provide a remedy by itself. These obligations belong in the design alongside performance requirements, which is why the practice this principle demands is to instrument first. Subgroup error rates, privacy accounting, and audit trails must be collected before the decision they are meant to justify, because a disparity that was never measured cannot be detected, attributed, or remedied after deployment.

↩︎ The calibration and score-balance conditions that the fairness impossibility theorem relates are established in **[[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/04 The Responsible Fleet/16 Responsible AI/V2-16.06 Fairness and Bias Mitigation#^sec-responsible-ai-fairness-criteria-conflict|Why the fairness criteria conflict]]**.

The sixth principle is that sustainability is a first-order cost. Environmental impact of large-scale ML elevates resource efficiency to a primary engineering constraint ([[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/04 The Responsible Fleet/17 Conclusion/V2-17.10 Source References#^ref-strubell2019|Strubell et al. 2019]]; [[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/04 The Responsible Fleet/17 Conclusion/V2-17.10 Source References#^ref-patterson2021carbon|Patterson et al. 2021]]). The energy-scale invariant of **[[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/00 Introduction/01 Introduction/V2-01.06 The C³ Taxonomy and Foundations of Scale#^sec-introduction-energyscale-invariant-a0d6|The energy accounting boundary]]** supplies the accounting discipline, a fixed boundary for useful work and for the energy it consumes. The Fleet Law explains why exposed communication and synchronization cost joules as well as seconds, since a step’s energy is approximately its average power times ${T_{\text{step}}}({N})$. The Jevons paradox of AI adapts Jevons’s resource-efficiency argument ([[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/04 The Responsible Fleet/17 Conclusion/V2-17.10 Source References#^ref-jevons1865coal|Jevons 1865]]) to explain why hardware efficiency gains alone do not eliminate power and carbon costs (principle [[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/04 The Responsible Fleet/V2 Responsible Fleet Principles#^pri-jevons-paradox|25]]). Cheaper computation can expand deployment, so usage growth can offset per-query savings. An absolute carbon budget bounds emissions only when accounting and admission decisions enforce it over the stated lifecycle and time horizon.

↩︎ Carbon accounting, energy metrics, and Jevons rebound effects are established in **[[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/04 The Responsible Fleet/15 Sustainable AI/V2-15 Sustainable AI#^sec-sustainable-ai|Sustainable AI]]**.

Sustainability thus becomes an engineering discipline with its own accounting chain, in which IT energy scaled by the facility’s power usage effectiveness and the grid’s carbon intensity gives operational emissions, and the embodied carbon of manufacturing the hardware joins the total. Two links show how far the chain can swing. Training GPT-3 consumed about 1,287 MWh, roughly 120 US household-years of electricity ([[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/04 The Responsible Fleet/17 Conclusion/V2-17.10 Source References#^ref-li2020estimating|Li 2020]]), and the same energy drawn from Poland’s coal-heavy grid emits about 41× the carbon it would on Quebec’s hydroelectric grid. The chain also explains the practice this principle demands. Small efficiency gains compound across every accelerator and every query in the fleet, but usage growth can absorb them, so compounding efficiency bounds total emissions only when an absolute budget caps the total. Carbon-aware scheduling, lifecycle assessment,[[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/04 The Responsible Fleet/17 Conclusion/V2-17.11 Footnotes#^fn3|³]] and efficiency optimization are the engineering means of meeting that budget.

↩︎ The operational and embodied carbon calculation is established in **[[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/04 The Responsible Fleet/15 Sustainable AI/V2-15.06 Carbon Footprint Calculation#^sec-sustainable-ai-carbon-footprint-calculation-c2fe|Carbon Footprint Calculation]]**.

Taken together, the six principles describe the qualitative change that scale produces across a fleet. Each larger regime brings behavior that emerges from familiar mechanisms, as the scale moment first showed. A training job that scales from 8 GPUs to 8,000 can meet communication bottlenecks, load imbalance, or synchronization overhead that were negligible at 8, and a serving fleet handling a million requests a day expects a one-in-a-million per-request failure about once a day. Stragglers that bottleneck clusters, network partitions that split training, and heterogeneity across hardware generations that complicates load balancing therefore become routine design inputs rather than anomalies. Because empirical scaling laws (principle [[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/02 Distributed ML/V2 Distributed ML Principles#^pri-universal-scaling|9]]) keep carrying teams that pursue lower loss into larger regimes, a production design must weigh the quality gain those laws predict against the full-stack costs the six principles govern.

**Self-Check: Question**

1.  A synchronous distributed training job achieves 94% linear scaling efficiency on 8 GPUs. When scaled to 8,000 GPUs on the same network fabric without changing hyperparameters, scaling efficiency plummets to 38%. Cluster telemetry reveals that per-device arithmetic execution speed remained constant, but workers spend over 55% of step time waiting at collective barriers. Which of the six principles best explains why small-scale profiling failed to forecast this collapse?

    1.  Communication can dominate, because collective synchronization, fabric contention, and straggler waiting grow with worker count while each device’s share of the compute shrinks, so the exposed communication and synchronization time that is negligible on 8 GPUs becomes most of the step on 8,000.
    2.  Failure is routine, because the cluster experiences silent hardware data corruption that forces the optimizer to recalculate gradient steps.
    3.  Infrastructure determines capability, because 8,000-GPU clusters automatically downclock GPU core frequencies by 60% to comply with data-center power capping.
    4.  Sustainability is a first-order cost, because thermal throttling occurs only when aggregate cluster power exceeds one megawatt.

2.  An inference optimization team profiles an autoregressive LLM decode engine on an H100 GPU and finds that realized throughput is only 12% of theoretical FP16 tensor core peak FLOP/s, even though `nvidia-smi` reports 98% GPU utilization. The execution profile shows that execution time is dominated by matrix-vector kernels (one weight matrix times one activation vector) with a batch size of 1, and measured HBM traffic approaches sustainable bandwidth with stable clocks. Applying the chapter’s memory wall and serving principles, what is the root cause of this behavior, and what is the proper engineering response?

    1.  The GPU arithmetic units are saturated; the team must upgrade to an accelerator with higher FP16 peak TFLOP/s to unblock the matrix kernels.
    2.  The low realized throughput indicates severe GPU thermal throttling; the team should increase cooling fan speeds and lower clock targets.
    3.  High `nvidia-smi` utilization proves compute saturation; the team should convert model weights to FP32 to ensure the arithmetic units perform more floating-point work per cycle.
    4.  Decode is memory-bandwidth bound because loading the full parameter matrix for a single token yields an arithmetic intensity well below the hardware roofline ridge; the team should evaluate batching to reuse weights, quantization to reduce weight bytes, or tensor parallelism to distribute work and memory traffic, subject to latency, quality, and communication costs.

3.  Meta documented that training Llama 3 on 16,384 GPUs over 54 days experienced 419 unexpected interruptions, corresponding to an average Mean Time Between Failures (MTBF) of approximately 3.1 hours. Which systems invariant does this empirical data establish for frontier training?

    1.  Frontier clusters are poorly engineered, and hardware reliability should be improved until a 16,384-GPU cluster operates for months without interruption before training begins.
    2.  In synchronous distributed training across thousands of devices, cluster-wide failure is routine and continuous; checkpointing cadence, multi-tier storage offload, and automated recovery must be designed into the architecture from inception.
    3.  Failures at this scale are primarily caused by software syntax bugs in user-level Python code, so rewriting training scripts in C++ eliminates the need for fault tolerance.
    4.  Checkpoint overhead can be ignored because a 3.1-hour failure cadence leaves sufficient time for manual cluster inspection and restart by human operators.
