# V2-17.04 The Complete Production System

[[Home|Home]] · [[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/Volume 2 Overview|Volume Overview]] · [[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/04 The Responsible Fleet/17 Conclusion/V2-17 Conclusion|V2-17 Conclusion]]

^sec-conclusion-complete-production-system-3885

A team that doubles its training cluster to chase the next point on a scaling curve halves the ideal arithmetic time per step, yet the added accelerators also increase collective traffic, shorten the interval between hardware interruptions, and draw against a fixed facility power budget. Empirical scaling laws set the demand for that fleet growth and the Fleet Law ([[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/04 The Responsible Fleet/17 Conclusion/V2-17.03 Six Principles of Distributed ML Systems#^eq-conclusion-fleet-law|equation 1]]) sets its price, so together they say when growth pays and where it breaks. The scaling curve estimates what growth buys; it does not oblige an organization to buy it. Scaling pays only while the predicted quality gain justifies what the fleet charges across every term that moves with ${N}$. Increasing ${N}$ reduces the ideal compute term ${T_{\text{compute}}}/{N}$ for fixed work and unchanged per-device rates, whereas ${T_{\text{comm}}}({N})$, ${T_{\text{sync}}}({N})$, and interruption exposure depend on the partition, schedule, and component count and carry no such guarantee. Within a fixed facility, the power and cooling envelope does not grow with the job, and a successful model then pays its serving cost for as long as it runs.

Growth can therefore break on either side. On the model side, the breakdown conditions of **[[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/00 Introduction/01 Introduction/V2-01.04 AI Scaling Laws#^tbl-scaling-breakdown|Possible Scaling Breakdowns]]** mark where extrapolating a fitted quality curve becomes unreliable, for example when model capacity outgrows the available tokens or added data stops supplying new information. On the systems side, the scaling wall of **[[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/02 Distributed ML/06 Distributed Training/V2-06.05 Scaling Efficiency and Convergence#^sec-distributed-training-scaling-math|The mathematics of scaling efficiency]]** marks where added communication and coordination consume the compute time that added accelerators save (principle [[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/02 Distributed ML/V2 Distributed ML Principles#^pri-scaling-efficiency|12]]), while deliverable site power caps the configuration regardless of how work is partitioned. A complete production design has to evaluate both sides together.

Consider Archetype A (GPT-4/Llama-3). Because GPT-4’s configuration is not published, its worked examples in this book use a GPT-3-class dense transformer, trained with mixed precision on a reference cluster of 1,024 GPUs and then shipped into a global serving fleet. During training, checkpoint bursts must land without starving the data loaders, while collective results must arrive before the computations that depend on them. Communication can overlap independent work, but its exposed remainder delays progress. At that scale, recovery planning uses observed write costs and failure rates, with the Young-Daly interval supplying an approximation where its assumptions hold ([[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/04 The Responsible Fleet/17 Conclusion/V2-17.10 Source References#^ref-young1974|Young 1974]]; [[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/04 The Responsible Fleet/17 Conclusion/V2-17.10 Source References#^ref-daly2006|Daly 2006]]). Serving SLOs require headroom for rollback and fallback, while governance controls enforce approved conditions on which data, models, and outputs may reach users. These are coupled rates, budgets, failure exposures, and policy constraints that must be designed before deployment.

The production system begins with physical capacity, but capacity matters only when the rates line up. Storage, fabric, and compute must therefore be co-designed so that data ingestion meets accelerator consumption, checkpoint writes fit the recovery budget, and the fabric carries the actual collective traffic. Matching these distinct rates to workload demand matters more than making their raw bandwidths equal.

Parallelization strategies must fit those physical rates and the expected failure pattern. Data parallelism, tensor parallelism, and pipeline parallelism are not interchangeable templates; each changes memory pressure, communication volume, checkpointing behavior, and scheduler complexity. Hybrid strategies become useful only when the surrounding storage, fabric, and recovery design can support the shape of the partition.

↩︎ How data, tensor, pipeline, and hybrid partitioning each move memory pressure, communication volume, and the pipeline bubble is established in **[[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/02 Distributed ML/06 Distributed Training/V2-06.09 Parallelism Strategy Comparison#^sec-distributed-training-systems-systems-parallelism-strategy-comparison-d92a|Parallelism Strategy Comparison]]**.

Serving adds a separate acceptance boundary. The contract of the fourth principle judges the model by its latency, availability, and cost on live traffic, whether it runs in a data center replica or on a phone-class device. Some security and robustness controls execute on the request path; others operate during training, release review, or asynchronous monitoring. Each needs an evidence and response path suited to its obligation. Training throughput therefore does not establish that a model will meet live latency, recovery, security, subgroup, and carbon requirements. A fleet expansion raises two linked decisions, whether to accept the larger training job and whether to release the model it produces, and each needs its own evidence.

To evaluate the first of those decisions on the Archetype A baseline, suppose a proposed expansion doubles the reference cluster to 2,048 GPUs while retaining the fabric and checkpoint tier. Because doubling ${N}$ halves the ideal ${T_{\text{compute}}}/{N}$ term, the first measurement is whether that saving survives the resulting changes in ${T_{\text{comm}}}({N})$ and ${T_{\text{sync}}}({N})$ and their overlap. The recovery side can be estimated before any measurement. Assume independent GPU failures at an identical constant rate, with each failure interrupting the job. Counting those failures alone at an assumed mean time to failure (MTTF) of 50,000 hours per GPU, the job’s mean time between interruptions is that MTTF divided by ${N}$, so it falls from 48.8 hours to 24.4 hours.

At the Young-Daly interval of **[[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/02 Distributed ML/07 Fault Tolerance/V2-07.09 Checkpointing and Progress Preservation#^eq-ft-young-daly-interval|equation 7.7]]**, the checkpoint-and-rework overhead is about $\sqrt{2{T_{\text{write}}}/{\text{MTBF}_{\text{system}}}}$ of wall-clock time. Because the job’s mean time between interruptions falls in inverse proportion to ${N}$, that overhead grows as $\sqrt{{N}}$ (**[[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/02 Distributed ML/07 Fault Tolerance/V2-07.09 Checkpointing and Progress Preservation#^sec-fault-tolerance-young-daly|The Young-Daly checkpoint interval]]**), so doubling the cluster multiplies it by about 1.41×. The model’s 2.45 TB resumable checkpoint, which stores its weights and optimizer state, takes about 24.5 seconds to write at the assumed checkpoint write bandwidth, which puts the overhead at about 1.7 percent of wall-clock time on the reference cluster and about 2.4 percent on the expanded one.

These figures are optimistic. They count GPU failures alone, while switches, network interface cards, and software add interruptions of their own. The full-system analysis in **[[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/02 Distributed ML/07 Fault Tolerance/V2-07.09 Checkpointing and Progress Preservation#^sec-fault-tolerance-reliability-reliability-checkpoint-interval-failure-analysis-c28d|Checkpoint interval from failure analysis]]**, which adds PCIe-switch and network-interface failures for a cluster of 10,000 GPUs, arrives at a correspondingly larger overhead from the same formula. Write contention with data loading or a changed checkpoint size would also require revising them. **[[05 Reference/Volume II - Scaling/V2 System Assumptions#^sec-appdx-fleet-system-assumptions|System Assumptions]]** lists the parameter count, checkpoint bytes per parameter, GPU MTTF, and checkpoint write bandwidth behind these figures, so a reader can recompute them or substitute other values.

These estimates still yield a decision rule. In this example, measured step time covers uninterrupted execution and excludes the checkpoint-and-rework overhead, so that overhead, which runtime goodput counts as lost time (**[[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/03 The Fleet in Production/09 Fleet Efficiency/V2-09.06 Runtime Goodput#^sec-performance-engineering-runtime-goodput|Runtime Goodput]]**), enters the run-time estimate as a separate factor. A step time measured over the whole run already includes recovery pauses and must not take the factor again. Because only the fraction $1 - \text{overhead}$ of each wall-clock hour produces retained training progress, total run time for fixed work is proportional to ${T_{\text{step}}}({N})/(1 - \text{overhead})$. Doubling the cluster raises the checkpoint-and-rework overhead from 1.7 percent to 2.4 percent, which leaves a slightly smaller productive fraction of each hour on the expanded cluster. To finish in less wall-clock time, the expanded cluster’s uninterrupted step-time speedup must therefore exceed the ratio of the reference productive fraction to the expanded productive fraction, $(1 - \text{overhead}_{\text{ref}})/(1 - \text{overhead}_{\text{exp}})$. Here that break-even step speedup is about 1.01×, so recovery overhead alone does not block the expansion.

Short of a superlinear step speedup, however, the expansion cannot save accelerator-hours. Total accelerator-hours equal the GPU count times wall-clock run time. Even if uninterrupted step time halves at the ideal 2$\times$ speedup, the higher checkpoint-and-rework overhead keeps the expanded cluster running for slightly more than half the original wall-clock duration, so doubling the GPUs multiplies total accelerator-hours by the same ratio of productive fractions that set the break-even speedup, an increase of about 0.7 percent. Every point of scaling efficiency lost to exposed communication raises that bill further. The expansion therefore buys calendar time with accelerator-hours and energy, and the communication measurement, not the recovery estimate, sets that price.

That price buys time, not quality, because the work is fixed. Quality enters the comparison only if the doubled cluster spends twice the compute instead of finishing sooner, and there the fitted scaling curve prices the gain. In the compute power law that Kaplan et al. ([[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/04 The Responsible Fleet/17 Conclusion/V2-17.10 Source References#^ref-kaplan2020scaling|2020]]) fitted (charted in the scaling laws of **[[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/00 Introduction/01 Introduction/V2-01.04 AI Scaling Laws#^sec-vol2-intro-ai-scaling-laws-a043|AI Scaling Laws]]**, **[[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/00 Introduction/01 Introduction/V2-01.04 AI Scaling Laws#^fig-kaplan-scaling|Scaling Laws and Compute Optimality]]**), loss falls as compute raised to the power $-0.050$, so doubling compute multiplies loss by $2^{-0.050} \approx 0.966$, a reduction of about 3.4 percent. A frontier run lies beyond the range that fit studied, so the figure shows the shape of the trade rather than this run’s gain. Each doubling buys the same fractional reduction, and therefore a smaller absolute one than the doubling before it, while its price, twice the accelerator-hours plus the recovery and communication overhead above, is paid in full every time.

The release decision then applies its own test to the model the expanded run produces. An end-to-end evaluation contract (principle [[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/03 The Fleet in Production/V2 Fleet-In-Production Principles#^pri-evaluation-contract|21]]) fixes that test before exposure. The contract is one versioned release record that binds the candidate to production-equivalent measurements, accountable acceptance authority, tested recovery, and explicit conditions that invalidate the evidence. Its serving gate requires the model to meet its p99 latency target under production-equivalent load with headroom left for rollback and fallback. For Archetype A, the governance gates require a provenance record that pins the data, code, and weights, evidence bounding what released outputs can disclose, and training-energy accounting over a stated boundary. A failing gate sends the revision back to the fleet-stack row that owns the failure, which may mean changing the partition, placement, capacity allocation, or release plan, and the release proceeds only after the revised candidate passes.

↩︎ The end-to-end evaluation contract and its versioned release record are established in **[[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/03 The Fleet in Production/12 ML Operations at Scale/V2-12.06 CI CD for ML at Scale#^sec-ops-scale-end-to-end-evaluation-contract|The end-to-end evaluation contract]]**.

The constraints behind those revisions can conflict. A communication optimization may increase failure exposure, sustainability requirements may restrict infrastructure choices, and governance controls may consume serving latency or capacity. The displacement of overhead requires measuring those transferred costs before accepting a local speedup.

Archetype A traces only one path through these cross-layer trade-offs; taken together, the three lighthouse archetypes show that no single corner of the C$^3$ taxonomy dominates. Archetype B (DLRM at Scale) is a recommendation model whose embedding tables are sharded across many nodes, and Archetype C (Federated MobileNet) is a small vision model trained across millions of phones that keep their data local. Each workload was first characterized by a representative binding term, and following each one up the fleet stack shows what each band, and the part of the book that develops it, added to that first characterization ([[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/04 The Responsible Fleet/17 Conclusion/V2-17.04 The Complete Production System#^tbl-conclusion-archetype-binding-terms|table 2]]).

Each column traces the constraints one workload encounters across the bands, while each row compares how a single band constrains different workloads. These constraints need not all bind at once. The representative terms describe operating regimes, not permanent assignments of archetypes to C$^3$ terms. Federated MobileNet can be device-compute-bound during local training yet communication- or coordination-bound during a global round with intermittent clients. The same six principles apply to all three; the engineer must measure the workload at the relevant boundary before choosing which term to reduce. Because the binding term must be found rather than assumed, the book’s method reduces to a diagnostic procedure, a sequence of steps that is distinct from the principles it applies:

1.  Start with the observed symptom, the user-visible or operator-visible failure.
2.  Attach the metric that makes the symptom falsifiable.
3.  Map the symptom to C$^3$ by identifying whether compute, communication, or coordination binds first.
4.  Locate the fleet-stack row that owns the intervention.
5.  State the cost that the proposed fix displaces elsewhere.
6.  Preserve the audit, safety, security, or responsibility evidence needed to justify the decision.

On the expanded cluster, a training run that falls behind its schedule exercises every step of that procedure. The schedule lag is the symptom, and the falsifiable metric is model FLOPs utilization measured per step, the measure of program goodput in **[[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/03 The Fleet in Production/09 Fleet Efficiency/V2-09.03 ML Goodput#^sec-performance-engineering-fleet-efficiency-metric-dff4|Program goodput and its measure]]**, which has dropped since the expansion. A step profile shows accelerators idle at gradient-synchronization barriers, but that wait alone does not distinguish slow collective transfer from ranks arriving late after computation, data loading, or scheduling delays. Aligned per-rank traces must therefore separate arrival skew from exposed collective completion time. When those traces show little arrival skew and increased exposed transfer time, they point to communication as the binding term and to the distributed-execution row, where the collective and its overlap are scheduled, as the owner of the intervention. A proposed fix such as gradient compression shrinks the exchanged bytes while displacing cost into convergence and accuracy risk, which means the evidence to preserve is the convergence and evaluation record showing that the compressed run still reaches its quality target.

The same procedure redirects other symptoms. A fairness alert may point to missing labels, insufficient review capacity, or a serving-path threshold policy rather than model weights alone. A security incident may point to artifact provenance, access permissions, or rollback evidence rather than network perimeter failure. The discipline is to follow the constraint until the row that owns it and the trade-off it carries are visible. When the fix changes what ships, the evidence from the final step enters the release record of the same end-to-end evaluation contract.

**Self-Check: Question**

1.  A data-center engineering team upgrades its training storage tier to provide 200 GB/s of sustained read bandwidth per node, while the inter-node network fabric remains at 25 GB/s per accelerator. Despite the 4x storage upgrade, overall training throughput on a multi-node Vision Transformer benchmark does not improve. Matched step traces show that data loading finishes before it is needed, ranks enter the collective with little arrival skew, and exposed gradient transfer saturates the relevant fabric links. Applying the chapter’s rate-matching and co-design principles, why did this upgrade fail to yield end-to-end performance gains?

    1.  The matched traces identify exposed gradient transfer as the critical-path constraint; increasing already sufficient storage bandwidth does not shorten that transfer.
    2.  Storage read bandwidth is only utilized during model checkpointing, so data loader throughput has zero impact on training iteration step time.
    3.  Upgrading storage beyond 100 GB/s triggers automatic PCI Express bus arbitration throttles that disable GPU tensor cores.
    4.  Vision Transformers cannot ingest training samples at rates exceeding 10 GB/s due to algorithmic limits in the self-attention mechanism.
