# V2-14.08 Data Poisoning Defenses

[[Home|Home]] · [[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/Volume 2 Overview|Volume Overview]] · [[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/04 The Responsible Fleet/14 Robust AI/V2-14 Robust AI|V2-14 Robust AI]]

^sec-robust-ai-data-poisoning-defenses-d070

A recommendation model may retain its overall accuracy while promoting an attacker’s chosen item to a small group of users. Defending against that failure requires testing the targeted behavior as well as securing the integrity of the training supply chain. Source permissions set the security boundary, and robustness evaluation measures what happens when poisoned records or updates cross it anyway.

Poisoning changes the data from which a model learns rather than perturbing a fixed model at inference time, and its threat runs from early attacks on support-vector machines ([[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/04 The Responsible Fleet/14 Robust AI/V2-14.14 Source References#^ref-biggio2012poisoning|Biggio et al. 2012]]) to web-scale and generative-model data pipelines ([[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/04 The Responsible Fleet/14 Robust AI/V2-14.14 Source References#^ref-shan2024|Shan et al. 2023]]). Microsoft’s Tay chatbot, which its operator reported was subverted by a coordinated attack within a day of launch ([[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/04 The Responsible Fleet/14 Robust AI/V2-14.14 Source References#^ref-microsoft2016tay|Lee 2016]]), shows why any system that learns from live interaction makes every user a potential contributor of training evidence. The engineering question therefore becomes which contributions may enter training and how their influence can be audited.

↩︎ Data poisoning, the corruption of training data or updates so that the learned model misbehaves later, and its sequence of admission, training, and exploitation are established in **[[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/04 The Responsible Fleet/13 Security & Privacy/V2-13.06 Model-Specific Attack Vectors#^sec-security-privacy-data-poisoning-351f|Data poisoning]]**.

A dirty-label attack corrupts the label, so a label audit can catch it. Its counterpart, clean-label poisoning, keeps every label correct and corrupts the features instead, which is why several of the defenses below must look past labels to features and representations. Either form can plant a backdoor through the poisoned records, leaving the trained model to behave normally until an input contains the trigger. Generative-model training corpora are exposed to the same threat in a concept-poisoning form, in which a few hundred perturbed images can redirect what a text-to-image model draws for a prompt. **[[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/04 The Responsible Fleet/13 Security & Privacy/V2-13.06 Model-Specific Attack Vectors#^sec-security-privacy-data-poisoning-351f|Data poisoning]]** examines these mechanisms, that case, and the paths by which poisoned records enter the pipeline.

Poisoned records can resemble legitimate data, so clean aggregate validation accuracy alone cannot establish that a training corpus is trustworthy, and a targeted attack is designed to hide inside otherwise normal performance. Attacks may originate from internal actors with privileged pipeline access or from external adversaries who exploit crowdsourced collection or open data pipelines that lack integrity checks and lineage tracking. The defense therefore runs as four ordered layers along the data path:

1.  **Ingress controls** establish provenance and access limits, dictating which sources may contribute or modify data.
2.  **Anomaly detection and sanitization** inspect candidate records before training and filter suspicious submissions.
3.  **Robust training objectives** reduce the weight that extreme residuals receive for poisoned samples that evade ingestion filtering, although they do not by themselves bound parameter updates.
4.  **Post-training checks** test the trained model’s behavior on the poisoning objectives that aggregate accuracy hides before the model is released.

Each layer covers a distinct point along the data path and has its own blind spot, so no single detector can substitute for end-to-end controls. Representation learning sits beneath all four as a foundation rather than a fifth gate: it reduces the model’s reliance on brittle features that poisoned instances can exploit, but it cannot prove that the training corpus is uncompromised.

For the recommendation system, the interception layer in [[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/04 The Responsible Fleet/14 Robust AI/V2-14.08 Data Poisoning Defenses#^fig-adversarial-attack-injection|figure 11]] examines contributed ratings before they reach training. Its decisions must preserve provenance so later model failures can be traced back to the accepted records.

Figure 11: **Data Poisoning and Interception Workflow**: Adversaries inject malicious rows into a user-item rating matrix to manipulate model behavior. A defense layer analyzes contributed data and removes suspected malicious records before training. This can reduce poisoning risk but does not guarantee an uncorrupted model.

### Ingress and Anomaly Controls

^sec-robust-ai-anomaly-detection-techniques-bc27

If an unverified data source or a compromised contributor account can write directly into a training table, statistical outlier filters will only ever see the attacks that fail to blend in. Poisoning defense therefore begins at the data boundary, before any statistical detector runs. The pipeline must know which sources are allowed to contribute training data, how their authenticity is verified, and which roles can modify accepted records. Strong governance enforces the principle of least privilege,[[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/04 The Responsible Fleet/14 Robust AI/V2-14.15 Footnotes#^fn15|¹⁵]] logs data access and modification events, and gives each accepted batch a source identity. These controls do not prove that every record is clean, but they bound the attack surface and give anomaly findings a traceable origin.

**Lighthouse 14.1: Archetype B (DLRM at Scale): Fake profile injection**

Archetype B (DLRM at Scale) is especially exposed to **fake profile injection**. Recommendation systems often use online learning to adapt to user trends in real time. An adversary can therefore create a network of “sybil” accounts, fake identities controlled by the same attacker, that interact with specific items to artificially boost their popularity or associate them with high-value demographics. This poisoning bypasses traditional firewalls because the malicious interactions look like legitimate user behavior, motivating checks on contributor identity, interaction patterns, and learned representations. Spectral-signature defenses (developed in [[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/04 The Responsible Fleet/14 Robust AI/V2-14.08 Data Poisoning Defenses#^sec-robust-ai-sanitization-preprocessing-f883|section 14.7.1.1]]) search activation patterns for suspicious separation, but their effectiveness depends on whether the poisoned and clean contributions separate in that representation.

After the source boundary, anomaly detection asks whether a candidate training example belongs to the distribution that the pipeline intended to learn from. The cheapest tests compare each record against the bulk distribution: Z-score filtering, Tukey’s method, and Mahalanobis distance flag examples whose feature values sit far from normal ranges. These tests are useful because they are fast enough to run at ingestion, but they catch only poisons that look like statistical outliers in raw feature space. For high-dimensional or multimodal data, raw-feature distances are a weak signal: a manipulated image or caption that passes raw-feature checks may separate from clean examples in a pretrained vision or language model’s embedding space. That separation depends on the encoder and attack, so it must be measured rather than inferred from the use of a learned representation. Applying Mahalanobis distance or clustering to those representations tests a different hypothesis from raw-feature screening. The pipeline must check whether the resulting outliers correspond to malicious contributions or to legitimate variation it needs to retain.

More coordinated attacks require structure-aware checks. Clustering methods such as K-means, DBSCAN, and hierarchical clustering look for anomalous groups or isolated points rather than single extreme values. The autoencoder ingestion gate in [[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/04 The Responsible Fleet/14 Robust AI/V2-14.08 Data Poisoning Defenses#^fig-autoencoder|figure 12]] adds a learned representation to the screening pipeline. The model compresses inputs through a bottleneck layer ${\mathbf{z}}$, accepting records whose reconstruction error ${\mathcal{L}_{\text{rec}}}$ falls below threshold ${\tau}$ and quarantining anomalous outliers. Each method has a blind spot. Clean-label poisons need not be statistical outliers, clustering depends on the representation, and autoencoders can reconstruct unfamiliar inputs well or learn an attack pattern from a contaminated reference corpus. Each gate therefore requires evaluation on both poisons and legitimate rare examples.

![[90 Assets/Volume II/14 Robust AI/Autoencoder 4d9b9b1d94.svg]]

Figure 12: **Autoencoder Ingestion Gate**: An autoencoder trained on trusted data learns to compress inputs through a narrow latent layer ${\mathbf{z}}$ and reconstruct them. Reconstruction error supplies an anomaly score, not proof that an input is poisoned. The gate accepts records whose reconstruction error ${\mathcal{L}_{\text{rec}}}$ stays at or below a threshold ${\tau}$ calibrated on held-out clean data and quarantines the rest.

#### Sanitization and Preprocessing

^sec-robust-ai-sanitization-preprocessing-f883

Sanitization turns anomaly signals into training-set changes before the poison reaches optimization. A suspicious record can be rejected, quarantined for review, down-weighted during training, or kept with an explicit provenance flag. Routine cleaning still matters: deduplication, missing-value handling, type checks, range constraints, and cross-field validation remove many low-effort poisoning attempts while improving ordinary data quality.

Data provenance and lineage tracking make those decisions reversible. Each datum needs a record of its source, transformations, validation outcomes, and movement through the pipeline. When a model later shows a poisoning symptom, lineage lets the operator trace model dependencies back to candidate source batches and investigate which contributions caused the behavior. Lineage also lets the operator estimate which trained models consumed confirmed compromised examples and decide whether to remove, relabel, or reweight the affected records.

When suspicious data has already entered the corpus, sanitization moves from source checks to representation checks. Spectral signatures exploit separation between clean and poisoned samples in a learned representation ([[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/04 The Responsible Fleet/14 Robust AI/V2-14.14 Source References#^ref-tran2018spectral|Tran et al. 2018]]). In a backdoor attack, the poisoned records carry the target label, so the network must learn two distinct internal pathways to assign it: genuine semantic features for clean examples and the injected trigger feature for poisoned examples originating from other classes. That dual mechanism shifts the mean activation vector of the poisoned subpopulation away from the mean of the clean target-class examples, which creates a variance spike along the direction connecting the two subpopulation means. In the backdoor attacks studied by Tran and colleagues, projecting centered target-class representations onto their top singular vector captured that offset direction, and removing high-scoring samples exposed many poisoned records without requiring prior knowledge of the trigger. The method depends on sufficient mean separation relative to normal within-class variation, so an effective backdoor does not by itself guarantee a usable spectral signature. Filtering therefore requires attack-specific evaluation and checks for discarded clean samples.

Influence functions ([[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/04 The Responsible Fleet/14 Robust AI/V2-14.14 Source References#^ref-koh2017understanding|Koh and Liang 2017]]) serve a narrower debugging role. They approximate how infinitesimally increasing a training point ${z}$’s weight changes the model’s loss for a test point ${z_{\text{test}}}$, without retraining. The fitted parameters are the trained model’s weights, and the Hessian is the curvature matrix of the average training loss at those parameters. Its inverse translates a small change in a training point’s weight into an approximate change in the fitted parameters. Calculated through inverse-Hessian-vector products, influence ${I}({z}, {z_{\text{test}}}) \approx -\nabla_{\theta}{\mathcal{L}}({z_{\text{test}}}, {\theta^*})^T {\mathbf{H}}^{-1} \nabla_{\theta}{\mathcal{L}}({z}, {\theta^*})$, this metric measures local sensitivity to upweighting ${z}$. For an average training loss over ${D}$ examples, removing one point corresponds approximately to a weight change of $-1/{D}$, reversing the sign and scaling this influence by $1/{D}$. If a model begins assigning an attacker’s target label to one narrow class of inputs, influence functions can rank the training examples that contributed most to that decision. The method avoids forming an explicit inverse Hessian by using iterative approximations, but its results remain approximate in deep nonconvex networks. At modern model scale, influence analysis therefore requires validation against the specific architecture, approximation, and debugging task rather than serving as exact attribution.

### Robust Training Objectives

^sec-robust-ai-robust-training-objectives

![[90 Assets/Volume II/14 Robust AI/Vol2 Robust AI Margin 004 45417fcba7.svg]]

*Red curve labeled MSE and blue curve labeled Huber. They coincide near zero, where two blue dots mark the threshold on either side; beyond the dots the blue curve continues in straight lines while the red parabola climbs steeply.*

*Huber limits a surviving outlier by growing linearly where MSE keeps squaring.*

Under a standard squared-error objective, a single poisoned sample that survives ingestion filtering with a large residual error contributes quadratically to the loss and pulls the gradient far harder than a typical point. To limit the influence of surviving outliers before release evaluation, robust optimization modifies the training objective itself. Robust loss functions such as the Huber loss,[[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/04 The Responsible Fleet/14 Robust AI/V2-14.15 Footnotes#^fn16|¹⁶]] the Tukey loss ([[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/04 The Responsible Fleet/14 Robust AI/V2-14.14 Source References#^ref-beaton1974|Beaton and Tukey 1974]]), and the trimmed mean loss down-weight or ignore the contribution of abnormal instances during training. Regularization techniques ($\ell_1$ or $\ell_2$ regularization) constrain model complexity and can reduce sensitivity to some poisoned data. At a higher level, minimax[[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/04 The Responsible Fleet/14 Robust AI/V2-14.15 Footnotes#^fn17|¹⁷]] and distributionally robust objectives optimize performance over a specified uncertainty set. The objective supplies a formal target; an approximate optimizer does not by itself prove that the trained model attains the target or resists attacks outside that set.

Data augmentation expands training diversity by applying horizontal flips, padded crops, rotations, and color jitter ([[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/04 The Responsible Fleet/14 Robust AI/V2-14.08 Data Poisoning Defenses#^fig-data-augmentation|figure 13]]). The variants preserve the label when the transform respects the class (a horizontal flip need not preserve a digit’s label), and they can disrupt some localized poison patterns. Other transformations preserve an attack’s trigger, so augmentation does not guarantee dilution. Random subsampling or bootstrap aggregating changes which poisoned records each model encounters; whether combining those models reduces the attack’s effect requires evaluation against coordinated poisoning.

![[90 Assets/Volume II/14 Robust AI/Cell 18 Output 1 23a9ae69cb.png]]

Figure 13: **Data Augmentation Techniques**: Horizontal flips, padded crops, rotations, and color jitter create variants of an existing training example, shown here on an illustrative synthetic digit. When a transformation preserves the task label, it can improve robustness to the represented variation and reduce overfitting; the flipped 3 shows a transformation that does not. The effect on generalization and poisoning resistance requires evaluation.

^fig-data-augmentation

Distributed and federated training adds a contributor axis to the same threat, and robust aggregation is the training-objective layer’s counterpart at the level of contributors. A malicious client need not insert a visible record into a central corpus; it can submit a model update chosen to move the aggregate, implant a backdoor, or exploit sybil identities to gain disproportionate weight. Archetype C (Federated MobileNet) makes this concrete: the server aggregates updates from a large population of smartphone-class clients whose raw data it never sees, so contributor-level checks screen updates before aggregation. Krum (which keeps the one update whose nearest neighbors lie closest to it), coordinate-wise median, and trimmed mean (**[[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/02 Distributed ML/07 Fault Tolerance/V2-07.04 Failure Taxonomy#^sec-fault-tolerance-reliability-reliability-byzantine-failures-8a24|Byzantine failures]]**) tolerate bounded Byzantine behavior only under their stated assumptions ([[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/04 The Responsible Fleet/14 Robust AI/V2-14.14 Source References#^ref-blanchard2017machine|Blanchard et al. 2017]]; [[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/04 The Responsible Fleet/14 Robust AI/V2-14.14 Source References#^ref-yin2018byzantine|Yin et al. 2018]]); high dimensionality and non-i.i.d. client updates can make honest contributors look anomalous. These rules also change the collective, because a median, trimmed mean, or Krum selection needs the individual updates rather than their sum, which raises per-worker traffic over a ring AllReduce.

↩︎ The traffic cost of gather-based robust aggregation relative to ring AllReduce is established in **[[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/04 The Responsible Fleet/13 Security & Privacy/V2-13.07 Fleet-Internal Trust Boundaries#^sec-security-privacy-gradient-sync-integrity|Gradient synchronization integrity]]**.

### Post-Training Behavioral Checks

^sec-robust-ai-post-training-checks

Admitting a poisoned record does not by itself guarantee that training will learn the attacker’s behavior, so the last layer tests the trained model rather than its inputs. The evidence that exposes poisoning differs across the four objective classes that **[[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/04 The Responsible Fleet/13 Security & Privacy/V2-13.06 Model-Specific Attack Vectors#^sec-security-privacy-data-poisoning-351f|Data poisoning]]** distinguishes: availability, targeted, backdoor, and subpopulation poisoning. The robustness question is which evidence exposes each class and what that evidence costs ([[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/04 The Responsible Fleet/14 Robust AI/V2-14.14 Source References#^ref-oprea2022|Oprea et al. 2022]]). Successful availability attacks can lower aggregate validation accuracy, giving broad evaluation a signal to investigate. Targeted attacks, which aim at specific inputs, and subpopulation attacks, which aim at a whole cohort, can leave that aggregate nearly intact, making evaluation on the named targets and cohorts particularly important. Backdoors can also preserve clean-data accuracy while responding to a trigger the evaluator has never seen, as the BadNets pixel-pattern trigger does ([[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/04 The Responsible Fleet/14 Robust AI/V2-14.14 Source References#^ref-gu2017badnets|Gu et al. 2017]]). Possible detection strategies include trigger search, canary inputs (probe inputs with known expected outputs, kept out of training), and activation-space inspection when poisoned and clean representations separate ([[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/04 The Responsible Fleet/14 Robust AI/V2-14.14 Source References#^ref-tran2018spectral|Tran et al. 2018]]).

For federated and distributed contributors the release gate takes the same form. Held-out behavioral tests on the resulting global model must vary the number and coordination of malicious contributors, their data heterogeneity, participation schedule, and knowledge of the aggregation rule. Global clean accuracy is again insufficient: the gate needs attack success on the trigger or target subpopulation, contributor-level anomaly recall, and the clean-utility cost of robust aggregation. Provenance, participation limits, robust aggregation, and held-out canaries must therefore work together rather than treating one distance rule as a poisoning proof.

The operating rule is layered: authenticate and govern sources before ingestion, sanitize suspicious data before training, limit surviving outliers during optimization, test the trained model on the poisoning objectives before release, and preserve lineage so an incident can be traced back to the compromised source. Data poisoning remains an active research area, but production systems should treat the training corpus as a supply chain rather than a passive dataset.

The four layers act on records, objectives, and the trained model. A representation that does not lean on brittle shortcuts gives a poisoned shortcut less to exploit, and the same property helps the model survive ordinary deployment variation, so representation learning serves the poisoning defense and the environmental pillar at once. Beyond detecting and correcting shifts after they occur ([[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/04 The Responsible Fleet/14 Robust AI/V2-14.05 Environmental Shifts#^sec-robust-ai-environmental-shifts-a2cf|section 14.4]]), a complementary approach builds shift-resilient representations from the start, drawing on transfer-learning and domain-adaptation foundations ([[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/04 The Responsible Fleet/14 Robust AI/V2-14.14 Source References#^ref-pan2010|Pan and Yang 2010]]).

### Self-Supervised Representations for Robustness

^sec-robust-ai-selfsupervised-learning-robustness-0c94

Supervised models often overfit to whichever labeled shortcut is easiest to exploit, such as a background texture, an annotation artifact, or a narrow phrasing pattern, and fail when deployment shifts or a poisoning attack targets that cue. Self-supervised learning (SSL) changes the source of supervision so the model learns by solving pretext tasks that require structure in the data itself. A pretraining task that rewards stable structure across views, missing tokens, or masked image patches gives the representation a chance to learn signals that survive more deployment variation.

Contrastive learning methods such as SimCLR ([[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/04 The Responsible Fleet/14 Robust AI/V2-14.14 Source References#^ref-chen2020simclr|Chen et al. 2020]]) make this idea concrete by pushing different views of the same example toward a shared representation. The model is rewarded for treating a crop, color shift, or augmentation as the same underlying object rather than as a new class-specific cue. **Masked language modeling** in BERT ([[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/04 The Responsible Fleet/14 Robust AI/V2-14.14 Source References#^ref-devlin2019|Devlin et al. 2019]]) and masked autoencoding in vision ([[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/04 The Responsible Fleet/14 Robust AI/V2-14.14 Source References#^ref-he2022|He et al. 2021]]) use a different route: they hide part of the input and force the model to reconstruct or predict it from context. Both families provide supervision beyond the downstream task labels. Whether the resulting representations transfer better depends on the pretraining data, objective, and deployment shift; removing task labels does not remove shortcut learning.

That transfer benefit is the main systems reason to use SSL in a robustness pipeline. Larger unlabeled corpora expose the model to domains, transformations, and rare cases that would be too expensive to label exhaustively. The same uncurated scale widens the poisoning surface, so SSL pretraining data still needs the ingress and sanitization controls described earlier. In production, SSL usually acts as a foundation rather than a complete defense: pretrain on broad unlabeled data, fine-tune on the supervised task, then apply the adversarial, drift, and poisoning defenses established earlier in this chapter to the task-specific model. Multi-task training can preserve some of this benefit by keeping a self-supervised objective active while the supervised task pulls the representation toward the deployment metric.

The limitation is that SSL is not a robustness guarantee. A contrastive or masked-pretraining objective can still learn brittle shortcuts, and an attacker who understands the pretext task can target those shortcuts directly. The theory explaining when SSL improves robustness remains incomplete, and the compute cost can be substantial because pretraining moves work earlier in the lifecycle. Whether broader representation learning lowers the expected cost of drift, relabeling, and robust fine-tuning enough to justify the added pretraining budget is a per-service calculation. When the model generates open-ended text rather than discrete class labels, the robustness contract itself must expand beyond label stability.
