# V2-14.15 Footnotes

[[Home|Home]] · [[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/Volume 2 Overview|Volume Overview]] · [[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/04 The Responsible Fleet/14 Robust AI/V2-14 Robust AI|V2-14 Robust AI]]

^footnotes

<sup>1</sup> **Systemic vulnerability**: A shared dependency can expose many components to the same defect or attack. Pinning one CUDA or PyTorch version across a fleet makes that version a common dependency, but exploitation still depends on the affected code paths, configuration, and attacker access. Dependency inventories and staged updates help identify and contain the exposed population; replication alone does not remove a shared weakness.

^fn1

<sup>2</sup> **AWS S3 outage (2017)**: A mistyped command during routine maintenance removed too much capacity from S3’s index and placement subsystems in US-East-1. While those subsystems restarted, S3 could not service requests and dependent AWS services experienced elevated errors or impaired functionality. The incident exposed a single-region dependency pattern: systems that assume regional storage availability as an invariant can fail even when their own application code and model-serving logic remain unchanged.

^fn2

<sup>3</sup> **Failsafe mechanism**: A mechanism that moves the system to a validated safe state when a fault is detected. Circuit breakers, model fallback, rejection, and rollback are possible components, but none is inherently safe for every application. The service must establish that its chosen response meets the hazard and timing constraints; rejection thresholds also trade accepted workload against residual error and review capacity.

^fn3

<sup>4</sup> **ASIL (automotive safety integrity levels)**: ISO 26262 uses automotive safety integrity levels to scale functional-safety obligations according to the hazard analysis. ASIL D is the most stringent level, but it is not a universal component-reliability percentage. An ML perception component complicates the resulting safety case because its behavior depends on the operational distribution and cannot be justified by aggregate test accuracy alone.

^fn4

<sup>5</sup> **Reliability engineering**: Uses fault models and failure-mode analysis to connect component behavior to system outcomes. A series model gives system reliability as ${R_{\text{system}}}({t})=e^{-{N_{\text{series}}}{\lambda} {t}}$ when all ${N_{\text{series}}}$ components must survive, failures are independent, and each has the same constant exponential failure rate. ML deployments also need behavioral evidence for drift, crafted inputs, and uncertainty; the component-survival model alone does not cover these failure modes.

^fn5

<sup>6</sup> **Maximum mean discrepancy (MMD)**: A discrepancy between kernel mean representations of distributions, used to construct two-sample tests. A kernel on input vectors lets MMD respond to joint-distribution changes that per-feature comparisons can miss; sensitivity depends on the kernel and test calibration. Direct pairwise estimation costs $\mathcal{O}({n}^2)$ in sample size, while linear-time estimators and approximations trade computation against statistical efficiency.

^fn6

<sup>7</sup> **Human vs. machine perception**: First highlighted by Szegedy et al. ([[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/04 The Responsible Fleet/14 Robust AI/V2-14.14 Source References#^ref-szegedy2013intriguing|2013]]), neural networks learn statistical correlations in pixel space rather than the semantic invariances human vision enforces. Agreement on clean examples does not establish agreement under every small image change. Evaluation must therefore specify which transformations should preserve the label and test whether the model preserves that behavior; gradient-based training alone neither explains every attack nor guarantees the required invariance.

^fn7

<sup>8</sup> **Fast gradient sign method (FGSM)**: Proposed by Goodfellow et al. ([[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/04 The Responsible Fleet/14 Robust AI/V2-14.14 Source References#^ref-goodfellow2015explaining|2015]]) at ICLR, FGSM generates adversarial examples in a single gradient step, making it practical to use during training. This dual role is its lasting systems significance: the same attack that exposed neural network fragility became a simple adversarial-training primitive, where FGSM-generated perturbations augment clean batches with examples chosen to increase loss.

^fn8

<sup>9</sup> **White-box attack**: Adversarial attack with complete model knowledge (architecture, weights, gradients); methods like PGD and Carlini-Wagner (C&W) are strong because they optimize directly against the model rather than probing it from the outside. Though less realistic than black-box scenarios for many deployed systems, white-box analysis establishes a demanding benchmark within a specified norm, perturbation budget, and attack procedure. Passing PGD is evidence for that threat model, not a universal guarantee against every weaker-looking attack.

^fn9

<sup>10</sup> **Carlini and Wagner (C&W) attack**: Proposed in 2016 (IEEE S&P 2017), C&W formulates adversarial example generation as a constrained optimization problem that searches for a small perturbation causing misclassification without guaranteeing a global minimum. Its significance is methodological: C&W broke defensive distillation (an early defense that masked gradients to blunt weaker attacks) and other evaluated defenses, establishing the principle that robustness claims must be evaluated against optimization-based attacks, not merely gradient-sign heuristics.

^fn10

<sup>11</sup> **Lipschitz continuity**: A mathematical property that bounds how much a function’s output changes relative to its input change ($\lVert {f}({x}) - {f}({x'}) \rVert \le {K}\lVert {x}- {x'}\rVert$). A small Lipschitz constant $({K})$ limits score movement but does not by itself prevent a label flip. Reducing ${K}$ can enlarge the certified radius, though enforcing a low constant can trade away clean-data accuracy and add training cost.

^fn11

<sup>12</sup> **Feature squeezing**: In the original evaluation ([[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/04 The Responsible Fleet/14 Robust AI/V2-14.14 Source References#^ref-xu2018|Xu et al. 2018]]), squeezing 256 color levels to 16 or applying median filtering eliminated many adversarial examples while maintaining high clean accuracy. The same transformations can remove useful clean-input features and do not cover every attack mechanism, so adaptive attacks that anticipate the squeezer require separate validation before the defense is treated as production-ready.

^fn12

<sup>13</sup> **Model uncertainty (epistemic uncertainty)**: Uncertainty associated with limited knowledge of the predictive model, distinguished from aleatoric uncertainty in the observations. An estimate can support deferral, but it must be evaluated against actual errors and unfamiliar inputs; a low estimate does not certify competence. Repeated-pass or ensemble estimators add inference work, and any human fallback must meet the application’s response deadline.

^fn13

<sup>14</sup> **Monte Carlo dropout**: Proposed by Gal and Ghahramani ([[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/04 The Responsible Fleet/14 Robust AI/V2-14.14 Source References#^ref-gal2016dropout|2016]]), MC dropout reinterprets dropout as approximate Bayesian inference by keeping dropout active across repeated stochastic forward passes. The variance across predictions provides an uncertainty estimate with no architectural changes. Each additional sample adds inference work, so deployments must select the sample count against their latency and uncertainty budgets.

^fn14

<sup>15</sup> **Principle of least privilege**: Articulated by Saltzer and Schroeder ([[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/04 The Responsible Fleet/14 Robust AI/V2-14.14 Source References#^ref-saltzer1975|1975]]), this principle restricts access rights to the minimum necessary for each component. In an ML pipeline, a training job allowed to read unverified sources has an additional path through which adversarial data can enter, so write and read rights become part of the poisoning attack surface. The same principle governs the model registry, the artifact store that maps version identifiers to weight files and controls which alias (for example, `staging`, `production`) resolves to which checkpoint. Promotion to the `production` alias should be restricted to a narrow, audited set of automated evaluation services and named release engineers; if a researcher’s credentials are compromised, excluding those credentials from promotion authority blocks that direct path to replacing the live model, while other authorized paths still require scrutiny.

^fn15

<sup>16</sup> **Huber loss**: This piecewise loss transitions from quadratic to linear beyond a chosen residual threshold, bounding its derivative with respect to the residual. The parameter gradient also multiplies that derivative by the model’s prediction Jacobian, so Huber loss alone does not bound parameter updates or prevent poisoning. Under MSE, an outlier with 100× normal error contributes 10,000× squared-error loss and 100× residual-gradient magnitude. Under Huber loss, once that residual passes the threshold the loss grows only linearly and the residual derivative stays at the threshold value, so the same outlier pulls no harder than any other point beyond the threshold.

^fn16

<sup>17</sup> **Minimax**: Game-theoretic strategy from Neumann ([[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/04 The Responsible Fleet/14 Robust AI/V2-14.14 Source References#^ref-vonneumann1928theorie|1928]]) that minimizes the maximum possible loss. In adversarial robustness, Madry et al. ([[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/04 The Responsible Fleet/14 Robust AI/V2-14.14 Source References#^ref-madry2018towards|2018]]) formulate training as $\min_{\theta}\max_{\|{\boldsymbol{\delta}_{\text{adv}}}\| \leq {\epsilon_{\text{adv}}}} {\mathcal{L}}({f_{\theta}}({x}+ {\boldsymbol{\delta}_{\text{adv}}}), {y})$: the model learns to minimize loss under a worst-case perturbation within the chosen norm ball. The inner maximization is computationally expensive because it is usually approximated with iterative attacks such as PGD, so PGD-adversarial training is a budgeted robustness choice rather than a free objective change ([[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/04 The Responsible Fleet/14 Robust AI/V2-14.14 Source References#^ref-bai2021|Bai et al. 2021]]; [[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/04 The Responsible Fleet/14 Robust AI/V2-14.14 Source References#^ref-shafahi2019adversarial|Shafahi et al. 2019]]).

^fn17
