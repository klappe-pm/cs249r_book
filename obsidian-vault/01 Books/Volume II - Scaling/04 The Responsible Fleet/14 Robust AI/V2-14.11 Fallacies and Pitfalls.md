# V2-14.11 Fallacies and Pitfalls

[[Home|Home]] · [[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/Volume 2 Overview|Volume Overview]] · [[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/04 The Responsible Fleet/14 Robust AI/V2-14 Robust AI|V2-14 Robust AI]]

^sec-robust-ai-fallacies-pitfalls-087e

Robustness spans environmental shifts, input-level attacks, and system-level faults. Each threat domain introduces misconceptions that lead to inadequate defenses or misallocated engineering resources.

**Fallacy**: *Adversarial examples are an academic curiosity with no real-world impact.*

Published physical-world attacks, such as adversarial patches on clothing or stickers on stop signs, have fooled evaluated vision models under controlled patch and road-sign settings without digital access. Defense requires evaluation under the relevant physical conditions. Physical-world data augmentation, patch-aware training, and serving-time detection are candidate interventions, each with its own coverage and cost. PGD-style adversarial training on digital images adds work with each attack step, but its results do not establish resistance to physical attacks. Ordinary digital test sets can miss these deployment failure modes.

**Pitfall**: *Treating test-set success as robustness proof.*

A test set drawn from the same i.i.d. distribution as training data measures performance in that population; it does not establish resilience to a different deployment distribution or to a critical slice inside the same one. The Google Photos labeling failure shows how a harmful error on one population slice can hide inside aggregate accuracy, and the night-driving and clinic-imaging examples of distribution shift fail on populations the test set underrepresents. Robustness evidence needs held-out shifts, named slices, and the other cells of [[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/04 The Responsible Fleet/14 Robust AI/V2-14.04 A Unified Framework for Robust AI#^tbl-robust-ai-evaluation-matrix|table 1]].

**Fallacy**: *Distribution shift monitoring is optional after deployment.*

Models often degrade silently, maintaining high confidence scores even as predictive performance falls due to drift. Monitoring metrics like PSI can surface population shifts before accuracy falls below service level agreement (SLA) thresholds, enabling proactive intervention when the monitored features are predictive of downstream performance.

**Pitfall**: *Ignoring poisoning defenses unless the attacker controls the training pipeline.*

Clean-label poisoning compromises models by injecting correctly labeled but manipulated samples into public datasets or scraped data sources, requiring no access to internal code. Low-dose attacks can still target a particular prediction or implant a backdoor; the size of the poisoned slice and the apparent correctness of its labels do not establish integrity.

**Fallacy**: *Average accuracy is sufficient for robustness measurement.*

High average accuracy often masks fragility. A model that is accurate on nearly all clean test inputs can still fail on every input in a critical slice once an attacker applies a small, targeted perturbation ([[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/04 The Responsible Fleet/14 Robust AI/V2-14.14 Source References#^ref-madry2018towards|Madry et al. 2018]]). Reliable evaluation requires evidence for the claimed failure class: slice-level performance under shifts, empirical attack accuracy under a stated budget, or a certified radius where a formal guarantee is available. A finite attack search supplies empirical evidence rather than exact worst-case accuracy.

**Pitfall**: *Treating adversarial training as a complete robustness solution.*

Adversarial training does not establish universal robustness. A model trained against $\ell_\infty$ attacks needs separate evaluation for $\ell_2$ or geometric attacks. Perturbation sets can overlap, so some resistance can carry over to other norms, but the training threat model alone does not establish the extent of that transfer.

**Fallacy**: *Software faults do not matter when the model is correct.*

Focusing solely on algorithmic robustness ignores the reality that software bugs in data pipelines and serving infrastructure are a major cause of ML failures. Pipeline and data issues, not model architecture or adversarial attacks alone, can sit at the center of failures. Checksums can detect changed bytes, while behavioral validation must also catch incorrect transformations of intact data. A model hardened against adversarial perturbations can still fail when a preprocessing bug supplies validly stored but wrongly computed inputs.

**Pitfall**: *Hardening the model while leaving pipeline checks untested.*

Robustness work can concentrate on adversarial training, certified radii, or poisoning defenses while leaving schema validation, preprocessing parity, feature freshness, and rollback drills under-tested. That imbalance creates a system that resists one class of attack but fails on ordinary operational faults. In the credit-risk example of [[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/04 The Responsible Fleet/14 Robust AI/V2-14.10 System-Level Faults#^sec-robust-ai-fault-shift-diagnosis|section 14.9.2]], a changed library default would have been caught at release time by a golden-request replay comparing preprocessed tensors across pipeline versions. Without that check, the fault surfaced as a drift alert, and retraining on the corrupted features would have taught the model the bug. A robust deployment validates the model and the pipeline together and exercises the replay harness and rollback path before an incident needs them.

**Self-Check: Question**

1.  True or False: Achieving 98 percent accuracy on a held-out i.i.d. test set is sufficient proof that a computer vision model will operate reliably in production without experiencing silent degradation.

2.  An engineering team wants to reform its model evaluation metrics to avoid the pitfall of using aggregate average accuracy as the sole measure of model quality. Which evaluation practice directly resolves this pitfall?

    1.  Measure consequential population slices, empirical accuracy under specified attacks, and certified accuracy where a valid certificate is available.
    2.  Expand the held-out i.i.d. test split by 10$\times$ to reduce the variance of the average accuracy estimate.
    3.  Replace all predictive accuracy metrics with p99 serving latency benchmarks.
    4.  Evaluate the model exclusively on synthetic training data generated by the same architecture.

3.  A perception model is hardened using multi-step PGD adversarial training bounded by an $\ell_\infty$ norm of $\epsilon_{\text{adv}} = 8/255$. When deployed, an adversary attacks the model using an $\ell_2$ optimization attack (such as C&W) and spatial rotation perturbations. What does the chapter’s discussion of adversarial training pitfalls predict?

    1.  The model will be mathematically immune to all attacks because adversarial training provides universal robustness across all norms.
    2.  The model will automatically detect and reject the $\ell_2$ attack because $\ell_\infty$ robustness is strictly stronger than all other defenses.
    3.  The model may remain highly vulnerable to the $\ell_2$ and spatial attacks, because adversarial training hardens boundaries specifically against the threat model and norm ball used during training.
    4.  The model will fail to execute forward passes because the runtime input tensor dimensions will be rejected by the GPU driver.

4.  A perception model achieves a certified robustness radius against digital $\ell_\infty$ input perturbations. An engineer concludes that upstream data-pipeline validation and schema checks can now be deprioritized. Explain why this conclusion is dangerous, using the chapter’s systems-layer failure analysis.
