# V2-14.07 Adversarial Defenses

[[Home|Home]] · [[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/Volume 2 Overview|Volume Overview]] · [[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/04 The Responsible Fleet/14 Robust AI/V2-14 Robust AI|V2-14 Robust AI]]

^sec-robust-ai-input-attack-detection-defense-19d3

The classifier that called a panda a gibbon in [[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/04 The Responsible Fleet/14 Robust AI/V2-14.06 Adversarial Attacks#^fig-adversarial-googlenet|figure 7]] was not broken by random noise; a perturbation budget of 0.007 per pixel, spent along the gradient sign, was enough. Defending a deployed perception model against that attacker requires a threat model that names the attacker’s access and a budget for testing and mitigation. The resulting robustness claim must state what the system pays in clean accuracy, training compute, inference latency, or operational coverage.

### Adversarial Defense Workflow

^sec-robust-ai-adversarial-attack-defenses-1dc8

A team that applies adversarial training without first naming the perturbation norm and budget can multiply training compute several-fold and lose clean accuracy while leaving the actual deployment threat uncovered. Preventing that misallocation requires an adversarial-defense workflow with four ordered layers:

1.  **Define the threat model**: Specify the perturbation budget, attacker knowledge, and whether the attack occurs at inference time or during training.
2.  **Choose where to spend the defense budget**: Decide whether to spend it in training, certification, input detection, or serving-time guardrails.
3.  **Measure the trade-off**: Evaluate both sides of the defense, because improving worst-case behavior can reduce clean accuracy, raise inference latency, or multiply training cost.
4.  **Keep evaluation adversarial**: Test the defense against attacks stronger than the ones used to design it.

The order matters because spending and evaluation choices made before the threat model is specified cannot show protection against the attacker the deployment actually faces. For model-facing perturbation threats, the most direct training-time defense is **adversarial training**, which incorporates adversarial examples into the training process itself. The mechanism reuses the attacks of the previous section as a data generator. Each training step augments the batch with perturbed copies of its own inputs, so the model is trained on the high-loss points an attacker would choose. In the simplest form, FGSM generates the perturbations and each batch mixes clean and adversarial examples ([[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/04 The Responsible Fleet/14 Robust AI/V2-14.07 Adversarial Defenses#^lst-adversarial-training|listing 1]]).

``` text
function adversarial_training_step(model, clean_batch, labels, epsilon):
    logits = model(clean_batch)
    clean_loss = loss(logits, labels)

    input_gradient = gradient(clean_loss, clean_batch)
    perturbation = epsilon * sign(input_gradient)
    adversarial_batch = stop_gradient(
        clip(clean_batch + perturbation, valid_input_range))

    mixed_batch = concatenate(clean_batch, adversarial_batch)
    mixed_labels = concatenate(labels, labels)

    return loss(model(mixed_batch), mixed_labels)
```

Listing 1: **Adversarial Training Pseudocode**: Framework-neutral adversarial training using FGSM-style perturbations during training, mixing clean and perturbed data to improve robustness against gradient-based attacks.

^lst-adversarial-training

In [[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/04 The Responsible Fleet/14 Robust AI/V2-14.07 Adversarial Defenses#^lst-adversarial-training|listing 1]], the sign step bounds each coordinate’s change by the perturbation budget, while clipping keeps the perturbed input inside the valid input range (for example, $[0,1]$ for normalized pixels). Concatenation doubles the effective batch size. The stop-gradient operation treats generated adversarial inputs as fixed during the outer weight update, rather than differentiating through attack construction. The caller differentiates the returned loss with respect to the model parameters and performs the optimizer update. The perturbation budget ${\epsilon_{\text{adv}}}$ (the `epsilon` argument) bounds the allowed input change, while the attack algorithm determines how effectively it searches that set; computation reuse can reduce its training overhead ([[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/04 The Responsible Fleet/14 Robust AI/V2-14.14 Source References#^ref-shafahi2019adversarial|Shafahi et al. 2019]]).

Adversarial example generation increases training time because each training step requires additional forward and backward passes through the model ([[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/04 The Responsible Fleet/14 Robust AI/V2-14.14 Source References#^ref-madry2018towards|Madry et al. 2018]]; [[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/04 The Responsible Fleet/14 Robust AI/V2-14.14 Source References#^ref-bai2021|Bai et al. 2021]]). Memory overhead depends on whether the implementation stores clean and adversarial examples together, recomputes perturbations, or reuses gradient information. Iterative attacks like PGD add multiple optimization steps, so the implementation must budget that gradient work; optimized variants reduce overhead by reusing computations rather than treating every attack step as a separate full training pass ([[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/04 The Responsible Fleet/14 Robust AI/V2-14.14 Source References#^ref-shafahi2019adversarial|Shafahi et al. 2019]]). A robustness recipe that increases model capacity also adds memory and training cost. The perturbation budget and training recipe require matched clean and adversarial validation, and the perspective below shows why that matching decides what the robustness penalty actually is.

**Systems Perspective 14.1: Pricing the robustness penalty correctly**

Suppose a standard ResNet-50 records 76 percent Top-1 accuracy on clean ImageNet, while an adversarially trained model records about 50 percent under a specified $\ell_\infty$ attack at ${\epsilon_{\text{adv}}}=4/255$, the budget conventionally used for ImageNet. Subtracting these values yields 26 percentage points, but that gap compares different models under different evaluation conditions. It is not the clean-accuracy cost of robustness.

The clean-accuracy penalty must compare standard and robust models on the same clean test set. The robustness benefit must compare those models under the same attack, perturbation budget, and attack strength. The four cells are the standard model on clean data, the standard model under attack, the robust model on clean data, and the robust model under attack. The penalty is the first minus the third, the benefit is the fourth minus the second, and the quoted pair supplies only the first and the fourth. Reporting all four cells prevents an apparent trade-off from mixing a model effect with an evaluation-condition effect.

**Systems insight**: Robustness can reduce clean accuracy and increase compute, but each cost needs a like-for-like baseline. A deployment gate should report clean accuracy, empirical robust accuracy, attack specification, and evaluation cost separately.

The robustness compute penalty (principle [[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/04 The Responsible Fleet/V2 Responsible Fleet Principles#^pri-robustness-compute-penalty|24]]) quantifies this cost: PGD-style adversarial robustness can demand several times more compute per epoch than standard optimization because each batch runs inner attack steps. In Fleet Law terms (**[[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/00 Introduction/01 Introduction/V2-01.06 The C³ Taxonomy and Foundations of Scale#^eq-fleet-law|equation 1.3]]**) the multiplier lands on the per-device compute term ${T_{\text{compute}}}$. At a fixed worker count it adds local work, but the elapsed-time penalty depends on reuse and on which term limits the step. Adding workers changes communication and synchronization costs with ${N}$ and may not recover the original duration. Notebook [14.2](https://mlsysbook.ai/vol2/14_robust_ai/14_robust_ai.html#nbk-robust-ai-cost-defense) makes the multiplier concrete.

**Napkin Math 14.2: The cost of defense**

**Problem**: A team is training a robust classifier for an autonomous vehicle. Instead of the single FGSM step in [[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/04 The Responsible Fleet/14 Robust AI/V2-14.07 Adversarial Defenses#^lst-adversarial-training|listing 1]], it uses adversarial training with a seven-step PGD attack (PGD-7) that searches for a high-loss perturbation inside a specified norm ball for every training batch. Following the standard PGD adversarial-training recipe ([[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/04 The Responsible Fleet/14 Robust AI/V2-14.14 Source References#^ref-madry2018towards|Madry et al. 2018]]), it trains on the adversarial batch alone rather than on the listing’s doubled clean-plus-adversarial batch. How much does this extra attack-generation work slow down the training run?

**Math**: In this example, generating the adversarial input uses ${K_{\text{PGD}}}$ additional gradient steps per training sample, each a forward and backward pass with respect to the input, before the one weight-update pass on the adversarial batch. The estimate treats each attack step as equal in cost to the standard training pass, with no reuse; it is a work model rather than a measured wall-clock slowdown. By the same accounting, the FGSM listing spends one attack pass plus an update pass over a batch twice the original size, about three standard passes in all, so PGD-7 trades a further factor of more than two in training work for a stronger inner attack.

1.  **Forward/backward passes**: 1 (Standard) + 7 (Attack Generation) = 8 total passes.
2.  **Training slowdown**: 8× under the equal-cost-pass assumption.
3.  **Utility evidence**: The added work must be justified by measured robust accuracy under the specified attack. Generalization to other attacks requires separate evaluation. Pricing any clean-accuracy cost requires the like-for-like comparison in perspective [14.1](https://mlsysbook.ai/vol2/14_robust_ai/14_robust_ai.html#psp-robust-ai-robustness-tax), not a subtraction of robust accuracy from another model’s clean accuracy.

**Systems insight**: In this PGD-7 work model, the team budgets 8× the baseline compute before measuring the robustness benefit or actual elapsed-time penalty. In a machine learning fleet, robustness is not a setting that can be switched on; it is a budget the team spends, and the budget must be justified by the failure consequence of the component being hardened.

For lower-risk components, external guardrails such as input filtering and output verification can cost less than training intrinsic robustness into the weights, provided adaptive evaluation ([[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/04 The Responsible Fleet/14 Robust AI/V2-14.07 Adversarial Defenses#^sec-robust-ai-evaluation-testing-4b54|section 14.6.1.3]]) supports acceptable residual risk against an attacker who knows the guardrail. Passing a finite set of attacks does not prove that no bypass exists. Safety-critical paths more often justify the training and certification budget.

#### Certified Defenses

^sec-robust-ai-certified-defenses

Adversarial training provides empirical resistance that must be tested against the deployment threat model. It can generalize beyond the examples generated during training, but attack evaluation alone does not prove invariance throughout an allowed perturbation set. **Certified robustness** offers a mathematical guarantee: for a given input ${x}$ and radius ${\epsilon_{\text{adv}}}$, no perturbation $\|{\boldsymbol{\delta}_{\text{adv}}}\|_{p}< {\epsilon_{\text{adv}}}$ exists that changes the model’s prediction. This certifies local prediction stability, not that the unchanged prediction is correct. The guarantee formalizes the intuition that a robust model limits how quickly its outputs can change as its inputs change. The model’s **Lipschitz constant**[[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/04 The Responsible Fleet/14 Robust AI/V2-14.15 Footnotes#^fn11|¹¹]] bounds that rate. If no class score can move by more than the Lipschitz constant times the perturbation size, the margin between the top score and the runner-up score can shrink by at most twice that amount, so the predicted label holds for any perturbation smaller than the margin divided by twice the Lipschitz constant. The certified radius is a provable lower bound on the input-perturbation part of the robustness margin that compression and other efficiency changes must preserve.

Computing or constraining a tight Lipschitz constant directly across a deep network is difficult because layer-by-layer norm bounds multiply across depth and quickly become too loose to certify useful radii. A widely used technique that avoids layerwise bound propagation on high-dimensional inputs like ImageNet is **randomized smoothing**. Instead of classifying ${x}$ directly, it classifies the smoothed function ${g}({x})$, defined as the most probable class predicted by the base classifier ${f}$ under Gaussian noise: ${g}({x}) = \operatorname{arg\,max}_{j} \Pr({f}({x}+{\boldsymbol{\delta}}) = {j})$ where ${\boldsymbol{\delta}} \sim {\mathcal{N}}({\mathbf{0}}, {\sigma}^2 {\mathbf{I}})$. Because two Gaussian distributions centered at ${x}$ and ${x}+{\boldsymbol{\delta}_{\text{adv}}}$ overlap heavily whenever $\|{\boldsymbol{\delta}_{\text{adv}}}\|_2$ is small relative to the noise scale ${\sigma}$, averaging the base classifier over Gaussian draws analytically bounds how fast class probabilities can shift with ${\boldsymbol{\delta}_{\text{adv}}}$, regardless of how sharp the underlying neural network is internally.

Cohen et al. ([[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/04 The Responsible Fleet/14 Robust AI/V2-14.14 Source References#^ref-cohen2019certified|2019]]) proved a tight multiclass bound for the certified radius ${R}$ using a lower bound on the top-class probability ${p_{A}}$ and an upper bound on the runner-up probability ${p_{B}}$: ${R}= \frac{{\sigma}}{2}\left({\Phi}^{-1}({p_{A}}) - {\Phi}^{-1}({p_{B}})\right)$, where ${\Phi}^{-1}$ is the inverse standard normal CDF. In the binary special case, where ${p_{B}}= 1 - {p_{A}}$, this reduces to ${R}= {\sigma}{\Phi}^{-1}({p_{A}})$. This Gaussian-smoothing bound certifies a Euclidean ($\ell_2$) perturbation radius, measured in the same units as the input to which the noise is added; it does not automatically certify the other norms used in attack benchmarks. If a binary decision has top-class probability ${p_{A}}$ = 0.999 under noise ${\sigma}$ = 0.5, the simplified radius is approximately 1.5 in those input units. For an image scaled to $[0,1]$, that radius bounds the Euclidean length of the whole perturbation vector. It is not directly comparable to the per-coordinate $\ell_\infty$ budget of the robustness-penalty comparison. The radius and that budget differ because an $\ell_\infty$ perturbation of size ${\epsilon_{\text{adv}}}$ spread over ${d}$ pixels can have Euclidean length up to ${\epsilon_{\text{adv}}}\sqrt{{d}}$, so a per-pixel budget that looks tiny can correspond to a long Euclidean vector on a high-dimensional image.

The multiclass case rarely estimates the runner-up probability separately. Because the class probabilities sum to one, no competing class can exceed $1 - {p_{A}}$, so a lower bound on ${p_{A}}$ alone also bounds every competitor. Estimating that lower bound from samples turns certification into a statistical estimation problem and adds a confidence level to the certificate; an observed class frequency alone is not a certified probability bound.

Certification also carries accuracy and compute trade-offs. On ImageNet, the same work reports nontrivial certified accuracy for randomized smoothing, including certification at radius 0.5, but certification requires sampling many noise vectors per inference to estimate class probabilities with sufficient confidence. The increase in inference latency makes the sample budget a deployment constraint. Two designs keep certification outside the tight serving budget. Asynchronous auditing certifies a sampled fraction of served requests offline, so the fleet learns how often its answers were certifiable without delaying any of them. A selective safety interlock certifies only the requests routed to a safety-critical action, such as a braking decision, and lets the remaining traffic take the uncertified path.

Certified defenses cover one part of the adversarial workflow. Production systems still need detection paths for suspicious inputs, mitigation strategies that change training or serving behavior, and evaluation procedures that verify which threat models remain covered.

#### Detection Techniques

^sec-robust-ai-detection-techniques-0e7e

Detecting adversarial examples before they reach the model forms the first line of defense only when the signal reaches a serving decision: reject, transform, route, or audit. A population-level signal asks whether a window of current inputs still resembles the reference population. The two-sample tests of [[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/04 The Responsible Fleet/14 Robust AI/V2-14.05 Environmental Shifts#^sec-robust-ai-statistical-significance-testing-dfde|section 14.4.2.3]] serve here unchanged. A KS test, or the Anderson-Darling test, a variant that weights differences in the distribution tails more heavily, can flag a traffic window whose inputs no longer resemble the reference. Such an alert does not identify an individual request as adversarial; per-request rejection requires an additional decision rule. These tests also inherit the drift monitors’ blind spot, since an adversarial perturbation can preserve each feature’s marginal distribution while corrupting the joint structure that joint-distribution or embedding-space comparisons would expose.

An attack that preserves marginal distributions can evade that signal. A complementary per-request signal asks whether a prediction survives a simplifying transformation of its input. **Feature squeezing**[[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/04 The Responsible Fleet/14 Robust AI/V2-14.15 Footnotes#^fn12|¹²]] ([[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/04 The Responsible Fleet/14 Robust AI/V2-14.14 Source References#^ref-xu2018|Xu et al. 2018]]) reduces input-space complexity through color bit-depth reduction or spatial smoothing, then compares the model’s prediction before and after the transformation. A large prediction change flags sensitivity to the transformation. It does not by itself establish malicious intent or identify which image detail caused the change; legitimate inputs can also trigger the detector.

Another signal asks whether the model is uncertain about its own answer. An estimate of model uncertainty[[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/04 The Responsible Fleet/14 Robust AI/V2-14.15 Footnotes#^fn13|¹³]] can route an input for rejection, human review, or a more robust model. Bayesian neural networks estimate uncertainty by placing a probability distribution over each weight and averaging predictions over sampled weights, so disagreement among those samples signals uncertainty. Ensemble methods compare predictions from independently trained models and use disagreement as the warning signal ([[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/04 The Responsible Fleet/14 Robust AI/V2-14.14 Source References#^ref-lakshminarayanan2017|Lakshminarayanan et al. 2017]]). Their detection value depends on whether uncertainty distinguishes harmful inputs from legitimate ones under the chosen attack. Repeated predictions add inference work, so deployments must evaluate both missed attacks and serving cost rather than assume that a more expensive estimator detects more.

Dropout, the training-time regularizer that randomly deactivates neurons ([[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/04 The Responsible Fleet/14 Robust AI/V2-14.14 Source References#^ref-hinton2012improvingneuralnetworkspreventing|Hinton et al. 2012]]), offers a cheaper approximation to that weight sampling. It can be repurposed for uncertainty estimation through **Monte Carlo dropout**[[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/04 The Responsible Fleet/14 Robust AI/V2-14.15 Footnotes#^fn14|¹⁴]] at inference time, where multiple forward passes with different dropout masks approximate the uncertainty distribution. Monte Carlo dropout is itself an approximate Bayesian method. Its uncertainty estimates must be validated on the deployment population rather than ranked by the original purpose of dropout.

Beyond detection, adversarial training, and certification, the remaining mitigation strategies move the spending point rather than eliminating the cost. **Defensive distillation** ([[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/04 The Responsible Fleet/14 Robust AI/V2-14.14 Source References#^ref-papernot2016|Nicolas Papernot et al. 2016]]) teaches a student model from the teacher’s soft labels. Its apparent adversarial protection came from gradient masking, in which a defense leaves adversarial inputs in place but makes the model’s gradients too flat or uninformative for a gradient-based attack to find them. Attacks that bypassed the masked gradients defeated it ([[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/04 The Responsible Fleet/14 Robust AI/V2-14.14 Source References#^ref-carlini2017|Carlini and Wagner 2017]]), so making an attack optimizer fail is not evidence that nearby adversarial inputs are absent. Input preprocessing spends the defense budget at serving time: image denoising, JPEG compression, random resizing, padding, and random transformations try to erase perturbations before the model sees them. Ensembles spend it through redundancy, combining models with different architectures, training data, or preprocessing paths so that a perturbation that fools one member is less likely to fool all of them.

#### Evaluation and Testing

^sec-robust-ai-evaluation-testing-4b54

Evaluation closes the budget loop: the system must measure which attacks the defense actually covers and what performance it sacrifices under controlled attack conditions. Robustness metrics quantify resilience through accuracy on adversarial examples, the distortion required to fool the model, and performance over attack budgets. Standardized benchmarks such as MNIST-C ([[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/04 The Responsible Fleet/14 Robust AI/V2-14.14 Source References#^ref-mu2019mnistc|Mu and Gilmer 2019]]), CIFAR-10-C, and ImageNet-C ([[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/04 The Responsible Fleet/14 Robust AI/V2-14.14 Source References#^ref-hendrycks2019benchmarking|Hendrycks and Dietterich 2019]]) measure common corruptions rather than malicious worst-case behavior, so their results occupy a different cell of [[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/04 The Responsible Fleet/14 Robust AI/V2-14.04 A Unified Framework for Robust AI#^tbl-robust-ai-evaluation-matrix|table 1]].

Empirical attack evaluation must be adaptive to the defense. The evaluator should know the preprocessing, detector, randomness, ensemble, and rejection rule. It should optimize through differentiable components and, for randomized components, use expectation over transformation, here averaging the attack gradient over the defense’s random draws rather than over physical transformations. It should test transfer and query-based attacks when gradients are unavailable, and include the complete pipeline rather than a saved model alone. Multiple random restarts and attack budgets can strengthen evidence within that threat model, but a passed PGD test proves neither that PGD found the inner maximum nor that semantic, physical, or differently normed attacks are covered. Only a certificate establishes a guarantee, and only for its stated input, norm, radius, and assumptions.

RobustBench approximates that discipline at scale with one standardized, parameter-free attack ensemble, AutoAttack, applied to every published defense under one fixed threat model, which for CIFAR-10 is a perturbation of at most 8/255 per pixel in the $\ell_\infty$ norm ([[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/04 The Responsible Fleet/14 Robust AI/V2-14.14 Source References#^ref-croce2021robustbench|Croce et al. 2020]]; [[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/04 The Responsible Fleet/14 Robust AI/V2-14.14 Source References#^ref-croce2020autoattack|Croce and Hein 2020]]). Across the 91 dated entries ([[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/04 The Responsible Fleet/14 Robust AI/V2-14.07 Adversarial Defenses#^fig-robustbench-progress|figure 10]]), the best accuracy under attack rose from 44 percent in 2018 to 75 percent in 2024, while the same model kept 94 percent accuracy on clean images. Even the strongest defense therefore still fails on about 25 percent of attacked inputs. The four entries at the bottom of the plot are published defenses that keep high clean accuracy yet fall below 5 percent accuracy under the standardized attack, the result an evaluation that is not adaptive to the defense can miss.

Adversarial training, certified defenses, and input detection address attacks on model inputs at inference time within bounded threat models. They do not establish that the model was trained on trustworthy data. If an adversary compromises the data pipeline long before the model is compiled, inference-time behavioral tests may reveal a backdoor, a trigger pattern planted through training data that makes the model output an attacker’s chosen label, but cannot by themselves establish the integrity of the training pipeline. Poisoning attacks therefore demand their own class of defenses.

**Checkpoint 14.3: Choosing and budgeting an adversarial defense**

An adversarial defense is a budgeted response to a specific threat model. Stronger guarantees commonly change clean accuracy, compute, latency, or coverage, but the direction and magnitude depend on the workload, model, defense, and evaluation boundary.

**Matching defense to threat**

**Worst-case evaluation**: explain why passing a white-box PGD test provides empirical evidence only for the evaluated model, norm, budget, restarts, and attack implementation rather than a general worst-case guarantee.

**Threat-model fit**: match the defense emphasis to an API-facing classifier and a safety-critical road-sign detector, and explain why adversarial training alone does not transfer between them.

**Reasoning about the budget**

**Certified vs. empirical**: explain when randomized smoothing’s sampling cost favors asynchronous auditing or a selective serving path rather than assuming that every certified deployment has the same latency constraint.

**Defense budget**: compare input detection with intrinsic model robustness for a low-risk component using measured clean accuracy, robust accuracy, compute, latency, and coverage rather than assuming a fixed clean-accuracy penalty.
