# V2-16.07 Privacy and Data Governance

[[Home|Home]] · [[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/Volume 2 Overview|Volume Overview]] · [[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/04 The Responsible Fleet/16 Responsible AI/V2-16 Responsible AI|V2-16 Responsible AI]]

^sec-responsible-ai-risk-mitigation-techniques-b4d6

Every fairness audit and monitor consumes demographic evidence, and that evidence is itself sensitive data. The threat models and privacy mechanisms of **[[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/04 The Responsible Fleet/13 Security & Privacy/V2-13 Security & Privacy#^sec-security-privacy|Security & Privacy]]** provide the technical controls; governance defines the evidence, ownership, and lifecycle interfaces that production systems require when inheriting them. A deletion workflow must identify which records and derived artifacts fall within an applicable obligation, such as an erasure requirement under the GDPR ([[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/04 The Responsible Fleet/16 Responsible AI/V2-16.15 Source References#^ref-gdpr2016|European Parliament and Council of the European Union 2016]]). Removing a database row may leave replicas, backups, or trained models to address. Where removal of training influence is required, lineage, retraining, validation, deployment, and audit become part of the same workflow.

Governing these risks coordinates two classes of control that are easy to conflate. Privacy mechanisms bound specified information exposures, while machine unlearning ([[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/04 The Responsible Fleet/16 Responsible AI/V2-16.07 Privacy and Data Governance#^sec-responsible-ai-machine-unlearning-cdf1|section 16.6.4]]) targets the influence a record retains in trained weights under a stated removal criterion. A privacy guarantee does not by itself remove a record from the training history, so the assurance case must distinguish these obligations and supply evidence for each. Robustness and generative-system controls receive the same evidence-and-ownership treatment in [[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/04 The Responsible Fleet/16 Responsible AI/V2-16.09 AI Safety, Robustness, and Generative Controls#^sec-responsible-ai-ai-safety-robustness-generative-controls-4297|section 16.8]].

### Core Data Governance Principles

^sec-responsible-ai-privacy-data-governance-f8a4

That coordination starts at data ingestion, where a compliance rule demanding data minimization can collide directly with a fairness audit that needs a protected demographic field. Whereas technical privacy mechanisms constrain exposure and leakage, data governance determines whether collecting the field is justified, who may access it, and how long the audit may retain it. This decision connects privacy to fairness ([[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/04 The Responsible Fleet/16 Responsible AI/V2-16.06 Fairness and Bias Mitigation#^sec-responsible-ai-fairness-machine-learning-2ba4|section 16.5.1]]) and to transparency ([[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/04 The Responsible Fleet/16 Responsible AI/V2-16.08 Explainability and Transparency#^sec-responsible-ai-transparency-explainability-b137|section 16.7.1]]). Group-based monitoring needs evidence about group membership, but the audit design determines whether operators receive individual fields, restricted audit records, or aggregate statistics. Explainability methods can also reveal information about training examples, while detailed disclosure can conflict with individual privacy. Responsible AI systems must justify the evidence collected and limit its exposure while retaining enough information to evaluate the chosen requirements.

↩︎ Federated learning, encrypted computation, and synthetic data, each bounding who can observe raw records, are established in **[[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/04 The Responsible Fleet/13 Security & Privacy/V2-13.09 Comprehensive Defense Architectures#^sec-security-privacy-privacypreserving-data-techniques-64f8|Privacy-preserving data techniques]]**.

Because training and personalization rely on personal data, privacy is a design constraint across the whole system lifecycle, and its central difficulty is the tension between data utility and individual protection. Rich, high-resolution datasets can enhance model accuracy and adaptability but also heighten the risk of exposing sensitive information, particularly when datasets are aggregated or linked with external sources. For example, large language models trained on broad text corpora have been shown to memorize specific strings that can later be retrieved through model queries or adversarial prompting ([[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/04 The Responsible Fleet/16 Responsible AI/V2-16.15 Source References#^ref-carlini2021extracting|Carlini et al. 2021]]).

↩︎ The GPT-2 extraction attack, which recovered verbatim training records through ordinary generated outputs, is established in **[[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/04 The Responsible Fleet/13 Security & Privacy/V2-13.06 Model-Specific Attack Vectors#^sec-security-privacy-case-study-gpt2-data-extraction-attack-5126|GPT-2 data extraction case study]]**.

Privacy risks also depend on how records are linked over time and across sources. A wearable device’s heart-rate or location record can already be sensitive; combining physiological and behavioral signals can reveal additional details about a user’s health, routines, or relationships. Limited visibility or control over processing, retention, and transmission makes it harder for users to understand or contest these uses.

Addressing these challenges requires understanding privacy as a system principle that entails robust data governance. This includes defining what data is collected, under what conditions, and with what degree of consent and transparency. Foundational data engineering practices, including data validation, schema management, versioning, and lineage tracking, provide the technical infrastructure for implementing these governance requirements. Responsible governance requires attention to labeling practices, access controls, logging infrastructure, and compliance with jurisdictional requirements. These mechanisms serve to constrain how data flows through a system and to document accountability for its use.

Weak data governance can obscure both quality defects and privacy exposures, but they require different evidence. In clinical applications, missing values or demographic skew can compromise predictive performance; neither alone establishes a privacy violation. Privacy assessment must also examine identifiability, access, and disclosure. Documentation should make these distinct risks traceable to the data practices that create them.

Privacy is not solely the concern of isolated algorithms or data processors; like fairness, it must be addressed as a structural property of the system. Decisions about consent collection, data retention, model design, and auditability all contribute to the privacy posture of a machine learning pipeline. This requires anticipating risks during inference and continuous operation as well as during training. Threats such as membership inference, which infers from a deployed model’s outputs whether a record was in its training set, show why privacy safeguards must be embedded in both model architecture and interface behavior.

↩︎ Membership inference, its mechanism, and why an empirical audit gives only a lower bound on leakage are established in **[[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/04 The Responsible Fleet/13 Security & Privacy/V2-13.06 Model-Specific Attack Vectors#^sec-security-privacy-membership-inference|Membership inference]]**.

Legal frameworks make privacy an enforceable design concern. The GDPR, the California Consumer Privacy Act (CCPA),[[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/04 The Responsible Fleet/16 Responsible AI/V2-16.16 Footnotes#^fn10|¹⁰]] and Japan’s Act on the Protection of Personal Information establish different obligations and individual rights within their respective scopes ([[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/04 The Responsible Fleet/16 Responsible AI/V2-16.15 Source References#^ref-gdpr2016|European Parliament and Council of the European Union 2016]]; [[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/04 The Responsible Fleet/16 Responsible AI/V2-16.15 Source References#^ref-california2023ccpa|California Legislature 2023]]; [[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/04 The Responsible Fleet/16 Responsible AI/V2-16.15 Source References#^ref-japan2023appi|Personal Information Protection Commission, Japan 2023]]). An implementation must identify which rules govern its collection, use, retention, and removal of data rather than treating consent or deletion as a uniform requirement across jurisdictions. That applicability decision belongs in the system’s documented data-governance requirements.

Privacy mechanisms limit specified data exposures under their threat models; they do not establish that a system’s decisions are safe. A model may satisfy its privacy requirements while still failing catastrophically on unexpected inputs, a complementary concern about *how systems fail* that [[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/04 The Responsible Fleet/16 Responsible AI/V2-16.09 AI Safety, Robustness, and Generative Controls#^sec-responsible-ai-ai-safety-robustness-generative-controls-4297|section 16.8]] takes up. Within privacy itself, the next decision is where the protected boundary sits in each deployment architecture.

### Privacy Across Deployment Tiers

^sec-responsible-ai-privacy-architectures-1f9a

Training a model with differential privacy does nothing to protect a user whose raw prompt is copied into a plaintext serving log at the API gateway. Keeping raw records on a phone, by contrast, protects against central database breaches at the cost of hiding fleet-wide telemetry from operators. Privacy inherits the same centralization trade-off across deployment tiers (privacy row of [[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/04 The Responsible Fleet/16 Responsible AI/V2-16.05 Architectural Trade-Offs#^tbl-ml-principles-comparison|table 4]]). Aggregating data in the cloud enables high-capacity modeling and monitoring but concentrates exposure to breaches and surveillance, so it must be paired with strong encryption, access control, and auditability. Keeping data local on mobile, federated, or TinyML clients reduces that central exposure but changes the evidence available to fleet operators. Privacy controls must cover local storage, outputs, telemetry, and update channels; an endpoint with no update path needs controls that remain valid for its approved service life.

The constraint the table cannot show is that privacy claims must name their boundary. Differentially private training bounds training-data leakage through the released model; ordinary inference on fixed weights remains within that post-processing guarantee. It does not protect new private inputs copied into service logs or unrelated private-data accesses. Interface design, retention rules, rate limiting, and access control address those separate exposures. Opaque data collection can also violate user expectations, making consent surfaces and clear disclosure part of the privacy architecture.

Federated training draws three such boundaries ([[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/04 The Responsible Fleet/16 Responsible AI/V2-16.07 Privacy and Data Governance#^fig-privacy-risk-flow|figure 7]]). Raw records stay on the client. Secure aggregation lets the server see only the sum of the client updates, and it hides individual updates only under its assumptions about collusion among parties and client dropout. Central differential privacy bounds what the released model reveals only if the curator that adds the noise is trusted. Each protection covers a different quantity against a different observer, so a claim about one says nothing about the others. The privacy unit, clipping rule, and protocol assumptions define each guarantee, and so does composition, the rule by which privacy loss adds up across training rounds ([[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/04 The Responsible Fleet/16 Responsible AI/V2-16.15 Source References#^ref-bonawitz2017|Bonawitz et al. 2017]]; [[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/04 The Responsible Fleet/16 Responsible AI/V2-16.15 Source References#^ref-dwork2014|Dwork and Roth 2014]]).

![[90 Assets/Volume II/16 Responsible AI/Privacy Flow 89974bcd4e.svg]]

Figure 7: **Privacy Boundaries in Federated Training**: Each protection holds only at its own boundary. Raw records stay on clients, secure aggregation protects individual updates under its protocol assumptions, and central differential privacy bounds the released model relative to the specified privacy unit, provided the curator is trusted. Neither implies a universal ordering of privacy strength.

^fig-privacy-risk-flow

An architecture decides where the privacy boundary sits; the model decides how much crosses it. A service can keep raw records behind every access control and still release training data through the weights it serves, which is where the governance of privacy guarantees begins.

### Governing Privacy Guarantees

^sec-responsible-ai-privacy-preservation-91b1

A language model that regurgitates a patient’s phone number from its training set or a classifier whose confidence scores reveal whether a specific customer was in the training cohort leaks private data through authorized outputs even when no database is breached. Governing a privacy guarantee starts from these output leaks, developed in **[[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/04 The Responsible Fleet/13 Security & Privacy/V2-13.06 Model-Specific Attack Vectors#^sec-security-privacy-leakage-authorized-outputs|Leakage through authorized outputs]]**, and turns each into an audit obligation across data collection, model behavior, and user interaction. Because a membership-inference audit must state the attacker access it assumes and reads only as a lower bound on leakage, a formal bound has to come from the training mechanism. Differential privacy supplies such a bound. Its privacy budget ${\epsilon}$ limits how much the probability of any output can change when one privacy unit, the person or record the guarantee protects, is added or removed, except with a small probability ${\delta}$. A privacy accountant totals the budget that training spends across all its steps.

For differential privacy, governance consumes the mechanism and accounting established in **[[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/04 The Responsible Fleet/13 Security & Privacy/V2-13.10 Differential Privacy#^sec-security-privacy-differential-privacy-8c2b|Differential Privacy]]**. A differentially private stochastic gradient descent (DP-SGD) deployment must retain the clipping, noise, sampling, and composition configuration that supports its reported bound ([[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/04 The Responsible Fleet/16 Responsible AI/V2-16.15 Source References#^ref-abadi2016deep|Abadi et al. 2016]]). Reviewers then evaluate that bound alongside utility, subgroup performance, and the private-data accesses outside training. Naming the method is insufficient evidence for approving the complete service.

Differential privacy introduces system-level trade-offs on the path where private data is accessed. Training noise can reduce utility, and attempts to recover it through additional data or optimization steps must be evaluated with the resulting privacy accounting and compute cost. If training occurs centrally, those costs do not automatically become extra work for an edge device performing ordinary inference on the released model. Local training or local randomization places different demands on the device. Data minimization, retention limits, and access controls remain separate requirements; feature obfuscation alone provides no differential privacy guarantee.

**Systems Perspective 16.1: The price of privacy**

Training a large language model on sensitive messages with DP-SGD reduces training-data extraction risk, but the protection is priced in compute and accuracy.

With the privacy unit, the neighboring relation (adding or removing one unit versus replacing one), and ${\delta}$ fixed, a smaller ${\epsilon}$ gives a stronger bound, and every change that lowers it (more noise, a smaller sampling rate, fewer steps) tends to cost accuracy or extra training. **[[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/04 The Responsible Fleet/13 Security & Privacy/V2-13.10 Differential Privacy#^sec-security-privacy-differential-privacy-8c2b|Differential Privacy]]** derives these effects and lists the accountant inputs a reproducible DP-SGD result must record. Those recorded inputs, not the ${\epsilon}$ value alone, are what a governance review retains. The three configurations below are planning assumptions whose ${\epsilon}$ values an external accountant would have to confirm, as the CIFAR-10 accountant inputs of **[[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/04 The Responsible Fleet/13 Security & Privacy/V2-13.10 Differential Privacy#^sec-security-privacy-practical-privacy-budget-example-dpsgd-image-classification-2662|DP-SGD accountant inputs for image classification]]** illustrate. Read them for the direction of each trade. The noise multiplier sets the noise scale as a multiple of the clipping norm. The stronger configuration doubles the noise multiplier of the weaker one but also trains for more epochs, and more steps by themselves raise ${\epsilon}$; only the accountant can confirm that the extra noise more than offsets the extra steps.

**Illustrative stronger-privacy configuration** (assumed accountant result ${\epsilon}=$ 1): The scenario assumes per-example gradients are clipped to norm ${C}= 1.0$, 40 percent of updates are affected by clipping, and the noise multiplier is ${\sigma}= 1.0$. It further assumes 3× more epochs, a training-cost increase from a GPT-3-scale baseline of \$4.6M to approximately \$13.8M, and an accuracy drop of 6 percent.

**Illustrative weaker-privacy configuration** (assumed accountant result ${\epsilon}=$ 8): The scenario assumes a noise multiplier of ${\sigma}= 0.5$, training overhead of 30 percent, and an accuracy drop of 1 percent.

**Illustrative weakest configuration** (assumed accountant result ${\epsilon}=$ 10): The scenario assumes less noise and less than 1 percent accuracy loss, with a weaker formal privacy bound than the other two configurations under otherwise comparable accounting assumptions.

These scenarios compare formal privacy bounds, utility, and training cost; none of those quantities alone establishes user trust. Once a fixed model has been trained with differential privacy, ordinary inference is post-processing and does not consume additional training-data privacy budget. Additional privacy loss must be accounted for when a postdeployment mechanism makes new accesses to private data or releases additional differentially private statistics. The key engineering decision is to document and account for every private-data access and communicate the resulting trade-offs to users and regulators.

Released datasets need a boundary of their own. A ${k}$-anonymous release (**[[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/04 The Responsible Fleet/13 Security & Privacy/V2-13.09 Comprehensive Defense Architectures#^sec-security-privacy-deidentification-k-anonymity|De-identification and k-anonymity]]**) hides each record among at least ${k_{\text{anon}}}$ that share its quasi-identifiers, a property of a specified release rather than of one transformed request. Differential privacy likewise requires a defined release mechanism, privacy unit, neighboring relation, and privacy accountant; noise injection alone does not establish a guarantee.

Privacy enforcement also depends on infrastructure beyond the model itself. Data collection interfaces must support informed consent and transparency. Logging systems must avoid retaining sensitive inputs unless strictly necessary, and must support access controls, expiration policies, and auditability. Model serving infrastructure must be designed to prevent overexposure of outputs that could leak internal model behavior or allow reconstruction of private data. These system-level mechanisms require close coordination between ML engineering, platform security, and organizational governance.

Privacy must be enforced throughout the machine learning lifecycle. Retraining pipelines must respect the approved deletion and data-use policies. Monitoring should collect only the identifying information justified by its purpose, with access and retention controls appropriate to that information. Privacy-aware telemetry collection and protected per-user audit trails can support investigation and remedy without making sensitive records broadly available in logs or dashboards.

The privacy assurance case therefore spans the whole pipeline. The formal privacy accountant covers the private-data accesses in its stated mechanism; interface controls, retention policies, and access records support separate claims about the surrounding service. Keeping these claims distinct makes gaps visible when a model, telemetry path, or data source changes.

Privacy preservation techniques create complex sociotechnical tensions that extend well beyond technical implementation. Differential privacy mechanisms may reduce model accuracy in ways that disproportionately affect underrepresented groups, creating conflicts between privacy and fairness objectives. These challenges require ongoing stakeholder engagement as detailed in [[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/04 The Responsible Fleet/16 Responsible AI/V2-16.10 Accountability, Governance, and Sociotechnical Dynamics#^sec-responsible-ai-normative-pluralism-value-conflicts-d61f|section 16.9.4.3]], where organizations must navigate competing values around data control, personalization, and regulatory compliance. Even a strong training-time privacy bound, however, treats the training set as fixed, leaving open what happens when a user revokes consent or demands erasure after the model is already in production.

### Machine Unlearning

^sec-responsible-ai-machine-unlearning-cdf1

Deleting a user’s row from a production database after an erasure request under GDPR or CCPA ([[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/04 The Responsible Fleet/16 Responsible AI/V2-16.15 Source References#^ref-gdpr2016|European Parliament and Council of the European Union 2016]]; [[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/04 The Responsible Fleet/16 Responsible AI/V2-16.15 Source References#^ref-california2023ccpa|California Legislature 2023]]) leaves their influence intact inside a 175-billion-parameter model trained on last month’s snapshot. Because privacy preservation does not end at training time, machine unlearning supplies a technical response when the approved deletion policy also requires addressing retained model state. The legal requirement and the evidence of removal must be specified separately, since retained influence alone does not establish that every deployment violates a deletion obligation. When the approved response does require removing training influence, the architecture must support the chosen method and its verification: an endpoint without sufficient data, training state, or compute needs a replacement model trained elsewhere, whereas a device with the required resources may perform the work locally. Placement follows those resource and state requirements rather than the deployment label alone.

Three update strategies differ in what they recompute and what removal they guarantee ([[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/04 The Responsible Fleet/16 Responsible AI/V2-16.07 Privacy and Data Governance#^fig-machine-unlearning|figure 8]]). Retraining from scratch on the retained data, the training set with the forget set ${D_{\text{forget}}}$ removed, supplies a reference for exact removal relative to that procedure. Gradient ascent on the forget set increases its loss through updates of the form ${\theta} + {\eta} \nabla {\mathcal{L}}$, but increased loss alone does not demonstrate removal. Sharded, isolated, sliced, and aggregated (SISA) training partitions data into isolated shards ${S}_1, {S}_2, {S}_3$ whose constituent models are combined by aggregating their predictions at serving time. Within each shard, the data is further ordered into sequential slices introduced incrementally during training, with a checkpoint saved before each slice is added. A deletion therefore affects only the constituent model trained on the record’s shard, and rewinding that shard to the checkpoint saved just before the affected slice avoids replaying earlier slices.

![[90 Assets/Volume II/16 Responsible AI/Unlearning Strategies c5b78d9fb5.svg]]

Figure 8: **Model Update Strategies**: Three approaches to removing a data point’s influence. Full retraining gives exact unlearning at the highest cost; gradient ascent provides an approximate update by maximizing loss on the forget set; and SISA training partitions data into shards so only the affected shard is retrained. The accompanying cost model quantifies the full-retraining and SISA cases.

^fig-machine-unlearning

Full retraining is a useful reference, but its feasibility depends on access to the permitted remaining data, the training procedure, and sufficient compute within the response deadline. A retention policy may remove data that retraining would need; a large training run may exceed the available budget. An alternative unlearning method must state its own data and state requirements rather than assume that avoiding full retraining also eliminates those dependencies.

Machine unlearning seeks to remove specified training influence without repeating the entire training run. Cao and Yang ([[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/04 The Responsible Fleet/16 Responsible AI/V2-16.15 Source References#^ref-cao2015|2015]]) proposed converting suitable learning algorithms into a summation form, in which the trained model depends on the data only through sums over training examples, allowing a deletion to subtract its terms from those sums and recompute the model. SISA instead localizes retraining to affected shards ([[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/04 The Responsible Fleet/16 Responsible AI/V2-16.15 Source References#^ref-bourtoule2021|Bourtoule et al. 2021]]). It still needs the retained data and training state required by that procedure. The removal claim must specify its reference and verification method; a loss increase on deleted examples is different evidence from retraining without them. Method selection therefore depends on retained state, model quality, operational cost, and the removal guarantee the deployment must support.

![[90 Assets/Volume II/16 Responsible AI/Responsible AI Unlearning Cost Ladder 69534efddb.svg]]

*Two-rung cost ladder on a log scale. Top rung, full retraining at 4.6 million dollars; bottom rung, SISA sharded unlearning at 46 thousand dollars, about 100× cheaper.*

*For one isolated erasure, SISA cuts retraining cost about 100 times; a daily batch that touches most shards erodes the saving.*

**Napkin Math 16.6: The cost of forgetting**

A user requests erasure of personal data under GDPR Article 17 (“Right to Erasure”). This scenario assumes that the approved response includes removing its influence from a model trained on 1 TB of data; that model-removal requirement is a scenario premise, not a conclusion derived from invoking the article.

**Baseline (full retraining)**: In this sizing scenario, a 175B-parameter model at GPT-3 scale retrains on 1,024 NVIDIA A100 (80 GB SXM4) GPUs (dense FP16 ${R_{\text{peak}}} =$ 312 TFLOP/s per accelerator at an assumed model FLOPs utilization (MFU) of about 32 percent) for approximately 36 days at a cost of roughly \$4.6M.

**Engineering fix**: SISA training partitions data into ${K}=$ 100 independent shards, training 100 sub-models. For one datum confined to one shard, the example retrains that shard (1 percent of data). Assuming cost and elapsed time scale linearly with the data fraction at the same resource allocation gives \$46,000 and approximately 8.6 hours. This simplified estimate omits slicing, checkpoint storage, validation, and deployment overhead; it is not a measured GPT-scale SISA result.

**Trade-off**: The scenario assumes an accuracy drop of 3–7 percent; the cost calculation does not establish that quality result. Serving must evaluate and aggregate 100 sub-models, with latency depending on parallelism and aggregation. Batch removal cost depends on how many of the 100 shards are touched. If the 1,000 requests/day arrive independently and uniformly across shards, a daily batch is likely to touch nearly all of them, eroding the sharding-only saving. Concentrated requests, batching policy, and saved slice states change the work. The operating comparison must therefore include the request distribution and response deadline, rather than the shard count alone.

Those cost and batching trade-offs explain why statutory erasure rights under GDPR, CCPA, and similar laws ([[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/04 The Responsible Fleet/16 Responsible AI/V2-16.15 Source References#^ref-gdpr2016|European Parliament and Council of the European Union 2016]]; [[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/04 The Responsible Fleet/16 Responsible AI/V2-16.15 Source References#^ref-california2023ccpa|California Legislature 2023]]) require context-dependent engineering triage rather than a universal command to retrain every model from scratch. Where model state itself retains personal data, as highlighted by incidents in which generative models reproduced personal content or copyrighted text, machine unlearning provides a technical strategy for reducing or removing a deleted record’s influence within a feasible compute budget.

From a systems perspective, machine unlearning introduces nontrivial architectural and operational requirements. Systems must be able to track data lineage, including which datapoints contributed to a given model version. This often requires structured metadata capture and training pipeline instrumentation. Additionally, systems must support user-facing deletion workflows, including authentication, submission, and feedback on deletion status. Verification may require maintaining versioned model registries, along with mechanisms for testing the stated removal guarantee against its reference training procedure. Such tests can check that membership inference no longer distinguishes the deleted records from records the model never saw, and that the updated model’s outputs match those of a model retrained without the deleted records within a stated tolerance. These operations must span data storage, training orchestration, model deployment, and auditing infrastructure, and they must be robust to failure or rollback.

Resource-constrained deployments amplify these challenges further. A TinyML device may lack the compute needed for retraining while still accepting a replacement model trained elsewhere. Unlearning then requires fleet inventory, authenticated updates, and evidence that affected versions have been replaced or retired. If no update path exists, operators must plan for physical servicing or withdrawal; initial data minimization reduces exposure but does not erase influence already present in deployed weights. Even in cloud-based systems, unlearning must coordinate model replicas, snapshots, and logs.

The need to remove training influence should be assessed during architecture and policy design, when teams can still plan the required lineage, retained state, and update paths. Retrofitting those capabilities after deployment may require reconstructing provenance or rebuilding training infrastructure. The assurance case should specify which removal obligations apply, how the chosen method will be evaluated, and how approved replacement models reach deployed instances.

Machine unlearning represents a shift in privacy thinking, from protecting what data is collected to controlling how long that data continues to affect system behavior. Exercise of any data right or challenge to an automated outcome, however, depends on whether users, clinicians, and auditors can see how the system works and why it produced a specific decision.

    2.  When the daily volume of data erasure requests is high enough that requests touch essentially all ${K}$ shards in every retraining batch
    3.  When the primary storage system transitions from NVMe SSDs to distributed object storage
    4.  When the training pipeline uses Adam optimizer instead of stochastic gradient descent

2.  In Differentially Private Stochastic Gradient Descent (DP-SGD), what are the two core operations applied to gradients before weight updates, and what are their primary systems impacts?

    2.  Gradient checkpointing and layer freezing, eliminating backpropagation overhead
    3.  Per-example gradient clipping to a maximum norm ${C}$ and calibrated Gaussian noise addition, whose privacy bound requires sampling and composition accounting and whose compute and utility costs depend on the configuration
    4.  Spectral normalization of weight matrices and label smoothing, which eliminate adversarial vulnerability with zero compute overhead
