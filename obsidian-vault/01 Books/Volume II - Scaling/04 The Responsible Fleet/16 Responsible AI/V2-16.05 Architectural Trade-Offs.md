# V2-16.05 Architectural Trade-Offs

[[Home|Home]] · [[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/Volume 2 Overview|Volume Overview]] · [[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/04 The Responsible Fleet/16 Responsible AI/V2-16 Responsible AI|V2-16 Responsible AI]]

^sec-responsible-ai-responsible-ai-across-deployment-environments-e828

A centralized audit can join predictions, demographic attributes, and delayed outcomes when access to those records is authorized. A federated model running on a million smartphones may provide no such joined dataset. The same fairness criterion then requires a different evidence path, such as restricted aggregate statistics or a separately governed audit sample. Deployment architecture determines where the evidence can be assembled and who can act on it.

These architectural differences redistribute both feasible safeguards and accountable parties. The comparison that follows uses four deployment tiers that differ in compute, connectivity, and update authority: *Cloud ML* runs on serving replicas in data-center fleets, whereas *Edge ML* moves execution to gateways and servers near the data source. On the client side, *Mobile ML* runs on phone-class devices with sustained power budgets of a few watts, the regime of Archetype C (Federated MobileNet). *TinyML* operates on microcontrollers with hundreds of kilobytes of memory and milliwatt power budgets, an extreme below Archetype C that often lacks any update channel. This book’s other lighthouses sit in the cloud tier: Archetype A (GPT-4/Llama-3) and Archetype B (DLRM at Scale) serve from data-center fleets, and even Archetype C relies on a cloud aggregator for its fleet-level evidence. TinyML has no lighthouse of its own; it appears here because it marks the limit case, a deployment whose safeguards must be fixed before it ships.

↩︎ Archetype C (Federated MobileNet) and its few-watt per-device envelope are established in **[[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/00 Introduction/01 Introduction/V2-01.07 The Fleet Stack and the Lighthouse Archetypes#^sec-introduction-archetype-c-federated-mobilenet-65a3|Archetype C (Federated MobileNet)]]**.

Resource availability, latency constraints, user interface design, and the presence or absence of connectivity determine whether responsible AI principles can be enforced consistently across deployment contexts. The same model may support rich explanation, continuous monitoring, and centralized auditability in the cloud, yet require static validation and simplified safeguards when embedded in a device with intermittent connectivity. Tier choice is also an equity decision, because a safeguard that needs a data-center round trip reaches only users whose connectivity supports it ([[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/04 The Responsible Fleet/16 Responsible AI/V2-16.05 Architectural Trade-Offs#^sec-responsible-ai-resource-requirements-equity-implications-b967|section 16.4.3]]).

### Design Trade-Offs

^sec-responsible-ai-design-tradeoffs-6f5a

Moving across those four tiers forces concrete trade-offs whenever two responsible AI goals compete for the same finite budget. An autonomous braking controller or a wearable gesture recognizer on the edge cannot spend hundreds of milliseconds computing feature attributions on every sensor frame, and a personalized model adapting on one phone cannot see whether its local updates widen error gaps across the rest of the population. Because each tier fixes how much compute, latency, connectivity, and data visibility a safeguard can draw, optimizing for one principle routinely constrains another.

Cloud-based systems can provision extensive monitoring, fairness audits, interpretability services, and privacy-preserving tools using shared compute and storage. Centralized data handling also concentrates exposure to surveillance and breaches. On-device execution can keep raw data local, but user control still depends on interfaces, permissions, retention rules, and update authority. Postdeployment visibility, fairness instrumentation, and adaptation depend on which evidence and maintenance paths the architecture provides.

Tensions between goals often become apparent at the architectural level. When an endpoint has too little latency budget for a costly explanation on every inference, designers can evaluate lightweight methods within that budget, precompute suitable summaries, or retain decision records for asynchronous analysis. The explanation’s audience and required delivery time determine which of these paths can satisfy the obligation.

Conflicts also emerge between personalization and fairness. Systems that adapt to individuals based on local usage data often lack the global context necessary to assess disparities across population subgroups. To prevent personalized predictions from creating systematic exclusion, a system must balance user-level adaptation with mechanisms for group-level equity and auditability.

Privacy and robustness objectives can also conflict. Logging rare events or user outliers can help diagnose reliability failures, but the records may expose sensitive behavior. If raw records must remain local or encrypted, the improvement process needs an authorized evidence path, such as restricted review or aggregate diagnostics. Robustness requirements belong in the initial design; subsequent refinement depends on whether the retained evidence and update mechanism support it.

Energy budgets can constrain the frequency and placement of safeguards. Reducing model cost does not itself require weaker fairness monitoring; teams can compare local checks, sampled audits, and off-device analysis against the evidence and response deadlines. Privacy safeguards face the same constraint. A differentially private (DP) training mechanism clips each person’s contribution and adds calibrated noise, so that adding or removing any one person’s record changes the probability of any released result only within a stated bound. In federated learning, clipping, noise generation, and communication can consume additional energy, but their placement and cost depend on the mechanism. A central-DP design may add noise at the curator, a trusted server that combines the clipped updates, whereas a local-DP design places randomization on the client. Battery feasibility therefore requires measuring the chosen implementation.[[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/04 The Responsible Fleet/16 Responsible AI/V2-16.16 Footnotes#^fn4|⁴]]

↩︎ Differential privacy, its privacy budget, and the accountant that tracks it are established in **[[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/04 The Responsible Fleet/13 Security & Privacy/V2-13.10 Differential Privacy#^sec-security-privacy-differential-privacy-8c2b|Differential Privacy]]**.

Together, these deployment examples reveal a broader systems-level challenge. Responsible AI principles cannot be considered in isolation. They interact, and optimizing for one may constrain another. The appropriate balance depends on deployment architecture, stakeholder priorities, domain-specific risks, the consequences of error, and the environmental and distributional impacts of computational resource requirements. Responsible machine learning design therefore depends on making those constraints explicit before choosing methods, so that the resulting system can be evaluated against the deployment context it must actually inhabit.

Comparing these settings ([[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/04 The Responsible Fleet/16 Responsible AI/V2-16.05 Architectural Trade-Offs#^tbl-ml-principles-comparison|table 4]]) demonstrates that no deployment context dominates across all principles; each makes distinct architectural compromises. Cloud systems support computationally expensive explanation methods and centralized fairness monitoring. Shapley-value attribution (SHAP) credits each input feature with its average marginal contribution to a prediction across subsets of the other features. Local interpretable model-agnostic explanations (LIME) fit a simple surrogate model to many perturbed copies of one input. Both require many extra model evaluations per explained decision, and [[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/04 The Responsible Fleet/16 Responsible AI/V2-16.08 Explainability and Transparency#^sec-responsible-ai-architecting-explainability-transparency-75e8|section 16.7]] develops them. Cloud deployments can afford that work, but introduce privacy risks through data aggregation. Edge and mobile deployments offer stronger data locality but limit postdeployment observability and global fairness assessment. TinyML systems face particularly tight budgets; their validation, monitoring, and update plans must reflect the connectivity and storage actually provided. These constraints also decide which users receive which safeguards, the equity question raised in [[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/04 The Responsible Fleet/16 Responsible AI/V2-16.05 Architectural Trade-Offs#^sec-responsible-ai-resource-requirements-equity-implications-b967|section 16.4.3]]. Understanding these deployment constraints provides necessary context for the technical methods that translate responsible AI principles into production controls.

| **Principle**      | **Cloud ML**                                                                                        | **Edge ML**                                                        | **Mobile ML**                                                                       | **TinyML**                                                                        |
|:-------------------|:----------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------|:-------------------------------------------------------------------|:------------------------------------------------------------------------------------|:----------------------------------------------------------------------------------|
| **Explainability** | Supports complex models and methods like SHAP and sampling approaches                               | Needs lightweight, low-latency methods like saliency maps          | Requires interpretable outputs for users, often defers deeper analysis to the cloud | Severely limited due to constrained hardware; mostly static or compile-time only  |
| **Fairness**       | Large datasets allow bias detection and mitigation                                                  | Localized biases harder to detect but allows on-device adjustments | High personalization complicates group-level fairness tracking                      | Minimal data limits bias analysis and mitigation                                  |
| **Privacy**        | Centralized data at risk of breaches but can use strong encryption and differential privacy methods | Sensitive personal data on-device requires on-device protections   | Tight coupling to user identity requires consent-aware design and local processing  | Distributed data reduces centralized risks but poses challenges for anonymization |
| **Safety**         | Vulnerable to hacking and large-scale attacks                                                       | Real-world interactions make reliability important                 | Operates under user supervision, but still requires graceful failure                | Needs static safety envelopes, watchdogs, and fail-safe behavior                  |
| **Accountability** | Corporate policies and audits allow traceability and oversight                                      | Fragmented supply chains complicate accountability                 | Requires clear user-facing disclosures and feedback paths                           | Traceability required across long, complex hardware chains                        |
| **Governance**     | Applicable law and oversight; centralized evidence and named owners                                 | Applicable law and oversight; allocate duties across integrators   | Applicable law and oversight; coordinate platform and app duties                    | Applicable law and oversight; preserve evidence through servicing and retirement  |

Table 4: **Deployment Trade-Offs**: Responsible AI principles manifest differently across deployment contexts due to varying constraints on compute, connectivity, and governance; cloud deployments support complex explainability methods, while TinyML severely limits them. Prioritizing certain principles like explainability, fairness, privacy, safety, and accountability requires careful consideration of these constraints when designing machine learning systems for cloud, edge, mobile, and TinyML environments.

The deployment analysis identifies where each obligation must be implemented. A constrained endpoint may log bounded decision metadata while a backend aggregates subgroup metrics, stores audit evidence, and serves explanations. An offline endpoint instead needs a defined evidence-retrieval and update path, or an operating envelope that does not depend on unavailable controls. Device capacity limits placement and frequency; it does not by itself remove the fleet operator’s responsibility to provide the control.

Tier placement decides where a control runs; it does not decide which control a defect needs. As the hiring case at the start of the chapter showed, predictive accuracy on historical labels does not establish that the target is legitimate. Threshold postprocessing can change a decision-rate disparity, while an unsuitable target may require changes to labels and training, and both need validation and operational ownership.

Across the principle sections beginning in [[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/04 The Responsible Fleet/16 Responsible AI/V2-16.06 Fairness and Bias Mitigation#^sec-responsible-ai-architecting-fairness-bias-mitigation-9507|section 16.5]], every technical control performs one of three jobs. Detection exposes a problem early enough to intervene, mitigation changes data, training, or decision policy to reduce the harm, and validation assembles the evidence a launch authority needs to accept the remaining risk. Deciding where in the four tiers each job can run requires first pricing what those mechanisms cost in accuracy, computation, and memory.

### Computational Overhead of Responsible AI Techniques

^sec-responsible-ai-computational-overhead-responsible-ai-techniques-79c2

Adding adversarial training, which computes a seven-step attack on each batch before the training step, multiplies batch compute roughly eightfold, and running model-agnostic SHAP attributions on every request can multiply inference work by hundreds or thousands of evaluations. Detection, mitigation, validation, privacy, and explanation techniques each impose a distinct cost on training, inference, memory, and accuracy ([[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/04 The Responsible Fleet/16 Responsible AI/V2-16.05 Architectural Trade-Offs#^tbl-responsible-ai-overhead|table 5]]). Among SHAP algorithms, exhaustive enumeration evaluates the model on every subset of features, sampled KernelSHAP estimates the attributions from a random sample of subsets, and TreeSHAP computes them exactly from a tree model’s structure ([[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/04 The Responsible Fleet/16 Responsible AI/V2-16.08 Explainability and Transparency#^sec-responsible-ai-explainability-interpretability-8d06|section 16.7.2]]). Pricing those overheads up front turns responsible AI methods from costless ethical labels into concrete capacity choices.

↩︎ Adversarial training is established in **[[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/04 The Responsible Fleet/14 Robust AI/V2-14.07 Adversarial Defenses#^sec-robust-ai-adversarial-attack-defenses-1dc8|Adversarial defense workflow]]**.

| **Technique**                     | **Accuracy loss** | **Training overhead** | **Inference cost** | **Memory overhead** |
|:----------------------------------|:-----------------:|:---------------------:|:------------------:|:--------------------|
| **Differential Privacy (DP-SGD)** |       1–20%       |        30–300%        |      Minimal       | 10–20%              |
| **Fairness-Aware Training**       |       1–3%        |         5–15%         |      Minimal       | 5–10%               |
| **Sampled KernelSHAP**            |        N/A        |          N/A          |      50–200%       | 20–100%             |
| **Exhaustive SHAP Enumeration**   |        N/A        |          N/A          |     50–1,000×      | 50–200%             |
| **Adversarial Training**          |       3–15%       |         2–11×         |      Minimal       | 50–100%             |
| **Federated Learning**            |       5–15%       |       200–500%        |      Minimal       | 100–300%            |

Table 5: **Performance Impact of Responsible AI Techniques**: The table gives planning ranges for capacity exercises rather than universal benchmark results. Differential privacy and fairness constraints mainly affect training and validation budgets; explainability cost depends on the value function and algorithm: model-agnostic subset enumeration grows combinatorially, sampled kernel methods require repeated evaluations, and model-specific TreeSHAP can compute exact attributions in polynomial time under its tree and value-function assumptions. The differential-privacy accuracy range runs from fine-tuning a publicly pretrained model (low end) to training from scratch on a small image dataset (high end), the two regimes whose published private accuracies are compared in **[[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/04 The Responsible Fleet/13 Security & Privacy/V2-13.10 Differential Privacy#^sec-security-privacy-privacy-utility-tradeoff-b3e7|Quantifying the privacy-utility trade-off]]**. Adversarial training is shown as a multiple of standard training cost because each step first generates an attack; a seven-step attack costs about eight passes per batch. These sizing ranges help engineers reason about production constraints before choosing a responsible-AI implementation.

^tbl-responsible-ai-overhead

These overhead ranges should be read as scenario assumptions, not as claims that any cited paper establishes a single universal overhead.[[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/04 The Responsible Fleet/16 Responsible AI/V2-16.16 Footnotes#^fn5|⁵]] Actual overhead varies significantly based on model architecture, dataset size, attack strength, explanation method, hardware, communication pattern, and implementation quality. Responsible-AI controls consume real training, serving, memory, or review capacity, so they must be budgeted before launch. An organization unable to afford one configuration must assess alternatives such as a smaller model, sampled audits, or asynchronous explanations against the same required evidence. Resource limits can restrict the feasible deployment scope; they do not remove the obligation. Who can afford which configuration is therefore an equity question as well as a capacity question.

### Resource Requirements and Equity Implications

^sec-responsible-ai-resource-requirements-equity-implications-b967

A municipal clinic or school district operating on a tight computing budget cannot afford to double its training cluster for differential privacy or provision extra GPU replicas for real-time Shapley explanations. Each cell of [[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/04 The Responsible Fleet/16 Responsible AI/V2-16.04 Core Principles and the ML Lifecycle#^tbl-principles-lifecycle|table 3]] is work that someone must pay for: representative sampling needs data acquisition, subgroup metrics need labeled evaluation data and analysis capacity, and user-facing explanations need serving capacity. When smaller organizations cannot fund every safeguard in the table, the people they serve receive fewer protections than the users of a better-resourced deployment. Large-scale deployments that depend on accelerator clusters and low-latency connectivity can likewise exclude users whose networks or devices cannot meet those assumptions.

The costs in [[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/04 The Responsible Fleet/16 Responsible AI/V2-16.05 Architectural Trade-Offs#^tbl-responsible-ai-overhead|table 5]] set how high these barriers are, and they are paid in energy as well as capacity. Model-specific algorithms, approximation, caching, and selective generation change that cost; asynchronous execution moves work off the request path without eliminating it. A high-traffic system serving these features to 10 million users must budget the work on the paths where it runs, then determine whether existing capacity is sufficient or additional resources are needed.

Infrastructure placement adds two further distributional effects. Data-center siting decisions that optimize power cost and latency can concentrate local environmental costs on nearby communities that had little say in the decision.[[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/04 The Responsible Fleet/16 Responsible AI/V2-16.16 Footnotes#^fn6|⁶]] Connectivity also affects access to safeguards. A user-facing explanation that requires a low-latency round trip to a data center may miss its delivery deadline on a slow network, whereas server-side fairness monitoring can analyze recorded decisions without another user round trip. The architecture must distinguish immediate user needs from audit work that can run asynchronously. Computational equity therefore depends on placement decisions as well as algorithmic design, and the assurance case should record these distributional effects alongside the model’s group metrics.

Computational costs and architectural constraints determine which controls a deployment can sustain. With those tier and capacity bounds in place, the next four sections examine each principle in turn, beginning with how a system defines, measures, and mitigates unfair disparities.

**Self-Check: Question**

1.  Why do resource-constrained TinyML deployments emphasize validated local safety envelopes and lightweight checks rather than continuous on-device SHAP explanations or large rolling fairness analyses?

    1.  Tight compute, memory, power, and connectivity budgets limit heavy runtime analysis; local checks must be paired with fleet or servicing evidence and a defined update or retirement path.
    2.  Because TinyML devices execute exclusively on cloud tensor processing units where telemetry logging is prohibited by law
    3.  Because embedded C++ compilers automatically convert nonlinear deep neural networks into linear decision trees
    4.  Because differential privacy guarantees are mathematically impossible on 32-bit microcontroller registers

2.  Why does federated learning across mobile devices make group-level fairness auditing substantially more difficult than in centralized cloud architectures?

3.  Order the following engineering actions for embedding responsible AI constraints across the ML lifecycle from earliest to latest development phase: (1) In-processing bias-aware loss constraints and DP-SGD noise injection, (2) Representative sampling and consent collection protocols, (3) Decision threshold policy calibration and human override routing, (4) Subgroup drift telemetry and rolling metric alerting, (5) Group-level disaggregated metric evaluation and stress testing.
