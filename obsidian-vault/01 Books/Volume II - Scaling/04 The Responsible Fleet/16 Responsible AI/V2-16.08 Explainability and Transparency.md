# V2-16.08 Explainability and Transparency

[[Home|Home]] · [[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/Volume 2 Overview|Volume Overview]] · [[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/04 The Responsible Fleet/16 Responsible AI/V2-16 Responsible AI|V2-16 Responsible AI]]

^sec-responsible-ai-architecting-explainability-transparency-75e8

A rejected loan applicant, a clinician questioning a risk score, and an auditor reconstructing a contested decision each need a different account of the same model output, on a different deadline. Serving them requires disclosure about the system as a whole, explanation methods whose cost and fidelity fit the deployment, and an appeal path through which the affected person can act on what is disclosed.

### The Need for Transparency

^sec-responsible-ai-transparency-explainability-b137

When ProPublica audited the COMPAS recidivism-risk score used in U.S. courts, it found that Black defendants were falsely flagged as future offenders at nearly twice the rate of white defendants ([[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/04 The Responsible Fleet/16 Responsible AI/V2-16.15 Source References#^ref-angwin2016|Angwin et al. 2016]]).[[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/04 The Responsible Fleet/16 Responsible AI/V2-16.16 Footnotes#^fn11|¹¹]] Meanwhile, the vendor’s trade-secret claims prevented defendants, judges, and external investigators from inspecting the scoring formula or training provenance. Machine learning models often operate as opaque “black boxes,” producing outputs that users, developers, and regulators struggle to scrutinize. In high-stakes domains such as criminal justice, healthcare, and finance, that opacity blocks investigation and remediation even when external outcome audits expose a disparity.

The COMPAS case separates the two properties defined among the core principles. What investigators lacked was transparency, meaning disclosure of data sources, training procedures, evaluation protocols, and known limitations. Explanations of individual scores would have been a different kind of evidence, about particular decisions rather than the system. An attribution describes how a method assigns influence under its assumptions rather than supplying a causal account, a normative justification, or a path to recourse. [[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/04 The Responsible Fleet/16 Responsible AI/V2-16.08 Explainability and Transparency#^sec-responsible-ai-explainability-interpretability-8d06|Section 16.7.2]] develops the methods, their costs, and their limits; the principle established here is that both properties are obligations the architecture must provision.

The importance of explainability and transparency extends beyond technical considerations to legal requirements. In many jurisdictions, these principles are legal obligations rather than optional guidance. Under the General Data Protection Regulation (GDPR), solely automated decisions with legal or similarly significant effects trigger Article 22 safeguards and related transparency duties, including meaningful information about the logic involved under access and notice provisions, subject to statutory exceptions ([[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/04 The Responsible Fleet/16 Responsible AI/V2-16.15 Source References#^ref-gdpr2016|European Parliament and Council of the European Union 2016]]; [[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/04 The Responsible Fleet/16 Responsible AI/V2-16.15 Source References#^ref-edpb2018automatedProfiling|European Data Protection Board 2018]]).[[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/04 The Responsible Fleet/16 Responsible AI/V2-16.16 Footnotes#^fn12|¹²]] Similar regulatory pressures in other domains reinforce the need to treat explainability and transparency as core architectural requirements.

Implementing these principles requires anticipating the needs of different stakeholders, whose competing values and priorities are examined comprehensively in [[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/04 The Responsible Fleet/16 Responsible AI/V2-16.10 Accountability, Governance, and Sociotechnical Dynamics#^sec-responsible-ai-normative-pluralism-value-conflicts-d61f|section 16.9.4.3]]. Designing for explainability and transparency therefore requires decisions about how and where to surface relevant information across the system lifecycle.

Transparency and explainability also support system reliability over time. As models are retrained or updated, mechanisms for interpretability and traceability allow detection of unexpected behavior, enable root cause analysis, and support governance. Embedded into the structure and operation of a system, these mechanisms provide the foundation for trust, oversight, and alignment with institutional and societal expectations.

While transparency and explainability enable stakeholders to understand system behavior, they do not guarantee that this behavior is equitable. A model can be fully transparent about how it makes decisions while still systematically disadvantaging certain groups, which is why the fairness criteria of [[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/04 The Responsible Fleet/16 Responsible AI/V2-16.06 Fairness and Bias Mitigation#^sec-responsible-ai-fairness-machine-learning-2ba4|section 16.5.1]] remain a separate test. Fulfilling the explainability obligation itself requires choosing between models whose decision structure can be inspected directly and post-hoc methods that estimate feature influence after training.

### Models of Interpretability

^sec-responsible-ai-explainability-interpretability-8d06

A loan officer using a traditional rules-based system can identify the rule that produced a rejection: “The debt-to-income ratio exceeds 40 percent.” A neural network, however, produces a score through many interacting operations for which no single rule supplies the decision rationale. Explainability and interpretability provide bounded evidence about model behavior, such as feature influence, sensitivity, representative examples, or counterfactual changes. That evidence can support investigation and communication, but it becomes a justification only when the institution connects it to a valid decision policy, and it becomes recourse only when the user can take a feasible action that the system will honor.

Explanation evidence plays a role in system validation, error analysis, incident investigation, user communication, and some compliance workflows. In high-stakes domains such as healthcare, financial services, and autonomous decision systems, explanation methods can generate hypotheses about spurious correlations that engineers must test independently. For instance, a saliency[[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/04 The Responsible Fleet/16 Responsible AI/V2-16.16 Footnotes#^fn13|¹³]] or sensitivity analysis might indicate that a diagnostic model responds to image artifacts rather than medical features; controlled evaluation must then determine whether that dependence is causal and operationally significant. For qualifying automated decisions under GDPR-style access and notice provisions, systems may need to provide meaningful information about the logic involved, reinforcing the need for systematic support for explanation, documentation, and contestability.

Two scopes of explanation place different demands on the system. Local explanations characterize influences or sensitivities around an individual prediction, so their cost depends on which decisions are explained and whether results can be reused. Global explanations characterize broader model behavior, using model structure, representative inputs, or aggregated decision evidence. They may be computed offline or updated as new evidence arrives. Scope describes what the explanation covers; the method and delivery requirement determine where and when it runs.

Explainability methods can be broadly categorized based on when they operate and how they relate to model structure. Post-hoc methods are applied after training, but they are not necessarily black-box methods. Model-agnostic approaches such as LIME ([[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/04 The Responsible Fleet/16 Responsible AI/V2-16.15 Source References#^ref-ribeiro2016|Ribeiro et al. 2016]]) and KernelSHAP can operate through model queries, whereas input gradients, integrated gradients[[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/04 The Responsible Fleet/16 Responsible AI/V2-16.16 Footnotes#^fn14|¹⁴]] ([[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/04 The Responsible Fleet/16 Responsible AI/V2-16.15 Source References#^ref-sundararajan2017axiomatic|Sundararajan et al. 2017]]), and gradient-weighted class activation mapping (GradCAM)[[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/04 The Responsible Fleet/16 Responsible AI/V2-16.16 Footnotes#^fn15|¹⁵]] ([[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/04 The Responsible Fleet/16 Responsible AI/V2-16.15 Source References#^ref-selvaraju2017|Selvaraju et al. 2017]]) require differentiable-model access to gradients or internal activations. Other SHAP implementations ([[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/04 The Responsible Fleet/16 Responsible AI/V2-16.15 Source References#^ref-lundberg2017unified|Lundberg and Lee 2017]]) may exploit model structure.

Post-hoc approaches are widely used in image and tabular domains, where explanations can be rendered as saliency maps or feature rankings. To illustrate how SHAP attribution works in practice, consider a trained random forest model predicting loan approval ($\text{approve}=1$, $\text{deny}=0$) based on three features: `income`, `debt_ratio`, and `credit_score`. For a specific applicant who was denied, with income of \$45,000, debt ratio of 0.55 (55 percent of income goes to debt), and credit score of 620, the model outputs $\Pr(\text{approve}) = 0.08$, that is, denial with probability 0.92. SHAP values, based on Shapley values from cooperative game theory,[[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/04 The Responsible Fleet/16 Responsible AI/V2-16.16 Footnotes#^fn16|¹⁶]] measure each feature’s contribution to moving the approval probability from a baseline (the expected prediction over a declared background distribution, here $\Pr(\text{approve}) = 0.50$) to this individual prediction.

The contribution of a feature depends on which other features are already known, so adding income first and adding it last can shift the prediction by different amounts. SHAP resolves that ambiguity by averaging a feature’s marginal contribution over every order in which the features could be added, under a specified value function. The value function states what the model outputs when some features are unknown; here it averages the prediction over the declared background distribution, which sets the 0.50 baseline. In this illustrative decomposition the averaged contributions are $-0.05$ for `income`, $-0.25$ for `debt_ratio`, and $-0.12$ for `credit_score`.

Shapley values are additive, so the contributions sum from the baseline to the prediction: $0.50 - 0.05 - 0.25 - 0.12 = 0.08 = \Pr(\text{approve})$, and therefore $\Pr(\text{deny}) = 0.92$. Under this attribution setup, debt ratio has the largest assigned contribution to the output, followed by credit score and income. This ranking is a hypothesis about model sensitivity under the chosen background distribution, not evidence that debt ratio caused the decision or that lowering it below 40 percent would flip the output. A recourse claim requires a separately validated counterfactual that respects feature dependencies, immutable attributes, feasible user actions, and the decision policy that will apply when the user returns.

The computational cost depends on the attribution algorithm. Enumerating all subsets would evaluate $2^3=8$ coalitions in this example and $2^{20}\approx1$ million for twenty features. A random forest need not use enumeration: TreeSHAP exploits tree structure to compute exact attributions under its supported value-function assumptions. KernelSHAP estimates contributions from sampled coalitions, while DeepSHAP propagates approximations through network components. Gradient-based attribution can need fewer model evaluations still, but only where the deployed runtime supports gradient computation, and lower cost does not establish explanation fidelity. Explanation capacity must therefore be budgeted for the chosen method rather than inferred from feature count alone.

Another post-hoc approach involves counterfactual explanations, which estimate how a model output changes under specified input modifications. These are relevant for decision-facing applications such as credit or hiring systems, but a mathematical output flip is not automatically recourse. A useful counterfactual must distinguish mutable from immutable features, preserve domain constraints and feature dependencies, identify an action within the user’s control, and remain valid under the institution’s future decision policy ([[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/04 The Responsible Fleet/16 Responsible AI/V2-16.15 Source References#^ref-wachter2017|Wachter et al. 2017]]). Counterfactual generation and validation therefore require domain models, policy versioning, and operational commitments in addition to constrained optimization.

A third class of techniques relies on concept-based explanations, which attempt to align learned model features with human-interpretable concepts. For example, testing with concept activation vectors (TCAV) represents user-defined concepts as vectors in activation space and tests how sensitive a model’s predictions are to those concepts in its internal representation ([[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/04 The Responsible Fleet/16 Responsible AI/V2-16.15 Source References#^ref-kim2018interpretability|Kim et al. 2018]]). These methods are especially useful in domains where subject matter experts expect explanations in familiar semantic terms. However, they require training data with concept annotations or auxiliary models for concept detection, which introduces additional infrastructure dependencies.

Post-hoc methods answer method-specific questions about model behavior. Even an exact attribution under a chosen value function does not recover a causal rationale or justify the decision policy. Approximate methods add estimation or surrogate error to those interpretive limits. Evaluation must therefore establish both what the method computes and whether that evidence serves the intended debugging, communication, or review task.

Inherently interpretable models expose a decision structure that the intended audience can inspect. Small decision trees, short rule lists, and sparse linear models with meaningful features can support this goal; a large tree or high-dimensional linear model may not. Nearest-neighbor comparisons likewise require an understandable similarity metric and representative examples. Interpretability therefore depends on structure, features, and audience, not merely the model-family label. In high-stakes applications, model selection should evaluate whether a sufficiently accurate, inspectable model meets the task before assuming that post-hoc explanations are necessary ([[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/04 The Responsible Fleet/16 Responsible AI/V2-16.15 Source References#^ref-rudin2019|Rudin 2019]]).

Six common model families fall along an interpretability continuum in three groups ([[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/04 The Responsible Fleet/16 Responsible AI/V2-16.08 Explainability and Transparency#^fig-vol2-interpretability-spectrum|figure 9]]). Sparse linear models (explained via coefficients) and small decision trees (via split paths) are interpretable by design. Prototype and concept models are hybrid architectures explained via exemplars. Tree ensembles (via TreeSHAP), deep networks (via gradients and SHAP), and foundation models (via probing, which trains a small classifier to read a property from internal activations) require post-hoc explanation. While this ordering provides a structural heuristic across model families, dimensionality and scale can shift boundaries; a million-node decision tree or dense linear regression can prove just as opaque as a deep neural network. This distinction is central to choosing an appropriate model for a given application, particularly in settings where regulatory scrutiny or stakeholder trust is essential.

Small linear models and shallow decision trees can offer transparent reasoning, while neural networks typically require post-hoc explanation techniques; size, feature meaning, and audience can move any model along the spectrum. Hybrid approaches aim to combine the representational capacity of deep models with the transparency of interpretable components. Concept bottleneck models ([[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/04 The Responsible Fleet/16 Responsible AI/V2-16.15 Source References#^ref-koh2020concept|Koh et al. 2020]]), for example, first predict intermediate, interpretable variables and then use a simple classifier to produce the final prediction. ProtoPNet models ([[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/04 The Responsible Fleet/16 Responsible AI/V2-16.15 Source References#^ref-chen2019looks|Chen et al. 2019]]) classify examples by comparing them to learned prototypes, offering visual analogies for users to understand predictions. These hybrid methods are attractive in domains that demand partial transparency, but they introduce new system design considerations, such as the need to store and index learned prototypes and surface them at inference time.

Work such as Olah et al. ([[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/04 The Responsible Fleet/16 Responsible AI/V2-16.15 Source References#^ref-olah2020|2020]]) and Geiger et al. ([[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/04 The Responsible Fleet/16 Responsible AI/V2-16.15 Source References#^ref-geiger2021causal|2021]]) applies mechanistic interpretability to reverse-engineer the internal operations of neural networks. This line of work, inspired by program analysis and neuroscience, attempts to map neurons, layers, or activation patterns to specific computational functions. Claims about such mechanisms require instrumentation and tests that support the proposed interpretation. These research methods can contribute evidence about internal computation, but do not establish a complete audit of every deployed model.

From a systems perspective, explainability introduces a number of architectural dependencies. Explanations must be generated, stored, surfaced, and evaluated within system constraints. The required infrastructure may include explanation APIs, memory for storing attribution maps, visualization libraries, and logging mechanisms that capture intermediate model behavior. Models must often be instrumented with hooks or configured to support repeated evaluations, particularly for explanation methods that require sampling, perturbation, or backpropagation.

The sociotechnical challenges of explainability center on the gap between technical explanations and human understanding. While algorithms can generate feature attributions and gradient maps, stakeholders often need explanations that align with their mental models, domain expertise, and decision-making processes. A radiologist reviewing an AI-generated diagnosis needs explanations that reference medical concepts and visual patterns, not abstract neural network activations. This translation challenge requires ongoing collaboration between technical teams and domain experts to develop explanation formats that are both technically accurate and practically meaningful. Explanations can shape human decision-making in unexpected ways, creating new responsibilities for how explanatory information is presented and interpreted. That gap between a technically valid explanation and a usable one is the first instance of a broader problem. Every control in this chapter operates inside human workflows and institutions that no model-level method governs.

### Explanation Placement

^sec-responsible-ai-system-explainability-5087

Bridging that gap under production deadlines starts with where the explanation computation can afford to run. Running sampled KernelSHAP inline on a microcontroller or a 10 ms control loop will miss the actuation deadline before a single attribution finishes, whereas deferring every attribution to an offline batch queue leaves a rejected applicant with no immediate reason for the outcome. Computational capacity, latency budget, interface design, and data access constrain both the explanation method and its placement. A cloud worker can execute expensive SHAP or LIME attribution; a constrained endpoint may use a cheaper gradient-based saliency map if its runtime supports gradients. Devices unable to generate explanations locally may preserve bounded decision records for later analysis by a backend or during servicing, the per-tier split that the explainability row of [[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/04 The Responsible Fleet/16 Responsible AI/V2-16.05 Architectural Trade-Offs#^tbl-ml-principles-comparison|table 4]] records.

Audience and timing further constrain explanation design. An end user may need a concise summary (“elevated heart rate during sleep”), while a developer or auditor may need attribution maps, concept activations, or decision traces. The required evidence depends on what each person must understand or do. A drone or industrial controller must keep explanation work within its control deadline or move that work off the critical path. Financial and clinical workflows likewise need a specified delivery deadline, which may allow analysis after the automated decision but before review or appeal. Planning for the audience and deadline, within the deployment’s resource limits, makes interpretability a design constraint.

Storage requirements also scale significantly with explanation needs. Storing SHAP values for tabular data requires approximately 4–8 bytes per feature per prediction, so monthly storage is 30 million predictions times the feature count times 4–8 bytes for a system logging 1 million predictions daily. Gradient attribution maps for images can require 1–10 MB per explanation depending on resolution; logging every image explanation at the same 1 million-per-day rate would require roughly 30–300 TB per month unless explanations are sampled, compressed, or retained for a shorter window. These volumes require careful data lifecycle management and retention policies.

Compression and optimization techniques also affect explainability. Pruning, quantization, and architectural simplifications often used in TinyML or mobile settings can distort internal representations or disable gradient flow, degrading the reliability of attribution-based explanations. In such cases, interpretability must be validated postoptimization to ensure that it remains meaningful and trustworthy. Even an explanation that survives optimization and fits its latency and storage budget, however, protects the user only if the surrounding service provides a way to act on it.

### Transparency and Contestability

^sec-responsible-ai-transparency-contestability-2f2c

Showing a rejected loan applicant a feature-attribution chart accomplishes nothing if the app offers no button to dispute an error in the credit file, no human reviewer with authority to overturn the rejection, and no immutable snapshot of the inputs that produced the score. Explanations serve accountability only when stakeholders can both understand and challenge system decisions. Transparency allows users, developers, auditors, and regulators to inspect how a system functions, assess its limitations, and identify sources of harm, but disclosure alone is not enough. In high-stakes domains, individuals and institutions need contestability: the operational ability to interrogate, appeal, and reverse an automated decision.

Documentation tools such as model cards, standardized reports published with a trained model, and datasheets for datasets, their counterpart for a dataset, formalize system-level disclosure by recording how models are trained, what data they rely on, what assumptions are embedded in their design, and what known limitations affect their use. Those artifacts improve governance, support compliance, and inform user expectations, yet static documentation still leaves affected individuals without interface tools, procedural access, or institutional support when a specific decision harms them.

To move from transparency to contestability, machine learning systems must integrate mechanisms for explanation, recourse, and feedback. Explanation provides understandable reasons for system outputs, tailored to the operational context and needs of the person receiving them. Recourse identifies feasible changes through which individuals could receive a different outcome under the stated decision policy. A proposed change needs to be actionable for that person; a counterfactual prediction alone does not guarantee an outcome after the model or policy changes. Feedback enables users and domain experts to dispute outcomes, report errors, or signal concerns, routing those signals into oversight processes and model updates.

These mechanisms can be missing from the user-facing workflow even when internal documentation exists. A mobile loan application might display a rejection without explaining how to submit additional information or reach an appeal reviewer. An external appeal process can still exist, but users need a discoverable route to it and access to the evidence relevant to their case. Similarly, a clinical model may produce a risk score without giving the physician enough context to question its use for a particular patient. If subgroup errors are neither observable nor contestable, the resulting harm becomes harder to diagnose and correct.

From a systems perspective, enabling contestability requires coordination across technical and institutional components. Models must expose sufficient information to support explanation. Interfaces must surface that information in a usable and timely way, so that organizational processes can review feedback, respond to appeals, and update system behavior. Logging and auditing infrastructure must also track user interventions and override decisions alongside model outputs. Human overrides and decision abstention can support this process only when qualified reviewers have capacity, evidence, and authority to resolve the deferred cases within the required deadline.

Implementing contestability imposes concrete infrastructure costs that scale with system throughput and complexity. Storing the necessary metadata to reconstruct a decision (input features, model version, and decision thresholds) requires persistent storage whose footprint depends on feature dimensionality, explanation payloads, and retention windows. Generating on-demand explanations using Shapley values or counterfactuals can add enough latency that contested decisions often need asynchronous processing queues to preserve serving service level agreements (SLAs). Meeting record-keeping and automatic-logging obligations for high-risk systems under frameworks such as the EU AI Act requires storage, provenance, retention controls, and oversight capacity to be budgeted as part of the inference fleet rather than as a separate policy artifact ([[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/04 The Responsible Fleet/16 Responsible AI/V2-16.15 Source References#^ref-euaiact2024|European Parliament and Council of the European Union 2024]]).

**War Story 16.1: Apple Card: A systems reconstruction of contested decisions (2019)**

**Context**: In 2019, Apple and Goldman Sachs faced intense public scrutiny after a customer reported a credit limit 20$\times$ that offered to his wife despite joint tax filings. Other customers raised similar concerns about unequal terms ([[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/04 The Responsible Fleet/16 Responsible AI/V2-16.15 Source References#^ref-nydfs2021applecard|New York State Department of Financial Services 2021]]).

**Evidence boundary**: The public record documents complaints, the investigation, the regulator’s finding, and changes to appeal policy. It does not disclose the underwriting pipeline’s latency target or internal explanation architecture. The systems account below is therefore an explicit reconstruction, not a report of Apple or Goldman Sachs implementation details.

**Reconstructed mechanism**: A low-latency underwriting service that returns only a limit and generic adverse-action reason leaves support staff unable to inspect the decision snapshot, distinguish data errors from policy effects, or route a contested outcome to a reviewer. The missing interface is broader than feature attribution. It includes versioned input provenance, policy and model versions, reasons appropriate to the decision process, appeal state, and authority to correct an error.

**Observed impact**: The New York Department of Financial Services found no violation of fair-lending law in its investigation, but the complaints still exposed a trust and contestability failure. Customers and frontline staff lacked a satisfactory account of how apparently inconsistent outcomes could be reviewed ([[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/04 The Responsible Fleet/16 Responsible AI/V2-16.15 Source References#^ref-nydfs2021applecard|New York State Department of Financial Services 2021]]).

**Systems lesson**: High-stakes decision systems need a contestability interface, not merely an attribution API. A defensible architecture preserves the decision-time evidence needed for audit, generates appropriate reasons without claiming that attribution is causation, routes appeals to an authorized human, records remediation, and tests the full workflow under its latency and retention constraints.

The Apple Card reconstruction names the pieces such a system lacks. One architectural pattern that supplies them is a contestability stack, analogous to distributed tracing in microservices, that coordinates four coupled stages across the operational pipeline. Decision provenance preserves a verifiable link from each output back to its governing policy, model version, and input snapshot, protected by authenticated logs or cryptographic integrity checks. Explanation generation budgets explanation methods tailored for appeals, audits, and routine communication, offloading compute-intensive methods to asynchronous workers while keeping lightweight techniques on the request path. When decisions are challenged, appeal routing directs contested cases to qualified human reviewers equipped with domain context. Closing the loop, outcome tracking records whether the appeal upheld or overturned the machine decision, feeding empirical discrepancies back into evaluation and retraining. Missing decision-time records can prevent investigators from reconstructing a contested decision, even if aggregate debugging remains possible, so the records to retain follow from what an investigation and a remedy will need.

*Vertical chain of four stages joined by downward arrows: provenance in dark blue, then explanation, appeal, and outcome in gray. A return arrow labeled retrain runs from outcome up the left side back to provenance.*

*Provenance anchors every appeal; tracked outcomes loop back into retraining.*

The stack need not run where the decision runs. An edge or TinyML endpoint can hand its decision records to a backend review process at the next synchronization or servicing visit, which lengthens the appeal’s evidence path without removing the obligation to offer one. In every tier, transparency supports accountability only when users and institutions can use the disclosed information to question and correct automated decisions. Contestability and appeals, however, operate after a decision has been made; when a system acts in safety-critical environments or generates open-ended outputs at scale, governance must also bound how the model can fail before harm occurs.
