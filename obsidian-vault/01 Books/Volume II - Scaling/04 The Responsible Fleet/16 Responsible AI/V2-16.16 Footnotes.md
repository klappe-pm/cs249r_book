# V2-16.16 Footnotes

[[Home|Home]] · [[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/Volume 2 Overview|Volume Overview]] · [[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/04 The Responsible Fleet/16 Responsible AI/V2-16 Responsible AI|V2-16 Responsible AI]]

^footnotes

<sup>1</sup> **Sociotechnical system**: Coined by the Tavistock Institute in the 1950s to describe the interdependent relationship between humans and technology in the workplace. ML fleets are sociotechnical systems in exactly this sense, because their “performance” is not merely a benchmark score but an emergent property of how model outputs interact with user behavior, legal frameworks, and physical resource constraints.

^fn1

<sup>2</sup> **Value alignment**: The problem of ensuring AI systems optimize for human values rather than proxy objectives. Russell ([[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/04 The Responsible Fleet/16 Responsible AI/V2-16.15 Source References#^ref-russell2021|2021]]) argues that specifying the objective is harder than optimizing it. The engineering consequence is that the training objective becomes a governed artifact with an owner and a review path, not a fixed input chosen once by the modeling team.

^fn2

<sup>3</sup> **HITL (human-in-the-loop)**: Human participation in model decisions adds response delay and improves safety only when the operator can identify and correct the relevant error. Staffing demand depends on the decision arrival rate, the fraction escalated, and the time required per review. In ML serving architectures, HITL requires routing logic, escalation criteria, and queue management that reshape the inference pipeline.

^fn3

<sup>4</sup> **Energy-privacy trade-off**: Privacy-preserving techniques like differential privacy and secure multi-party computation can increase computation, communication, and battery use. In federated learning on mobile devices, the equity implication is direct. Users with older devices, limited data plans, or limited battery life may be excluded from privacy-protected AI services, creating a system where privacy protection becomes contingent on hardware resources.

^fn4

<sup>5</sup> **Measurement context**: The sizing exercise assumes common production reference points such as GPU training, GPU or CPU inference, image/text/tabular models, and benchmark datasets. These figures are not canonical constants; they are order-of-magnitude planning anchors for deciding whether a safeguard belongs inline, asynchronous, offline, or in the training pipeline.

^fn5

<sup>6</sup> **Data center environmental justice**: Data center siting can place backup diesel generators, cooling heat, and grid strain near communities that had little say in the siting decision; the distributional effect must be measured site by site rather than assumed.

^fn6

<sup>7</sup> **Healthcare algorithm scale**: The study examined one widely used algorithm in a broader class of risk-prediction tools that industry estimates placed at approximately 200 million U.S. patients annually ([[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/04 The Responsible Fleet/16 Responsible AI/V2-16.15 Source References#^ref-obermeyer2019|Obermeyer et al. 2019]]). In the study’s simulated correction at the automatic-enrollment threshold (the risk-score cutoff above which patients were automatically identified for the program), the fraction of Black patients among those identified for additional care rose from 17.7 percent to 46.5 percent. These are shares of the selected population, not the fraction of all Black patients identified or a measured nationwide enrollment reduction.

^fn7

<sup>8</sup> **Fairness impossibility theorems**: Kleinberg et al. ([[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/04 The Responsible Fleet/16 Responsible AI/V2-16.15 Source References#^ref-kleinberg2016inherent|2017]]) studies groupwise score calibration together with balance of mean scores in each outcome class. Chouldechova ([[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/04 The Responsible Fleet/16 Responsible AI/V2-16.15 Source References#^ref-chouldechova2017|2017]]) studies predictive parity and error-rate constraints for recidivism prediction. These are distinct conditions; their incompatibility results require assumptions on base rates and predictive performance. Demographic parity is a distinct constraint on selection rates that can also conflict with calibration or error-rate goals in practice. The systems consequence is that no amount of engineering can satisfy every normative fairness criterion in all settings, so fairness becomes a constrained multi-objective optimization requiring explicit policy choices about which criterion to prioritize for a given deployment context.

^fn8

<sup>9</sup> **Federated learning and fairness**: Google’s public Gboard trial illustrated training across devices without centralizing raw examples ([[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/04 The Responsible Fleet/16 Responsible AI/V2-16.15 Source References#^ref-hard2018federated|Hard et al. 2018]]). Model updates alone do not necessarily reveal the demographic labels and outcomes needed for a fairness audit, so the communication cost, privacy protection, and statistical uncertainty of whatever evidence path replaces them must be evaluated for that design.

^fn9

<sup>10</sup> **CCPA (California Consumer Privacy Act)**: CCPA grants California residents rights over covered personal information, including a deletion right subject to statutory exceptions ([[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/04 The Responsible Fleet/16 Responsible AI/V2-16.15 Source References#^ref-california2023ccpa|California Legislature 2023]]). For ML serving infrastructure, deletion requests create an architectural constraint that must be planned from the data pipeline forward, because raw-record deletion does not automatically remove a record’s influence from trained model parameters.

^fn10

<sup>11</sup> **COMPAS (Correctional Offender Management Profiling for Alternative Sanctions)**: ProPublica’s 2016 analysis found Black defendants were falsely flagged as future criminals at nearly twice the rate of white defendants (45 percent vs. 23 percent false positive rate) ([[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/04 The Responsible Fleet/16 Responsible AI/V2-16.15 Source References#^ref-angwin2016|Angwin et al. 2016]]). The proprietary, black-box nature of the system limited full inspection of model internals and training data, making the source of the observed error-rate disparity harder to investigate. Error-rate parity and predictive parity are distinct criteria, so the evaluation must name the criterion rather than treating one rate as a complete fairness verdict.

^fn11

<sup>12</sup> **GDPR (General Data Protection Regulation) Article 22**: Article 22 gives data subjects protections against decisions based solely on automated processing that produce legal or similarly significant effects, with exceptions for contract necessity, authorization by law, or explicit consent plus safeguards ([[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/04 The Responsible Fleet/16 Responsible AI/V2-16.15 Source References#^ref-gdpr2016|European Parliament and Council of the European Union 2016]]). Related notice and access provisions, including Article 15, require meaningful information about the logic involved for qualifying automated decision-making, and EDPB guidance treats those duties as part of a broader profiling and automated-decision governance regime ([[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/04 The Responsible Fleet/16 Responsible AI/V2-16.15 Source References#^ref-edpb2018automatedProfiling|European Data Protection Board 2018]]).

^fn12

<sup>13</sup> **Saliency maps**: Gradient-based explanation that highlights which input regions most influenced a prediction by computing a backward pass, the same infrastructure used for training. Saliency methods are often cheaper than perturbation-heavy or subset-enumeration methods, making them attractive for edge and mobile deployments when the model supports gradients. The trade-off is that raw gradients can be noisy and may highlight input artifacts rather than meaningful features, so smoothing or repeated-gradient variants trade additional compute for more stable visualizations.

^fn13

<sup>14</sup> **Integrated gradients**: An attribution method that integrates gradients along the straight path from a baseline input to the actual input. Sensitivity requires a nonzero attribution when a single changed feature accounts for a changed prediction; implementation invariance requires identical attributions for functionally equivalent models ([[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/04 The Responsible Fleet/16 Responsible AI/V2-16.15 Source References#^ref-sundararajan2017axiomatic|Sundararajan et al. 2017]]). The authors argued that most earlier attribution methods violate at least one of these axioms. These properties concern the exact method, while numerical integration introduces an approximation whose accuracy depends on the sampling of the path. The deployment must therefore budget repeated gradient evaluations and check approximation error rather than treating a fixed step count as sufficient for every model and input.

^fn14

<sup>15</sup> **GradCAM (gradient-weighted class activation mapping)**: Selvaraju et al. ([[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/04 The Responsible Fleet/16 Responsible AI/V2-16.15 Source References#^ref-selvaraju2017|2017]]) generalized Class Activation Mapping to any convolutional neural network (CNN) architecture by using gradients flowing into the final convolutional layer to produce spatial importance maps. Because it reuses gradient computation, GradCAM can be far cheaper than perturbation-heavy explanation methods, but whether it fits real-time medical imaging or autonomous-vehicle pipelines depends on the model, hardware, batch size, and explanation-frequency budget.

^fn15

<sup>16</sup> **Shapley values**: From cooperative game theory, Shapley values fairly distribute a payoff among players based on marginal contributions across all possible orderings. In ML explainability, features are “players” and the prediction is the “payoff.” The mathematical guarantees (efficiency, symmetry, null player) make SHAP a widely used attribution framework with clear axiomatic appeal, but exact subset enumeration grows as $2^{{n_{\text{feat}}}}$ for ${n_{\text{feat}}}$ features, which is why production systems often rely on model-specific algorithms, sampling approximations, caching, or asynchronous explanation paths ([[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/04 The Responsible Fleet/16 Responsible AI/V2-16.15 Source References#^ref-lundberg2017unified|Lundberg and Lee 2017]]).

^fn16

<sup>17</sup> **Abstention**: The practice of refusing predictions when confidence falls below a threshold. Abstention can reduce high-risk errors, but only by shifting some cases to fallback infrastructure such as human reviewers, rule-based defaults, or escalation queues. A driving system must select a validated fallback, such as a minimal-risk maneuver; handing control to a driver is viable only if a driver is present, ready, and has sufficient response time. Medical AI may route ambiguous cases to specialist review, subject to its own queue-capacity and response-time budget.

^fn17

<sup>18</sup> **RLHF (reinforcement learning from human feedback)**: Christiano et al. ([[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/04 The Responsible Fleet/16 Responsible AI/V2-16.15 Source References#^ref-christiano2017deep|2017]]) demonstrated deep reinforcement learning from human preferences by training agents from non-expert comparisons between trajectory segments. In the LLM setting, Ouyang et al. ([[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/04 The Responsible Fleet/16 Responsible AI/V2-16.15 Source References#^ref-ouyang2022instructgpt|2022]]) adapted this pipeline for InstructGPT: supervised fine-tuning from demonstrations, reward-model training from ranked model outputs, and reinforcement-learning fine-tuning against the learned reward model. The systems cost is an additional human-feedback data pipeline, reward-model training and evaluation, and governance over rater instructions; the representativeness of the rater pool and labeling policy shapes whose preferences the model internalizes.

^fn18

<sup>19</sup> **Reward hacking**: When an AI system maximizes its reward function through unintended means that violate designer intent. A hypothetical cleaning robot rewarded for collected dirt might create messes it can clean up, illustrating how a proxy can reward behavior contrary to the intended task. Specification gaming is an overlapping term; the gap may lie in the stated objective or in its implementation, so neither term is restricted to software bugs. For production ML systems, reward hacking manifests subtly, as in recommendation models that maximize engagement by promoting addictive content, or chatbots that maximize helpfulness ratings by being sycophantic rather than accurate. The failure mode scales with model capability.

^fn19

<sup>20</sup> **Model cards**: Proposed by Mitchell et al. ([[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/04 The Responsible Fleet/16 Responsible AI/V2-16.15 Source References#^ref-mitchell2019|2019]]) as standardized documentation accompanying trained ML models. Cards report evaluation across relevant groups, intended uses, and known limitations. Production systems must link this documentation to the actual deployed model and its provenance; a card alone neither establishes that linkage nor supplies every record required for an audit.

^fn20

<sup>21</sup> **Datasheets for datasets**: Proposed by Gebru et al. ([[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/04 The Responsible Fleet/16 Responsible AI/V2-16.15 Source References#^ref-gebru2021|2021]]), modeled after electronics component datasheets that specify operating conditions and tolerances. Each datasheet documents a dataset’s motivation, composition, collection process, and recommended uses. For ML pipelines, this provenance and coverage information helps engineers identify assumptions and design evaluations. A datasheet does not itself validate a model’s operating envelope or predict all deployment failures.

^fn21

<sup>22</sup> **Automation bias**: Inappropriate reliance on automated recommendations, including acceptance of erroneous outputs. Its prevalence depends on the task, interface, workload, and users’ ability to check the output. Strong average performance can encourage reliance without making rare errors easier to recognize. Uncertainty displays, selective review, or requests for justification should be evaluated for their effects on correction rates, workload, and response time rather than assumed to improve safety.

^fn22

<sup>23</sup> **Red teaming**: From Cold War military simulations where the “Red Team” acted as the Soviet adversary to probe US defenses. In Responsible AI, red teaming is the adversarial audit phase: specialized teams (hackers, linguists, ethicists) deliberately probe models for jailbreaks, bias, or toxic outputs before deployment. This discovery process identifies the long-tail risks that standard unit tests cannot catch.

^fn23

<sup>24</sup> **Data Nutrition Project**: A nonprofit initiative that develops standardized dataset documentation modeled on nutrition labels, summarizing provenance, composition, distribution, and known issues for datasets used in ML pipelines. The project’s labels make dataset risks legible to downstream engineers who lack access to the original collection process, which bridges the organizational fragmentation described here.

^fn24

<sup>25</sup> **MLOps (machine learning operations)**: ML CI/CD pipelines must handle data versioning, training reproducibility, and A/B testing of algorithm changes beyond traditional software concerns. High-velocity ML organizations may deploy model or configuration changes far more often than responsible-AI reviews can be completed manually, creating a velocity gap in which deployment cycles measured in hours compete against ethical validation requiring days or weeks. This tension explains why responsible AI commitments present at the prototype stage can be deprioritized as systems scale.

^fn25
