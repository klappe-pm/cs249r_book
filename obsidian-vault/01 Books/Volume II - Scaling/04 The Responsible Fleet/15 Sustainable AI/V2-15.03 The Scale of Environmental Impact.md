# V2-15.03 The Scale of Environmental Impact

[[Home|Home]] · [[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/Volume 2 Overview|Volume Overview]] · [[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/04 The Responsible Fleet/15 Sustainable AI/V2-15 Sustainable AI|V2-15 Sustainable AI]]

^sec-sustainable-ai-scale-environmental-impact-ac9a

A training run measured in megawatt-hours is hard to reason about, because few engineers carry an intuition for what a megawatt-hour of grid electricity costs the atmosphere. The introduction quoted GPT-3’s training emissions as one of the costs that scaling imposes (**[[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/00 Introduction/01 Introduction/V2-01.04 AI Scaling Laws#^sec-vol2-intro-sustainability-cost-implications-0473|Sustainability and cost implications]]**). The derivation behind that figure converts the run’s energy into emissions with a grid factor and then into a familiar anchor, the CO<sub>2</sub> of a trans-Atlantic flight, which turns an abstract number into one with physical stakes.

**Napkin Math 15.1: The carbon cost of training**

**Problem**: A team trains a large model (GPT-3 size), treating 1,287 MWh as facility electricity for this comparison. How much CO<sub>2</sub> is emitted under the stated grid factor, and how does that compare to a trans-Atlantic flight?

**Math**:

1.  **Energy**: Training energy consumption is 1,287 MWh = 1,287,000 kWh.
2.  Carbon intensity (US average): $\approx$ 0.429 kg/kWh.
3.  Total Emissions: Training emissions are 1,287,000 kWh $\times$ 0.429 kg/kWh $\approx$ 552,123 kg.
4.  Comparison:
    - One passenger, NY to London (round trip): $\approx$ 1000 kg.
    - Ratio: 552,123 kg / 1000 kg = 552.1.

**Systems insight**: Under these assumptions, a single training run emits as much carbon as hundreds of trans-Atlantic passenger round trips. The calculation holds facility energy fixed, so changing the grid factor changes the attributed emissions. The same energy on a hydro-heavy grid would emit a small fraction as much, the placement lever that notebook [15.2](https://mlsysbook.ai/vol2/15_sustainable_ai/15_sustainable_ai.html#nbk-sustainable-ai-geography-carbon) quantifies.

The same run’s emissions depend on where its electricity comes from. For a fixed workload, the grid emissions factor and the facility overhead together determine the attributed carbon, and the placement calculations separate those effects, starting with the grid factor alone.

The introduction previewed this geographic factor as a spread of roughly 41× between hydro- and coal-dominated grids. Notebook [15.2](https://mlsysbook.ai/vol2/15_sustainable_ai/15_sustainable_ai.html#nbk-sustainable-ai-geography-carbon) derives that spread by holding facility energy constant and varying only the grid factor.

**Napkin Math 15.2: The geography of carbon**

**Problem**: A team is choosing a data center for a 10,000 MWh training run, comparing Site A in Quebec (hydropower, 20 g/kWh $\text{CO}_2$) against Site B in Poland (coal-heavy, 820 g/kWh $\text{CO}_2$). Both factors are rounded annual grid averages on the same emissions boundary, the values [[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/04 The Responsible Fleet/15 Sustainable AI/V2-15.06 Carbon Footprint Calculation#^fig-carbon-intensity|figure 7]] later applies across nine regions; they are not hourly or marginal signals. How does the location affect a model’s carbon footprint?

**Math**: Carbon = Energy $\times$ Grid Intensity.

1.  Site A emissions: 10,000,000 kWh $\times$ 20 g/kWh = 200,000,000 g = 200 t $\text{CO}_2$.
2.  Site B emissions: 10,000,000 kWh $\times$ 820 g/kWh = 8,200,000,000 g = 8200 t $\text{CO}_2$.
3.  Ratio: 8200 t / 200 t = 41× difference.

**Systems insight**: The Quebec-versus-Poland pair gives a 41× difference in attributed emissions at the same facility energy. This comparison isolates the grid factor; it does not rank placement against an unmeasured algorithmic change. A fleet decision must compare feasible sites and schedules using both workload energy and the emissions associated with supplying it.

The ratio holds only at equal facility electricity and these annual-average factors. A second gain from waiting for cleaner hours, the temporal lever of [[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/04 The Responsible Fleet/15 Sustainable AI/V2-15.10 Mitigation Strategies#^sec-sustainable-ai-carbonaware-scheduling-c2db|section 15.9.4]], therefore cannot simply be multiplied onto it; the joint schedule must be evaluated as one decision.

The GPT-3 household-year calculation above anchors the workload scale. The fleet-scale consequence is larger. The International Energy Agency (IEA) projects global data-center electricity consumption to reach about 945 TWh by 2030, just under 3 percent of global electricity demand, with AI-accelerated servers driving much of the growth ([[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/04 The Responsible Fleet/15 Sustainable AI/V2-15.15 Source References#^ref-iea2025energyai|International Energy Agency 2025]]).[[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/04 The Responsible Fleet/15 Sustainable AI/V2-15.16 Footnotes#^fn2|²]] Historical studies document rapid growth in training compute ([[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/04 The Responsible Fleet/15 Sustainable AI/V2-15.15 Source References#^ref-sevilla2022|Sevilla et al. 2022]]), but a compute multiplier is not an energy multiplier without a matching efficiency measurement.

Beyond direct energy consumption, AI systems drive environmental impact through hardware manufacturing and resource consumption. Producing processors and their supporting equipment requires materials and industrial processes whose impacts precede the first training run.[[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/04 The Responsible Fleet/15 Sustainable AI/V2-15.16 Footnotes#^fn3|³]] At the other end of the hardware’s life, retired equipment joins the electronic-waste stream whose scale [[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/04 The Responsible Fleet/15 Sustainable AI/V2-15.09 Hardware Lifecycle and E-Waste#^sec-sustainable-ai-disposal-phase-b4f1|section 15.8.5]] quantifies. Rising workload requirements can motivate accelerator replacement before physical failure; whether replacement creates waste depends on the equipment’s reuse and disposal path.[[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/04 The Responsible Fleet/15 Sustainable AI/V2-15.16 Footnotes#^fn4|⁴]]

These environmental challenges are part of the energy ceiling, not an add-on to it, because the communities that host land, water, grid capacity, and waste streams also carry part of the system cost. These scale calculations turn sustainability into an allocation problem. AI progress creates benefits in one part of the system while assigning electricity, water, land, and disposal costs to another, so environmental responsibility must be treated as part of systems design rather than as postdeployment reporting ([[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/04 The Responsible Fleet/15 Sustainable AI/V2-15.11 Policy, Regulation, and the Path Forward#^sec-sustainable-ai-environmental-justice-responsible-development-3923|section 15.10.4]] examines how site selection distributes these burdens across communities). A static snapshot of today’s footprint still understates the engineering challenge, however, because the computational demand driving these electricity, water, and hardware budgets is compounding far faster than hardware efficiency can absorb it.

**Self-Check: Question**

1.  A 2,000 MWh training run can be placed at a site whose annual-average grid factor is 400 g CO<sub>2</sub> per kWh or at one whose factor is 20 g CO<sub>2</sub> per kWh, with facility electricity held equal. Which conclusion follows?

    1.  The cleaner site also cuts the run’s energy twentyfold, because low-carbon grids deliver electricity more efficiently.
    2.  Attributed emissions fall from about 800 tonnes to about 40 tonnes, a twentyfold difference that isolates the grid factor at equal facility energy.
    3.  This comparison alone ranks placement against an algorithmic change, because both reduce emissions.
    4.  Waiting for cleaner hours at the cleaner site would multiply the saving by a further twentyfold.

2.  True or False: Because training compute for notable models has grown by many orders of magnitude, training energy has grown by the same multiplier.

3.  An operator reports its AI fleet’s environmental footprint as metered electricity multiplied by the grid factor. What does this account leave out?

    1.  Nothing, because operational electricity is the only environmental cost of AI hardware.
    2.  Only facility overhead, because grid factors already include the cost of manufacturing.
    3.  A fixed disposal charge per device, because retiring any accelerator necessarily creates e-waste.
    4.  Manufacturing impacts that precede the first training run, such as embodied carbon, ultrapure water, and specialty gases, and the land, water, and grid capacity that host communities supply.

4.  A Quebec-versus-Poland comparison shows a 41-fold emissions difference for the same 10,000 MWh run, and carbon-aware scheduling can also shift work to cleaner hours. Explain why the geographic and temporal gains cannot simply be multiplied, and how the decision should be evaluated.
