# V2-15.04 Exponential Growth Vs. Physical Constraints

[[Home|Home]] · [[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/Volume 2 Overview|Volume Overview]] · [[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/04 The Responsible Fleet/15 Sustainable AI/V2-15 Sustainable AI|V2-15 Sustainable AI]]

^sec-sustainable-ai-exponential-growth-vs-physical-constraints-0f4e

Between 2012 and 2023, the training compute of frontier models expanded from roughly $4.7 \times 10^{17}$ FLOPs for AlexNet to $2 \times 10^{25}$ for GPT-4-class systems, an increase of about 45 million times in 11 years that outpaced hardware efficiency gains by orders of magnitude. Sevilla et al. ([[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/04 The Responsible Fleet/15 Sustainable AI/V2-15.15 Source References#^ref-sevilla2022|2022]]) estimate that the training compute of notable machine learning models doubled roughly every six months after 2010, far faster than the two-year cadence historically associated with transistor scaling. Extending the introductory series (**[[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/00 Introduction/01 Introduction/V2-01.02 The Scale Moment#^fig-training-compute-trend|Where Training-Compute Growth Comes From]]**) across 468 notable runs through 2026 ([[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/04 The Responsible Fleet/15 Sustainable AI/V2-15.15 Source References#^ref-epochai2026notable|Epoch AI 2026]]), [[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/04 The Responsible Fleet/15 Sustainable AI/V2-15.04 Exponential Growth Vs. Physical Constraints#^fig-ai-compute-growth|figure 1]] shows that the 69 runs flagged as frontier at release grew about 4.6× per year, doubling every 5.5 months and carrying the post-2012 span past nine orders of magnitude to $1 \times 10^{27}$ FLOPs. Larger models, longer training runs, and rising serving volume each add to that total work through a different mechanism. Energy per operation can fall while total fleet energy rises; the outcome depends on how much useful work the fleet performs and how efficiently it performs that work.

Red circles mark the 69 runs flagged as frontier at release; their least-squares trend grows about 4.6× per year (95 percent confidence interval 4.3× to 4.9×), doubling every 5.5 months. ^fig-ai-compute-growth

Historically, semiconductor scaling combined greater transistor density with lower switching energy. Moore’s law described the growth in transistor count, while Dennard scaling explained how reducing dimensions and voltage together could preserve power density. Transistor count alone did not guarantee better energy efficiency. Once voltage scaling encountered leakage and other device limits, a denser chip could no longer assume proportional power savings.[[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/04 The Responsible Fleet/15 Sustainable AI/V2-15.16 Footnotes#^fn5|⁵]] Further efficiency gains require improvements in devices, architecture, and the work performed by the application.

At a fixed accounting boundary, energy depends on total work divided by realized work per joule. Improvements in execution efficiency reduce IT energy, and PUE then adds the facility overhead on top of that IT energy. Algorithmic changes can also reduce the work required to meet a quality target. These effects must be measured together rather than inferred from a historical compute curve.

Per-job efficiency and total demand can move in opposite directions. Fleet energy is the energy per completed job multiplied by the number of completed jobs, so a fleet forecast must state both factors, including the workload mix. Cheaper jobs can raise the job count faster than they lower the energy of each one. If energy per task falls tenfold while task volume rises a hundredfold, fleet energy rises tenfold. This rebound is a second way demand can outrun efficiency, distinct from the scaling-driven growth described at the start of the chapter. Scaling-driven growth concerns how much compute each frontier model requires as scaling laws push quality upward; rebound concerns how many tasks run once each task becomes cheaper. The mitigation strategies of [[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/04 The Responsible Fleet/15 Sustainable AI/V2-15.10 Mitigation Strategies#^sec-sustainable-ai-part-iii-implementation-solutions-232d|section 15.9]] treat rebound under its historical name, the Jevons paradox.

### Power Envelopes and the Energy Wall

^sec-sustainable-ai-energy-wall

A short training burst can trip substation breakers or overwhelm chillers even when its cumulative megawatt-hour budget is modest, whereas a month-long job on a smaller cluster may consume far more total energy while staying safely inside its power envelope. The energy account divides work by realized work per joule and applies facility overhead at the boundary. Physical infrastructure, however, imposes a separate instantaneous power constraint: the grid connection, electrical distribution, and cooling system limit how much active fleet can operate safely at once.

The two limits obey different arithmetic ([[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/04 The Responsible Fleet/15 Sustainable AI/V2-15.04 Exponential Growth Vs. Physical Constraints#^fig-energy-wall|figure 2]]). The power limit is instantaneous. A facility cannot draw more than its grid feed (${P_{\text{facility}}}\le {P_{\text{grid}}}$). Because IT power is facility power divided by PUE, that feed limit also caps the number of servers active at once (${N}\le {P_{\text{IT}}}/ {P_{\text{server}}}$) regardless of how long a job runs. The energy account is cumulative, integrating average power over wall-clock time to give run energy (${E_{\text{total}}} = {P_{\text{avg}}}\times {T_{\text{job}}}$). Exceeding electrical or thermal capacity can force power limiting, throttling, or protective shutdown. Cumulative energy instead enters the electricity and emissions accounts, which also depend on tariffs and the applicable grid emissions factors.

![[90 Assets/Volume II/15 Sustainable AI/Power Energy Boundaries f5f086361c.svg]]

Figure 2: **Power and Energy Boundaries**: Substation grid feeds and facility cooling capacities impose instantaneous power caps on active accelerator concurrency. Integrating instantaneous power over wall-clock duration yields cumulative run energy; instantaneous power constraints and cumulative energy budgets represent distinct physical limits.

^fig-energy-wall

The energy wall is therefore a workload-specific feasibility problem, not a universal multiplier. Engineers must fit the required work, deadline, and quality target within available power and cooling, then calculate energy over the actual runtime. More efficient hardware helps only to the extent that the workload uses its efficiency and the resulting demand remains within those constraints. At frontier scale, the binding question is whether enough power can be delivered to the cluster at all.

**Lighthouse 15.1: Archetype A (GPT-4/Llama-3): The energy wall**

Training at the scale of Archetype A (GPT-4/Llama-3) exposes the power problem most sharply. At frontier scale, a single 25,000-GPU cluster is roughly 24 times the 1,024-GPU reference cluster used throughout this book. At 700 W per chip, it requires 17.5 MW of accelerator power before server, network, and cooling overhead. The constraint is physical, not financial: it is a grid capacity problem. Organizations operating Archetype A workloads may need dedicated power infrastructure or regions with excess renewable energy. Carbon-aware scheduling (shifting nonurgent work toward cleaner grid hours) and geographic optimization (placing workloads where power, cooling, and carbon constraints fit the job) therefore become fleet decisions as consequential as capacity planning.

That accelerator draw alone, before server, network, and cooling overhead, makes grid capacity a condition of deployment. Even when the grid can supply that sustained average power, the synchronized execution of a training job swings the load up and down across the cluster in unison.

### Data Center Grid Dynamics

^sec-sustainable-ai-grid-dynamics

When thousands of accelerators enter a collective communication barrier simultaneously and then resume matrix multiplication together, their power draw steps down and up in lockstep across the data hall. Unlike conventional cloud workloads whose independent requests smooth out across racks, synchronized training phases correlate power swings and push those transients toward the electrical grid interface.

**War Story 15.1: When the grid became the bottleneck (2022)**

**Context**: In July 2022, the Government of Ireland published its *Government Statement on the Role of Data Centres in Ireland’s Enterprise Strategy*, addressing electricity constraints on further development in a country where data centers had become a substantial source of demand ([[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/04 The Responsible Fleet/15 Sustainable AI/V2-15.15 Source References#^ref-ireland2022datacentres|Government of Ireland 2022]]; [[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/04 The Responsible Fleet/15 Sustainable AI/V2-15.15 Source References#^ref-iea2024datacentresireland|International Energy Agency 2024]]).

**Mechanism**: Demand growth outpaced grid expansion. The statement reported that Dublin needed significant transmission reinforcement before it could accommodate new data-center power requests.

**Impact**: Grid connection capacity became the gating input for proposed Dublin facilities. New requests had to wait for transmission reinforcement or look to other regions, while contracted projects and existing sites continued to operate.

**Fix**: The statement prioritized network reinforcement, suitable regional locations, and development aligned with additional renewable supply. These were proposed responses to the constraint, not evidence that operators had already resolved it.

**Systems lesson**: Sustainable AI is constrained by interconnection, geography, and power-system adequacy. Efficient accelerators help, but the fleet cannot scale faster than the grid that feeds it, moving the binding constraint from the chip to the substation.

Training phases can change accelerator demand rapidly, but a device-side load step does not propagate unchanged to the utility connection. Its amplitude depends on per-device power, the number of coordinated workers, and computation-communication overlap. Local regulators, decoupling capacitors, power converters, and uninterruptible power supply (UPS) controls shape the upstream response. **Energy buffering** supplies or absorbs energy during a load change. A buffer discharges when the cluster steps up into computation and recharges when it falls back into communication, so the utility connection sees a smaller swing than the accelerators draw. Its location, capacity, and control response must match the electrical boundary being protected.

↩︎ Transient filtering by voltage regulators, decoupling capacitors, and UPS controls is established in **[[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/01 The Fleet/02 Compute Infrastructure/V2-02.07 The Rack#^sec-compute-power-delivery-transients|Power delivery networks and transient dynamics]]**.

### Training-Scale Energy Concentration

^sec-sustainable-ai-training-scale-energy-concentration

The grid and siting constraints become severe because large training campaigns concentrate energy demand into long, synchronized runs. The GPT-3 anchor established the total scale; the new systems concern is its temporal concentration and the additional energy from distributed-training communication.[[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/04 The Responsible Fleet/15 Sustainable AI/V2-15.16 Footnotes#^fn6|⁶]]

Concentration in time is what separates a training campaign from the same energy spread across a year of ordinary load. A frontier run holds the 17.5 MW accelerator draw of the Archetype A cluster, before servers, network, and cooling, for the weeks or months the run lasts. Its synchronized computation and communication phases swing that draw together across the cluster ([[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/04 The Responsible Fleet/15 Sustainable AI/V2-15.04 Exponential Growth Vs. Physical Constraints#^sec-sustainable-ai-grid-dynamics|section 15.3.2]]). The facility must therefore provision for the sustained peak rather than for the annual average, and the grid sees one large, correlated load rather than many small independent ones. Large generative models intensify the problem when successive generations increase parameter counts, token budgets, or both.

Over the decade after 2016, those successive generations pushed single-run pretraining energy past the daily output of a utility-scale power plant ([[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/04 The Responsible Fleet/15 Sustainable AI/V2-15.04 Exponential Growth Vs. Physical Constraints#^fig-frontier-training-energy|figure 3]]). Across 122 standalone runs that document both cluster hardware and training duration, Epoch AI models peak facility power from accelerator count, chip thermal design power (TDP), a 1.82× server-chassis allowance (the ratio of peak server power to accelerator TDP), and facility PUE, then integrates that draw over wall-clock runtime ([[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/04 The Responsible Fleet/15 Sustainable AI/V2-15.15 Source References#^ref-epochai2026notable|Epoch AI 2026]]; [[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/04 The Responsible Fleet/15 Sustainable AI/V2-15.15 Source References#^ref-cottier2024rising|Cottier et al. 2024]]). Along the 23 frontier pretraining runs in that set, upper-bound energy grew about 3.0× per year, slower than the 4.6× compute trend because hardware advances raised modeled FLOPs per joule by roughly 1.5× per year. Even with those efficiency gains, frontier runs crossed 24,000 MWh, one full day of continuous output from a 1 GW power plant at rated capacity, and reached roughly 240,000 MWh for Grok 3 before commercial nondisclosure began withholding duration data on later releases.

^fig-frontier-training-energy

Placing builder-reported anchors alongside those modeled upper bounds shows why an energy claim is incomplete without its measurement boundary. For GPT-3, Patterson et al. ([[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/04 The Responsible Fleet/15 Sustainable AI/V2-15.15 Source References#^ref-patterson2021carbon|2021]]) report 1,287 MWh at the facility meter from OpenAI’s 330 W per-GPU server average and a 1.10 PUE, whereas Epoch AI’s peak-capacity model yields roughly 1,800 MWh (1.4× higher) because its 1.82× server-chassis allowance exceeds metered host and network draw. On TPU clusters where communication and I/O also pull sustained chip draw well below TDP, peak-capacity estimates exceed metered energy by two to three times ([[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/04 The Responsible Fleet/15 Sustainable AI/V2-15.15 Source References#^ref-patterson2021carbon|Patterson et al. 2021]]; [[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/04 The Responsible Fleet/15 Sustainable AI/V2-15.15 Source References#^ref-cottier2024rising|Cottier et al. 2024]]).

Boundary scope widens the gap further for Llama 3.1-405B, whose model card ([[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/04 The Responsible Fleet/15 Sustainable AI/V2-15.15 Source References#^ref-meta2024llama31card|Meta AI 2024]]) reports 30.84M H100 GPU-hours at 700 W chip TDP ([[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/04 The Responsible Fleet/15 Sustainable AI/V2-15.15 Source References#^ref-dubey2024llama|Dubey et al. 2024]]); multiplying the two implies 21,588 MWh at the accelerator-TDP boundary before host CPUs, network fabric, and cooling, or roughly 24,000 MWh once a 1.10 PUE is included. Epoch AI’s full-facility upper bound reaches roughly 48,000 MWh, or 2.2× the accelerator-TDP product, and that ratio is the product of two factors. A 1.97× server-and-PUE multiplier lifts the accelerator-TDP product for Meta’s reported GPU-hours to roughly 43,000 MWh, and a 1.14× longer inferred wall-clock window (35.1M GPU-hours) lifts that figure to the Epoch bound. Even at the narrower accelerator-plus-PUE boundary, a single frontier training run now concentrates roughly a rated gigawatt-day of electricity into a few months of continuous operation.

The scaling laws introduced in **[[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/00 Introduction/01 Introduction/V2-01.04 AI Scaling Laws#^sec-vol2-intro-ai-scaling-laws-a043|AI Scaling Laws]]** explain why that concentration keeps growing. Within the ranges studied by Kaplan et al. ([[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/04 The Responsible Fleet/15 Sustainable AI/V2-15.15 Source References#^ref-kaplan2020scaling|2020]]), test loss falls along smooth power laws in compute, dataset tokens, and parameters ([[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/04 The Responsible Fleet/15 Sustainable AI/V2-15.04 Exponential Growth Vs. Physical Constraints#^fig-model-scaling|figure 4]]), and for an energy account the small compute exponent is the quantity of interest. It means each fixed fractional reduction in loss costs a multiplicative increase in the resource. Because required compute scales as the ratio of the old loss to the new loss raised to the power 20, lowering compute-limited loss by 10 percent takes 8.2× the compute, and the next 10 percent costs the same multiple again. The diminishing return in principle [[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/02 Distributed ML/V2 Distributed ML Principles#^pri-universal-scaling|9]] therefore arrives at the facility as a multiplicative increase in training energy unless realized FLOPs per joule improve at the same pace. Within these studied regimes, quality gains were purchasable with hardware and data, which is why compute demand has outrun hardware efficiency. Beyond training, high-volume deployed systems such as large-scale recommender systems and generative services require continuous inference at scale, consuming energy even after training completes. The cumulative energy burden therefore depends on both the one-time training run and the sustained query volume that follows deployment.

![[90 Assets/Volume II/15 Sustainable AI/Cell 16 Output 1 2ec8417d64.png]]

Figure 4: **Model Scaling Laws**: In the ranges studied by Kaplan et al. ([[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/04 The Responsible Fleet/15 Sustainable AI/V2-15.15 Source References#^ref-kaplan2020scaling|2020]]), increasing model size, dataset size, and compute consistently reduced test loss. Each curve is normalized to its loss at the smallest studied value, so on these log-log axes its slope is its exponent; the dashed staircase on the compute curve marks the compute multiple that buys each successive fixed fractional reduction in loss. The figure reproduces source-paper notation, where ${N}$ denotes parameter count and ${C_{\text{min}}}$ denotes compute; elsewhere this book uses ${P}$ for parameter count and ${O}$ for operation count. These scaling laws explain why larger models, more data, and increased compute became attractive design choices within that regime.

^fig-model-scaling

Building out the clusters required by this concentration adds emissions before an operator runs a single training step, so expanding the fleet increases manufacturing impact even when each new accelerator uses less energy per useful task. An operator’s inventory records that fabrication under Scope 3, the category for supply-chain emissions rather than on-site generation or purchased electricity ([[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/04 The Responsible Fleet/15 Sustainable AI/V2-15.06 Carbon Footprint Calculation#^sec-sustainable-ai-embodied-carbon-assessment-9de0|section 15.5.5]] quantifies the manufacturing term, and [[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/04 The Responsible Fleet/15 Sustainable AI/V2-15.06 Carbon Footprint Calculation#^sec-sustainable-ai-comprehensive-carbon-accounting-methodologies-62fc|section 15.5.2]] assigns it to reporting scopes). Bounding any of these operational or embodied costs requires decomposing facility power into component-level models and direct telemetry, with every estimate declaring what it counts so that two estimates can be compared.

**Self-Check: Question**

1.  An inference optimization cuts energy per task by a factor of 4, and the lower cost raises task volume by a factor of 20, with the workload mix unchanged. What happens to fleet energy?

    1.  It falls by a factor of 4, because per-task efficiency sets fleet energy.
    2.  It stays the same, because efficiency and demand cancel.
    3.  It rises by a factor of 5, because fleet energy is energy per task multiplied by the number of tasks.
    4.  It rises by a factor of 80, because the efficiency gain and the demand growth compound.

2.  A team plans a three-day run that needs 120 MW of accelerator power at once in a facility with an 80 MW grid feed. They argue the run fits because its total energy is less than the facility uses in a month. What is wrong with the argument?

    1.  The power limit is instantaneous, so the feed caps how many servers can be active at once regardless of run length; the plan must lower concurrency and accept a longer run, or obtain more deliverable power.
    2.  Nothing, because over a month an energy budget and a power cap are interchangeable.
    3.  The run fails only on cost, because energy drawn above the feed is billed at a peak tariff.
    4.  The only error is omitting PUE; adding facility overhead would show that the run’s energy exceeds the monthly budget.

3.  Synchronized training phases swing accelerator draw together across thousands of devices, and a battery or UPS stage that supplies or absorbs energy during each load change provides energy \_\_\_\_, whose location, capacity, and control response must match the electrical boundary being protected.

4.  True or False: Because a frontier training run’s yearly energy is a modest share of a region’s consumption, the facility can be provisioned for that run’s annual-average power.

5.  In the regime studied by Kaplan et al., each 10 percent reduction in compute-limited loss costs about 8.2 times the compute. Explain what two successive 10 percent reductions imply for training energy, and what would have to hold for energy not to grow by the same factor.
