# V2-15.07 Data Center Energy and Resource Consumption

[[Home|Home]] · [[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/Volume 2 Overview|Volume Overview]] · [[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/04 The Responsible Fleet/15 Sustainable AI/V2-15 Sustainable AI|V2-15 Sustainable AI]]

^sec-sustainable-ai-data-center-energy-consumption-patterns-09c0

A training cluster can fit its accelerator power budget yet exceed its facility budget once support systems and cooling enter the account. Whether a facility can host the cluster at all is a feasibility question; the sustainability question is whether a change in power delivery or cooling reduces lifecycle cost while preserving that feasible operating envelope. The relevant comparison includes annual energy, local water use, equipment lifetime, and the energy wasted when cooling failures interrupt useful work.

Facility sustainability therefore has to be read as a chain of constraints rather than as a single PUE number. Persistent megawatt demand sets the grid and emissions exposure. Power delivery determines how much electricity becomes useful IT load. Cooling sets the facility overhead and the water drawn to remove heat. Water use then determines whether a technically efficient site is locally sustainable. The physical mechanisms below matter here because each assigns losses or resource use to a different accounting boundary.

### Data Center Energy and AI Workloads

^sec-sustainable-ai-data-center-energy-ai-workloads-b1a8

Even when an engineer halves the energy of a single model, campus electricity demand still climbs if new data-center capacity and serving traffic expand faster than per-job efficiency. At facility scale, the optimization target is no longer a single model in isolation but the aggregate demand, facility overhead, and grid context surrounding every watt of IT power. The same IEA *Energy and AI* analysis behind the 2030 electricity projection cited at the chapter’s opening also tracks emissions, a different quantity. It estimated emissions from data-center electricity use at about 180 Mt at the time of the analysis and projected roughly 300 Mt by 2035 in its Base Case, still below 1.5 percent of total energy-sector emissions ([[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/04 The Responsible Fleet/15 Sustainable AI/V2-15.15 Source References#^ref-iea2025energyai|International Energy Agency 2025]]).[[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/04 The Responsible Fleet/15 Sustainable AI/V2-15.16 Footnotes#^fn12|¹²]] The energy burden grows with data-center capacity, training workloads, and inference demand ([[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/04 The Responsible Fleet/15 Sustainable AI/V2-15.15 Source References#^ref-patterson2021carbon|Patterson et al. 2021]]), so component efficiency must be evaluated against fleet expansion ([[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/04 The Responsible Fleet/15 Sustainable AI/V2-15.15 Source References#^ref-thompson2021|Thompson et al. 2021]]; [[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/04 The Responsible Fleet/15 Sustainable AI/V2-15.15 Source References#^ref-dodge2022|Dodge et al. 2022]]).

The relevant facility quantity is persistent megawatt-class load, not the model name alone. Hyperscale campuses house large fleets of accelerator servers and hold loads of 100 MW or more for years at a time.[[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/04 The Responsible Fleet/15 Sustainable AI/V2-15.16 Footnotes#^fn13|¹³]] A newer accelerator lowers a job’s energy only when that workload realizes the efficiency gain. Even then, lower energy per job need not reduce campus demand if the fleet uses the released capacity to perform more work.

Whether that demand keeps growing depends on the race between workload growth and efficiency. Masanet et al. ([[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/04 The Responsible Fleet/15 Sustainable AI/V2-15.15 Source References#^ref-masanet2020energy|2020]]) show that efficiency gains held global data-center energy nearly flat through the 2010s even as computing output grew several-fold. Sustained AI demand growth can erode that offset, which is why the IEA projection cited earlier rises rather than remaining flat.

Beyond computational demands, cooling can account for 30–40 percent of facility energy in the conventional air-cooled data-center designs surveyed by Ebrahimi et al. ([[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/04 The Responsible Fleet/15 Sustainable AI/V2-15.15 Source References#^ref-ebrahimi2014|2014]]). This is a design-regime range, not a constant for modern hyperscale or liquid-cooled facilities; [[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/04 The Responsible Fleet/15 Sustainable AI/V2-15.07 Data Center Energy and Resource Consumption#^sec-sustainable-ai-cooling|section 15.6.3]] derives the site-specific overhead through PUE and the cooling architecture.

The United States alone illustrates how cloud and AI infrastructure can reshape national energy planning. US data center electricity consumption more than tripled from 58 TWh in 2014 to 192 TWh in 2024, reaching 4.7 percent of national supply, according to the Lawrence Berkeley National Laboratory (LBNL) estimates that [[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/04 The Responsible Fleet/15 Sustainable AI/V2-15.07 Data Center Energy and Resource Consumption#^fig-energy-gap|figure 9]] plots ([[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/04 The Responsible Fleet/15 Sustainable AI/V2-15.15 Source References#^ref-shehabi2024united|Shehabi et al. 2024]]; [[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/04 The Responsible Fleet/15 Sustainable AI/V2-15.15 Source References#^ref-shehabi2026united|Smith et al. 2026]]). LBNL’s reference case projects 649 TWh for 2030, about 11.8 percent of US electricity, inside a scenario range of 521 to 843 TWh ([[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/04 The Responsible Fleet/15 Sustainable AI/V2-15.15 Source References#^ref-shehabi2026united|Smith et al. 2026]]). These scenarios describe possible demand, not a measured capacity ceiling. They motivate evaluating efficiency gains alongside the generation, transmission, and connection capacity needed to serve that demand.

Figure 9: **The Energy Gap**: US data center electricity consumption more than tripled from 58 TWh (2014) to 192 TWh (2024). Every kilowatt-hour in those national totals begins as electricity delivered to a rack and leaves as heat that must be removed from it. Delivering 120 kW to a single cabinet (the rated draw of one GB200 NVL72 rack) starts at the substation, and every conversion stage between the grid and the chip takes its share along the way.

### Power Delivery

^sec-sustainable-ai-power-delivery

Capacity planning fails when engineers budget only accelerator TDP, because transformers, uninterruptible power supplies, rack distribution, and voltage regulators each dissipate part of the incoming grid power as heat before a single watt reaches the silicon. **[[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/01 The Fleet/02 Compute Infrastructure/V2-02.07 The Rack#^sec-compute-power-wall|Power and thermal envelopes]]** traced that electrical path from the utility feed to the voltage regulator, and [[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/04 The Responsible Fleet/15 Sustainable AI/V2-15.05 Energy Measurement and Modeling#^eq-pue|equation 2]] measures the facility overhead along it. For a sustainability account, the question is where along that path energy and reliability margin are lost, and which boundary carries each loss. The first constraint sits outside the building: large grid-supplied ML facilities need sufficient substation and utility-interconnection capacity. Internal distribution improvements cannot raise that import limit, and on-site generation or storage adds a separate supply whose capacity, duration, and reliability must enter the design.

*Substation lead time can run 4× longer than GPU procurement.*

**Systems Perspective 15.2: The interconnection queue**

Deploying 10,000 GPUs and deploying the electrical substation required to power their IT load (9.6 MW, after the 7 MW GPU subtotal is expanded for rack support power) operate on different timelines. In this planning comparison, GPU procurement takes 6 months, while permitting, engineering, procurement, construction, and grid connection for a new 10+ MW substation take 24 months. The infrastructure path is therefore 4× longer than the accelerator path. Actual schedules depend on the site, utility, equipment availability, and approvals, so the ratio illustrates a dependency rather than a universal lead time.

**Systems insight**: In this comparison, power delivery determines when the fleet can begin useful work even if the accelerators arrive on schedule. The US generation-and-storage interconnection queue is distinct from a data center’s load-connection request; neither queue total directly measures available capacity for a new ML facility. Procurement must therefore follow a site-specific power-delivery schedule. Otherwise, delivered silicon can remain “electrically stranded” while the facility waits for its connection or transformer.

Inside the facility, every conversion stage turns part of the delivered power into heat. Along the path traced in **[[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/01 The Fleet/02 Compute Infrastructure/V2-02.07 The Rack#^sec-compute-power-wall|Power and thermal envelopes]]** (**[[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/01 The Fleet/02 Compute Infrastructure/V2-02.07 The Rack#^fig-power-path|The Power Delivery Path]]**), the utility transformer loses about 1–2 percent, an online double-conversion UPS 3–5 percent (or 1–2 percent in eco mode), the power distribution unit (PDU) 2–3 percent, and baseboard voltage regulation 5–8 percent. Compounded, these stages dissipate roughly 9–17 percent of grid power before it reaches a transistor, and server power supplies add their own conversion loss on top. The accounting boundary decides where each loss appears. Losses upstream of the IT boundary, such as the transformer, UPS, and facility distribution, raise PUE. Server power supplies and baseboard voltage regulators sit inside the measured IT load, and their heat must be removed by the same cooling system that serves the accelerators.

Higher-voltage distribution is one of the few design changes that shrinks this overhead directly, because the same power at a higher voltage needs less current, and resistive distribution losses ${I}^2{R}$ fall with the square of that current. The 48 V rack distribution of **[[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/01 The Fleet/02 Compute Infrastructure/V2-02.07 The Rack#^sec-compute-power-delivery-transients|Power delivery networks and transient dynamics]]** applies that principle, and its realized saving depends on conductor design and the added conversion stages.

At ML rack power densities, the difference between 95 percent and 98 percent distribution efficiency is meaningful. Using 33 kW as a reference rack load, a first-order estimate applies the difference between those efficiencies as 3 percent of that load, yielding approximately 0.99 kW per rack. Across 300 racks, the estimated reduction is 297 kW, equivalent to roughly 36 GPU nodes or 9 four-node racks under this power mix. Sustaining that reduction over a 3-year lifecycle at \$0.07/kWh gives estimated electricity savings of ~\$546,361. An exact fixed-load comparison divides delivered power by each efficiency before subtracting input powers. The released capacity can support more work or lower consumption; spending it on additional nodes does not preserve the same electricity saving.

**[[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/01 The Fleet/02 Compute Infrastructure/V2-02.07 The Rack#^sec-compute-rack|The Rack]]** sizes the power envelope of a four-node DGX H100 rack; the same rack also shows which parts of a budget are IT load and which are facility overhead. A rack-attributed power budget itemizes more than GPU TDP ([[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/04 The Responsible Fleet/15 Sustainable AI/V2-15.07 Data Center Energy and Resource Consumption#^tbl-rack-power|table 7]]). In this scenario, host, fabric, and equipment-side conversion losses belong to the IT load and sum to the IT subtotal; the row below it is an illustrative non-IT allocation at the stated PUE. A facility calculation multiplies the IT subtotal by its own PUE rather than reusing that allocation, and facility-side conversion losses are covered by PUE and must not be added again.

Evaluating DGX H100 power allocation ([[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/04 The Responsible Fleet/15 Sustainable AI/V2-15.07 Data Center Energy and Resource Consumption#^tbl-rack-power|table 7]]) reveals that 32 GPUs at 700 W each deliver 22.4 kW. Host, fabric, and conversion losses, however, raise the IT subtotal to 30.8 kW, about 38 percent above the GPU subtotal. That IT subtotal is the load to multiply by a facility’s PUE; the table’s final row is one such allocation, which brings the rack-attributed total to about 50 percent above the GPU subtotal. Infrastructure planners who size their facility based on GPU TDP alone will underestimate the electrical load and may discover during commissioning that their power capacity is insufficient.

Synchronized training also changes the shape of the load. The correlated power swings introduced with grid dynamics ([[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/04 The Responsible Fleet/15 Sustainable AI/V2-15.04 Exponential Growth Vs. Physical Constraints#^sec-sustainable-ai-grid-dynamics|section 15.3.2]]) now appear inside the delivery chain, where coordinated computation and communication phases align across thousands of accelerators. **[[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/01 The Fleet/02 Compute Infrastructure/V2-02.07 The Rack#^sec-compute-power-delivery-transients|Power delivery networks and transient dynamics]]** analyzes how decoupling capacitors, voltage regulators, and UPS controls filter these **synchronous transients** before they reach the utility connection. The same section estimates the aggregate ramp, in gigawatts per second, when a training cluster steps from communication to computation in unison. That estimate describes a load-side scenario rather than a measured utility input, and its sustainability consequence appears at the interconnection. Rack- or facility-level energy buffering ([[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/04 The Responsible Fleet/15 Sustainable AI/V2-15.04 Exponential Growth Vs. Physical Constraints#^sec-sustainable-ai-grid-dynamics|section 15.3.2]]), sized to the measured energy deficit, the energy a step draws above what the utility connection supplies during the ramp, rather than to a fixed allowance per rack, lets the grid see a smoother load. A well-characterized, controllable load is easier both to connect and to schedule against grid conditions.

**Checkpoint 15.3: Power delivery physics**

Verify the data center power path:

**Load correlation**: explain why synchronous ML training produces more correlated load changes at the facility connection than independent web traffic.

**Loss accounting**: identify which conversion losses in the delivery chain raise PUE and which remain inside the measured IT load.

**Power budgeting**: use [[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/04 The Responsible Fleet/15 Sustainable AI/V2-15.07 Data Center Energy and Resource Consumption#^tbl-rack-power|table 7]] to explain why a node with 8 GPUs at 700 W each must be budgeted well above its 5.6 kW GPU subtotal.

**AC-DC conversion**: identify the conversion-stage efficiencies, distribution losses, load profile, and reliability requirements needed to compare rack-level and server-level conversion at the same delivered IT load.

At the largest scales, the data center’s power draw represents a significant fraction of the local electrical grid’s capacity. A 100,000-GPU cluster, roughly a hundred times the book’s 1,024-GPU reference cluster, at 700 W per GPU has a 70 MW accelerator subtotal. Rack-profile support power, however, raises the IT load to 96.2 MW before PUE. With PUE, the total facility draw approaches 107.8 MW, the load of a small city. Such installations require dedicated feeds from the electrical grid, often with purpose-built substations and transmission lines. The substation lead time of 24 months in the interconnection-queue perspective above makes power availability one of the longest-lead-time constraints in building ML infrastructure.

At large-cluster scale, the same delivery limit becomes a grid-procurement problem rather than an electricity bill. The problem worsens because compute-optimal allocation (**[[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/00 Introduction/01 Introduction/V2-01.04 AI Scaling Laws#^sec-vol2-intro-computeoptimal-resource-allocation-541a|Compute-optimal resource allocation]]**) grows parameters and training tokens together, so training work, which scales with their product, grows roughly with the square of model size, and energy follows it unless realized FLOPs per joule improve.

The electricity consumed by a single large-model training campaign can become nonnegligible relative to the output of a power plant. A 100 MW training facility operating at full capacity for one year consumes 876 GWh. A 100 MW wind farm cannot match that figure (at a 30 percent capacity factor, the share of its rated output it delivers on average, a wind farm produces about 262.8 GWh per year, so the training facility would require the equivalent of approximately 3.3 large wind farms). Once power is available, its carbon intensity determines the emissions attributed to each megawatt-hour under the chosen accounting method, and at facility scale that factor becomes a siting decision that persists for the life of the building. Low-carbon electricity can coincide with favorable prices, but price and emissions factor are separate tests that a siting comparison must evaluate alongside power availability and the workload’s location constraints.

### Cooling Energy, Heat Rejection, and Water

^sec-sustainable-ai-cooling

Nearly every watt of electrical power that enters a server cabinet leaves as heat, so a dense rack acts as a continuous heater whose thermal output must be removed at the rate it consumes electricity or the silicon throttles and shuts down. **[[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/01 The Fleet/02 Compute Infrastructure/V2-02.07 The Rack#^sec-compute-cooling|Cooling class and heat removal]]** established how air and liquid loops carry that heat and which cooling class a given rack density requires; for a sustainability account, the question is what rejecting that heat to the outside environment costs in additional facility electricity and local water.

PUE ([[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/04 The Responsible Fleet/15 Sustainable AI/V2-15.05 Energy Measurement and Modeling#^eq-pue|equation 2]]) captures facility fans, chillers, and facility-side conversion losses as overhead on top of the IT load. If facility cooling consumes 3–8 percent of IT power, that contribution alone adds 0.03–0.08 to PUE, and facility-side electrical losses and other non-IT loads add to it. Server fans, by contrast, lie within the IT load itself, so their energy sits in the denominator as well as the facility total, and reducing server-fan power changes the IT load rather than producing the same PUE reduction. Cooling choices must therefore be compared using total facility energy for the same useful work, not PUE alone.

The cooling class of **[[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/01 The Fleet/02 Compute Infrastructure/V2-02.07 The Rack#^sec-compute-cooling|Cooling class and heat removal]]** settles thermodynamic feasibility, that the proposed cooling medium maintains die temperatures and component serviceability under peak workload. Lifecycle economics then compares installed capital expenditure, pumping and fan parasitics, water consumption, maintenance overhead, and equipment lifespan under the same computational load. A higher-density liquid-cooled installation may reduce operational energy, but its capital payback period cannot be inferred from rack power alone.

**Napkin Math 15.7: The cooling overhead**

Consider a 1,024-GPU cluster of H100s at 700 W each. To isolate the cooling choice, this simplified calculation treats accelerator power as the entire IT load and assigns all non-IT overhead to cooling; a procurement estimate must restore host, memory, network, and storage support before applying PUE.

- IT power: 1,024 $\times$ 700 W = 716.8 kW
- Overhead removed: with the $(\text{PUE}-1)$ overhead arithmetic of notebook [15.3](https://mlsysbook.ai/vol2/15_sustainable_ai/15_sustainable_ai.html#nbk-sustainable-ai-pue-cost-cooling), moving from air cooling (PUE 1.55) to liquid cooling (PUE 1.08) removes 716.8 kW $\times$ 0.47 = 336.9 kW of continuous power.

**Savings**: At \$0.07/kWh, the annual savings are approximately \$206,585. Over a 3-year hardware lifecycle, the cooling savings alone total \$619,754, which is the figure to set against the installed capital cost of the liquid-cooling system when computing its payback period.

The final link in the cooling chain is **heat rejection**: getting heat from the facility water loop to the outside environment. A cooling tower sprays warm water over a fill medium, packing that spreads the water into thin films to enlarge its contact with the air, and uses evaporation to carry heat into the atmosphere. Evaporating a kilogram of water removes its latent heat of vaporization, 2,260 kJ/kg. Warming the water without evaporating it, called sensible heating, instead depends on both the specific heat capacity, 4.18 kJ/kg/K, and the permitted temperature rise. Evaporation can therefore carry substantial heat with a small mass flow, but the evaporated water must be replaced.

A 10 MW data center with evaporative cooling towers can consume well over 100 million liters of water per year, depending on climate and cooling design, which has become a significant concern in water-stressed regions. At the evaporative WUE of 1.8 L/kWh used earlier, continuous operation consumes about 158 million liters per year, roughly 350–415 household-years of water use under the earlier annual household baseline. As ML data centers grow to 100 MW and beyond, their water footprint becomes a meaningful factor in local resource planning.

Dry coolers use fans to blow air over a radiator without evaporating cooling water. They avoid that direct water consumption, but the added fan electricity carries its own water burden at the generating plant, in a different watershed from the facility ([[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/04 The Responsible Fleet/15 Sustainable AI/V2-15.07 Data Center Energy and Resource Consumption#^fig-datacenter-water-accounts|figure 10]]). Heat rejection depends on the temperature difference between coolant and ambient air, so hot weather can reduce capacity or require more fan power. Hybrid designs use dry cooling when conditions permit and evaporative assistance when needed, trading water consumption against electrical demand and thermal margin.

![[90 Assets/Volume II/15 Sustainable AI/Datacenter Water Accounts 2de2f1a894.svg]]

Figure 10: **Direct and Indirect Water Accounts**: An evaporative cooling tower (a) rejects heat by consuming water withdrawn from the local watershed, returning only its blowdown. A dry cooler (b) draws no local cooling water but spends more electricity on fans, and generating that electricity consumes water in the power plant’s watershed. Water is therefore a separate, location-weighted account traded against energy. The schematic shows which flows exist, not their sizes.

^fig-datacenter-water-accounts

**Waste heat reuse** treats thermal output as a resource. A **district heating** connection supplies a municipal heat network, while **industrial coupling** directs recoverable heat toward a compatible process. The benefit depends on the temperature, timing, and distance of the receiving demand, along with any pumping or heat-pump energy needed to supply it. Useful recovery can displace another heat source, although it does not eliminate the fleet’s electrical consumption[[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/04 The Responsible Fleet/15 Sustainable AI/V2-15.16 Footnotes#^fn14|¹⁴]] ([[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/04 The Responsible Fleet/15 Sustainable AI/V2-15.15 Source References#^ref-ebrahimi2014|Ebrahimi et al. 2014]]). A liquid-cooling loop returning water at 50–65 degrees Celsius supplies low-grade heat that may serve nearby district heating, greenhouses, or other suitable sinks. Usability depends on the required supply temperature, seasonal demand, and transport losses. Because municipal heating networks often require supply temperatures above 70 degrees Celsius, the facility may need an electric heat pump to raise the coolant temperature before delivery. If an illustrative 10 MW thermal stream has an 80–90 percent recovery fraction, 8–9 MW is recovered before accounting for heat-pump electricity. Heat-pump output includes that electrical input and must be accounted for separately.

Crediting useful heat does not reduce PUE below 1.0 because PUE compares total facility electrical input against IT electrical input, and exporting heat downstream does not reduce the electricity the building draws from the grid. Exported heat is instead reported through energy reuse effectiveness (ERE), which subtracts the reused energy from total facility energy before dividing by IT energy, or through a separate net-energy balance.

The viability of waste heat reuse depends on the proximity of heat consumers. Urban data centers, despite their higher land and electricity costs, are often better positioned for waste heat reuse than remote facilities because they are close to residential and commercial heating loads. The result is a counterintuitive economic optimization: a data center in a Nordic city may have higher electricity costs but lower net operating costs after waste heat revenues, compared to a remote facility with cheaper electricity but no heat consumers nearby.

A cooling failure also costs energy, because the run repeats the work done since its last checkpoint. Rerunning 30 to 60 minutes on all 10,000 accelerators of a training cluster at rated power repeats about 3.5–7 MWh of accelerator energy, before host and facility overhead. Power drawn by idle accelerators during the outage adds to that total. ML goodput, which counts only useful fleet output, discounts the repeated work, but the energy account still pays for it. **[[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/01 The Fleet/02 Compute Infrastructure/V2-02.07 The Rack#^sec-compute-infrastructure-physical-security-environmental-controls-7132|Facility reliability and environmental controls]]** develops the cooling failure modes and their blast radius, and **[[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/02 Distributed ML/07 Fault Tolerance/V2-07.09 Checkpointing and Progress Preservation#^sec-fault-tolerance-reliability-reliability-checkpoint-restart-fundamentals-0ac2|Checkpointing and Progress Preservation]]** develops the checkpoint recovery that bounds the rollback. Together, power-delivery losses and heat rejection set the facility overhead on every kilowatt-hour entering the building; how that electricity budget divides between one-time model training and continuous production serving determines where workload-level optimization must focus next.
