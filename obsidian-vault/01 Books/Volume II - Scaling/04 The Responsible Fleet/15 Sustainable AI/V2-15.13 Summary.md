# V2-15.13 Summary

[[Home|Home]] · [[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/Volume 2 Overview|Volume Overview]] · [[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/04 The Responsible Fleet/15 Sustainable AI/V2-15 Sustainable AI|V2-15 Sustainable AI]]

^sec-sustainable-ai-summary-8cec

The lifecycle boundary connects training, serving, embodied carbon, grid mix, hardware lifetime, and demand growth. Sustainable AI asks whether the machine learning fleet can operate within available energy, water, and material resources even after its models and infrastructure have been optimized.

The measurement contract makes comparisons meaningful by fixing useful work, required quality, system boundaries, attribution, time, location, and uncertainty. Operational and embodied carbon accounting then identify which lifecycle term an intervention can change. Memory traffic, idle equipment, cooling, and power delivery can all consume energy beyond useful arithmetic; lower FLOP counts alone do not establish lower environmental impact.

The Fleet Law connects execution time to the fleet energy account. A step’s energy is approximately its average fleet power times ${T_{\text{step}}}({N})$ ([[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/04 The Responsible Fleet/15 Sustainable AI/V2-15.08 Training Vs. Inference Energy Analysis#^eq-sustainable-ai-step-energy|equation 13]]), so exposed communication and synchronization extend the interval over which the system draws power. Recovery adds further work outside successful steps. The C³ terms therefore matter to both performance and sustainability, although the power trace determines their energy cost. PUE extends an IT-energy estimate to the facility boundary, the grid factor attributes operational emissions, and embodied carbon adds the hardware lifecycle burden rather than converting operating joules.

Mitigation follows that diagnosis. Algorithm and hardware changes must reduce measured resource use while preserving quality, latency, and reliability; scheduling must account for grid intensity and the cost of moving work; hardware replacement must weigh operational savings against new manufacturing emissions. Water availability and the distribution of environmental burdens also matter when choosing where and how a fleet can grow.

**Key Takeaways: Efficiency alone is not enough**

- **Power is a hard ceiling**: A fleet cannot compute past the megawatts, cooling, water, and grid capacity its site can supply. Sustainability is therefore an existence constraint on model scale, not a public-relations layer around an otherwise finished architecture.
- **A footprint is only as good as its boundary**: An energy or carbon figure supports a comparison only when it names the functional unit, system boundary, attribution rule, carbon signal, and time and location. The boundary alone can more than double the energy reported for the same prompt.
- **Demand can outrun efficiency**: Lower energy per query does not ensure lower fleet energy use. At fixed work per query, total energy rises when query volume grows by a larger factor than per-query energy falls, so efficiency targets need an absolute resource budget.
- **Memory traffic can dominate serving energy**: During bandwidth-bound autoregressive decode, accelerators can draw power while waiting for weights and state. Quantization, batching, or memory-optimized hardware help sustainability only when they reduce measured energy at the required quality, latency, and reliability.
- **Carbon starts before boot**: Hardware manufacturing creates emissions before the first query runs, and its lifecycle share rises on clean grids and under low utilization. Keeping or replacing hardware therefore requires comparing new manufacturing emissions with operational savings over the same future service.
- **Location changes the footprint**: Moving flexible work to a cleaner grid can reduce operational carbon only when the saving exceeds transfer, restart, and idle-capacity emissions. Placement must also preserve deadlines, data access, capacity, and reliability; regional intensity ratios alone do not establish the net benefit.
- **Water is its own account**: Evaporative cooling saves facility energy by consuming local water, while dry cooling saves that water by spending electricity whose generation consumes water elsewhere. Report withdrawal and consumption, direct and indirect, weighted by the stress of each watershed.

^callout-takeaways--15-13-57d727f1

Sustainability changes the scale at which efficiency must be judged. A reduction per query is a local result; the fleet result depends on how many queries run, where they run, and what hardware their service requires over time. Two forces push demand past efficiency. Scaling-driven growth is the first, as scaling laws (principle [[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/02 Distributed ML/V2 Distributed ML Principles#^pri-universal-scaling|9]]) price each further quality gain in more compute per model. The Jevons paradox of AI (principle [[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/04 The Responsible Fleet/V2 Responsible Fleet Principles#^pri-jevons-paradox|25]]) is the second, as cheaper work invites more of it. Both make the distinction between efficiency per task and absolute demand consequential, because efficiency creates room within a resource budget while demand determines how much of that room is consumed. Deployment decisions therefore need both a useful-work efficiency target and an absolute resource budget.

**What’s Next: From sustainability to responsibility**

A fleet that is secure, robust, and within its power budget is not automatically fit to deploy. Security, robustness, and sustainability each answer a technical question about what a production system can support. [[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/04 The Responsible Fleet/15 Sustainable AI/V2-15.11 Policy, Regulation, and the Path Forward#^sec-sustainable-ai-environmental-justice-responsible-development-3923|Section 15.10.4]] already showed that a feasible fleet can place its water, grid, and waste burdens on communities that see little of its benefit. The model’s decisions raise the same question about the people they act on. An accurate, fast model can still concentrate its errors among people with little power to challenge them. Responsible AI therefore asks who receives the system’s benefits, who bears its costs, and how an affected person can contest a decision. The carbon inventory and lifecycle account built here become one input to the assurance case that answers it. They supply evidence for that judgment; they do not settle whose interests the fleet should serve.
