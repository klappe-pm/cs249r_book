# V2-15.11 Policy, Regulation, and the Path Forward

[[Home|Home]] · [[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/Volume 2 Overview|Volume Overview]] · [[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/04 The Responsible Fleet/15 Sustainable AI/V2-15 Sustainable AI|V2-15 Sustainable AI]]

^sec-sustainable-ai-policy-regulation-9668

Electricity prices reward some efficiency gains, but they need not capture water stress, manufacturing emissions, or local environmental burdens. A training cluster can reduce its electricity bill by moving to a cheaper region while increasing its emissions. If the bill omits that environmental cost, a cost-minimizing placement decision may favor the higher-emissions site. Reporting requirements expose the trade-off; carbon pricing, procurement conditions, and other policy instruments can change the incentives. Their effect depends on which activities they cover and how organizations implement them.

For a systems reader, policy is the control plane that changes the objective function. Reporting rules make hidden energy and embodied-carbon costs measurable, carbon pricing turns location into a scheduling variable, and procurement standards make lifecycle accounting part of infrastructure design. The regulatory mechanisms in [[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/04 The Responsible Fleet/15 Sustainable AI/V2-15.11 Policy, Regulation, and the Path Forward#^sec-sustainable-ai-regulatory-mechanisms-global-coordination-4180|section 15.10.1]] matter because they determine which optimizations become economically rational at fleet scale.

### Regulatory Mechanisms

^sec-sustainable-ai-regulatory-mechanisms-global-coordination-4180

An organization training a frontier model above $10^{25}$ FLOPs or operating a large European subsidiary can face legal requirements to disclose energy consumption and value-chain emissions, yet neither law requires metering every individual AI job. Reporting rules act at different organizational boundaries: the EU AI Act[[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/04 The Responsible Fleet/15 Sustainable AI/V2-15.16 Footnotes#^fn24|²⁴]] includes model-level technical-documentation obligations, while the **Corporate Sustainability Reporting Directive (CSRD)**[[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/04 The Responsible Fleet/15 Sustainable AI/V2-15.16 Footnotes#^fn25|²⁵]] addresses sustainability reporting by covered companies. Model energy records can inform both, so engineers must identify the covered entity or model, applicable dates and exemptions, and the evidence the reporting method requires.

Policy can also impose a priced resource constraint. A hypothetical emissions cap for AI operations would specify tonnes of CO<sub>2</sub>e and allow trading of unused emissions allowances, following the general logic of **emissions trading systems (ETS)**.[[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/04 The Responsible Fleet/15 Sustainable AI/V2-15.16 Footnotes#^fn26|²⁶]] A power cap in MW or an energy budget in MWh would constrain different quantities and must not be called the same credit. The EU **Carbon Border Adjustment Mechanism**, by contrast, applies to embedded emissions in specified imported goods and electricity; it does not generally price an AI service by its compute location.

Incentives and procurement conditions can change which designs organizations choose. A grant can support efficiency research, while a procurement contract can require declared energy measurements or lifecycle evidence. These conditions need measurable criteria rather than an undefined “green” label. Scope 3 emissions also depend on whose inventory is being prepared: purchased hardware and outsourced cloud services enter the customer’s value-chain account, while the supplier classifies its own activities within its organizational boundary. The same physical emissions must not be added twice merely because two organizations report them.

### Industry Self-Regulation and Standards

^sec-sustainable-ai-selfregulation-02df

A cloud provider can announce “100 percent annual renewable matching” while its data centers still draw fossil-generated electricity every night when solar output drops to zero. Voluntary commitments and common standards supplement government requirements, but an announced target is not evidence of a measured reduction. Annual renewable-energy matching and 24/7 carbon-free energy (CFE) address different time resolutions: hourly matching makes periods of unmatched demand visible, while the resulting physical emissions still depend on the generation and grid context ([[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/04 The Responsible Fleet/15 Sustainable AI/V2-15.15 Source References#^ref-monyei2018|Monyei and Jenkins 2018]]). Evaluating a provider’s progress therefore requires the reported method and results, rather than merely its announced target.

Internal carbon pricing gives emissions a weight in project decisions. A “shadow price” lets an organization compare alternatives using both financial cost and attributed emissions; its influence depends on the price, accounting boundary, and whether decision makers use it. Measurement tools supply inputs to that comparison. Projects like **CodeCarbon** and **ML CO<sub>2</sub> Impact** let developers estimate model carbon footprints within their workflows ([[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/04 The Responsible Fleet/15 Sustainable AI/V2-15.15 Source References#^ref-schmidt2021codecarbon|Schmidt et al. 2021]]; [[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/04 The Responsible Fleet/15 Sustainable AI/V2-15.15 Source References#^ref-lacoste2019quantifying|Lacoste et al. 2019]]), but the estimates still need declared power, location, and allocation assumptions.

Standardized benchmarks provide evidence for particular efficiency claims. **MLCommons** defines MLPerf power-measurement protocols that connect system energy with workload performance under declared conditions. The Green Software Foundation’s Software Carbon Intensity specification addresses a different boundary, combining operational and allocated embodied emissions per functional unit. A benchmark result or a calculation method is not itself an independent sustainability audit. Accountability requires checking the reported inputs, scope, and comparison against the claim being made.

### Public Engagement and Transparency

^sec-sustainable-ai-public-engagement-9850

A sustainability report that trumpets a 50 percent drop in energy per query while omitting a fourfold expansion in total cluster capacity, or that leaves hardware manufacturing out of its inventory, masks a growing footprint behind a selective boundary. Misleading presentation of environmental performance, or **greenwashing**,[[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/04 The Responsible Fleet/15 Sustainable AI/V2-15.16 Footnotes#^fn27|²⁷]] can arise from selective boundaries or omitted trade-offs as well as unsupported offset claims. Effective AI sustainability governance requires evidence that matches the claim, combining workload telemetry with facility measurements and supply-chain inventories. Runtime power counters alone cannot establish Scope 1–3 completeness or the environmental costs of manufacturing.

Pledge-style disclosure is useful only when it creates auditable data rather than reputational cover. The **Montréal Carbon Pledge**, originally a commitment by institutional investors to measure and disclose carbon footprints annually, is a useful model precisely because its value lies in the disclosed data, not the pledge itself. The same standard applies to an AI organization, whose sustainability claims are only as credible as the workload-level measurements behind them.

### Environmental Justice and Responsible Development

^sec-sustainable-ai-environmental-justice-responsible-development-3923

When a hyperscale data center is sited in a drought-prone agricultural basin or beside a neighborhood served by peaking gas turbines, local residents absorb the water stress and air pollution while the model’s outputs serve users thousands of miles away. Transparency establishes the evidence base for those impacts; environmental justice asks how the burdens and benefits revealed by that evidence are distributed. Site selection is where much of that distribution is decided, which makes it an environmental-justice decision rather than a facilities decision alone. Environmental sustainability extends ML systems responsibility from model behavior to ecological stewardship ([[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/04 The Responsible Fleet/15 Sustainable AI/V2-15.15 Source References#^ref-vinuesa2020|Vinuesa et al. 2020]]). The computational resources required for AI development concentrate environmental costs on specific communities while distributing benefits unequally across global populations. Data centers impose both grid demand and cooling-water demand, and the local burden depends on the energy mix, watershed, and competing regional uses.

That geographic concentration creates environmental-justice risks that align with broader responsible AI frameworks.[[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/04 The Responsible Fleet/15 Sustainable AI/V2-15.16 Footnotes#^fn28|²⁸]] The fairness claim here is narrow and systems-specific. An ML fleet allocates benefits, electricity demand, water use, land pressure, and e-waste across different communities. Communities hosting AI infrastructure can bear disproportionate environmental burdens while having limited access to AI’s economic benefits.

These distributional concerns fall into four groups ([[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/04 The Responsible Fleet/15 Sustainable AI/V2-15.11 Policy, Regulation, and the Path Forward#^fig-ethical-ai|figure 21]]). Environmental justice concerns local water withdrawal and grid strain. Intergenerational equity concerns atmospheric $\text{CO}_2$ persistence and long-lived infrastructure. Resource competition concerns agricultural and municipal claims on land, water, and minerals. The transparency gap concerns undisclosed per-model energy, carbon, and water records. Engineering acts most directly on the last group, because auditable telemetry is what allows the other three to be assessed at all; the measurement contract of [[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/04 The Responsible Fleet/15 Sustainable AI/V2-15.05 Energy Measurement and Modeling#^sec-sustainable-ai-part-ii-measurement-assessment-fb0b|section 15.4]] supplies that telemetry.

Impact assessments and community participation make these trade-offs visible before a site is committed. Any claim that such an assessment is legally required must identify the applicable jurisdiction, rule, and project scope. Where siting and governance bound the burden of current facilities, longer-term research targets the architectural and measurement limits that make AI workloads so resource-intensive in the first place.

### Future Research Directions

^sec-sustainable-ai-future-challenges-58e2

Even after a team applies every scheduling, cooling, and compression lever in this chapter, conventional accelerators still spend most of their dynamic energy shuttling weights and activations back and forth across memory buses, while most teams still lack real-time, per-job carbon telemetry. The research agenda therefore follows the same engineering logic as the mitigation framework: reduce data movement, close the measurement gap, and avoid redundant computation. **Non-von Neumann computing architectures**,[[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/04 The Responsible Fleet/15 Sustainable AI/V2-15.16 Footnotes#^fn29|²⁹]] including **neuromorphic computing** and **in-memory computing**, can reduce selected transfers, extending the event-driven and spiking designs of [[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/04 The Responsible Fleet/15 Sustainable AI/V2-15.10 Mitigation Strategies#^sec-sustainable-ai-theoretical-efficiency-limits-d880|section 15.9.2.2]] from avoided work to avoided movement. Evaluate the remaining arithmetic, conversion, control, and peripheral energy at the required quality; eliminating one cost does not eliminate the whole system account.

A critical implementation barrier is the “measurement gap”: AI teams need standardized, workload-level reporting of energy use and carbon emissions rather than relying only on coarse proxy metrics ([[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/04 The Responsible Fleet/15 Sustainable AI/V2-15.15 Source References#^ref-henderson2020towards|Henderson et al. 2020]]). Coarse methods often rely on proxies such as GPU-hours multiplied by average grid intensity, which fail to capture the real-world dynamics required by reporting regimes that care about time, location, and workload boundary. Developing and standardizing granular, real-time energy and carbon accounting tools is essential for both compliance and effective optimization.

A second research direction reduces redundant computation before it reaches the accelerator. Data selection, model design, and training procedure all affect the environmental cost of learning ([[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/04 The Responsible Fleet/15 Sustainable AI/V2-15.15 Source References#^ref-wu2022sustainable|Wu et al. 2022]]). Smarter data sampling, active learning, and data valuation can reduce work spent on low-value examples, but the resulting model quality and coverage must be measured rather than assumed. An integrated approach combining algorithmic efficiency, hardware innovation, renewable energy adoption, and transparent governance is necessary to align AI development with environmental constraints.

Reducing redundant computation can improve operational efficiency, but compliance and environmental benefit require their own evidence. A sustainable design must show that the saved work survives the complete lifecycle account and the resulting deployment scale. Incorrect boundaries or assumptions can otherwise turn a local improvement into a larger total footprint.
