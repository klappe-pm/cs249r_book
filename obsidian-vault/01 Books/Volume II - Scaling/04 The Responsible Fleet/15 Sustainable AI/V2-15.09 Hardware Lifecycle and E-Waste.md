# V2-15.09 Hardware Lifecycle and E-Waste

[[Home|Home]] · [[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/Volume 2 Overview|Volume Overview]] · [[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/04 The Responsible Fleet/15 Sustainable AI/V2-15 Sustainable AI|V2-15 Sustainable AI]]

^sec-sustainable-ai-hardware-lifecycle-environmental-assessment-66ee

The environmental cost of an AI accelerator begins long before its first FLOP is calculated ([[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/04 The Responsible Fleet/15 Sustainable AI/V2-15.15 Source References#^ref-gupta2022chasing|Gupta et al. 2022]]; [[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/04 The Responsible Fleet/15 Sustainable AI/V2-15.15 Source References#^ref-luccioni2023estimating|Luccioni et al. 2023]]; [[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/04 The Responsible Fleet/15 Sustainable AI/V2-15.15 Source References#^ref-nvidia2025hgxh100pcf|NVIDIA Corporation 2025]]; [[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/04 The Responsible Fleet/15 Sustainable AI/V2-15.15 Source References#^ref-schneider2025life|Schneider et al. 2025]]). The per-H100 manufacturing footprint quantified in [[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/04 The Responsible Fleet/15 Sustainable AI/V2-15.06 Carbon Footprint Calculation#^sec-sustainable-ai-embodied-carbon-assessment-9de0|section 15.5.5]] is incurred before the device is ever powered on.[[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/04 The Responsible Fleet/15 Sustainable AI/V2-15.16 Footnotes#^fn17|¹⁷]] A training fleet of a few thousand such accelerators therefore carries a manufacturing debt of the same order as the operational emissions of a GPT-3-scale training run on the US-average grid (about 552,123 kg).

A comprehensive **life cycle assessment (LCA)** quantifies the cumulative environmental impact across four key phases: design, manufacture, use, and disposal. LCA can reveal manufacturing as a major share of lifecycle impact, making it a critical sustainability lever that operational efficiency improvements alone cannot address. The first published LCA of an AI accelerator, covering five generations of Google’s TPU across all four phases, introduced compute carbon intensity, the lifecycle emissions per unit of computation. Unlike performance per watt, the metric charges each chip for its own manufacturing, and by that measure the TPU v6e is about 3× better than the TPU v4i ([[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/04 The Responsible Fleet/15 Sustainable AI/V2-15.15 Source References#^ref-schneider2025life|Schneider et al. 2025]]).

These four phases organize the same emissions by time rather than by activity or reporting scope, as the second column of [[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/04 The Responsible Fleet/15 Sustainable AI/V2-15.06 Carbon Footprint Calculation#^tbl-sustainable-ai-emission-views|table 5]] records, so a phase total must never be added to an activity or scope total. Evaluated across that full timeline, life cycle assessments can show that discarding functional hardware purely for modest efficiency gains causes more environmental harm through embodied carbon than it saves in operational power. The tipping point where new hardware becomes environmentally justified depends on utilization and grid intensity, a break-even worked in [[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/04 The Responsible Fleet/15 Sustainable AI/V2-15.09 Hardware Lifecycle and E-Waste#^sec-sustainable-ai-extending-hardware-lifespan-b028|section 15.8.6]].

Each of the four lifecycle stages adds its burden through a distinct physical mechanism ([[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/04 The Responsible Fleet/15 Sustainable AI/V2-15.09 Hardware Lifecycle and E-Waste#^fig-ai-lca|figure 14]]). System design fixes much of the lifetime load, manufacturing embeds carbon, water, and chemical burdens during fabrication, operation draws grid electricity for training and serving, and decommissioning produces electronic waste unless hardware is refurbished or its components recovered.

![[90 Assets/Volume II/15 Sustainable AI/AI Lca d85d8c947e.svg]]

Figure 14: **AI System Lifecycle**: Four stages in sequence, each dropping the burden it adds, with a recovery loop from decommissioning back to manufacture. Design decisions fix much of the load the later stages carry; manufacturing adds embodied carbon, water, and chemicals; use adds operational energy at the grid’s carbon intensity; decommissioning adds e-waste unless hardware is refurbished or its components recovered.

^fig-ai-lca

The lifecycle sequence connects design choices to costs incurred at different times. Experimentation consumes resources before deployment; manufacturing adds embodied carbon; and use couples the workload to grid and cooling constraints. Retirement creates a choice among reuse, recovery, and disposal. Extending useful service spreads the hardware’s embodied burden over more work, but it does not reverse emissions already incurred. The environmental benefit depends on the replacement it avoids and the operating cost of keeping the existing equipment in service.

### Design and Experimentation Phase

^sec-sustainable-ai-design-phase-0954

Public training-energy figures almost always report the single final run that produced the published checkpoint, omitting the months of failed experiments, hyperparameter sweeps, and architecture searches that preceded it. Those preliminary runs belong to the design phase, which covers research, development, and optimization before deployment. Automated neural architecture search (NAS) can evaluate many configurations. [[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/04 The Responsible Fleet/15 Sustainable AI/V2-15.08 Training Vs. Inference Energy Analysis#^sec-sustainable-ai-ai-energy-consumption-compared-industries-3d5d|Section 15.7.1]] showed that two estimates of the same search can differ by orders of magnitude depending on whether the boundary includes proxy searches, full runs, data-center overhead, grid mix, and reuse across downstream models ([[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/04 The Responsible Fleet/15 Sustainable AI/V2-15.15 Source References#^ref-strubell2019|Strubell et al. 2019]]; [[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/04 The Responsible Fleet/15 Sustainable AI/V2-15.15 Source References#^ref-patterson2021carbon|Patterson et al. 2021]]). Later efficient NAS methods use weight sharing, continuous relaxations, and hardware-aware search to reduce that search budget ([[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/04 The Responsible Fleet/15 Sustainable AI/V2-15.15 Source References#^ref-elsken2019|Elsken et al. 2019]]). Excluding search does not make the remaining figures comparable either, because final runs also resist ranking by model size ([[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/04 The Responsible Fleet/15 Sustainable AI/V2-15.09 Hardware Lifecycle and E-Waste#^tbl-training-emissions|table 10]]).

The reported final run for the 600-billion-parameter GShard model emitted under a tenth as much as the 11-billion-parameter T5 run. One reason is architectural, because GShard-600B and the 1.5-trillion-parameter Switch Transformer are Mixture-of-Experts models that route each token to only a small subset of experts rather than activating every parameter on every token as dense T5, Meena, and GPT-3 do. The table also records both lower energy use and lower emissions per megawatt-hour for GShard. Emissions per megawatt-hour is each run’s effective grid factor, and across the five runs it varies by a factor of about 3. Because the runs also differ in token budgets, accelerator generations, and data-center grids, the table’s totals do not isolate the saving from sparse activation alone. Parameter count therefore cannot rank their emissions, and operation count would still need an execution-efficiency and grid account. Search and failed experiments require a separate development inventory before these final-run figures can support a lifecycle comparison.

Addressing the design phase’s sustainability challenges requires innovations in training efficiency: sparse training, low-precision arithmetic, weight-sharing, and energy-aware NAS approaches. Transfer learning and fine-tuning pretrained models reuse pretrained representations instead of requiring every task to be trained from scratch ([[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/04 The Responsible Fleet/15 Sustainable AI/V2-15.15 Source References#^ref-Raffel2020exploring|Raffel et al. 2020]]).

### Water, Chemicals, and Critical Materials

^sec-sustainable-ai-water-usage-caae

Before an accelerator exists, its fabrication consumes physical resources that an energy account alone does not quantify: ultrapure water, process chemicals, and materials. Their burdens occur across extraction, refining, fabrication, and assembly sites, rather than solely where the finished hardware operates. Water demand must be evaluated against local availability and competing uses, while material supply depends on the component and manufacturing process ([[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/04 The Responsible Fleet/15 Sustainable AI/V2-15.15 Source References#^ref-jha2014|Jha 2014]]; [[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/04 The Responsible Fleet/15 Sustainable AI/V2-15.15 Source References#^ref-chen2006|Chen 2006]]). These resource accounts extend the lifecycle boundary beyond carbon without reducing unlike impacts to a single energy total.

Water accounting requires more than a single WUE ratio. **Water withdrawal** measures water removed from a source, whereas **water consumption** measures the portion not returned to the same watershed in a usable form. The account must separate direct on-site cooling water, which [[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/04 The Responsible Fleet/15 Sustainable AI/V2-15.05 Energy Measurement and Modeling#^eq-wue|equation 3]] and the cooling-tower calculation in [[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/04 The Responsible Fleet/15 Sustainable AI/V2-15.07 Data Center Energy and Resource Consumption#^sec-sustainable-ai-cooling|section 15.6.3]] estimate, from indirect water used to generate electricity ([[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/04 The Responsible Fleet/15 Sustainable AI/V2-15.07 Data Center Energy and Resource Consumption#^fig-datacenter-water-accounts|figure 10]]) and manufacture hardware. The account must then attach each flow to its watershed, season, and local scarcity ([[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/04 The Responsible Fleet/15 Sustainable AI/V2-15.15 Source References#^ref-iso140462014|Environmental Management—Water Footprint—Principles, Requirements and Guidelines 2014]]; [[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/04 The Responsible Fleet/15 Sustainable AI/V2-15.15 Source References#^ref-thegreengrid2011wue|The Green Grid 2011]]). One liter consumed during a wet season in a low-stress basin is not equivalent to one liter consumed during a drought in a stressed basin, so decision-grade comparisons report both physical volume and a stress-weighted result. Reclaimed-water percentages also need a declared denominator because recirculating water inside a facility can reduce withdrawal without eliminating evaporative consumption.

Semiconductor fabrication uses ultrapure water for chemical-mechanical planarization, wet etching, and wafer rinsing ([[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/04 The Responsible Fleet/15 Sustainable AI/V2-15.15 Source References#^ref-cooper2011|Cooper et al. 2011]]).[[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/04 The Responsible Fleet/15 Sustainable AI/V2-15.16 Footnotes#^fn18|¹⁸]] Complete environmental accounting requires auditing gross process flow, freshwater withdrawal, recycled volume, and evaporative consumption as distinct quantities, because internal reuse and flows that leave the facility change different totals ([[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/04 The Responsible Fleet/15 Sustainable AI/V2-15.09 Hardware Lifecycle and E-Waste#^fig-water-cycle|figure 15]]). Treatment returns reclaimed water to the intake stream and can reduce freshwater withdrawal, whereas evaporation remains a consumptive loss within the local water account. That classification does not mean the water permanently disappears from the hydrological cycle.

![[90 Assets/Volume II/15 Sustainable AI/Water Cycle c1bcc33d7d.svg]]

Figure 15: **Semiconductor Water Cycle**: Intake supplies process water; treatment returns part of the flow for reuse. Recycling can reduce freshwater withdrawal, while evaporation and other unreturned flows determine consumption. State the denominator of any reuse fraction.

^fig-water-cycle

The separate flows in [[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/04 The Responsible Fleet/15 Sustainable AI/V2-15.09 Hardware Lifecycle and E-Waste#^fig-water-cycle|figure 15]] show why a recycling fraction cannot determine absolute consumption. The same reuse percentage can accompany very different intake volumes and evaporative losses. Facility siting therefore requires the underlying water volumes and local watershed conditions, not a reuse percentage alone.

Fabrication also uses chemical products for etching, doping, and cleaning: strong acids such as hydrofluoric and sulfuric acid, organic solvents, and toxic dopant gases such as arsine and phosphine. Their hazard comes from toxicity, reactivity, and the treatment burden of the waste streams they produce, not from their energy content. Chemical inventories and safety data sheets identify hazardous ingredients, although undisclosed formulations can limit the assessment ([[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/04 The Responsible Fleet/15 Sustainable AI/V2-15.15 Source References#^ref-kim2018|Kim et al. 2018]]). The environmental account needs the substances and quantities used at the specific facility, together with their release and treatment pathways. An energy total alone cannot describe these chemical burdens.

AI hardware and its manufacturing supply chain depend on **critical materials** whose supply disruption can constrain production. Criticality is not synonymous with geological rarity or membership in the rare earth group. Materials such as indium appear in critical-materials and endangered-elements analyses because supply can depend on byproduct extraction, substitution options, and recycling constraints ([[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/04 The Responsible Fleet/15 Sustainable AI/V2-15.15 Source References#^ref-rhodes2019endangered|Rhodes 2019]]). The relevant dependency may lie in a processor, package, peripheral component, or fabrication process ([[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/04 The Responsible Fleet/15 Sustainable AI/V2-15.09 Hardware Lifecycle and E-Waste#^tbl-material-depletion-be0249d8|table 11]]).

The construction and operation of fabs and data centers also directly impact natural ecosystems through habitat disruption, water stress, and pollution from chemical discharge. Semiconductor-industry effluents can contaminate nearby fluvial sediments with heavy metals and trace elements ([[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/04 The Responsible Fleet/15 Sustainable AI/V2-15.15 Source References#^ref-hsu2016|Hsu et al. 2016]]). Waste generation from fabrication, including gaseous emissions, VOC-laden air, and metal-contaminated wastewater, requires advanced treatment systems, and end-of-life hardware adds the e-waste stream examined in [[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/04 The Responsible Fleet/15 Sustainable AI/V2-15.09 Hardware Lifecycle and E-Waste#^sec-sustainable-ai-disposal-phase-b4f1|section 15.8.5]].

### Manufacturing Phase

^sec-sustainable-ai-manufacturing-phase-4ce1

Fabricating an AI accelerator near the reticle limit, the maximum die area a single lithography exposure can print, leaves far fewer candidate dies per wafer than a small mobile processor, and every failed die transfers its share of ultrapure water, process chemicals, and lithography energy onto the working ones that survive. The manufacturing footprint combines the embodied carbon established in [[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/04 The Responsible Fleet/15 Sustainable AI/V2-15.06 Carbon Footprint Calculation#^sec-sustainable-ai-embodied-carbon-assessment-9de0|section 15.5.5]] with the water, chemical, and material demands in [[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/04 The Responsible Fleet/15 Sustainable AI/V2-15.09 Hardware Lifecycle and E-Waste#^sec-sustainable-ai-water-usage-caae|section 15.8.2]], and two structural properties of modern accelerators enlarge that resource investment per usable device.

First, because the probability that a die escapes every random wafer defect falls roughly exponentially with die area, a defect density that kills 5 percent of small dies may kill 20 percent or more of reticle-limit dies, concentrating the wafer’s fabrication burden onto a smaller surviving yield. Second, the memory bandwidth that large-scale training and inference require pushes accelerators toward advanced 2.5D and 3D packaging, such as TSMC’s Chip-on-Wafer-on-Substrate (CoWoS), which places high bandwidth memory stacks beside the logic die on an interposer. The inventory must include those memory stacks, the interposer, and the added assembly stages rather than counting only the accelerator die. Each usable accelerator therefore carries more process water, chemicals, and energy than its logic-die area alone suggests. Longer useful service spreads that manufacturing investment across more work, while lower-toxicity materials, recycling, and cleaner manufacturing address the production burden itself ([[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/04 The Responsible Fleet/15 Sustainable AI/V2-15.15 Source References#^ref-cenci2021|Cenci et al. 2021]]; [[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/04 The Responsible Fleet/15 Sustainable AI/V2-15.15 Source References#^ref-irimia-vladu2014|Irimia-Vladu 2014]]).

### Use Phase

^sec-sustainable-ai-use-phase-a0a1

A GPT-3-scale training run draws 1,287 MWh over weeks of sustained cluster operation, and when those megawatt-hours are drawn matters just as much to the grid and the atmosphere as their sum. The operational energy consumed during training and inference is detailed in [[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/04 The Responsible Fleet/15 Sustainable AI/V2-15.08 Training Vs. Inference Energy Analysis#^sec-sustainable-ai-training-vs-inference-energy-analysis-4cb5|section 15.7]]; how flexible workloads shift that draw toward cleaner hours is developed in [[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/04 The Responsible Fleet/15 Sustainable AI/V2-15.10 Mitigation Strategies#^sec-sustainable-ai-carbonaware-scheduling-c2db|section 15.9.4]]. Once that active service ends, the remaining lifecycle question is what happens to the physical equipment itself.

### Disposal, E-Waste, and Embedded AI

^sec-sustainable-ai-disposal-phase-b4f1

Data centers routinely retire working AI servers after three to four years, not because the silicon has physically failed, but because a newer generation’s capacity or efficiency leaves older racks uncompetitive for frontier workloads. Those hardware upgrade cycles ([[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/04 The Responsible Fleet/15 Sustainable AI/V2-15.15 Source References#^ref-slade2007|Slade 2007]]) connect AI deployment to the broader electronic waste (e-waste) problem. In 2019, global e-waste reached roughly 54 million metric tons ([[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/04 The Responsible Fleet/15 Sustainable AI/V2-15.15 Source References#^ref-forti2020ewaste|Forti et al. 2020]]), of which 17.4 percent was documented as formally collected and recycled ([[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/04 The Responsible Fleet/15 Sustainable AI/V2-15.15 Source References#^ref-singh2022|Singh and Ogunseitan 2022]]). These totals cover electrical and electronic equipment generally, not AI hardware alone. When equipment is finally discarded, hazardous substances in its components require controlled recovery and treatment rather than uncontrolled dumping or processing ([[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/04 The Responsible Fleet/15 Sustainable AI/V2-15.15 Source References#^ref-grossman2007high|Grossman 2007]]).

Before hardware ever reaches that waste stream, memory capacity, bandwidth, and interconnect compatibility govern whether an older rack can be upgraded in place or must be retired as a unit. In tightly integrated systems, a new accelerator generation may require baseboard, networking, or server changes, whereas PCIe-based designs can offer modular upgrade options. Evaluating the actual compatibility boundary separates commercial retirement from physical disposal, because a functioning node retired from frontier training can still serve less demanding batch, development, or inference workloads.

The deployment of **embedded AI** integrates inference into edge appliances and consumer electronics. The broader connected-device fleet ([[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/04 The Responsible Fleet/15 Sustainable AI/V2-15.09 Hardware Lifecycle and E-Waste#^fig-iot-number|figure 16]]) grew from an estimated 8.6 billion devices in 2019 to 15.1 billion in 2023, with 29.4 billion projected by 2030 ([[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/04 The Responsible Fleet/15 Sustainable AI/V2-15.15 Source References#^ref-Statista-2022-20299452|Statista 2022]]). These counts indicate the scale of the device ecosystem, not the number of AI systems or the amount of e-waste. Waste generation also depends on service life, repair, and reuse. Adhesive enclosures, nonreplaceable batteries, and proprietary components can make repair difficult, increasing the risk that a limited failure leads to whole-device disposal ([[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/04 The Responsible Fleet/15 Sustainable AI/V2-15.15 Source References#^ref-Balde-2017-33c90ecf|Baldé et al. 2017]]).

![[90 Assets/Volume II/15 Sustainable AI/Cell 68 Output 1 958fab7f04.png]]

Figure 16: **IoT Device Growth**: Connected devices worldwide, with historical counts through 2023 (unhatched) and projections from 2024 onward (hatched) ([[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/04 The Responsible Fleet/15 Sustainable AI/V2-15.15 Source References#^ref-Statista-2022-20299452|Statista 2022]]). Limited software support, restricted repair, and difficult disassembly can push devices toward replacement instead of reuse. The lifecycle boundary must then follow the retired equipment to its next use or treatment destination. Moving hardware out of the operator’s inventory does not remove its environmental burden; the account must distinguish useful reuse, controlled recovery, and disposal that transfers costs to other communities.

### Hardware Lifespan Extension

^sec-sustainable-ai-extending-hardware-lifespan-b028

Keeping an AI server in useful service for 5 years instead of 3 years cuts the embodied carbon allocated to each year of operation by 40 percent. Countering the linear “take-make-dispose” model requires a shift toward a **circular economy** ([[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/04 The Responsible Fleet/15 Sustainable AI/V2-15.15 Source References#^ref-stahel2016|Stahel 2016]]) that prioritizes reuse, refurbishment, and recycling, spreading a fixed manufacturing footprint over more service. This allocation result motivates evaluating longer use, but a claim of avoided emissions requires comparing the replacement and operating costs of the alternatives.

Four lifecycle interventions support longer hardware service by making systems repairable, upgradeable, supported, and reusable across their physical life. Physically, right-to-repair initiatives reduce service restrictions, including barriers to replacement parts, tools, and repair information ([[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/04 The Responsible Fleet/15 Sustainable AI/V2-15.15 Source References#^ref-ftc2021nixingfix|Federal Trade Commission 2021]]). Modular design separates replaceable subsystems so that an accelerator or network upgrade need not require discarding the entire chassis. Once the hardware remains serviceable, extended support cycles provide the driver updates and security patches needed to keep functioning equipment usable. When a node eventually falls behind frontier training demands, secondary-use programs match retired accelerators to workloads they can still serve, such as batch processing, development, or research. Each intervention must preserve the performance, compatibility, and security required for useful continued operation. These interventions move sustainability from disposal management to lifecycle engineering.

A replacement decision compares two ways of delivering the same future service, continued operation of the existing fleet and operation of a replacement fleet. The original manufacturing emissions have already occurred under either choice. Replacement adds a new manufacturing burden that pays back only through operational savings over the remaining service interval, so the tipping point is the time those savings take to equal it.

Suppose a replacement accelerator carries the same allocated manufacturing footprint as an H100 (164 kg CO<sub>2</sub>e) and completes the same work with 50 percent less energy. Against an existing accelerator running continuously at the rated 700 W, the replacement repays its manufacturing emissions in about 6.5 weeks on the US-average grid. On Quebec’s grid it repays them only after about 2.7 years, nearly the whole of a 3-year standard service life. Lower utilization stretches both intervals in proportion. The same upgrade can therefore repay itself within weeks on a fossil-heavy grid and become a net loss on a clean one if the replacement leaves service before it breaks even. This accelerator-only account omits host and facility overhead, and disposal and any useful second life for the retired hardware shift it further, so a lower annual amortized figure alone cannot settle the decision (the financial side of the same refresh decision is screened in **[[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/03 The Fleet in Production/12 ML Operations at Scale/V2-12.10 Fleet Economics and Utilization#^sec-ml-operations-scale-power-efficiency|Refresh screening]]**).

Once an engineering team has measured both the operational and lifecycle terms of its workload account, the remaining question is how to combine algorithmic, infrastructure, and scheduling levers to drive those terms down under a real power or carbon ceiling.

**Self-Check: Question**
