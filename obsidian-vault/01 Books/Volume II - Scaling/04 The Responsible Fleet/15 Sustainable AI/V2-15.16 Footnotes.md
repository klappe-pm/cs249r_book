# V2-15.16 Footnotes

[[Home|Home]] · [[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/Volume 2 Overview|Volume Overview]] · [[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/04 The Responsible Fleet/15 Sustainable AI/V2-15 Sustainable AI|V2-15 Sustainable AI]]

^footnotes

<sup>1</sup> **Joule energy scale**: An A100 modeled at its rated thermal design power consumes 400 J every second (400 W); actual workload power requires measurement. Large-model training has no single defensible joule range: model size, hardware count, realized power, utilization, and run duration can each shift total energy by orders of magnitude. The engineering lesson lies in the conversion from repeated per-operation costs to facility energy, so this chapter reports measured or modeled workload energy rather than assigning every large run to one fixed range.

^fn1

<sup>2</sup> **Data center industrial scale**: The IEA projection is an electricity-demand metric, not directly comparable to aviation or cement shares of global emissions. AI infrastructure competes with other loads for deliverable power; expansion requires available capacity, additional supply, or a reduction or shift in competing demand, not necessarily new generation within the same region.

^fn2

<sup>3</sup> **GPU manufacturing embodied carbon**: NVIDIA’s HGX H100 product carbon footprint reports 1,312 kg CO<sub>2</sub>e (CO<sub>2</sub>-equivalent, which expresses other greenhouse gases as the mass of CO<sub>2</sub> with the same warming effect) for an eight-H100 baseboard, or roughly 164 kg CO<sub>2</sub>e per H100 if allocated evenly across the eight GPUs ([[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/04 The Responsible Fleet/15 Sustainable AI/V2-15.15 Source References#^ref-nvidia2025hgxh100pcf|NVIDIA Corporation 2025]]). Advanced-node manufacturing also consumes ultrapure water, specialty gases, and chemicals that an energy-only account never sees, so a per-GPU carbon figure understates the full manufacturing footprint.

^fn3

<sup>4</sup> **AI hardware e-waste**: Retiring an accelerator does not necessarily create waste: equipment may be redeployed, resold, or recycled. Estimate disposal impacts from the actual equipment inventory, material composition, and end-of-life route. Extending useful service life can defer manufacturing and disposal, while certified handling limits exposure to hazardous materials in discarded electronics.

^fn4

<sup>5</sup> **Dennard scaling**: Robert Dennard observed in 1974 that smaller transistors could operate at constant power density by reducing voltage proportionally. This scaling pattern ended around 2005 when leakage current made further voltage reduction impractical. The discussion of Dennard scaling in **[[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/01 The Fleet/02 Compute Infrastructure/V2-02.07 The Rack#^sec-compute-power-wall|Power and thermal envelopes]]** drew the facility consequence, that every refresh must fit installed power and cooling. The consequence for AI sustainability is equally direct: without Dennard scaling, each process node no longer delivers proportional power savings, which pushes efficiency work toward specialized accelerators, such as GPUs and the Tensor Processing Unit (TPU), that exploit architectural parallelism rather than transistor physics alone.

^fn5

<sup>6</sup> **Training communication overhead**: Distributed training adds network transfers, synchronization stalls, and checkpoint I/O to the energy account. The fraction is workload- and topology-dependent: parallelism strategy, tensor sizes, link technology, utilization loss, and any extra iterations determine whether communication is a secondary term or the binding energy cost. An energy estimate must therefore meter or model these terms for the actual run rather than apply a universal percentage.

^fn6

<sup>7</sup> **Optimizer memory as energy cost**: Adaptive Moment Estimation (Adam) stores per-parameter first and second moment estimates, unlike plain stochastic gradient descent (SGD). For a 70B model in FP32, these two moment buffers total about 560 GB, bringing parameters plus moments to about 840 GB (excluding gradients). The sustainability implication is direct: larger optimizer state means more off-chip memory traffic per training step, and at roughly 160 pJ/byte for a DRAM access, memory movement can dominate the energy budget of parameter updates.

^fn7

<sup>8</sup> **Carbon intensity variance**: Regional averages and time-varying grid factors answer different questions. A generation technology’s lifecycle factor is neither a measured regional average nor the marginal emissions response to another job. Scheduling must choose the signal appropriate to its decision and retain the source, period, and boundary.

^fn8

<sup>9</sup> **Embodied carbon break-even**: Embodied carbon covers manufacturing, transport, and end-of-life treatment rather than use. Allocating NVIDIA’s HGX H100 baseboard product carbon footprint evenly across its eight GPUs gives roughly 164 kg CO<sub>2</sub>e per H100 ([[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/04 The Responsible Fleet/15 Sustainable AI/V2-15.15 Source References#^ref-nvidia2025hgxh100pcf|NVIDIA Corporation 2025]]); assuming continuous accelerator power of 700 W on the average U.S. grid, the modeled operational emissions match that allocation in roughly three to four weeks. This comparison omits host and facility overhead and uses rated power rather than a measured workload trace. A cleaner grid lengthens the modeled interval, but this equality alone does not decide whether replacing hardware reduces future emissions.

^fn9

<sup>10</sup> **Lithography and embodied energy**: Advanced semiconductor fabrication can use energy-intensive lithography, including extreme ultraviolet (EUV) processes. Emissions per usable chip depend on the process sequence, facility energy mix, yield, die area, and allocation method. Neither transistor count nor a nominal process-node label alone determines the number of exposure steps or manufacturing footprint.

^fn10

<sup>11</sup> **Edge AI energy trade-off**: Edge inference can reduce network transfers and response latency, while distributing computation across devices with different duty cycles and power states. It does not eliminate network or cloud work, and distributed consumption, although harder to attribute, remains part of the accounting boundary; [[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/04 The Responsible Fleet/15 Sustainable AI/V2-15.08 Training Vs. Inference Energy Analysis#^sec-sustainable-ai-edge-ai-impact-6694|section 15.7.4]] works through the comparison.

^fn11

<sup>12</sup> **Data center emissions scale**: Those emissions come from electricity demand that IEA’s 2025 *Energy and AI* analysis tracks separately. Data centers consumed about 1.5 percent of global electricity (415 TWh) in 2024, a share projected to approach 3 percent by 2030, with AI contributing to demand growth ([[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/04 The Responsible Fleet/15 Sustainable AI/V2-15.15 Source References#^ref-iea2025energyai|International Energy Agency 2025]]). The largest hyperscale facilities can draw over 100 MW continuously, equivalent to powering tens of thousands of homes.

^fn12

<sup>13</sup> **Hyperscale data center footprint**: A hyperscale campus makes long-lived investments in substations and transmission connections. Algorithmic improvements can reduce demand but do not remove the costs already incurred for that capacity. Supply contracts and operating schedules remain adjustable, subject to their terms and the physical connection.

^fn13

<sup>14</sup> **PUE boundary**: The facility-to-IT ratio glossed with the opening energy boundary ([[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/04 The Responsible Fleet/15 Sustainable AI/V2-15.15 Source References#^ref-thegreengrid2007pue|The Green Grid 2007]]), formalized in [[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/04 The Responsible Fleet/15 Sustainable AI/V2-15.05 Energy Measurement and Modeling#^sec-sustainable-ai-facilitylevel-power-metrics-5558|section 15.4.1.3]], says nothing about where recovered heat goes. Lowering non-IT overhead improves facility efficiency, but the metric does not capture the carbon intensity of electricity or the water consumed for cooling. Carbon usage effectiveness and water usage effectiveness therefore expose constraints that PUE alone leaves invisible.

^fn14

<sup>15</sup> **Neural architecture search (NAS) carbon cost**: The ~284,000 kg CO<sub>2</sub>e figure from Strubell et al. ([[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/04 The Responsible Fleet/15 Sustainable AI/V2-15.15 Source References#^ref-strubell2019|2019]]) represents an unoptimized estimate for the search that produced the Evolved Transformer architecture, and it drops sharply when accounting for TPU hardware, grid mix, and the smaller proxy tasks on which the search evaluated candidates ([[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/04 The Responsible Fleet/15 Sustainable AI/V2-15.15 Source References#^ref-patterson2021carbon|Patterson et al. 2021]]). The systems lesson is boundary discipline: search, tuning, and failed trials belong inside the carbon accounting boundary, but reusable architecture search should be amortized across all downstream deployments.

^fn15

<sup>16</sup> **Parallelism energy overhead**: Data, model, and pipeline parallelism impose distinct communication and idle-power costs. Data parallelism reduces gradients, tensor parallelism exchanges partial activations, and pipeline parallelism introduces bubbles. A valid comparison reaches the same model-quality target under the same completion constraint, then measures each strategy’s runtime and system energy, including communication and waiting. Fixing time-to-train by assumption would hide one of the effects the comparison needs to expose.

^fn16

<sup>17</sup> **Life cycle assessment (LCA)**: Standardized by ISO 14040/14044, LCA traces environmental impact from raw material extraction through disposal ([[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/04 The Responsible Fleet/15 Sustainable AI/V2-15.15 Source References#^ref-iso140402006lca|International Organization for Standardization 2006a]], [[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/04 The Responsible Fleet/15 Sustainable AI/V2-15.15 Source References#^ref-iso140442006lca|2006b]]). For AI hardware, LCA separates embodied manufacturing emissions from operational energy use, making hardware refresh cycles and accelerator lifespan extension first-order sustainability levers that operational efficiency alone cannot substitute.

^fn17

<sup>18</sup> **Semiconductor water scale**: A fab water inventory must separate intake, internal reuse, discharge, and consumption. Water per usable die depends on process flow, yield, allocation, and site conditions; a smaller process-node label does not establish a universal per-die multiplier.

^fn18

<sup>19</sup> **Jevons paradox**: Jevons ([[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/04 The Responsible Fleet/15 Sustainable AI/V2-15.15 Source References#^ref-jevons1865coal|1865]]) described how efficiency could stimulate demand enough to increase total coal use. Price reductions alone do not establish either energy factor; measure both unit energy and demand.

^fn19

<sup>20</sup> **Knowledge distillation**: Introduced by Hinton et al. ([[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/04 The Responsible Fleet/15 Sustainable AI/V2-15.15 Source References#^ref-hinton2015distilling|2015]]), distillation trains a compact “student” model on soft probability targets from a larger “teacher,” capturing inter-class relationships that hard labels discard. The sustainability comparison must amortize teacher and student training across the actual serving volume and measure energy at the required output quality.

^fn20

<sup>21</sup> **Spiking neural networks (SNNs)**: Sparse spiking activity can reduce communication and switching, but the complete comparison includes training, encoding, sensing, and the hardware used to execute the model. A saving measured on one temporal task does not transfer automatically to other workloads.

^fn21

<sup>22</sup> **PUE gap**: The PUE model in [[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/04 The Responsible Fleet/15 Sustainable AI/V2-15.05 Energy Measurement and Modeling#^sec-sustainable-ai-facilitylevel-power-metrics-5558|section 15.4.1.3]] shows that facility overhead scales with every watt of IT load. At 100 MW, even a small PUE change therefore represents megawatts of cooling and power-delivery capacity, but its carbon value still depends on the grid signal.

^fn22

<sup>23</sup> **24/7 carbon-free energy (CFE)**: Hourly matching strengthens the time resolution of electricity procurement and accounting. Its physical impact depends on deliverability, additional clean supply, storage, and grid conditions. It does not eliminate manufacturing emissions or prove that every consumed electron came from a carbon-free generator.

^fn23

<sup>24</sup> **EU AI Act (2024)**: The Act presumes high-impact capabilities above $10^{25}$ FLOPs of training compute and imposes additional obligations on general-purpose models with systemic risk. Annex XI includes computational resources and known or estimated energy consumption in required technical documentation; qualifying open-source models without systemic risk can be exempt from these documentation obligations. Article 101 permits fines for specified provider violations up to 3 percent of annual worldwide turnover or EUR 15 million, whichever is higher. Application dates, model scope, and exemptions must be checked before turning these provisions into an engineering reporting requirement.

^fn24

<sup>25</sup> **Corporate Sustainability Reporting Directive (CSRD)**: Directive (EU) 2026/470 narrows reporting thresholds to more than 1,000 employees and more than EUR 450 million in net annual turnover, assessed at group level where applicable. Member States must transpose its reporting amendments by March 19, 2027; entity scope, exemptions, and reporting-year rules still determine applicability. Covered reporting follows European Sustainability Reporting Standards rather than a separate inventory of AI jobs. The engineering contribution is traceable information on relevant electricity, procurement, and value-chain impacts.

^fn25

<sup>26</sup> **Emissions trading for compute**: Applying cap-and-trade to AI emissions is a policy analogy here, not a description of an established compute-allowance market. Such a design would need a declared emissions boundary, measurement method, enforcement mechanism, and rules for trading allowances.

^fn26

<sup>27</sup> **Greenwashing in AI**: Typical forms are claiming “carbon neutrality” through offsets while expanding data center capacity, or highlighting per-query efficiency gains while total compute grows faster. Green-claims rules can require verifiable evidence for environmental claims. For ML engineers, the technical litmus test is whether sustainability reporting covers all three GHG Protocol scopes, or conveniently omits Scope 3 (hardware manufacturing, cloud supply chain) where much of AI’s carbon can reside.

^fn27

<sup>28</sup> **Environmental justice in data center siting**: A site-cost model can omit burdens borne by nearby residents, such as competing water demand or changes to local infrastructure. A low financial cost therefore does not establish a low community burden. Site evaluation must identify who benefits, who bears the resource costs, and how affected communities participate in the decision alongside the analysis of grid intensity and water stress.

^fn28

<sup>29</sup> **Von Neumann bottleneck**: Separating processing from memory requires data transfers whose energy depends on locality and arithmetic intensity. If movement contributes a fraction ${q}$ of total energy, eliminating that term alone gives at most a $1/(1-{q})$ reduction factor when the remaining terms are unchanged. For an illustrative 60–80 percent share, that bound is 2.5–5$\times$, before accounting for replacement circuitry.

^fn29
