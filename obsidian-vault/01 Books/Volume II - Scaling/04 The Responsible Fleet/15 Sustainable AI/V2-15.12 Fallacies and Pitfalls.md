# V2-15.12 Fallacies and Pitfalls

[[Home|Home]] · [[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/Volume 2 Overview|Volume Overview]] · [[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/04 The Responsible Fleet/15 Sustainable AI/V2-15 Sustainable AI|V2-15 Sustainable AI]]

^sec-sustainable-ai-fallacies-pitfalls-ee9a

Sustainability involves counterintuitive physics where efficiency improvements can increase total consumption and geographic choices can outweigh component-level optimizations. These fallacies and pitfalls capture errors that waste compute budgets and planetary resources through misallocated optimization effort.

**Fallacy**: *Cloud computing automatically makes AI systems more environmentally sustainable.*

Engineers assume cloud providers operate efficiently and sustainably. Cloud infrastructure can improve utilization and facility efficiency, but it transfers neither the physical footprint nor the customer’s accounting responsibility out of existence. The 7B model trained on 64 A100s in notebook [15.5](https://mlsysbook.ai/vol2/15_sustainable_ai/15_sustainable_ai.html#nbk-sustainable-ai-training-emissions-calculation) emits roughly 21.4× as much CO<sub>2</sub> on the US-average grid as on Quebec’s hydroelectric grid, and the hydroelectric figure is an operational factor, not a lifecycle-zero one. As demonstrated in [[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/04 The Responsible Fleet/15 Sustainable AI/V2-15.06 Carbon Footprint Calculation#^sec-sustainable-ai-carbon-footprint-calculation-c2fe|section 15.5]], cloud comparisons must disclose region, energy boundary, utilization, embodied allocation, and whether the electricity is Scope 2 for the provider or Scope 3 for the customer.

**Pitfall**: *Focusing only on operational energy consumption while ignoring embodied carbon and lifecycle impacts.*

Teams optimize training efficiency while ignoring manufacturing emissions. In low-carbon grids, embodied carbon can dominate fleet-level footprint accounting. As quantified in [[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/04 The Responsible Fleet/15 Sustainable AI/V2-15.06 Carbon Footprint Calculation#^sec-sustainable-ai-embodied-carbon-assessment-9de0|section 15.5.5]], an A100 accelerator carries roughly 150 kg CO<sub>2</sub> from manufacturing ([[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/04 The Responsible Fleet/15 Sustainable AI/V2-15.15 Source References#^ref-luccioni2023estimating|Luccioni et al. 2023]]); for the earlier 14-day, 64-A100 training run, the unamortized upfront burden is roughly 9.6 t CO<sub>2</sub>. Amortized over a 4-year service life, the share attributable to this specific job is about 90 kg. The job comparison must use that allocated share; a fleet lifecycle comparison must instead compare the full manufacturing burden against operations over the same fleet lifetime. Comparing the entire manufacturing burden with one short job’s electricity would mix boundaries. Extending hardware lifetime from 3 years to 5 years reduces amortized embodied carbon by 40 percent, but procurement decisions must also evaluate the operational cost of retaining the hardware.

**Fallacy**: *TDP is actual power consumption.*

Thermal design power is a hardware design specification, not the measured power of a particular workload. Actual draw depends on the device, configured power limit, utilization, memory traffic, clocks, and idle intervals. A TDP-based calculation is a stated capacity scenario; it does not establish serving energy or facility energy. Carbon and electricity-cost estimates that drive placement decisions ([[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/04 The Responsible Fleet/15 Sustainable AI/V2-15.06 Carbon Footprint Calculation#^sec-sustainable-ai-carbon-footprint-calculation-c2fe|section 15.5]]) should use measured power over the workload interval and include the support systems within the chosen boundary.

**Pitfall**: *Using PUE as a complete environmental metric.*

Data-center operators report PUE in sustainability disclosures and engineers may treat it as a complete environmental score. PUE measures only facility energy relative to IT energy. A lower-PUE facility can still cause more electricity-related emissions if its grid factor is sufficiently higher, and neither factor accounts for hardware manufacturing or local water stress. Compare matched emissions factors and complete lifecycle boundaries rather than ranking sites by PUE alone.

**Fallacy**: *Efficiency improvements automatically reduce total environmental impact.*

Engineers assume that halving inference cost cuts environmental impact in half. The Jevons paradox warns that efficiency improvements can increase total consumption by enabling expanded usage. In a rebound scenario, suppose reducing token cost from \$0.06 to \$0.002 per 1,000 tokens accompanies a proportional 30× reduction in measured energy per query at fixed output length and quality. A 100× increase in query volume then raises total energy by 3.3× and, at unchanged grid intensity, emissions by the same factor. Any implementation change that lowers per-query energy can still raise total energy when usage grows by a larger factor. Teams must therefore evaluate deployment volume against the intended carbon or resource budget. Per-query gains alone do not control the rebound illustrated in [[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/04 The Responsible Fleet/15 Sustainable AI/V2-15.10 Mitigation Strategies#^fig-jevons-ai|figure 18]], where the red area of new consumption at the lower unit cost completely swallows the green area of initial efficiency savings.

**Pitfall**: *Treating carbon offsets as a substitute for reducing actual emissions.*

Organizations may purchase offsets without establishing that the credited reduction is additional, durable, independently verified, and free of double counting. An offset does not change the electricity consumed by the workload or its immediate emissions. Teams should report physical emissions reductions separately from compensation claims, with the offset boundary and uncertainty visible. Geographic placement can reduce electricity-related emissions when the complete workload comparison supports it; neither an offset purchase nor a renewable label substitutes for that calculation.

**Fallacy**: *Component-level optimization guarantees lifecycle improvement.*

Teams reduce training cost to improve sustainability without analyzing deployment scale. A training change that increases serving energy must be evaluated over expected query volume: the crossover occurs when accumulated additional serving emissions exceed the one-time training saving. Edge deployment adds device manufacturing and replacement to any avoided data-center and transmission emissions. Keeping hardware longer avoids new manufacturing but may consume more operating energy than replacement. Each comparison requires the same useful service, grid-factor boundary, and lifetime; there is no universal query count or upgrade age that settles it. Effective sustainability requires the complete account in [[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/04 The Responsible Fleet/15 Sustainable AI/V2-15.06 Carbon Footprint Calculation#^sec-sustainable-ai-lifecycle-carbon-accounting-99a1|section 15.5.6]].

**Pitfall**: *Evaluating sustainability at the component boundary instead of the lifecycle boundary.*

A pruned model may contain fewer weights while its sparse representation adds indexing and execution overhead. If those costs outweigh the arithmetic and transfers avoided, the smaller model need not consume less energy. The same boundary error appears when hardware teams report accelerator efficiency without procurement carbon, platform teams report PUE without grid intensity, or model teams report training emissions without expected serving volume. The lifecycle boundary in [[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/04 The Responsible Fleet/15 Sustainable AI/V2-15.06 Carbon Footprint Calculation#^sec-sustainable-ai-lifecycle-carbon-accounting-99a1|section 15.5.6]] requires evaluating training, serving, embodied carbon, regional grid mix, hardware lifetime, and demand growth together before calling a change sustainable.

**Self-Check: Question**

1.  True or False: Migrating a machine learning training workload from an on-premises enterprise cluster in a low-carbon region (20 $\text{gCO}_2/\text{kWh}$) to a commercial cloud data center in a coal-heavy region (750 $\text{gCO}_2/\text{kWh}$) is guaranteed to reduce the workload’s carbon footprint because hyperscale cloud providers operate at lower PUE.

2.  A systems team aggressively compresses a model using unstructured pruning to cut training FLOPs by 60 percent. In production, serving the sparse model requires specialized, custom-fabricated NPU accelerators and requires double the serving iterations to reach quality parity. Which pitfall does this scenario illustrate, and what is the proper engineering mitigation?

    1.  Local optimization of training compute without full-lifecycle assessment; the mitigation is conducting end-to-end Life Cycle Assessment (LCA) that accounts for hardware manufacturing, deployment scale, and serving energy before committing to architectural changes.
    2.  Failure to apply Amdahl’s law; the mitigation is increasing CPU clock frequency.
    3.  Violating the GHG Protocol; the mitigation is reclassifying Scope 3 emissions as Scope 1.
    4.  Ignoring PUE; the mitigation is installing liquid cooling on the development workstations.

3.  Explain why an offset purchase does not replace evidence of physical emissions reductions, and what must be established before claiming a reduction from placement, efficiency, or hardware reuse.
