# V2-15.10 Mitigation Strategies

[[Home|Home]] · [[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/Volume 2 Overview|Volume Overview]] · [[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/04 The Responsible Fleet/15 Sustainable AI/V2-15 Sustainable AI|V2-15 Sustainable AI]]

^sec-sustainable-ai-part-iii-implementation-solutions-232d

When a data center hits its absolute power ceiling, the operator cannot simply buy more GPUs. Supporting additional useful work requires reducing the power needed for that work, changing its schedule, or securing additional feasible capacity. Algorithmic optimization can reduce executed work and data movement; infrastructure changes can reduce overhead. Carbon-aware placement addresses a different constraint by changing the emissions associated with electricity use, even when energy demand stays fixed.

The measurement frameworks identify where environmental costs concentrate in a particular workload. Repeated experiments can make development dominant; sustained serving can make inference dominant; and clean electricity or limited use can increase the relative importance of manufacturing. The measured balance guides intervention. Algorithmic changes can reduce the number or cost of operations, infrastructure changes affect execution and facility overhead, and governance sets the budgets and incentives under which those choices operate.

Implementation must account for the rebound introduced with fleet growth in [[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/04 The Responsible Fleet/15 Sustainable AI/V2-15.04 Exponential Growth Vs. Physical Constraints#^sec-sustainable-ai-exponential-growth-vs-physical-constraints-0f4e|section 15.3]], known as the Jevons paradox[[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/04 The Responsible Fleet/15 Sustainable AI/V2-15.16 Footnotes#^fn19|¹⁹]] (principle [[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/04 The Responsible Fleet/V2 Responsible Fleet Principles#^pri-jevons-paradox|25]]). The tenfold-efficiency, hundredfold-volume example there supplied the arithmetic; the mechanism is that cheaper computation enables applications that were previously economically infeasible, so usage can grow enough to erase or exceed the expected energy savings. This **rebound effect** makes absolute outcomes, including carbon budgets, necessary companions to rate efficiency (FLOP/s per watt). Renewable sourcing changes the emissions associated with electricity but does not cap consumption. Governance must track workload growth against the intended resource budget to determine whether efficiency gains reduce the fleet’s total impact.

### Multi-Layer Mitigation

^sec-sustainable-ai-multilayer-mitigation-strategy-framework-80f2

Compressing a model in half saves no carbon if the freed cluster capacity immediately fills with new experiments or if the job runs on a coal-heavy grid without a facility cap. Because a local efficiency gain can itself raise total consumption, mitigation must decide both which layer owns each reduction and which absolute budget contains the growth in demand that follows. Energy-efficient model design, optimized hardware deployment, sustainable infrastructure operations, and carbon-aware scheduling each attack a different term in the lifecycle footprint. Framework selection matters only insofar as it changes computation, memory movement, utilization, or reporting. Lifecycle-aware design keeps the optimization from ending at deployment by checking whether savings survive training, inference, manufacturing, and use.

Each mitigation lever acts at one accounting tier ([[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/04 The Responsible Fleet/15 Sustainable AI/V2-15.10 Mitigation Strategies#^fig-energy-intervention|figure 17]]). Work reduction and execution efficiency change IT energy. Facility overhead scales that energy to the facility total. A regional grid factor converts facility kilowatt-hours into operational carbon. Hardware amortization adds embodied emissions at its own boundary. Reductions at the multiplicative tiers compound only when each is measured in the combined configuration on the same fixed useful work, because a cut in IT energy can itself change PUE. Isolated efficiency percentages measured on different workloads or baselines, or applied to the additive embodied term, cannot be multiplied into a single composite reduction factor.

![[90 Assets/Volume II/15 Sustainable AI/Intervention Cascade 048817db61.svg]]

^fig-energy-intervention

Rebound can cancel a reduction at any of these tiers. On an elastic demand curve (${\epsilon_{\text{elast}}}> 1$), where volume changes by a larger fraction than unit cost, a fall in unit cost raises volume enough to outweigh the saving ([[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/04 The Responsible Fleet/15 Sustainable AI/V2-15.10 Mitigation Strategies#^fig-jevons-ai|figure 18]]). When unit cost falls from ${P}_0$ to ${P}_1$, the green shaded area represents the saving on baseline volume ${Q}_0$. Demand expands to ${Q}_1$, creating a red area (${P}_1 ({Q}_1 - {Q}_0)$) that exceeds the original saving in this schematic. With monetary unit cost on the vertical axis, the areas measure expenditure. They represent energy only under an additional assumption that unit cost changes in proportion to energy per operation; an emissions comparison also needs the applicable carbon intensity.

Total energy depends on measured energy per query and query volume. Demand elasticity describes how volume responds to price; translating that response into energy requires a separate account of how unit energy changes.

**Checkpoint 15.5: The efficiency trap (Jevons paradox)**

An engineering team optimizes a translation service, reducing measured energy per query by 50 percent (2$\times$ efficiency gain) at fixed quality. Assume the service passes that saving through as a proportional price reduction and that grid intensity stays unchanged.

**Inelastic demand**: calculate the change in total energy consumption when demand is inelastic and price change does not affect usage.

**Elastic demand**: calculate the net change in total energy consumption when a 50 percent cost reduction leads to a 300 percent increase in query volume.

**Rebound effect**: define how this “rebound effect” challenges the assumption that “efficient models are automatically green models.”

The Jevons paradox does not invalidate efficiency as a strategy; it makes governance and capacity planning part of the result. At the level of individual systems, reducing energy per useful task remains a direct sustainability lever. A reduction in latency or monetary cost is a candidate improvement whose energy effect must still be measured.

**Systems Perspective 15.4: Efficiency as sustainability**

Model optimizations become sustainability tools only when they reduce measured lifecycle cost at fixed quality. Pruning can remove computation and memory traffic when the hardware skips the resulting structure. Quantization reduces representation size and may accelerate supported kernels, while knowledge distillation trades additional training for a smaller serving model. Each technique can also fail to reduce wall energy when its overhead or deployment regime leaves the dominant term unchanged.

Performance engineering supplies candidate interventions, but the environmental result still needs measurement. A faster run reduces energy only if the shorter duration outweighs any increase in average power; lower memory use helps only if it removes powered work or capacity. Hardware-software co-design becomes a sustainability gain when these savings survive the lifecycle boundary and the deployed workload volume.

Sustainable AI engineering overlaps strongly with efficient AI engineering but extends beyond it. The engineering principles that enable systems to scale, perform better, and cost less to operate can also reduce environmental impact, but sustainability adds lifecycle accounting, carbon-aware placement, absolute resource budgets, water and materials constraints, and governance against rebound effects. Sustainability is an integral part of good systems engineering, not a synonym for efficiency alone.

### Lifecycle-Aware Development

^sec-sustainable-ai-lifecycleaware-development-methodologies-bf40

Spending weeks quantizing a model whose footprint is dominated by repeated architecture searches, or moving a training job to a cleaner grid when year-round serving draws ten times more electricity, solves the wrong part of the inventory. Lifecycle-aware development starts from the largest environmental term in the workload and then selects the intervention that changes that term. Algorithmic design, infrastructure optimization, operational practice, and governance reduce impact only when they are sequenced around the measured bottleneck rather than applied as a generic checklist ([[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/04 The Responsible Fleet/15 Sustainable AI/V2-15.15 Source References#^ref-uddin2012|Uddin and Rahman 2012]]).

#### Energy-Efficient Algorithmic Design

^sec-sustainable-ai-energyefficient-algorithmic-design-e71c

Large deep learning models can require substantial computation during training and inference. The engineering question is how much of that work a particular task needs at its required quality. Parameter count alone does not identify redundant computation, and removing weights saves energy only if the resulting implementation avoids work or data movement. Sustainable algorithm design therefore evaluates the model and its execution together, measuring energy per useful task rather than treating a smaller representation as sufficient evidence of improvement.

When the measured bottleneck is unused structure, bit width, or serving scale, the sustainable design lever changes, because each bottleneck has its own intervention on the energy term ([[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/04 The Responsible Fleet/15 Sustainable AI/V2-15.10 Mitigation Strategies#^tbl-sustainable-ai-algorithmic-levers|table 12]]).

| **Technique**                                                                                                                                  | **Measured bottleneck** | **Energy mechanism**                                                                          | **Representative evidence**                                                                                                                                                                                                    |
|:-----------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------|:------------------------|:----------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------|:-------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------|
| **Pruning**                                                                                                                                    | Unused model structure  | Removes redundant weights, reducing model size, compute, and memory movement during inference | Structured pruning can remove complete channels or blocks; measure energy and quality for the resulting executable model                                                                                                       |
| **Quantization**                                                                                                                               | Bit width               | Lowers numerical precision so arithmetic units and memory transfers move fewer bits           | Q8BERT demonstrates an 8-bit BERT representation with a smaller model footprint and task-dependent quality retention ([[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/04 The Responsible Fleet/15 Sustainable AI/V2-15.15 Source References#^ref-zafrir2019\|Zafrir et al. 2019]]) |
| **Knowledge distillation** ([[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/04 The Responsible Fleet/15 Sustainable AI/V2-15.15 Source References#^ref-hinton2015distilling\|Hinton et al. 2015]]) | Serving scale           | Moves repeated inference cost into a one-time teacher-student training process                | DistilBERT retains 97% of BERT accuracy with 40% fewer parameters and 60% faster inference ([[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/04 The Responsible Fleet/15 Sustainable AI/V2-15.15 Source References#^ref-sanh2019distilbert\|Sanh et al. 2019]])                     |

Table 12: **Algorithmic Energy Levers by Bottleneck**: Pruning, quantization, and distillation reduce energy through different mechanisms, so the right choice depends on the measured bottleneck rather than on a generic compression goal.

^tbl-sustainable-ai-algorithmic-levers

The table’s three levers carry different deployment caveats. Pruning and quantization save energy only when the executed kernels skip arithmetic or move fewer bytes, which depends on sparsity structure, kernel support, and conversion overhead ([[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/04 The Responsible Fleet/15 Sustainable AI/V2-15.05 Energy Measurement and Modeling#^sec-sustainable-ai-optimization-techniques-save-energy-832c|section 15.4.1.2]]). Knowledge distillation[[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/04 The Responsible Fleet/15 Sustainable AI/V2-15.16 Footnotes#^fn20|²⁰]] instead amortizes its extra training cost across repeated serving.

Pruning, quantization, and distillation form the core toolkit for sustainable AI development, but their sustainability value depends on the measured bottleneck. The design question is not how to implement each compression method from first principles; it is how to rank these levers against memory movement, serving volume, carbon intensity, and lifecycle cost. A prioritized strategy orders them, together with carbon-aware scheduling, by the emissions term each one changes and attacks the largest term first.

**Checkpoint 15.6: Prioritizing decarbonization strategy**

Deploying a 70B LLM for a latency-sensitive application:

**Technique ranking**: state the measurements needed to rank INT4 quantization, unstructured pruning, carbon-aware scheduling, and knowledge distillation for this service. Separate reduced energy demand from reduced carbon per unit of energy.

**Systems justification**: propose a conditional ranking for a memory-bound service at fixed quality and latency, including hardware support, expected serving volume, extra training cost, and whether requests can move across regions or time.

#### Architectures That Avoid Unneeded Work

^sec-sustainable-ai-theoretical-efficiency-limits-d880

Architecture is a lever beyond compressing a given model, because it sets how much work the hardware performs at all. Specialized datapaths and local data reuse can reduce energy per useful operation, but a comparison must match workload, precision, achieved quality, and measurement boundary. Chip arithmetic alone excludes memory and supporting systems; end-to-end energy also includes their activity and execution time.

Under a fixed power envelope, the cheapest joule is the one the hardware never spends, so the choice of which work an architecture performs at all bears directly on the ceiling. Alternative computing architectures expose potential sources of efficiency: selective activation, local state, and reduced communication. Comparing their energy with a dense model requires a shared task, quality threshold, latency target, and measurement boundary.

The architectural opportunity is to avoid work that the task does not need. A periodically evaluated dense model may repeat computation on unchanged inputs, while an event-driven design can respond only when a relevant signal changes. The comparison reduces to two rates. A dense model evaluated ${f_{\text{eval}}}$ times per second at energy ${E_{\text{eval}}}$ per evaluation draws ${f_{\text{eval}}} {E_{\text{eval}}}$ whether or not its input changed. An event-driven design that fires ${r_{\text{event}}}$ times per second at energy ${E_{\text{event}}}$ draws ${r_{\text{event}}} {E_{\text{event}}}+ {P_{\text{detect}}}$, where ${P_{\text{detect}}}$ is the constant power of the circuitry that watches for events. Skipping work pays only while ${r_{\text{event}}} {E_{\text{event}}}+ {P_{\text{detect}}}< {f_{\text{eval}}} {E_{\text{eval}}}$, so the advantage shrinks as inputs change more often or as detection itself grows expensive. Dense execution can also reuse data and power down idle circuitry, so the comparison must examine the implemented workload rather than the architecture label alone.

Powering down inactive components helps either design only where the hardware supports it, and static power, sensing, and communication overhead remain part of the energy budget. Local learning and improved sample efficiency address different costs. Keeping an update local can reduce communication, while requiring fewer examples or optimization steps can reduce training work; neither property guarantees the other. Evaluation must include auxiliary preprocessing, adaptation, and inference costs at the required accuracy. Sparsity and sparse interconnects offer another route when execution skips arithmetic or transfers, but indexing, routing, load imbalance, and synchronization can consume the nominal saving.

Event-driven and sparse computation point toward research directions for sustainable AI. Architectures that implement **spiking neural networks (SNNs)**, event-driven models that communicate through discrete spikes, or sparse activation patterns can reduce energy. A neuron that does not spike neither computes nor sends a message, so switching and communication track input activity rather than a fixed clock[[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/04 The Responsible Fleet/15 Sustainable AI/V2-15.16 Footnotes#^fn21|²¹]] ([[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/04 The Responsible Fleet/15 Sustainable AI/V2-15.15 Source References#^ref-prakash2023tinyml|Prakash et al. 2023]]).

These directions expand the design space without making one architecture universally sustainable. The relevant improvement is less measured lifecycle impact per useful task at the required quality and service level. Dense, sparse, and event-driven implementations must meet the same comparison contract.

#### Lifecycle-Aware Systems

^sec-sustainable-ai-lifecycleaware-systems-5f26

Repeated model updates add to the lifecycle footprint even when each deployed version is efficient. The relevant comparison is the work required to maintain the service’s quality over time, including development and retraining. A retraining cycle that repeats the development sweep of [[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/04 The Responsible Fleet/15 Sustainable AI/V2-15.06 Carbon Footprint Calculation#^sec-sustainable-ai-comprehensive-carbon-accounting-methodologies-62fc|section 15.5.2]] pays its 5× final-run multiple again on every update. Transfer learning and fine-tuning can reuse a pretrained model instead of repeating training from scratch ([[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/04 The Responsible Fleet/15 Sustainable AI/V2-15.15 Source References#^ref-Raffel2020exploring|Raffel et al. 2020]]), but the comparison must include adaptation, evaluation, and any additional work needed to recover quality on the target task. Reuse is a candidate saving whose value depends on that complete update cycle.

The deployment boundary matters just as much as the training boundary. Edge deployment can reduce communication energy by running inference on specialized low-power hardware at the point of use ([[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/04 The Responsible Fleet/15 Sustainable AI/V2-15.15 Source References#^ref-xu2020edge|Xu et al. 2020]]), but the lifecycle account must include the embodied carbon of manufacturing and replacing those devices. Embedding LCA methodologies into AI workflows allows teams to see this trade-off early: a cloud model, an edge model, and a hybrid cascade may have different winners depending on query volume, device lifetime, grid carbon intensity, and retraining frequency ([[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/04 The Responsible Fleet/15 Sustainable AI/V2-15.15 Source References#^ref-iso140402006lca|International Organization for Standardization 2006a]], [[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/04 The Responsible Fleet/15 Sustainable AI/V2-15.15 Source References#^ref-iso140442006lca|2006b]]). Henderson et al. ([[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/04 The Responsible Fleet/15 Sustainable AI/V2-15.15 Source References#^ref-henderson2020towards|2020]]) argue for systematic reporting of ML energy and carbon footprints precisely so these comparisons can be made consistently. As the Jevons paradox warns, the accounting must also include usage growth, because optimizing one stage may increase total impact if lower costs enable wider deployment.

#### Benchmarks and Operating Metrics

^sec-sustainable-ai-policy-incentives-3370

Benchmarks matter when they make efficiency visible at procurement and design time. The ML.ENERGY Leaderboard ([[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/04 The Responsible Fleet/15 Sustainable AI/V2-15.15 Source References#^ref-mlenergy2023leaderboard|ML.ENERGY Initiative et al. 2023]]) reports inference energy measurements under declared model and serving configurations. MLPerf power submissions pair performance results with system-level power measurements under benchmark rules. These results support comparisons within the stated workload, quality, and measurement conditions; converting them into carbon footprints still requires electricity and lifecycle accounting beyond the benchmark.

The right efficiency metric depends on the serving regime. Batch inference is naturally expressed as correct samples per joule, latency-sensitive serving as queries per joule that meet the service-level objective (SLO), and generative workloads as joules per accepted output token at a declared quality threshold. Each is fleet energy productivity (**[[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/00 Introduction/01 Introduction/V2-01.06 The C³ Taxonomy and Foundations of Scale#^eq-energy-scale-invariant|equation 1.5]]**) with useful work counted in the regime’s own unit, and those queries are the requests that SLO goodput credits (**[[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/03 The Fleet in Production/10 Serving at Scale/V2-10.04 SLO Goodput and the Serving Tax#^sec-inference-scale-fundamental-inversion-training-vs-inference-8834|The inversion from training to inference]]**). Raw throughput per joule rewards systems that lower accuracy, truncate outputs, or miss latency objectives. Standardization does not make one platform universally green; it fixes useful work, quality, the measurement window, and the energy denominator well enough for procurement and architecture decisions to be argued quantitatively.

For sub-watt TinyML deployments, the MLPerf Tiny benchmark suite applies this discipline to constrained devices ([[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/04 The Responsible Fleet/15 Sustainable AI/V2-15.15 Source References#^ref-banbury2021mlperftiny|Banbury et al. 2021]]). Tasks include visual wake words, keyword spotting, anomaly detection, and image classification. The closed division constrains implementations to reference-equivalent models, while the open division permits broader model choices. Reading accuracy, latency, and energy together within the declared division prevents a change in task or model quality from masquerading as a hardware-efficiency gain.

Energy and latency still have to be read together through the energy-delay product (EDP) ([[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/04 The Responsible Fleet/15 Sustainable AI/V2-15.10 Mitigation Strategies#^eq-energy-delay-product|equation 16]]), which balances energy consumption against response time by penalizing solutions that save power only by taking too long:

$$
\text{EDP} = {E_{\text{total}}} \times {T}= {P_{\text{avg}}}\times {T}^2 \tag{16}
$$

^eq-energy-delay-product

where ${E_{\text{total}}}$ is energy consumed, ${T}$ is execution time, and ${P_{\text{avg}}}$ is average power. The quadratic delay term penalizes solutions that achieve low energy through excessive delays. Lower EDP is preferable under this particular trade-off score, after quality and latency requirements have been satisfied. It does not authorize missing a service deadline or replace energy per completed task.

For TinyML deployments, EDP provides a score for comparing feasible operating points. Reducing clock frequency can lower power while extending execution; reducing voltage can lower switching energy as well, when the hardware permits it. Static power and the longer active interval can offset those gains. The preferred setting therefore comes from measured energy and delay across supported configurations, after excluding settings that miss the workload’s deadlines.

Sustainability metrics complement performance benchmarks by recording the resource cost of useful work. Regulation is beginning to require such records. The EU AI Act’s documentation obligations for covered general-purpose models, examined with the other regulatory mechanisms in [[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/04 The Responsible Fleet/15 Sustainable AI/V2-15.11 Policy, Regulation, and the Path Forward#^sec-sustainable-ai-regulatory-mechanisms-global-coordination-4180|section 15.10.1]], include known or estimated energy consumption. Such requirements make the measurement boundary and provenance part of the reporting evidence rather than an optional annotation to a performance result.

### Infrastructure Optimization

^sec-sustainable-ai-infrastructure-optimization-1d41

Even the best-tuned training kernel cannot control how much power the chillers and transformers spend around the rack, or how much coal the regional grid burns to supply the substation. While algorithmic optimizations reduce operation counts, data movement, or the energy of execution, the surrounding operational environment determines the facility overhead and emissions associated with that work. Renewable energy integration, carbon-aware workload scheduling, and cooling optimization act on these surrounding terms, so each intervention must be evaluated at the boundary it changes.

A single hyperscale data center can consume over 100 MW of power, comparable to a small city.[[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/04 The Responsible Fleet/15 Sustainable AI/V2-15.16 Footnotes#^fn22|²²]] Renewable energy integration, advanced cooling, and AI-driven optimization offer complementary interventions. Their value depends on whether the measured problem lies in the electricity source, heat-removal overhead, or operating controls.

Power availability and power source are connected infrastructure decisions, but annual energy matching does not establish continuous supply. Organizations training large models may locate near renewable sources or purchase renewable energy certificates (RECs) to claim the attributes of renewable generation. A certificate does not physically deliver electricity or remove the facility’s demand from the grid; the design must still provide power when and where the workload runs.

Procurement can also support additional low-carbon supply. Microsoft’s 2024 agreement with Constellation supported a planned nuclear-reactor restart, while Google’s geothermal partnership with Fervo began supplying the Nevada grid in 2023. These examples address the need for generation beyond variable wind and solar output. They do not make one generation technology mandatory; the engineering requirement is a supply portfolio that meets the facility’s demand across time, with transmission, storage, and workload flexibility accounted for.

Major cloud providers have announced renewable-energy commitments, but variable generation leaves a supply-matching problem. Storage and flexible scheduling can help address it alongside transmission and other generation sources; the appropriate combination depends on the site and workload. Google’s published 2030 target for 24/7 carbon-free energy[[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/04 The Responsible Fleet/15 Sustainable AI/V2-15.16 Footnotes#^fn23|²³]] illustrates the harder version of this goal: matching electricity consumption with carbon-free generation, including nuclear as well as renewables, hour by hour rather than only on an annual basis.

Cooling overhead depends on rack density, climate, and the heat-removal design, as quantified in [[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/04 The Responsible Fleet/15 Sustainable AI/V2-15.07 Data Center Energy and Resource Consumption#^sec-sustainable-ai-cooling|section 15.6.3]]. Liquid cooling can support dense accelerator configurations, but its energy benefit depends on the complete pumping and heat-rejection system. Control policy supplies another lever. The DeepMind case in [[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/04 The Responsible Fleet/15 Sustainable AI/V2-15.05 Energy Measurement and Modeling#^sec-sustainable-ai-case-study-deepmind-energy-efficiency-84fd|section 15.4.1.4]] shows how measured operating data can guide cooling adjustments within thermal constraints; its reported savings describe the evaluated site rather than a universal reduction for any facility.

### Carbon-Aware Placement and Scheduling

^sec-sustainable-ai-carbonaware-scheduling-c2db

A batch training job that consumes the exact same megawatt-hours can emit several times more carbon if it runs during an evening fossil-gas peak or in a coal-heavy region than if it waits for a windy afternoon or runs on a hydro-heavy grid. Placement and scheduling change the emissions attributed to a fixed workload rather than the energy it uses: placement chooses the region a job runs in, and scheduling chooses its hours. Both depend on the grid signal they act on and on the additional energy the move itself causes.

↩︎ Scheduling against an external price signal, trading waiting and preemption against deadlines, is established in **[[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/02 Distributed ML/08 Fleet Orchestration/V2-08.09 Cost Optimization#^sec-fleet-orchestration-cost-scheduling|Cost-aware scheduling policies]]**.

#### Regional Grid Intensity Data Sources

^sec-sustainable-ai-regional-grid-intensity-data-sources-0b88

Every carbon-aware decision inherits the quality of the grid signal behind it, and the right signal depends on the decision. Grid carbon intensity varies with generation mix, which changes hourly with demand, renewable availability, and plant dispatch. The US Energy Information Administration publishes historical grid emissions factors by region, updated annually. These averages give a coarse baseline for inventories or early planning, but they cannot resolve the hourly effect of moving a flexible workload. Electricity Maps provides time-resolved, flow-traced average and lifecycle intensity estimates across covered grids, while WattTime provides marginal operating-emissions signals that estimate the generator response to added or removed load.

The two kinds of signal answer the two questions that the measurement contract ([[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/04 The Responsible Fleet/15 Sustainable AI/V2-15.05 Energy Measurement and Modeling#^sec-sustainable-ai-part-ii-measurement-assessment-fb0b|section 15.4]]) separated. Time-matched average factors support the attributional inventory of a completed run. Marginal factors support the consequential question of how shifting or eliminating a run would change system emissions, which is the question a scheduler asks.

#### Placement Across Regions and Hours

^sec-sustainable-ai-geographic-temporal-optimization-492c

Carbon intensity varies across geographic locations and time periods, creating optimization opportunities. Temporal scheduling can reduce emissions when deadline-tolerant workloads are shifted toward lower-carbon hours or regions, with the realized gain depending on workload flexibility, grid mix, and whether the scheduler uses average or marginal emissions ([[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/04 The Responsible Fleet/15 Sustainable AI/V2-15.15 Source References#^ref-patterson2022|Patterson et al. 2022]]; [[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/04 The Responsible Fleet/15 Sustainable AI/V2-15.15 Source References#^ref-radovanovic2021carbonaware|Radovanovic et al. 2021]]). **Carbon-aware scheduling** automates that shift, moving flexible, nonurgent work toward times and regions with lower carbon intensity while respecting its deadline and the available capacity.

A real placement decision weighs more than the grid factor. Adding electricity prices, facility overhead, and an internal carbon price turns the comparison into a placement objective, priced as a modeled total cost of ownership (TCO) over three candidate regions (the US-average grid, Quebec, and Iowa). Notebook [15.2](https://mlsysbook.ai/vol2/15_sustainable_ai/15_sustainable_ai.html#nbk-sustainable-ai-geography-carbon) isolated the grid factor by holding facility energy fixed. This objective must instead let facility overhead differ from region to region, so it holds the run’s IT energy fixed and lets each region apply its own PUE. That choice separates the carbon charge from the cost of facility overhead.

↩︎ The total-cost-of-ownership model that prices ownership, maintenance, and electricity is established in **[[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/03 The Fleet in Production/12 ML Operations at Scale/V2-12.10 Fleet Economics and Utilization#^sec-ml-operations-scale-tco-equation-169d|The TCO equation]]**.

**Example 15.1: Automated carbon-aware placement**

**Scenario**: A team is planning a large training run requiring 10,000 MWh of IT energy, which occupies an 8,192-GPU frontier-scale cluster for about 112 days. The team is choosing among three candidate regions (US average, Quebec, and Iowa) with different facility overheads (PUE) and grid carbon intensities.

**Diagnosis**: With an internal carbon tax of \$100/tonne, the placement model adds a carbon charge to each region’s modeled cost of ownership and selects Quebec for a total cost of \$25.3M. Hardware ownership and maintenance make up most of that total and are the same in every region, so the candidates differ only in facility overhead and carbon. Without a carbon price, Quebec’s lower PUE saves about \$72,000 against the US-average placement. With the tax, the US-average and Iowa placements cost \$0.5M and \$0.8M more than Quebec, and most of each gap is the carbon charge.

**Systems lesson**: Carbon-aware placement adds an explicit carbon price to the fleet scheduling objective. The selected region minimizes the modeled cost among these candidates under the stated assumptions. A deployment decision must also check capacity, data transfer, deadlines, and reliability.

^exmp-sustainable-ai-automated-carbon-aware-scheduling-tier-3-optimizer

The placement example compares regions using a fixed annual grid factor for each candidate. Temporal scheduling adds another decision by allowing a flexible job to wait for lower-carbon hours within one region. These are distinct levers, and their relative value depends on the available regions and the job’s deadline; [[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/04 The Responsible Fleet/15 Sustainable AI/V2-15.10 Mitigation Strategies#^sec-sustainable-ai-time-shifting-deadlines|section 15.9.4.3]] develops the temporal lever with the marginal signal that the measurement contract assigns to it.

#### Time-Shifting Under Deadlines

^sec-sustainable-ai-time-shifting-deadlines

Carbon-aware scheduling is a load-shifting software problem. The scheduler queries one of the grid signals compared above and combines it with workload flexibility. A feasible shift must finish before the deadline, retain access to data and accelerator capacity, and preserve the job’s reliability constraints. Its net benefit must exceed checkpoint, transfer, restart, and idle-capacity emissions. Pausing useful work without reducing the cluster’s powered load can erase the expected saving.

Google’s carbon-intelligent computing platform illustrates this approach by using forecasts and virtual capacity limits, hourly caps on the flexible compute a cluster may run that are set lower in high-carbon hours, to delay flexible work ([[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/04 The Responsible Fleet/15 Sustainable AI/V2-15.15 Source References#^ref-radovanovic2021carbonaware|Radovanovic et al. 2021]]). In the multi-tier accounting cascade of [[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/04 The Responsible Fleet/15 Sustainable AI/V2-15.10 Mitigation Strategies#^fig-energy-intervention|figure 17]], temporal load shifting modulates the grid emissions factor applied to facility electricity rather than reducing raw compute FLOPs. That is a carbon intervention, not automatically an energy reduction; checkpointing, transfer, and a different runtime can also change electricity consumption.

Because a scheduler asks how moving a job changes emissions, it needs a marginal signal rather than the average factor an inventory uses, and the providers compared above expose signals with different methodologies and coverage. A feasible schedule must satisfy three constraints on one time axis at once, the grid carbon signal $\text{CO}_{2,\text{grid}}({t})$, the job’s execution state, and cluster headroom ${N}({t})$ ([[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/04 The Responsible Fleet/15 Sustainable AI/V2-15.10 Mitigation Strategies#^fig-carbon-aware-scheduling|figure 19]]). The job waits, checkpointed, through the high-carbon peak, runs for ${T_{\text{job}}}$ in a low-carbon trough where headroom exists, and still finishes with slack before its deadline. Waiting pays only when the avoided grid emissions exceed the checkpoint I/O, idle standby, and state migration that the wait itself causes.

![[90 Assets/Volume II/15 Sustainable AI/Carbon Scheduling Constraints 551512c66f.svg]]

Figure 19: **A Feasible Carbon-Aware Schedule**: Three constraints share one time axis. The job waits, checkpointed, through the high-carbon hours of the grid signal and runs in the low-carbon window only where cluster headroom exists, finishing with slack before the deadline. The equation below the timeline charges the shift for its checkpoint, idle-wait, and migration carbon, so waiting pays only when the avoided emissions exceed those terms. The curve is schematic, not a measured grid trace.

^fig-carbon-aware-scheduling

A large distributed training run draws sustained aggregate power with the correlated phase transitions described in [[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/04 The Responsible Fleet/15 Sustainable AI/V2-15.07 Data Center Energy and Resource Consumption#^sec-sustainable-ai-power-delivery|section 15.6.2]], but its completion deadline may leave scheduling flexibility. Checkpoints created for fault recovery also provide the restart points for the deliberate pause in [[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/04 The Responsible Fleet/15 Sustainable AI/V2-15.10 Mitigation Strategies#^fig-carbon-aware-scheduling|figure 19]]. The decision requires a local emissions forecast and enough deadline slack; late-afternoon or overnight operation is not universally preferable. Fault tolerance thus supplies a mechanism for flexibility without proving that every pause saves emissions. Online inference has tighter delay budgets and may support bounded preemption, while offline inference can often be deferred more freely.

↩︎ Checkpoint-restart mechanics and the failure-driven choice of checkpoint interval are established in **[[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/02 Distributed ML/07 Fault Tolerance/V2-07.09 Checkpointing and Progress Preservation#^sec-fault-tolerance-reliability-reliability-checkpoint-restart-fundamentals-0ac2|Checkpointing and Progress Preservation]]**.

The **duck curve** describes the steep evening ramp in net load (total demand minus variable solar and wind output) as midday solar generation falls toward sunset while residential and commercial demand rises. Because grid operators typically meet that rapid net-load climb by dispatching fast-ramping fossil-gas generators, the evening ramp often coincides with a sharp spike in marginal carbon intensity. A constant data-center load raises the baseline level of net demand but does not by itself increase the evening ramp rate. A flexible training load, by contrast, can reduce both the required ramp and its own emissions by curtailing during that interval. Schedulers should still act on local forecasts and marginal emissions signals rather than a fixed daily pause window, which would ignore regional generation and transmission conditions.

Renewable generation variability complicates batch training dispatch across power grids. Over one week of November 2023, the European Union’s hourly generation mix moved across days as well as within each day ([[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/04 The Responsible Fleet/15 Sustainable AI/V2-15.10 Mitigation Strategies#^fig-europe-energy-grid|figure 20]]). Demand peaked each day between 326 GW and 367 GW. Nuclear held near 73 GW, solar contributed midday output, and wind ranged from 27 GW on Sunday to 111 GW on Thursday. Wind contributes to that variation, but demand, other generation, storage, and electricity exchanges also affect fossil dispatch. Carbon-aware scheduling therefore needs regional grid forecasts rather than a fixed assumption about which hours are cleanest.

![[90 Assets/Volume II/15 Sustainable AI/Cell 75 Output 1 f970a13ab6.png]]

Figure 20: **European Electricity Generation over One Week**: Hourly public net generation by source for the European Union in week 47 of 2023 (20 to 26 November), stacked, with the load curve on top. Nuclear forms a nearly flat base, hydro follows the daily demand cycle, and solar adds only small midday bulges this late in the year. Wind swings across days rather than hours, from about 27 GW on Sunday to about 111 GW on Thursday, so the fossil gas and coal that fill the remaining gap move from day to day. Lower-carbon windows for deferrable training therefore come from the forecast for a specific week and region, not from a fixed daily pattern. ### Google Sustainability Framework Case Study

^sec-sustainable-ai-case-study-googles-framework-e4c2

When Google engineers compared a baseline Transformer training setup against one that combined a sparser architecture, custom TPUs, an efficient cloud facility, and a low-carbon grid, they reported an 83-fold reduction in energy and a 747-fold reduction in operational $\text{CO}_2$ emissions ([[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/04 The Responsible Fleet/15 Sustainable AI/V2-15.15 Source References#^ref-patterson2022|Patterson et al. 2022]]). The value of Google’s case study is that it groups mitigation into four levers that correspond to terms this chapter has tracked, namely the model’s work, the machine’s efficiency, the facility’s overhead, and the grid’s carbon factor. Google engineers identified these “4 Ms” ([[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/04 The Responsible Fleet/15 Sustainable AI/V2-15.10 Mitigation Strategies#^tbl-sustainable-ai-google-four-ms|table 13]]) for reducing the carbon footprint of rapidly expanding AI workloads ([[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/04 The Responsible Fleet/15 Sustainable AI/V2-15.15 Source References#^ref-patterson2022|Patterson et al. 2022]]).

| **Lever**         | **Intervention**                                                                                      | **Reported efficiency effect**                                                                                               | **Example mechanism**                                                        |
|:------------------|:------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------|:-----------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------|:-----------------------------------------------------------------------------|
| **Model**         | Select efficient AI architectures such as sparse models or neural-architecture-search-derived designs | 5–10$\times$ lower computation requirements without compromising model quality                                               | Evolved Transformer and Primer                                               |
| **Machine**       | Use AI-specific hardware rather than general-purpose systems                                          | 2–5$\times$ performance-per-watt improvement; TPUs show 5–13$\times$ greater carbon efficiency relative to nonoptimized GPUs | Tensor Processing Units                                                      |
| **Mechanization** | Run optimized cloud infrastructure at high utilization                                                | 1.4–2$\times$ energy reduction compared to conventional on-premises data centers                                             | Lower facility PUE than the industry-average baseline used in the 2021 study |
| **Map**           | Place data centers in regions with low-carbon electricity supplies                                    | 5–10$\times$ lower gross emissions                                                                                           | Real-time monitoring of renewable energy usage across infrastructure         |

Table 13: **Google’s 4 Ms Sustainability Framework**: The framework combines model architecture, hardware specialization, infrastructure efficiency, and carbon-aware placement, each acting on a different term of the footprint. Their combined effect requires a consistent end-to-end account: component factors can be multiplied only when they describe compatible workloads and measurement boundaries.

^tbl-sustainable-ai-google-four-ms

In Patterson et al. ([[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/04 The Responsible Fleet/15 Sustainable AI/V2-15.15 Source References#^ref-patterson2022|2022]]), that 83-fold energy reduction and 747-fold CO<sub>2</sub> reduction come from a consistent accounting model supported by measurements across compatible workloads and boundaries; multiplying unrelated best-case component ratios would not establish the same result. In the period studied, AI workloads also represented less than 15 percent of Google’s total energy consumption despite expanding deployment. That share is evidence about relative consumption. It does not by itself establish constant or declining absolute energy when the denominator also changes, so engineers must assess absolute growth from measured energy totals alongside the reported efficiency improvements.

Empirical case studies demonstrate how sustainable-AI engineering can improve both capability and environmental impact. For example, Patterson et al. ([[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/04 The Responsible Fleet/15 Sustainable AI/V2-15.15 Source References#^ref-patterson2022|2022]]) compare GPT-3 with Google’s GLaM and report improved quality metrics alongside reduced training computation and lower-carbon energy sources, while the GLaM model paper explains the mixture-of-experts architecture behind that efficient scaling ([[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/04 The Responsible Fleet/15 Sustainable AI/V2-15.15 Source References#^ref-du2022glam|Du et al. 2022]]).

The strategy in the case study, which combines systematic measurement, carbon-aware development, transparency in reporting, and renewable energy transition, provides a practical template for sustainable AI scaling. The analysis also shows that earlier extrapolations overstated ML energy requirements by large factors when they ignored empirical efficiency gains and measurement boundaries. Technical levers alone, however, only save carbon when organizations have an incentive to deploy them and a bound that prevents rebound growth, which brings the engineering account to external policy, regulation, and governance.
