1.  **A hyperscaler plans a 500 MW campus for a new foundation model cluster, but the local utility interconnect approval is capped at 320 MW for the next three years. The organization’s capital reserves could cover the electricity cost multiple times over. Which framing best captures why sustainable AI treats energy as a first-class engineering constraint rather than a financial or reporting concern?**

    1.  Carbon accounting frameworks mandate disclosing total planned capacity upfront, creating an administrative filing hurdle before training begins.
    2.  Electricity price fluctuations create budget volatility, so the primary engineering intervention is financial hedging.
    3.  Corporate sustainability reporting requires purchasing voluntary offsets for unapproved megawatts, adding financial cost without altering physical infrastructure.
    4.  Power delivery and grid interconnect capacity impose a hard physical ceiling that capital cannot bypass on deployment timescales, making power an operational constraint that model architectures and cluster sizing must fit within.

    *Answer*: The correct answer is D. Power delivery and grid interconnect capacity impose a hard physical ceiling that capital cannot bypass on deployment timescales, making power an operational constraint that model architectures and cluster sizing must fit within. The chapter’s central thesis is that sustainability is a physical capacity ceiling, not a financial or reporting line item: a 500 MW cluster facing a 320 MW interconnect cannot operate at planned scale regardless of capital expenditure. An administrative-reporting framing mistakes accounting disclosures for physical bounds; a financial-hedging framing treats capacity as a price risk rather than a physical limit; and a voluntary-offset framing ignores physical grid interconnect constraints.

    *Learning Objective*: Classify energy and power delivery as physical system constraints rather than financial or reporting concerns

2.  **A research team plans a 10,000 MWh training run, measured as facility electricity. Quebec and Poland both meet its capacity, data-access, quality, and deadline requirements, and transfer overhead is negligible. The stated grid factors are approximately 20 and 820 grams of CO2 per kWh, respectively, on a common boundary. An algorithmic change would reduce facility energy in Poland by 15 percent at unchanged quality. Calculate the emissions under these alternatives and justify which offers the larger reduction in this scenario.**

    *Answer*: At the supplied factors, Quebec emits 200 tonnes of CO2 and Poland emits 8,200 tonnes. The algorithmic change in Poland lowers that result to 6,970 tonnes, so Quebec offers the larger reduction among these alternatives. This conclusion depends on the stated equal-energy baseline and feasible placements; a regional intensity ratio alone does not rank unmeasured interventions or establish the effect of shifting actual grid demand.

    *Learning Objective*: Calculate scenario emissions and compare feasible placement and algorithmic interventions under a declared accounting boundary

3.  **True or False: Because successive generations of specialized AI accelerators deliver substantial improvements in energy efficiency (FLOP/s per watt), organizations can rely on hardware innovation alone to keep total data center power consumption flat while scaling model parameters.**

    *Answer*: False. Hardware improvements alone do not guarantee flat fleet power. Total energy depends on workload volume and realized work per joule at a consistent boundary; power also depends on the execution schedule. A historical compute-growth curve does not determine either quantity without those measurements.

    *Learning Objective*: Evaluate the claim that hardware efficiency scaling alone can offset exponential growth in AI compute demand

4.  **Thousands of GPUs alternate between dense computation and collective communication, producing correlated changes in device power. Explain how bidirectional energy storage can buffer the change at a protected electrical boundary, and what measurements determine its timing and capacity.**

    *Answer*: When load falls, storage can absorb surplus input; when load rises, it can discharge to cover the deficit while upstream supply responds. The controller must follow the measured load and supply response at the protected boundary, including losses, state of charge, and power limits. Device-side changes need not propagate unchanged to the grid, and buffering can act during a transition rather than only after it.

    *Learning Objective*: Explain how energy storage buffers synchronized training load changes and identify the measurements needed to size it

1.  **A 2,000 MWh training run can be placed at a site whose annual-average grid factor is 400 g CO<sub>2</sub> per kWh or at one whose factor is 20 g CO<sub>2</sub> per kWh, with facility electricity held equal. Which conclusion follows?**

    1.  The cleaner site also cuts the run’s energy twentyfold, because low-carbon grids deliver electricity more efficiently.
    2.  Attributed emissions fall from about 800 tonnes to about 40 tonnes, a twentyfold difference that isolates the grid factor at equal facility energy.
    3.  This comparison alone ranks placement against an algorithmic change, because both reduce emissions.
    4.  Waiting for cleaner hours at the cleaner site would multiply the saving by a further twentyfold.

    *Answer*: The correct answer is B. Emissions equal facility energy times the grid factor, so 2,000,000 kWh gives about 800 tonnes at 400 g per kWh and about 40 tonnes at 20 g per kWh. The grid factor changes attributed emissions, not the energy the run consumes. A placement comparison isolates one factor and cannot rank an unmeasured algorithmic change, and a temporal gain cannot simply be multiplied onto an annual-average placement gain.

    *Learning Objective*: Calculate attributed training emissions from facility energy and grid factor, and identify what a placement comparison does and does not establish.

2.  **True or False: Because training compute for notable models has grown by many orders of magnitude, training energy has grown by the same multiplier.**

    *Answer*: False. A compute multiplier becomes an energy multiplier only after dividing by realized work per joule at a stated boundary. Efficiency gains in hardware and execution can make energy grow more slowly than compute, so the energy trend must be measured rather than read off a compute curve.

    *Learning Objective*: Distinguish growth in training compute from growth in training energy.

3.  **An operator reports its AI fleet’s environmental footprint as metered electricity multiplied by the grid factor. What does this account leave out?**

    1.  Nothing, because operational electricity is the only environmental cost of AI hardware.
    2.  Only facility overhead, because grid factors already include the cost of manufacturing.
    3.  A fixed disposal charge per device, because retiring any accelerator necessarily creates e-waste.
    4.  Manufacturing impacts that precede the first training run, such as embodied carbon, ultrapure water, and specialty gases, and the land, water, and grid capacity that host communities supply.

    *Answer*: The correct answer is D. Producing processors carries embodied carbon and consumes water, gases, and chemicals before any job runs, and hosting a fleet assigns land, water, grid capacity, and disposal burdens to communities. Grid factors describe electricity supply, not manufacturing. Retirement does not necessarily create waste, because equipment can be redeployed, resold, or recycled, so disposal must be estimated from the actual end-of-life route.

    *Learning Objective*: Identify the environmental costs that an electricity-only footprint omits.

4.  **A Quebec-versus-Poland comparison shows a 41-fold emissions difference for the same 10,000 MWh run, and carbon-aware scheduling can also shift work to cleaner hours. Explain why the geographic and temporal gains cannot simply be multiplied, and how the decision should be evaluated.**

    *Answer*: Both levers act on the same quantity, the emissions factor of the electricity that supplies a fixed amount of energy, and the 41-fold ratio already uses annual-average factors at equal facility energy. At a hydro-dominated site the hourly factor stays close to its low average, so waiting for cleaner hours there leaves little additional gain. The placement and the schedule must therefore be evaluated as one decision, using the hourly or marginal factors the schedule would actually see along with the job’s power, deadline, and capacity constraints.

    *Learning Objective*: Explain why geographic and temporal carbon levers must be evaluated jointly rather than multiplied.

1.  **An inference optimization cuts energy per task by a factor of 4, and the lower cost raises task volume by a factor of 20, with the workload mix unchanged. What happens to fleet energy?**

    1.  It falls by a factor of 4, because per-task efficiency sets fleet energy.
    2.  It stays the same, because efficiency and demand cancel.
    3.  It rises by a factor of 5, because fleet energy is energy per task multiplied by the number of tasks.
    4.  It rises by a factor of 80, because the efficiency gain and the demand growth compound.

    *Answer*: The correct answer is C. Fleet energy is energy per completed task times the number of completed tasks, so one quarter of the energy times 20 times the volume gives five times the energy. Reading only the per-task gain misses the demand response, and multiplying both factors as growth treats the efficiency gain as a cost. This rebound differs from scaling-driven growth, which raises the compute each frontier model requires.

    *Learning Objective*: Calculate fleet energy under rebound and distinguish rebound from scaling-driven demand growth.

2.  **A team plans a three-day run that needs 120 MW of accelerator power at once in a facility with an 80 MW grid feed. They argue the run fits because its total energy is less than the facility uses in a month. What is wrong with the argument?**

    1.  The power limit is instantaneous, so the feed caps how many servers can be active at once regardless of run length; the plan must lower concurrency and accept a longer run, or obtain more deliverable power.
    2.  Nothing, because over a month an energy budget and a power cap are interchangeable.
    3.  The run fails only on cost, because energy drawn above the feed is billed at a peak tariff.
    4.  The only error is omitting PUE; adding facility overhead would show that the run’s energy exceeds the monthly budget.

    *Answer*: The correct answer is A. Facility power cannot exceed the grid feed at any instant, which caps active servers whatever the job’s duration, while energy integrates average power over time and enters the electricity and emissions accounts. A modest energy total therefore cannot license a power draw above the feed. Treating the limits as interchangeable confuses the two physical constraints, and the tariff and PUE answers address the energy account rather than the power cap.

    *Learning Objective*: Distinguish the instantaneous power envelope from the cumulative energy account when judging a training plan’s feasibility.

3.  **Synchronized training phases swing accelerator draw together across thousands of devices, and a battery or UPS stage that supplies or absorbs energy during each load change provides energy \_\_\_\_, whose location, capacity, and control response must match the electrical boundary being protected.**

    *Answer*: buffering. Energy buffering smooths the load step that coordinated training phases would otherwise push toward the utility connection; it protects a specific electrical boundary rather than reducing the run’s total energy.

    *Learning Objective*: Identify the grid-interface mechanism that absorbs correlated training load swings and state what it does and does not change.

4.  **True or False: Because a frontier training run’s yearly energy is a modest share of a region’s consumption, the facility can be provisioned for that run’s annual-average power.**

    *Answer*: False. A training run concentrates its draw into weeks or months of sustained peak, and synchronized phases swing that draw together across the cluster. The facility must provision for the sustained peak, and the grid sees one large correlated load rather than many small independent ones.

    *Learning Objective*: Explain why temporal concentration, not annual energy, sets the facility and grid requirements of a training campaign.

5.  **In the regime studied by Kaplan et al., each 10 percent reduction in compute-limited loss costs about 8.2 times the compute. Explain what two successive 10 percent reductions imply for training energy, and what would have to hold for energy not to grow by the same factor.**

    *Answer*: The multiples compound, so two successive 10 percent reductions cost about 8.2 times 8.2, or roughly 67 times, the original compute, because the small compute exponent makes each fixed fractional loss reduction a multiplicative increase in work. Training energy grows by the same factor unless realized FLOPs per joule improve at the same pace, which historical hardware gains have not matched. The diminishing return of the scaling law therefore reaches the facility as a multiplicative energy demand, which is why compute demand has outrun hardware efficiency.

    *Learning Objective*: Analyze how the scaling-law compute exponent translates successive quality gains into multiplicative training energy.

1.  **An attention kernel has an arithmetic intensity of 3 FLOP/byte. An illustrative chip-and-memory energy model assigns 10 pJ per FLOP and 160 pJ per byte moved from DRAM, excluding static and facility energy. Which intervention directly targets the larger modeled energy contribution?**

    1.  Replacing the accelerator with one offering double the peak TFLOP/s while retaining the same memory bandwidth and memory technology.
    2.  Increasing arithmetic precision from FP16 to FP32 to execute more FLOPs per memory transaction.
    3.  Fusing normalization and attention operators and tiling intermediate activations to keep data in on-chip SRAM, reducing DRAM traffic.
    4.  Lowering facility PUE from 1.5 to 1.1 to eliminate memory stall cycles.

    *Answer*: The correct answer is C. The crossover is 16 FLOP/byte, so at 3 FLOP/byte movement contributes more energy than arithmetic in this model. Fusion and tiling can reduce that traffic when implemented successfully. Peak throughput alone establishes neither an energy saving nor the execution bottleneck; PUE does not eliminate memory stalls. Measure the complete workload to confirm the saving.

    *Learning Objective*: Apply the energy-roofline crossover to classify a kernel’s dominant energy component and select the corresponding optimization

2.  **A 2 MW IT load cluster improves its Power Usage Effectiveness (PUE) from 1.58 to 1.10 without altering any model weights or server SKUs. Calculate the annual electricity savings in megawatt-hours (MWh) assuming 24/7 continuous operation, and explain why facility optimization is considered a first-order sustainability lever.**

    *Answer*: At $\text{PUE} = 1.58$, total facility power is $2\text{ MW} \times 1.58 = 3.16\text{ MW}$. At $\text{PUE} = 1.10$, total power drops to $2\text{ MW} \times 1.10 = 2.20\text{ MW}$, saving $0.96\text{ MW}$ continuously. Over one year ($8{,}760\text{ hours}$), this saves $0.96\text{ MW} \times 8{,}760\text{ h} \approx 8{,}410\text{ MWh}$. This demonstrates that facility infrastructure optimization delivers multi-gigawatt-hour savings equivalent to massive algorithmic optimizations without requiring changes to model code or accuracy.

    *Learning Objective*: Calculate annual facility energy savings resulting from PUE improvements and justify infrastructure optimization as a high-leverage sustainability intervention

3.  **In the CMOS dynamic power equation $P_{\text{dynamic}}=\alpha_{\text{sw}}CV^2f$, the parameter $\alpha_{\text{sw}}$ is the `____`, summarizing capacitance-weighted switching per clock cycle under the chosen counting convention.**

    *Answer*: switching activity factor. It scales dynamic power linearly at fixed capacitance, voltage, and frequency; more switching is not necessarily more useful computation or greater energy efficiency.

    *Learning Objective*: Explain the role of the switching activity factor in the CMOS dynamic power equation

4.  **An engineer needs to profile the energy consumption of a sub-watt microcontroller executing an on-device wake-word detection model that spends 98 percent of its time in deep sleep drawing 30 $\mu\text{W}$. The microcontroller lacks on-chip energy counters. Which measurement approach is appropriate?**

    1.  Sampling the Linux RAPL interface at 100 Hz to capture CPU package power states.
    2.  Querying NVML/nvidia-smi programmatic bindings to integrate instantaneous milliwatt metrics.
    3.  Multiplying model parameter count by a theoretical constant of 5 pJ per parameter.
    4.  Using a calibrated external analyzer with verified current range, analog bandwidth, synchronized voltage measurement, and integration windows covering sleep, active execution, and transitions.

    *Answer*: The correct answer is D. An external analyzer must resolve both sleep and active states at the declared electrical boundary. Its configured range, bandwidth, calibration, voltage measurement, and integration interval determine whether it can do so; a fast sample rate or product name alone is insufficient. RAPL and NVML do not supply this microcontroller measurement, and parameter count omits duty-cycle and peripheral costs.

    *Learning Objective*: Select instrumentation capable of measuring the declared duty cycle and electrical boundary of a TinyML system

5.  **True or False: To determine the total environmental energy impact of an AI workload, an engineering team can simply sum the measured energy of the GPUs, CPUs, DRAM, and network interface cards reported by hardware telemetry.**

    *Answer*: False. Component measurements must cover disjoint IT loads over the same interval; GPU telemetry that includes device memory must not be added to that memory again. Restore omitted support loads, then use facility meters or a time-matched PUE to include non-IT overhead. Electricity alone still omits manufacturing and other lifecycle impacts.

    *Learning Objective*: Evaluate the requirement of incorporating facility overhead (PUE) into comprehensive AI energy accounting

6.  **Explain how the Energy-Delay Product ($\text{EDP} = E \times t$) guides operating point selection in TinyML systems, and identify how voltage scaling interacts with clock frequency to minimize EDP.**

    *Answer*: EDP compares energy multiplied by execution time across feasible operating points. First exclude settings that miss quality or deadline requirements, then measure energy and delay for supported voltage-frequency combinations. Lower voltage can reduce switching energy, but a longer runtime adds static and support energy. The measured curves determine the optimum; the formula does not guarantee a moderate frequency or a universally beneficial voltage reduction.

    *Learning Objective*: Explain how Energy-Delay Product (EDP) balances voltage scaling, clock frequency, and static leakage in edge systems

1.  **Which equation pair correctly formalizes operational carbon emissions and daily amortized embodied carbon for AI hardware according to the chapter’s accounting methodology?**

    1.  $C_{\text{operational}} = E_{\text{IT}} / \text{PUE}$ and $C_{\text{embodied,daily}} = C_{\text{manufacturing}} \times T_{\text{lifetime}}$.
    2.  $C_{\text{operational}} = E_{\text{total}} + \text{CI}_{\text{grid}}$ and $C_{\text{embodied,daily}} = C_{\text{manufacturing}} / 365$.
    3.  $C_{\text{operational}} = E_{\text{total}} \times \text{PUE} \times \text{CI}_{\text{grid}}$ and $C_{\text{embodied,daily}} = C_{\text{manufacturing}} \times 365 / T_{\text{lifetime}}$.
    4.  $C_{\text{operational}} = E_{\text{total}} \times \text{CI}_{\text{grid}}$ (where $E_{\text{total}} = E_{\text{IT}} \times \text{PUE}$) and $C_{\text{embodied,daily}} = \frac{C_{\text{manufacturing}}}{T_{\text{lifetime}} \times 365}$.

    *Answer*: The correct answer is D. $C_{\text{operational}} = E_{\text{total}} \times \text{CI}_{\text{grid}}$ (where $E_{\text{total}} = E_{\text{IT}} \times \text{PUE}$) and $C_{\text{embodied,daily}} = \frac{C_{\text{manufacturing}}}{T_{\text{lifetime}} \times 365}$. Operational carbon is facility energy (IT energy scaled by PUE) multiplied by grid carbon intensity, and embodied carbon is total manufacturing emissions divided by expected lifetime in days ($T_{\text{lifetime}} \times 365$). Dividing IT energy by PUE inverts facility overhead; adding energy to carbon intensity mixes incompatible units; and multiplying facility energy by PUE a second time double-counts facility overhead.

    *Learning Objective*: Compare the mathematical formulations for operational carbon and amortized embodied carbon

2.  **When a hyperscaler relocates a training cluster from a fossil-heavy grid (800 $\text{gCO}_2/\text{kWh}$) to a clean hydro grid (20 $\text{gCO}_2/\text{kWh}$), its sustainability dashboard reveals that embodied carbon becomes the dominant component of total lifecycle emissions. Explain the mechanism behind this inversion and its implications for hardware procurement.**

    *Answer*: With facility energy held constant, changing the scenario grid intensity from 800 to 20 gCO2/kWh reduces operational emissions by 97.5%, while manufacturing emissions are unchanged. The embodied share therefore rises, but its final percentage requires the actual manufacturing footprint and lifetime energy. Procurement should compare lifetime extension and replacement using that lifecycle boundary rather than assume a universal embodied-carbon share.

    *Learning Objective*: Analyze why grid decarbonization shifts the dominant lifecycle carbon component from operational to embodied emissions

3.  \*\*An engineering team is auditing the greenhouse gas emissions of an AI platform. Which option correctly maps the five operational sources to their respective GHG Protocol scopes?

- S1: Diesel fuel burned by backup generators during a power outage at an owned facility.

- S2: Grid electricity purchased to power an owned GPU cluster.

- S3: Grid electricity consumed by cooling chillers in the same owned facility.

- S4: Embodied carbon from manufacturing the GPUs at an external foundry.

- S5: Electrical energy consumed by end-user smartphones running the deployed mobile inference app.\*\*

  1.  S1: Scope 1; S2: Scope 2; S3: Scope 2; S4: Scope 3; S5: Scope 3.
  2.  S1: Scope 2; S2: Scope 1; S3: Scope 2; S4: Scope 3; S5: Scope 3.
  3.  S1: Scope 1; S2: Scope 2; S3: Scope 1; S4: Scope 2; S5: Scope 3.
  4.  S1: Scope 3; S2: Scope 2; S3: Scope 2; S4: Scope 1; S5: Scope 1.

  *Answer*: The correct answer is A. S1: Scope 1; S2: Scope 2; S3: Scope 2; S4: Scope 3; S5: Scope 3. Scope 1 encompasses direct emissions from owned combustion sources (on-site diesel generators). Scope 2 covers indirect emissions from purchased electricity (powering servers and facility cooling equipment). Scope 3 covers value-chain emissions upstream (semiconductor manufacturing at external foundries) and downstream (end-user mobile device execution). Classifying diesel as Scope 2 confuses direct combustion with purchased utility power; classifying cooling electricity as Scope 1 treats purchased utility power as on-site combustion; and classifying upstream semiconductor fabrication as Scope 1 misattributes supplier manufacturing to direct operations.

  *Learning Objective*: Classify AI system emissions sources into Scope 1, Scope 2, and Scope 3 under the GHG Protocol

4.  **The metric representing the mass of carbon dioxide emitted per unit of electricity generated, expressed in grams of $\text{CO}_2$ per kilowatt-hour, is known as `____`.**

    *Answer*: carbon intensity. Grid carbon intensity ($\text{gCO}_2/\text{kWh}$) varies by location and time, governing the operational emissions associated with electricity consumption.

    *Learning Objective*: Explain how grid carbon intensity translates electrical energy into operational carbon emissions

5.  **A production language model consumed 1,200 MWh during its one-time training run. In production, it serves 8 million requests per day, consuming 0.0005 kWh per request. After how many days of continuous serving will the cumulative inference energy reach the one-time training energy?**

    1.  120 days.
    2.  300 days.
    3.  600 days.
    4.  1,200 days.

    *Answer*: The correct answer is B. 300 days. Daily inference energy is $8{,}000{,}000\text{ requests/day} \times 0.0005\text{ kWh/request} = 4{,}000\text{ kWh/day} = 4\text{ MWh/day}$. Dividing total training energy by daily inference energy yields $1{,}200\text{ MWh} / 4\text{ MWh/day} = 300\text{ days}$. A calculation yielding 120 days underestimates the crossover period; 600 days assumes half the daily inference volume; and 1,200 days erroneously assumes 1 MWh/day serving energy.

    *Learning Objective*: Calculate the crossover point where cumulative inference energy exceeds one-time training energy

6.  **Give one valid order for the following steps in computing the specified operational-plus-accelerator-manufacturing carbon subtotal of a model training workload: (1) multiply IT energy by facility PUE to obtain total facility energy consumption, (2) amortize accelerator manufacturing footprint over total operational lifetime to calculate the job’s embodied share, (3) measure or estimate raw IT electrical power and duration across GPUs, CPUs, and memory, (4) sum operational and embodied carbon to determine the accounted subtotal, (5) multiply total facility energy by regional grid carbon intensity to determine operational carbon.**

    *Answer*: One valid order is: (3) measure or estimate raw IT electrical power and duration across GPUs, CPUs, and memory, (1) multiply IT energy by facility PUE to obtain total facility energy consumption, (5) multiply total facility energy by regional grid carbon intensity to determine operational carbon, (2) amortize accelerator manufacturing footprint over total operational lifetime to calculate the job’s embodied share, (4) sum operational and embodied carbon to determine the accounted subtotal. IT energy must be measured first; facility PUE is applied to calculate total electrical draw; grid carbon intensity converts facility energy into operational emissions; embodied manufacturing carbon is amortized over the run’s duration; and both components are summed for this partial lifecycle account. Manufacturing of hosts, networking, storage, and facilities, and any other lifecycle stages outside the stated inventory, must be added before claiming a complete footprint. PUE and carbon intensity may be multiplied in either order, but facility overhead must be included exactly once; embodied carbon can be computed independently before the final sum.

    *Learning Objective*: Analyze the sequential calculation workflow for AI lifecycle carbon accounting

1.  **A cloud provider can procure and install 10,000 GPUs within six months, but building the required electrical substation and securing utility interconnect approval takes 24 months. Explain why electrical power delivery represents the binding bottleneck for data center expansion, and state the systems consequence of this lead-time disparity.**

    *Answer*: In this scenario, the 24-month power-delivery path is four times the six-month GPU-procurement path and gates operation at the intended scale. Delivered accelerators can remain electrically stranded until the connection is ready. Actual lead times and alternatives are site-specific, so procurement must follow a qualified supply schedule rather than assume this ratio applies everywhere.

    *Learning Objective*: Explain how power delivery infrastructure and utility interconnect lead times constrain large-scale AI cluster deployments

2.  **A facility audit lists conversion losses in five places: the utility transformer, the online UPS, the facility power distribution units, the server power supplies, and the baseboard voltage regulators. Which losses raise the facility’s PUE?**

    1.  The transformer, UPS, and facility distribution losses, because they lie upstream of the IT boundary; server power supplies and voltage regulators sit inside the measured IT load.
    2.  All five, because every conversion loss turns delivered power into heat that the cooling system must remove.
    3.  Only the server power supplies and voltage regulators, because they sit closest to the accelerators that do the useful work.
    4.  None of them, because PUE measures cooling overhead and conversion losses are electrical rather than thermal.

    *Answer*: The correct answer is A. PUE divides facility energy by IT energy, so only losses outside the IT boundary add to the overhead: the transformer, UPS, and facility distribution. Server power supplies and baseboard regulators are part of the measured IT load and appear in both numerator and denominator, although their heat still loads the cooling system. Counting every loss as overhead double counts the IT-side terms, and PUE covers all non-IT facility load, not only cooling.

    *Learning Objective*: Assign power-delivery losses to the correct side of the PUE accounting boundary

3.  **Facility A reports a Power Usage Effectiveness (PUE) of 1.08 on an electrical grid with carbon intensity of $450\text{ g CO}_2/\text{kWh}$. Facility B reports PUE 1.35 on a grid with $50\text{ g CO}_2/\text{kWh}$. For a $10\text{ MW}$ IT compute load, calculate the total hourly electrical power drawn and carbon emissions for both facilities, and explain the limitation of using PUE as a sole environmental metric.**

    *Answer*: At $10\text{ MW}$ IT load: Facility A draws $10\text{ MW} \times 1.08 = 10.8\text{ MW}$ ($10{,}800\text{ kW}$) total power, emitting $10{,}800\text{ kW} \times 450\text{ g CO}_2/\text{kWh} = 4{,}860\text{ kg CO}_2/\text{hr}$ ($4.86\text{ tonnes/hr}$). Facility B draws $10\text{ MW} \times 1.35 = 13.5\text{ MW}$ ($13{,}500\text{ kW}$) total power, emitting $13{,}500\text{ kW} \times 50\text{ g CO}_2/\text{kWh} = 675\text{ kg CO}_2/\text{hr}$ ($0.675\text{ tonnes/hr}$). Facility B emits $\approx 7.2\times$ less carbon than Facility A despite having a significantly worse PUE (1.35 vs. 1.08). PUE measures only facility power relative to IT-equipment power, not the cleanliness of the electricity source, so optimizing PUE in isolation can mislead teams into high-carbon grid locations.

    *Learning Objective*: Calculate facility power draw and carbon emissions using PUE and grid carbon intensity to evaluate environmental impact.

4.  **A 10 MW facility in a water-stressed region can reject heat through evaporative cooling towers or through dry coolers. Explain the water and energy trade-off between the two, and why the operator might accept higher fan energy.**

    *Answer*: Evaporation carries large amounts of heat with a small water flow because each kilogram evaporated removes its latent heat, but the evaporated water is consumed and must be replaced, which shows up as WUE. Dry coolers avoid that direct water consumption, but their capacity depends on the coolant-to-ambient temperature difference, so hot weather reduces capacity or requires more fan power. In a water-stressed watershed the local water burden can outweigh the added electricity, so the operator may choose dry or hybrid rejection while still counting the upstream water of the electricity it buys.

    *Learning Objective*: Evaluate the water-energy trade-off between evaporative and dry heat rejection

1.  **Which systems analogy and operational reality best explains why inference often dominates total lifecycle energy for widely deployed foundation models?**

    1.  Inference requires higher instantaneous wattage per accelerator than training because real-time serving disables dynamic voltage scaling.
    2.  Training is like a one-time rocket launch that burns concentrated energy over several weeks, whereas inference is like operating a commercial airline fleet that burns energy continuously across billions of recurring user interactions over years.
    3.  Inference models must perform a full backward pass on every user query to maintain accuracy, doubling computational cost.
    4.  Inference cannot run on specialized accelerators, forcing cloud operators to use energy-inefficient legacy CPUs.

    *Answer*: The correct answer is B. A training run concentrates energy in a bounded interval, while serving repeats for the product’s lifetime. Sufficient demand can make cumulative serving energy exceed training energy, but volume, workload mix, idle capacity, and updates determine the crossover. Neither instantaneous power nor an assumed backward pass establishes it; inference can use specialized accelerators.

    *Learning Objective*: Analyze why cumulative inference energy dominates one-time training energy for large-scale production models

2.  **A distributed training job expands from 1,024 to 2,048 accelerators. Explain which mechanisms can increase communication energy and what measurements establish its share of step energy for the actual run.**

    *Answer*: Topology, collective algorithm, message volume, overlap, stragglers, and powered waiting determine the energy change; a larger participant count does not guarantee super-linear growth for every collective. Estimate phase energy from measured power integrated over each phase and compare with the whole-step account. Exposed communication time equals its energy fraction only under an explicit equal-average-power approximation; continued power draw alone does not justify that equality.

    *Learning Objective*: Analyze communication-energy mechanisms and estimate their contribution from phase power and duration

3.  **Profiling an autoregressive LLM serving pipeline shows that the prefill phase achieves 55 percent Model FLOPs Utilization (MFU), while the token-generation decode phase achieves only 6 percent MFU with high HBM bandwidth utilization. What is the physical mechanism causing decode energy inefficiency?**

    1.  Decode executes in FP32 precision while prefill executes in INT4 precision.
    2.  Prefill bypasses high-bandwidth memory entirely by storing all weights in CPU host RAM.
    3.  Decode requires running backpropagation to generate key-value cache embeddings for each new token.
    4.  Decode is memory-bandwidth bound: each generated token requires reading all model weights from HBM with low arithmetic intensity, leaving tensor execution units idling while drawing static leakage and clock distribution power.

    *Answer*: The correct answer is D. High HBM utilization with low MFU is consistent with a bandwidth-bound decode phase whose limited batch reuse leaves arithmetic units underused. Powered waiting can add static and support energy. These measurements do not establish a fixed prefill-to-decode energy ratio; compare measured energy under declared batch, context, output quality, latency, and hardware boundaries. Decode does not require backpropagation.

    *Learning Objective*: Explain the microarchitectural mechanism behind the decode phase’s low energy efficiency in LLM serving

4.  **A mobile application team proposes running nightly on-device full backpropagation fine-tuning for a 1B-parameter model on user smartphones. Using the concept of the ‘battery wall’, explain how to assess a 5 percent overnight battery budget and identify alternatives if the measured workload exceeds it.**

    *Answer*: A 15 Wh battery contains 54,000 J, so a 5% budget permits 2,700 J. Whether a 1B-parameter training workload fits depends on its measured energy per token, training length, memory footprint, and thermal limits; parameter count alone cannot establish infeasibility. PEFT can reduce trainable parameters and optimizer state, but LoRA still backpropagates through the relevant network and need not remove activation storage. Profile the complete update and consider fewer local steps, off-device training, or charging-only execution.

    *Learning Objective*: Evaluate an on-device update against battery, memory, thermal, and quality requirements and identify alternatives when it exceeds them

5.  **A TinyML sensor runs inference for 10 ms at 20 mW active power and sleeps for 990 ms at 20 $\mu\text{W}$ deep-sleep power during a 1-second monitoring period. Calculate the average power draw, and explain how duty cycling enables multi-year battery operation.**

    *Answer*: The active contribution to average power is $20\text{ mW}\times0.010=0.20\text{ mW}$; the sleep contribution is $0.020\text{ mW}\times0.990=0.0198\text{ mW}$. Their sum is $0.2198\text{ mW}$, about 91 times below continuous active power. This saves energy, but multi-year operation depends on usable battery capacity, self-discharge, and peripheral loads. Even one year at this average requires about 1.93 Wh before those additional costs.

    *Learning Objective*: Calculate average power in duty-cycled TinyML systems and explain how duty cycling extends operational battery life

6.  **Order the stages of a hierarchical wake-word cascade designed to minimize average energy consumption on a smart speaker from lowest power to highest power: (1) large language model or full automatic speech recognition running on host processor or cloud, (2) small convolutional or recurrent neural network wake-word classifier verifying the trigger phrase, (3) ultra-low-power Voice Activity Detection (VAD) circuit listening continuously at microwatt power.**

    *Answer*: The correct order is: (3) ultra-low-power Voice Activity Detection (VAD) circuit listening continuously at microwatt power, (2) small convolutional or recurrent neural network wake-word classifier verifying the trigger phrase, (1) large language model or full automatic speech recognition running on host processor or cloud. The cascade minimizes energy by placing the cheapest filter first: the microwatt VAD filters silence/ambient noise; upon detecting voice, it triggers the small milliwatt wake-word model; only when the trigger phrase is validated does the system power up the watt-scale host processor or cloud network stack for full inference. Inverting the sequence would burn hundreds of milliwatts or watts on continuous ambient silence.

    *Learning Objective*: Design a hierarchical wake-word cascade to minimize average power consumption in low-power edge systems

**Self-Check: Answer**

1.  **An AI company deploys quantization and knowledge distillation, reducing the energy cost per user query by 50 percent. Over the following year, the company’s total data center electricity consumption increases by 75 percent as lower per-query costs enable new product features and attract 3.5$\times$ more queries. Which phenomenon explains this outcome?**

    1.  Amdahl’s law, which states that sequential execution limits parallel speedup.
    2.  Dennard scaling breakdown, where transistor power density increases with scaling.
    3.  Jevons paradox, where technological efficiency improvements lower the effective cost of a resource, stimulating increased demand that outweighs per-unit efficiency gains.
    4.  The memory wall, where DRAM bandwidth fails to keep pace with arithmetic throughput.

    *Answer*: The correct answer is C. Jevons paradox, where technological efficiency improvements lower the effective cost of a resource, stimulating increased demand that outweighs per-unit efficiency gains. Jevons paradox (or the rebound effect) describes when per-unit efficiency gains make a service cheaper or faster, unlocking new use cases and user demand whose aggregate resource consumption exceeds the initial per-unit savings. This demonstrates why engineering efficiency alone cannot guarantee environmental sustainability without capacity governance or carbon budgets. Amdahl’s law governs parallel compute speedup; Dennard scaling describes CMOS power physics; and the memory wall addresses processor-memory bandwidth disparities.

    *Learning Objective*: Explain Jevons paradox in AI systems and justify why engineering efficiency must be paired with usage governance

2.  **A model compression team evaluates unstructured weight pruning, INT8 quantization, and knowledge distillation for serving a transformer. Explain the hardware mechanisms that can turn each technique into measured energy savings, and why theoretical compression alone is insufficient.**

    *Answer*: INT8 can reduce weight-transfer bytes relative to FP16 and use supported integer kernels; distillation can reduce dense operations and stored state at the required quality. Unstructured pruning saves energy only when the implementation exploits sparsity instead of moving and processing zeros or spending the savings on indexing. Compare joules per completed task at matched quality, precision assumptions, utilization, and measurement boundaries; no universal cross-precision energy multiplier follows from the operation count alone.

    *Learning Objective*: Compare theoretical model compression techniques against hardware-realizable energy reductions on modern AI accelerators

3.  **A systems team aims to overcome the energy wall for future foundation model architectures. Which design direction most directly targets the physical root cause of energy waste identified in the chapter?**

    1.  Increasing clock frequency and supply voltage to maximize compute throughput per unit silicon area.
    2.  Enforcing dense, synchronized execution across all model layers so accelerator execution units maintain 100 percent active duty cycles.
    3.  Adopting event-driven and sparse-activation architectures that execute compute only on salient tokens or state changes, minimizing dense data movement across memory hierarchies.
    4.  Replacing FP16 tensor cores with FP32 standard execution units to maximize numeric accuracy per memory access.

    *Answer*: The correct answer is C. Event-driven and sparse-activation designs can avoid unnecessary computation and transfers when inputs or activations permit it. The saving depends on event rate, sensing and static power, sparse execution overhead, and achieved quality. Raising voltage increases dynamic power at fixed activity and frequency; keeping units active does not establish useful work per joule. Dense computation is not universally movement dominated.

    *Learning Objective*: Design architectural strategies that address data-movement dominance to mitigate the AI energy wall

4.  **A platform team has recurring batch training jobs that can move across regions or time while preserving quality. Which intervention is a candidate for lowering operational carbon without changing the model, provided capacity, data access, deadlines, and net emissions are checked?**

    1.  Replacing all ReLU activation functions with GELU activations across training scripts.
    2.  Increasing the batch size to maximize GPU execution unit occupancy.
    3.  Switching from PyTorch to a C++ inference engine for training loops.
    4.  Implementing carbon-aware spatial and temporal scheduling to route training jobs to data center regions and time windows with surplus renewable energy and low carbon intensity.

    *Answer*: The correct answer is D. Carbon-aware placement and scheduling can exploit lower-emissions windows for flexible jobs. Compare complete facility energy and compatible grid signals, including checkpoint, transfer, restart, and idle costs, after filtering infeasible candidates. Regional averages motivate investigation but do not guarantee an order-of-magnitude net saving. Changing batch size or activations changes the workload and may change its quality or convergence.

    *Learning Objective*: Evaluate carbon-aware placement as a conditional mitigation candidate for feasible batch workloads

5.  **Explain the difference between average and marginal grid emissions, and identify the evidence a scheduler needs to estimate the emissions effect of a proposed workload shift.**

    *Answer*: An average factor attributes an electricity mix over a specified place and period; it can be annual or time-resolved. A marginal factor estimates the emissions response to changing demand. A scheduler evaluating a proposed shift needs consequential evidence matched to its region, forecast horizon, and workload, with uncertainty recorded. A marginal estimate need not identify one directly observable generator, and the full comparison must also include shifting costs.

    *Learning Objective*: Distinguish attributional and consequential grid evidence and select evidence appropriate to a workload-shifting decision

6.  **Order the following stages of a carbon-aware scheduler’s decision: (1) compare complete projected emissions of feasible candidates, including checkpoint, transfer, restart, and idle costs, (2) obtain documented grid forecasts and workload requirements, (3) dispatch the selected feasible action, (4) monitor progress and re-evaluate against constraints, (5) filter candidates by deadline, capacity, data access, power, cooling, and reliability requirements.**

    *Answer*: The correct order is: (2) obtain grid forecasts and workload requirements, (5) filter candidates by operational constraints, (1) compare complete projected emissions, (3) dispatch the selected feasible action, (4) monitor progress and re-evaluate. A lower-intensity region can be infeasible or lose its advantage once movement and waiting costs enter. Feasibility and net benefit must therefore precede dispatch, while progress monitoring can trigger another decision cycle.

    *Learning Objective*: Analyze the operational execution pipeline of a carbon-aware workload scheduler managing distributed AI jobs

1.  **An industry observer claims that policy interventions and carbon pricing are unnecessary because ‘free-market competition will naturally incentivize data center operators to minimize energy use and carbon emissions.’ Which counterargument best explains why market mechanisms alone fail to guarantee sustainable AI deployment?**

    1.  Data center operators are legally restricted from purchasing power from renewable energy utilities.
    2.  Electricity prices can omit emissions and other environmental burdens, so minimizing the electricity bill need not minimize the complete environmental account; pricing, reporting, or procurement requirements can change the decision incentives.
    3.  AI models running on renewable electricity automatically suffer from higher inference latency than models on fossil power.
    4.  Hardware vendors have already achieved the physical thermodynamic limit of computing efficiency, leaving no room for further optimization.

    *Answer*: The correct answer is B. A financial objective can omit environmental externalities, so a cheaper feasible site need not have lower emissions or water stress.     *Learning Objective*: Explain why market incentives alone fail to drive carbon reduction and justify the role of carbon pricing policy

2.  **How do modern regulatory frameworks such as the Corporate Sustainability Reporting Directive (CSRD) and the EU AI Act directly affect the systems engineering practices of organizations deploying large-scale AI?**

    1.  They impose an absolute global cap banning the training of any machine learning model exceeding 100 billion parameters.
    2.  For organizations and systems within their applicable scope, they create documentation and disclosure duties that require traceable energy and sustainability evidence; the exact obligations depend on the framework and deployment.
    3.  They mandate that all AI inference must be executed exclusively on battery-powered mobile devices rather than cloud servers.
    4.  They require that all machine learning models be retrained weekly using uncompressed full-precision floating-point arithmetic.

    *Answer*: The correct answer is B. Reporting and AI governance frameworks can turn energy and sustainability evidence into a deployment obligation for covered entities or systems. Engineering teams need reproducible measurements, documented boundaries, and retained records matched to the applicable requirement. The frameworks have different scopes; they do not impose one universal Scope 1–3 telemetry rule on every AI deployment, nor the parameter bans or edge-only mandates in the other choices.

    *Learning Objective*: Analyze how sustainability regulations convert environmental accounting into mandatory software and infrastructure engineering requirements

3.  **Explain how an applicable emissions trading system or carbon tax changes a workload scheduler’s cost objective, and what conditions determine whether cleaner placement becomes economically preferable.**

    *Answer*: A carbon price adds an emissions term to the cost objective. At equal facility energy and electricity tariffs, a higher applicable emissions factor produces a larger carbon charge. Cleaner placement becomes preferable only when the complete priced benefit exceeds transfer, waiting, runtime, and other costs and the candidate meets deadline, capacity, data, and reliability constraints. Carbon pricing changes incentives; it does not guarantee migration.

    *Learning Objective*: Analyze how carbon taxation aligns financial cost optimization with carbon-aware infrastructure scheduling

4.  **True or False: If a technology enterprise purchases enough annual Renewable Energy Certificates (RECs) to match 100 percent of its data centers’ annual electricity consumption, its evening foundation-model inference workloads running on a fossil-fueled regional grid can be accurately characterized as zero-carbon operations.**

    *Answer*: False. Annual certificates match electricity attributes over a year; they do not establish the emissions of an evening workload. Hourly matching makes unmatched intervals visible, but physical benefit still depends on deliverability, additional clean supply, storage, and grid conditions. Neither method eliminates hardware manufacturing emissions or proves a complete zero-carbon lifecycle.

    *Learning Objective*: Evaluate the difference between annual renewable energy netting (RECs) and 24/7 hourly clean energy matching

5.  **Explain how future non-von-Neumann computing paradigms (such as neuromorphic computing and in-memory analog computing) address the fundamental physical bottleneck responsible for AI energy consumption.**

    *Answer*: These architectures can reduce selected transfers or unnecessary switching, but the gain depends on the workload and implementation. If movement contributes a fraction q of total energy, eliminating that term alone yields at most a 1/(1-q) reduction factor with other terms unchanged. Conversion, control, sensing, peripheral energy, training, and achieved quality remain in the comparison; neither a universal movement share nor near-zero system energy follows from the architecture label.

    *Learning Objective*: Explain how alternative architectures target movement energy and bound their benefit within the complete system account

In production, serving the sparse model requires specialized, custom-fabricated NPU accelerators and requires double the serving iterations to reach quality parity. Which pitfall does this scenario illustrate, and what is the proper engineering mitigation?**

    1.  Local optimization of training compute without full-lifecycle assessment; the mitigation is conducting end-to-end Life Cycle Assessment (LCA) that accounts for hardware manufacturing, deployment scale, and serving energy before committing to architectural changes.
    2.  Failure to apply Amdahl’s law; the mitigation is increasing CPU clock frequency.
    3.  Violating the GHG Protocol; the mitigation is reclassifying Scope 3 emissions as Scope 1.
    4.  Ignoring PUE; the mitigation is installing liquid cooling on the development workstations.

    *Answer*: The correct answer is A.
