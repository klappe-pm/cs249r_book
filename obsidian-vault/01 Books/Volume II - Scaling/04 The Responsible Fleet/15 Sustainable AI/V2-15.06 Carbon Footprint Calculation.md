# V2-15.06 Carbon Footprint Calculation

[[Home|Home]] · [[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/Volume 2 Overview|Volume Overview]] · [[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/04 The Responsible Fleet/15 Sustainable AI/V2-15 Sustainable AI|V2-15 Sustainable AI]]

^sec-sustainable-ai-carbon-footprint-calculation-c2fe

Consider a data center supplied by a hydro-heavy grid. Its operational carbon intensity can be far below a fossil-heavy grid, but hydroelectric generation is not lifecycle-zero, and the AI system is not carbon-free. Reservoir construction and operation have impacts, while mining the silicon, manufacturing the GPUs, and pouring the concrete for the data center emitted carbon before the servers were turned on. A complete footprint calculation must account for operational electricity, embodied carbon, and the ecological effects excluded from a carbon-only metric.

Building that footprint term by term starts by asking which lifecycle phase dominates the workload and then whose inventory each emission belongs to, because the same physical emission can appear in more than one organization’s report. The grid factor, operational carbon, embodied carbon, and their lifecycle sum then each take an equation that generalizes across workloads, grids, and amortization assumptions.

### Carbon Accountability and Lifecycle Phases

^sec-sustainable-ai-carbon-footprint-analysis-ccc5

Published estimates of the energy of a single chat prompt vary by about an order of magnitude because different studies count different components of the serving stack ([[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/04 The Responsible Fleet/15 Sustainable AI/V2-15.15 Source References#^ref-elsworth2025measuring|Elsworth et al. 2025]]), and best-effort estimates stripped of their assumptions quickly circulate as facts ([[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/04 The Responsible Fleet/15 Sustainable AI/V2-15.15 Source References#^ref-luccioni2025misinformation|Luccioni et al. 2025]]). Without a workload-level accounting model that fixes those boundaries, teams cannot audit whether choosing a smaller model, lower-power hardware, a cleaner region, or deferred training actually reduces environmental impact.

Carbon footprint analysis turns sustainability from a general obligation into an auditable design constraint by linking energy consumption, grid carbon intensity, and lifecycle resource demands to the same decisions that govern performance and efficiency ([[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/04 The Responsible Fleet/15 Sustainable AI/V2-15.15 Source References#^ref-schwartz2020|Schwartz et al. 2020]]; [[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/04 The Responsible Fleet/15 Sustainable AI/V2-15.15 Source References#^ref-patterson2021carbon|Patterson et al. 2021]]). Traceability is the technical bridge between measurement and accountability, and it is the engineering response to a transparency gap: the absence of per-model energy, carbon, and water records that would let anyone outside the team check a claim. A team should be able to connect a model version, training run, serving workload, region, energy source, and lifecycle estimate well enough that carbon and water claims can be verified.

For measurement to constrain design, reported metrics must expose workload-level cost rather than aggregate cloud-scale claims, which stay opaque when reporting holds at the company-total level. The production measurement in [[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/04 The Responsible Fleet/15 Sustainable AI/V2-15.08 Training Vs. Inference Energy Analysis#^sec-sustainable-ai-inference-energy-costs-aec9|section 15.7.3]] shows how far the boundary alone moves a reported figure. The practical standard is therefore accountability for resource usage across the full AI lifecycle. It requires the same evidence chain used for latency, accuracy, and reliability: a claim should connect to logs, meters, model versions, and deployment decisions rather than to aggregate sustainability pledges alone. The footprint calculation makes that evidence chain explicit by combining workload energy, facility PUE, grid carbon intensity, and embodied carbon, and it starts from the lifecycle phase that dominates.

#### Three-Phase Lifecycle Assessment Framework

^sec-sustainable-ai-threephase-lifecycle-assessment-framework-883a

The practical question is which lifecycle phase dominates a given workload, because the answer determines the optimization lever. Effective carbon footprint measurement therefore separates three phases that collectively determine environmental impact: training, inference, and the manufacturing of the hardware both run on. Two other groupings appear later in the chapter, the reporting scopes of [[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/04 The Responsible Fleet/15 Sustainable AI/V2-15.06 Carbon Footprint Calculation#^sec-sustainable-ai-comprehensive-carbon-accounting-methodologies-62fc|section 15.5.2]] and the lifecycle stages of [[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/04 The Responsible Fleet/15 Sustainable AI/V2-15.09 Hardware Lifecycle and E-Waste#^sec-sustainable-ai-hardware-lifecycle-environmental-assessment-66ee|section 15.8]]. Both regroup these same emissions, by reporting organization and by time, rather than adding new ones. [[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/04 The Responsible Fleet/15 Sustainable AI/V2-15.06 Carbon Footprint Calculation#^tbl-sustainable-ai-emission-views|Table 5]] maps the three groupings onto one another.

For training-centric research workloads, the training phase often dominates operational emissions because mathematical optimization requires sustained parallel computation.[[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/04 The Responsible Fleet/15 Sustainable AI/V2-15.16 Footnotes#^fn7|⁷]] Geographic placement then changes the operational result, because an identical workload can encounter very different grid intensities.[[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/04 The Responsible Fleet/15 Sustainable AI/V2-15.16 Footnotes#^fn8|⁸]] For high-volume production services, by contrast, the inference phase can dominate lifetime emissions because serving repeats continuously after training ends, so its cumulative impact scales with deployment breadth and usage frequency. The deciding comparison is cumulative serving emissions over the deployment period against training emissions, including the experimental runs that preceded the final one. The more widely a model is served and the longer it stays deployed, the more likely inference is the larger term, and [[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/04 The Responsible Fleet/15 Sustainable AI/V2-15.08 Training Vs. Inference Energy Analysis#^sec-sustainable-ai-inference-energy-costs-aec9|section 15.7.3]] computes how many days of serving at a fixed query volume overtake a training run.

The manufacturing phase contributes embodied carbon from hardware production, including material extraction, semiconductor fabrication, and supply chain logistics.[[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/04 The Responsible Fleet/15 Sustainable AI/V2-15.16 Footnotes#^fn9|⁹]] For a fixed manufacturing footprint, longer operation on a carbon-intensive grid increases operational emissions and lowers the embodied share of the total. Cleaner electricity or less operating time raises that relative share without changing the manufacturing emissions already incurred. Operational efficiency does not undo those emissions; procurement, design, reuse, and service-life decisions determine future embodied costs.

### Emission Scopes and the Development Boundary

^sec-sustainable-ai-comprehensive-carbon-accounting-methodologies-62fc

When an AI organization rents a cloud GPU cluster, the electricity that powers the run appears on the cloud provider’s utility bill, the chip fabrication emissions appear in the foundry’s inventory, and neither registers as direct fuel or electricity on the renter’s own meters. Whereas the training, inference, and manufacturing account groups emissions by the physical activity that produces them, the three emission scopes defined by the **Greenhouse Gas (GHG) Protocol** ([[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/04 The Responsible Fleet/15 Sustainable AI/V2-15.15 Source References#^ref-ghgprotocol2023|World Resources Institute and World Business Council for Sustainable Development 2023]]) classify that same footprint relative to a reporting organization. These are different views of the same emissions, not additional totals to add together. Connecting the workload account to an organizational inventory requires naming who owns the equipment, purchases the electricity, and buys the service.

Within an owned data-center facility boundary, Scope 1 emissions include direct combustion in owned generators and refrigerant leakage from cooling equipment, whereas purchased electricity used to run cooling or IT equipment belongs in Scope 2, not Scope 1. Scope 2 emissions represent indirect emissions from purchased electricity, steam, heat, and cooling consumed by the reporting organization. Purchased electricity is often the dominant category for an operator’s owned facility, and it varies dramatically by geographic location and grid energy mix. Electricity used by a cloud provider is Scope 2 for that provider, but a customer’s purchased cloud service is generally accounted for in the customer’s Scope 3 value chain. The physical electrons do not change; the reporting boundary does. The geography notebook in [[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/04 The Responsible Fleet/15 Sustainable AI/V2-15.03 The Scale of Environmental Impact#^sec-sustainable-ai-scale-environmental-impact-ac9a|section 15.2]] uses a declared pair of illustrative factors; actual accounting requires factors matched to the location, time, and method.

Scope 3 emissions constitute the most complex category, encompassing hardware manufacturing, cloud supply chains, transportation, disposal, and downstream use. Semiconductor manufacturing is carbon-intensive.[[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/04 The Responsible Fleet/15 Sustainable AI/V2-15.16 Footnotes#^fn10|¹⁰]] The manufacturer reports the same fabrication emissions inside its own organizational boundary, so the two inventories describe one physical emission from two sides. How long operation takes to match that embodied term depends on how hard and on which grid the hardware runs. The H100 break-even worked with the lifecycle phases ([[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/04 The Responsible Fleet/15 Sustainable AI/V2-15.06 Carbon Footprint Calculation#^sec-sustainable-ai-threephase-lifecycle-assessment-framework-883a|section 15.5.1.1]]) stretches from a few weeks at rated power on the US-average grid to months or years at low utilization or on a clean grid. Under company-wide or value-chain AI accounting, Scope 3 can dominate even when Scope 2 dominates a single owned facility’s operational footprint.

Downstream use can also enter Scope 3, including end-user smartphones, IoT devices, and edge computing.[[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/04 The Responsible Fleet/15 Sustainable AI/V2-15.16 Footnotes#^fn11|¹¹]] Companywide totals do not by themselves identify the footprint of a particular AI service; that attribution still requires workload-level accounting.

The GHG Protocol arranges these scopes as concentric reporting boundaries that widen outward from the operator’s direct control ([[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/04 The Responsible Fleet/15 Sustainable AI/V2-15.06 Carbon Footprint Calculation#^fig-ghg-protocol|figure 6]]). On-site diesel generators sit in Scope 1, purchased grid electricity for compute and cooling in Scope 2, and upstream semiconductor fabrication and end-of-life recycling in Scope 3.

![[90 Assets/Volume II/15 Sustainable AI/Ghg Protocol 2e5ce9006b.svg]]

Figure 6: **GHG Emission Scopes**: Concentric rings place the reporting organization’s data center at the center, with Scope 1 (direct emissions) innermost, Scope 2 (purchased energy) around it, and Scope 3 (value chain) outermost. Representative sources sit inside each ring. The rings show reporting distance from the operator’s direct control, not relative size. Scope definitions follow the GHG Protocol ([[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/04 The Responsible Fleet/15 Sustainable AI/V2-15.15 Source References#^ref-ghgprotocol2023|World Resources Institute and World Business Council for Sustainable Development 2023]]).

^fig-ghg-protocol

The operational emissions of a final training run and its deployed service omit the development that produced them, including failed experiments, hyperparameter searches, and the build and test infrastructure. Repeated experiments can exceed the cost of the final run. A search that trains ${k}$ candidates at a fraction ${f}$ of the final run’s cost adds ${k} {f}$ final-run equivalents before the chosen model starts, so a sweep of 50 candidates at 10 percent of full scale costs 5× the run that gets reported. Continuous integration, compilation, and evaluation add further work whose contribution depends on run frequency and scale. The development boundary must record these activities before assigning their emissions to the reporting organization’s inventory; the phase of model development does not itself determine the emissions scope.

With the scopes and the development boundary defined, the activity phases of [[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/04 The Responsible Fleet/15 Sustainable AI/V2-15.06 Carbon Footprint Calculation#^sec-sustainable-ai-threephase-lifecycle-assessment-framework-883a|section 15.5.1.1]], the lifecycle stages of [[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/04 The Responsible Fleet/15 Sustainable AI/V2-15.09 Hardware Lifecycle and E-Waste#^sec-sustainable-ai-hardware-lifecycle-environmental-assessment-66ee|section 15.8]], and the scopes can be laid side by side ([[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/04 The Responsible Fleet/15 Sustainable AI/V2-15.06 Carbon Footprint Calculation#^tbl-sustainable-ai-emission-views|table 5]]). Each row is one set of emissions seen three ways, so a total built from one column must never be added to a total built from another.

| **Activity phase**                                | **Lifecycle stage**             | **Scope for an owner-operator**                                   | **Scope for a cloud customer**         |
|:--------------------------------------------------|:--------------------------------|:------------------------------------------------------------------|:---------------------------------------|
| **Training** (development runs and the final run) | Design and experimentation; use | Scope 2 for purchased electricity; Scope 1 for on-site generation | Scope 3 (purchased service)            |
| **Inference** (serving)                           | Use                             | Scope 2 for purchased electricity; Scope 1 for on-site generation | Scope 3 (purchased service)            |
| **Manufacturing** (embodied carbon)               | Manufacture; disposal           | Scope 3 (capital goods and end-of-life treatment)                 | Scope 3 (within the purchased service) |

Table 5: **Three Views of One Footprint**: The activity phases group emissions by the work that causes them, the lifecycle stages by when they occur, and the GHG scopes by which organization reports them. The same physical emissions appear once in each view; the scope assignment changes with the reporting organization, not with the hardware.

Applying the scope framework in practice requires classifying the emission sources across a typical ML platform’s lifecycle.

**Checkpoint 15.2: Accounting for invisible carbon**

Audit the carbon footprint of a machine learning platform by classifying the following emission sources into Scope 1 (Direct), Scope 2 (Indirect Energy), or Scope 3 (Value Chain). State the reporting organization before assigning each scope:

**Backup generation**: classify diesel burned by backup generators during a grid outage at an owned facility.

**Leased cloud cluster**: classify the electricity for both reporting entities, the cloud operator that purchases it and the customer that purchases the compute service.

**Embodied manufacturing**: classify the embodied carbon emitted during the manufacturing of the GPUs by TSMC.

**End-user operation**: classify emissions from the end-user’s smartphone battery while running a deployed mobile inference app.

### Grid Carbon Intensity and Regional Variation

^sec-sustainable-ai-carbon-intensity-regional-variation-db41-regional-variation-db41

Two reports can price the exact same megawatt-hour of data-center electricity at carbon intensities that differ several-fold simply by choosing a different emissions factor or boundary. Generation-technology lifecycle factors include fuel supply, construction, operation, and decommissioning, whereas regional grid factors reflect an electricity mix over a stated place and period and may exclude upstream lifecycle emissions. Each type of factor must be identified before it enters the calculation ([[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/04 The Responsible Fleet/15 Sustainable AI/V2-15.06 Carbon Footprint Calculation#^tbl-carbon-intensity|table 6]]).

| **Factor**               | **What it describes**                                              | **Appropriate use**                                                                          |
|:-------------------------|:-------------------------------------------------------------------|:---------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------|
| **Generation lifecycle** | Emissions across the lifecycle of an electricity technology        | Comparing generation options under consistent lifecycle assumptions                          |
| **Grid average**         | Emissions attributed to an electricity mix over a place and period | Attributing workload electricity emissions with a declared operational or lifecycle boundary |
| **Grid marginal**        | Estimated change in emissions when demand changes                  | Evaluating a proposed shift or reduction in electricity demand                               |

Table 6: **Electricity Emissions Factor Boundaries**: A technology factor is not a regional grid measurement. Record the period, location, imports, greenhouse gases, and lifecycle coverage before comparing or multiplying a factor by workload electricity.

^tbl-carbon-intensity

For its 2024 data centers, Google reports a location-based grid factor, the average intensity of the grids its data centers draw from, of 345 g CO<sub>2</sub>e/kWh and a market-based factor of 94 g CO<sub>2</sub>e/kWh after crediting its purchases of carbon-free electricity, a 3.7× difference for the same electricity ([[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/04 The Responsible Fleet/15 Sustainable AI/V2-15.15 Source References#^ref-elsworth2025measuring|Elsworth et al. 2025]]). The median Gemini text prompt whose water use [[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/04 The Responsible Fleet/15 Sustainable AI/V2-15.05 Energy Measurement and Modeling#^sec-sustainable-ai-facilitylevel-power-metrics-5558|section 15.4.1.3]] computed therefore carries about 0.08 g CO<sub>2</sub>e or 0.023 g CO<sub>2</sub>e of electricity emissions depending on the factor, and only the second matches the figure the company publishes. Neither is wrong, but a comparison is meaningful only when both sides declare the same factor type, period, and boundary.

Applied to a fixed 10,000 MWh facility-energy account, regional electricity factors change operational carbon by nearly two orders of magnitude ([[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/04 The Responsible Fleet/15 Sustainable AI/V2-15.06 Carbon Footprint Calculation#^fig-carbon-intensity|figure 7]]). The assumed factors range from 10 g/kWh for Norway (100 t $\text{CO}_2$ for the workload) to 820 g/kWh for Poland (8,200 t $\text{CO}_2$), producing a calculated 82× emissions ratio. These rounded annual-average factors ([[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/04 The Responsible Fleet/15 Sustainable AI/V2-15.15 Source References#^ref-iea2023weo|International Energy Agency 2023]]), the same ones notebook [15.2](https://mlsysbook.ai/vol2/15_sustainable_ai/15_sustainable_ai.html#nbk-sustainable-ai-geography-carbon) used for Quebec and Poland, illustrate the effect of placement while holding facility energy constant. They are not measurements of the same job run at every site. Actual placement decisions require time-matched grid evidence and must include changes in facility overhead and the energy needed to move or resume the work.

### Operational Carbon Calculation

^sec-sustainable-ai-operational-carbon-calculation-4627-calculation-4627

Multiplying raw GPU thermal design power by runtime and a regional grid factor understates a job’s operational carbon when it omits host servers and chiller power, or overstates it if facility overhead is accidentally applied a second time. [[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/04 The Responsible Fleet/15 Sustainable AI/V2-15.06 Carbon Footprint Calculation#^eq-operational-carbon|Equation 10]] computes operational carbon from the facility-level energy ${E_{\text{facility}}}$ of [[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/04 The Responsible Fleet/15 Sustainable AI/V2-15.05 Energy Measurement and Modeling#^eq-total-energy|equation 9]], which already includes IT support and $\text{PUE}$ once, scaled by the electricity grid’s carbon intensity $\text{CO}_{2,\text{grid}}$:

$$
\text{CO}_{2,\text{operational}} = {E_{\text{facility}}}\times \text{CO}_{2,\text{grid}} \tag{10}
$$

^eq-operational-carbon

Applied to a mid-scale training run, the equation separates the facility energy from the grid factor that converts it into emissions. The worked account below builds that facility energy step by step from accelerator draw, rack support, and PUE before comparing the resulting emissions across two regional grids.

**Napkin Math 15.5: Training emissions calculation**

**Problem**: Quantify the operational carbon footprint of training a 7B parameter model on 64 A100 GPUs for 14 days, assuming continuous accelerator draw at the rated power and the stated rack-support and facility overheads.

**Math**:

1.  Compute energy: 64 GPUs $\times$ 400 W $\times$ 336 hours = 8,601.6 kWh.
2.  Apply IT support and PUE: host processors, memory, and networking add to the accelerator load. This calculation borrows the ratio of IT power to accelerator power from the DGX H100 rack profile itemized later in [[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/04 The Responsible Fleet/15 Sustainable AI/V2-15.07 Data Center Energy and Resource Consumption#^tbl-rack-power|table 7]]. That ratio is a modeling proxy for an A100 server’s support load rather than a measurement of it. It scales accelerator energy by 1.38 to 11,827.2 kWh, so total facility energy = 11,827.2 kWh $\times$ 1.12 = 13,246.5 kWh.
3.  Calculate emissions: On US average grid (429 g/kWh $\text{CO}_2$), emissions = 5.7 t. On Quebec hydro grid (20 g/kWh), emissions = 264.9 kg.

**Systems insight**: The grid factor alone produces a 21.4× difference in training emissions for this pairing, the same placement lever quantified in notebook [15.2](https://mlsysbook.ai/vol2/15_sustainable_ai/15_sustainable_ai.html#nbk-sustainable-ai-geography-carbon). The model architecture is unchanged.

### Embodied Carbon Assessment

^sec-sustainable-ai-embodied-carbon-assessment-9de0

Moving the 64-A100 training run of the preceding section from the US-average grid to Quebec’s hydro-dominated grid cuts operational emissions by more than an order of magnitude, yet the carbon emitted to fabricate those 64 GPUs in the semiconductor foundry does not fall by a single gram. The training-emissions notebook priced electricity only, where a lower grid emissions factor reduces operational carbon and raises the amortized manufacturing share from about 1.6 percent of total emissions on the US-average grid to about 25.8 percent on Quebec’s grid. Whether embodied carbon remains a minor correction or becomes a major share depends on the manufacturing footprint, utilization, and service life, so those quantities must enter the comparison before an engineer can choose between reducing electricity use and extending hardware service.

Embodied carbon encompasses emissions from raw material extraction, semiconductor fabrication, assembly, transportation, and end-of-life disposal. For AI hardware, the energy-intensive nature of advanced semiconductor processes dominates manufacturing emissions, including wafer fabrication at advanced process nodes, high-bandwidth memory production, and packaging. An NVIDIA A100 GPU carries an embodied footprint of approximately 150 kg CO<sub>2</sub>e per unit ([[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/04 The Responsible Fleet/15 Sustainable AI/V2-15.15 Source References#^ref-luccioni2023estimating|Luccioni et al. 2023]]). NVIDIA’s HGX H100 product carbon footprint implies roughly 164 kg CO<sub>2</sub>e per H100 when the baseboard footprint is allocated evenly across its eight GPUs ([[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/04 The Responsible Fleet/15 Sustainable AI/V2-15.15 Source References#^ref-nvidia2025hgxh100pcf|NVIDIA Corporation 2025]]). [[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/04 The Responsible Fleet/15 Sustainable AI/V2-15.06 Carbon Footprint Calculation#^eq-embodied-daily|Equation 11]] amortizes this embodied carbon over the hardware lifetime to compute per-use emissions:

$$
\text{CO}_{2,\text{embodied,daily}} = \frac{\text{CO}_{2,\text{manufacturing}}}{{T_{\text{lifetime}}}\times 365} \tag{11}
$$

 with ${T_{\text{lifetime}}}$ in years.

^eq-embodied-daily

The manufacturing emissions have already occurred when the hardware enters service. Amortization allocates that burden over time; it does not describe additional manufacturing emissions accumulating each day. Utilization and service life determine how much useful work shares the initial burden.

**Systems Perspective 15.1: Embodied carbon amortization**

Renting a GPU for an hour uses hardware whose manufacturing emissions have already occurred. The workload account allocates a share of those emissions to the rental interval, regardless of whether the monetary fee explicitly prices carbon.

Expressing [[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/04 The Responsible Fleet/15 Sustainable AI/V2-15.06 Carbon Footprint Calculation#^eq-embodied-daily|equation 11]] per hour and multiplying by the job’s duration makes that allocation explicit:

$$
\text{CO}_{2,\text{total}} = \text{CO}_{2,\text{operational}} + \left( \frac{\text{CO}_{2,\text{manufacturing}}}{{T_{\text{lifetime}}} \times 365 \times 24} \times {T_{\text{job}}} \right)
$$

 with ${T_{\text{lifetime}}}$ in years and ${T_{\text{job}}}$ in hours.

**Scenario**: Training a model for 10 hours on 8 NVIDIA H100s. This accelerator-only illustration uses TDP as a constant-power assumption and excludes host, network, and facility energy. Its embodied share uses the allocated baseboard footprint; it is not a complete fleet lifecycle inventory.

- Operational: 8 $\times$ 0.7 kW $\times$ 10 hours = 56 kWh. At 0.429 kg/kWh (US-average grid) = 24 kg.
- Embodied: 8 $\times$ 164 kg = 1312 kg.
- Amortization: Lifetime = 3 years, the standard service life (26280 hours).
  - Hourly “Rent” = 1312 kg / 26280 hours $\approx$ 0.050 kg/h.
  - Job Cost = 0.050 kg/h $\times$ 10 hours = 0.5 kg.

**Systems insight**: For long-lived hardware in dirty grids, electricity dominates (24 kg vs. 0.5 kg). However, in clean grids (hydro, 0.020 kg/kWh), operational drops to 1.1 kg, making embodied carbon a significant fraction (~30.8 percent) of the total footprint.

The perspective’s H100 job, like the lifecycle notebook later in this chapter, amortizes over the 3-year standard service life. The A100 calculation below keeps its own setup’s 4-year extended life, which spreads the same manufacturing carbon over more service. For an accelerator with 150 kg embodied carbon (the A100 estimate cited earlier) and that 4-year data center lifetime, the first step is daily amortization: 150 kg / (4 years $\times$ 365 d/year) $\approx$ 0.103 kg/day. The second step assigns that daily share to the job: a training run lasting 14 days on 64 accelerators carries 64 $\times$ 14 days $\times$ 0.103 kg $\approx$ 92.1 kg CO<sub>2</sub> of amortized embodied carbon.

Adding that 92.1 kg embodied contribution to the 5,682.7 kg of operational emissions calculated earlier for the US-average grid yields the 1.6 percent manufacturing share noted at the start of this section, whereas pairing the same hardware burden with Quebec’s 264.9 kg of operational emissions raises the manufacturing share to 25.8 percent. The manufacturing term did not change; the cleaner grid shrank everything around it.

### Lifecycle Carbon Accounting

^sec-sustainable-ai-lifecycle-carbon-accounting-99a1

Reporting only the final training run’s electricity and the serving cluster’s power bill leaves out the failed architecture sweeps that preceded the run and the manufacturing emissions of the racks that host it. A complete workload account ([[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/04 The Responsible Fleet/15 Sustainable AI/V2-15.06 Carbon Footprint Calculation#^eq-lifecycle-carbon|equation 12]]) groups operational emissions into training and inference, adds embodied emissions, and requires that development experiments and supporting services enter the inventory rather than disappear when accounting starts at the final run:

$$
\text{CO}_{2,\text{lifecycle}} = \text{CO}_{2,\text{training}} + \text{CO}_{2,\text{inference}} + \text{CO}_{2,\text{embodied}} \tag{12}
$$

^eq-lifecycle-carbon

Each term has its own physical drivers ([[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/04 The Responsible Fleet/15 Sustainable AI/V2-15.06 Carbon Footprint Calculation#^fig-carbon-lifecycle|figure 8]]). The embodied term $\text{CO}_{2,\text{embodied}}$ is fixed before the hardware runs, at roughly 150 kg to 164 kg CO<sub>2</sub>e per accelerator for the devices priced above. The training term $\text{CO}_{2,\text{training}}$ is a one-time cost set by cluster scale, PUE, and checkpoint I/O, while the inference term $\text{CO}_{2,\text{inference}}$ recurs for as long as request traffic and KV-cache residency keep serving replicas powered. Their relative proportions therefore shift over the deployment lifetime, as the worked account in notebook [15.6](https://mlsysbook.ai/vol2/15_sustainable_ai/15_sustainable_ai.html#nbk-sustainable-ai-lifecycle-carbon-estimation) and the serving crossover in [[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/04 The Responsible Fleet/15 Sustainable AI/V2-15.08 Training Vs. Inference Energy Analysis#^sec-sustainable-ai-inference-energy-costs-aec9|section 15.7.3]] show.

![[90 Assets/Volume II/15 Sustainable AI/Carbon Lifecycle d0fdf0c509.svg]]

Figure 8: **The Carbon Lifecycle of an ML System**: Manufacturing contributes embodied carbon, while training and inference contribute operational carbon through energy, facility overhead, and grid intensity. Block widths show timing, not magnitude. The relative size of each term is workload-specific: serving volume, grid factor, and hardware lifetime determine which one dominates.

^fig-carbon-lifecycle

The equations become a decision tool once each factor is measured or declared. A worked account for a large training run shows how the operational and embodied terms combine, and which of them dominates.

**Napkin Math 15.6: Partial lifecycle carbon estimation**

**Problem**: Estimate facility operational emissions plus allocated accelerator-baseboard manufacturing emissions for training a 70B parameter model.

Variables: 2,048 H100 GPUs, 30 days, 700 W TDP, rack-profile support power, PUE 1.12, grid intensity 429 g $\text{CO}_2$/kWh.

**Math**: Accelerator power = 2,048 $\times$ 0.7 kW $\approx$ 1,433.6 kW. Applying the DGX H100 rack profile for host, memory, networking, and conversion support raises the IT load to 1,971.2 kW; facility power = 1,971.2 kW $\times$ 1.12 $\approx$ 2,207.7 kW. Energy = 2,207.7 kW $\times$ 24 h/day $\times$ 30 days $\approx$ 1,589,575.7 kWh. Emissions $\approx$ 681.9 t $\text{CO}_2$.

Embodied: Assume manufacturing footprint is $\approx$ 164 kg $\text{CO}_2$ per H100 GPU, allocating NVIDIA’s HGX H100 baseboard product carbon footprint evenly across its eight GPUs ([[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/04 The Responsible Fleet/15 Sustainable AI/V2-15.15 Source References#^ref-nvidia2025hgxh100pcf|NVIDIA Corporation 2025]]). This worked account uses time-based allocation: it assigns the job the fraction of manufacturing emissions corresponding to its 1-month occupancy of a 3-year service life. Under that declared rule, (2,048 $\times$ 164 kg) $\times$ (1 month / 36 months) $\approx$ 9.3 t $\text{CO}_2$.

**Result**: 681.9 t + 9.3 t $\approx$ 691.3 t $\text{CO}_2$.

**Systems insight**: The operational term dominates this partial lifecycle account. It includes facility electricity and allocated HGX baseboard manufacturing, but omits manufacturing of host systems, networking, storage, and facility infrastructure, as well as other unmodeled lifecycle stages. A complete footprint must extend the inventory to those boundaries.

Time is one defensible allocation basis, not a universal one. Shared fleets may instead allocate embodied emissions by accelerator-hours, reserved capacity, or a workload’s share of useful computation. Google’s cloud carbon reporting combines two of these bases, allocating the energy and emissions of shared machines by each customer’s resource reservations and hourly measured usage ([[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/04 The Responsible Fleet/15 Sustainable AI/V2-15.15 Source References#^ref-schneider2024carbon|Schneider and Mattia 2024]]). The chosen denominator must be reported and applied consistently; otherwise a low-utilization job can appear artificially inexpensive merely because another tenant absorbs the idle capacity. These workload shares support engineering comparisons; they do not determine the reporting year or category in a corporate emissions inventory. Procurement decisions need the unallocated fleet total as well as the workload-attributed share, because amortization changes ownership of an emission in the inventory but does not remove it from the atmosphere.

Every lifecycle estimate should therefore carry an uncertainty interval rather than a single authoritative total. Average workload power, PUE, grid intensity, hardware lifetime, utilization, and manufacturing footprint are measured or modeled with different errors. A sensitivity analysis varies the assumptions that could reverse the decision, including clean-grid versus carbon-intensive operation, three- versus five-year service life, and low versus high serving volume. It then reports whether the preferred design remains preferred. Precision in arithmetic cannot compensate for an unstable system boundary.

The balance among these terms changes with serving volume and duration. A high-volume service can exceed its entire training footprint within months, or within days at higher traffic, a crossover that [[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/04 The Responsible Fleet/15 Sustainable AI/V2-15.08 Training Vs. Inference Energy Analysis#^sec-sustainable-ai-inference-energy-costs-aec9|section 15.7.3]] works through for a deployed model.

The account becomes an engineering tool when it runs on every job rather than once after the fact. A partial lifecycle carbon calculator ([[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/04 The Responsible Fleet/15 Sustainable AI/V2-15.06 Carbon Footprint Calculation#^lst-carbon-calculation|listing 1]]) combines measured energy, the rack support multiplier, a declared facility PUE, an explicitly selected grid-intensity factor, and amortized embodied carbon. Its example reproduces the 64-A100 run, with each input drawn from the facility, grid, or hardware profile it describes; [[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/04 The Responsible Fleet/15 Sustainable AI/V2-15.10 Mitigation Strategies#^sec-sustainable-ai-regional-grid-intensity-data-sources-0b88|section 15.9.4.1]] compares the sources of the grid factor.

``` python
from mlsysim import Hardware, Infrastructure, ReferenceStats, Systems

def calculate_carbon_footprint(
    gpu_power_watts: float,
    num_gpus: int,
    training_hours: float,
    it_power_multiplier: float,
    pue: float,
    grid_intensity_gco2_kwh: float,
    gpu_embodied_kg: float,
    gpu_lifetime_years: float,
) -> dict:
    """Estimate operational emissions plus allocated GPU manufacturing emissions."""

    # Operational emissions (plain floats: watts, hours, kWh, grams)
    gpu_energy_kwh = gpu_power_watts * num_gpus * training_hours / 1000
    it_energy_kwh = gpu_energy_kwh * it_power_multiplier  # + host, net
    facility_energy_kwh = it_energy_kwh * pue  # overhead, applied once
    operational_kg = (
        facility_energy_kwh * grid_intensity_gco2_kwh / 1000
    )

    # Embodied emissions (amortized by time over the service life)
    daily_embodied = gpu_embodied_kg / (gpu_lifetime_years * 365)
    training_days = training_hours / 24
    embodied_kg = num_gpus * training_days * daily_embodied

    return {
        "energy_kwh": facility_energy_kwh,
        "operational_carbon_kg": operational_kg,
        "embodied_carbon_kg": embodied_kg,
        "total_carbon_kg": operational_kg + embodied_kg,
        "embodied_fraction": embodied_kg
        / (operational_kg + embodied_kg),
    }

# Example: 7B model training on a 64-GPU A100 cluster for 14 days.
# Each input names its source: PUE is a facility property, the
# grid factor a property of the electricity supply.
_rack = Systems.Racks.DGX_H100_4Node  # proxy support-load profile
_facility = Infrastructure.FacilityCooling.BestAir  # PUE 1.12
_grid = Infrastructure.Grids.US_Avg  # annual-average factor
_run = ReferenceStats.MidScaleTrainingCarbonProfile
result = calculate_carbon_footprint(
    gpu_power_watts=Hardware.Cloud.A100.tdp.m_as("W"),  # 400 W
    num_gpus=Systems.Clusters.Lab_64_A100.total_accelerators,  # 64
    training_hours=_run.TrainingDays.m_as("h"),  # 336 h
    it_power_multiplier=(
        _rack.it_power / _rack.accelerator_power
    ).m_as(""),
    pue=_facility.pue,
    grid_intensity_gco2_kwh=_grid.carbon_intensity_g_kwh,
    gpu_embodied_kg=Hardware.Cloud.A100.embodied_carbon_kg,
    gpu_lifetime_years=(  # 4-year extended service life
        Infrastructure.Pricing.Capital
        .AcceleratorExtendedServiceLifeYears.rate
    ),
)
print(
    f"Total carbon footprint: {result['total_carbon_kg']:.0f} kg CO2"
)
print(f"Embodied fraction: {result['embodied_fraction']:.1%}")
```
