# V2-15.08 Training Vs. Inference Energy Analysis

[[Home|Home]] · [[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/Volume 2 Overview|Volume Overview]] · [[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/04 The Responsible Fleet/15 Sustainable AI/V2-15 Sustainable AI|V2-15 Sustainable AI]]

^sec-sustainable-ai-training-vs-inference-energy-analysis-4cb5

Training a large language model is a concentrated, highly visible energy event that consumes thousands of megawatt-hours over weeks or months. Serving the same model to a billion daily queries is the opposite shape of load: energy spent continuously, query by query, year after year.

Training and inference share opportunities to reduce computation, data movement, and idle power, but their success criteria differ. Training must reach the target model quality within its completion budget; inference must serve the required demand at the agreed quality and latency. The lifecycle energy account identifies which phase contributes most, while those constraints determine which interventions can reduce its cost without sacrificing useful work.

### Carbon Benchmarks Across AI Workloads

^sec-sustainable-ai-ai-energy-consumption-compared-industries-3d5d

Two published carbon estimates for the same neural architecture search, an automated search that trains many candidate architectures to find a good one, can differ by orders of magnitude when one assumes older GPUs, an average facility PUE, and a fossil-heavy grid ([[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/04 The Responsible Fleet/15 Sustainable AI/V2-15.15 Source References#^ref-strubell2019|Strubell et al. 2019]]) while the other measures specialized accelerators in an efficient, low-carbon facility ([[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/04 The Responsible Fleet/15 Sustainable AI/V2-15.15 Source References#^ref-patterson2021carbon|Patterson et al. 2021]]).[[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/04 The Responsible Fleet/15 Sustainable AI/V2-15.16 Footnotes#^fn15|¹⁵]] Even though the IEA estimate cited earlier puts emissions from data-center electricity use at about 180 Mt, below 1.5 percent of total energy-sector emissions, workload-level carbon benchmarks govern where engineering teams invest optimization effort, and those benchmarks are only as reliable as the lifecycle boundaries behind them.

The GPT-3 calculation in notebook [15.1](https://mlsysbook.ai/vol2/15_sustainable_ai/15_sustainable_ai.html#nbk-sustainable-ai-carbon-cost-training) put one training run at hundreds of trans-Atlantic passenger round trips. Such run-level comparisons convey scale, but they do not by themselves describe the footprint of a deployed service. The operational lesson is that carbon accounting must distinguish training from recurring inference over a declared observation period. Meta’s recommendation workloads (RM-1 through RM-5) split operational emissions roughly evenly between training and inference, while inference dominates its language-model example ([[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/04 The Responsible Fleet/15 Sustainable AI/V2-15.08 Training Vs. Inference Energy Analysis#^fig-meta-analysis|figure 11]]). The public baselines, including GPT-3 and BERT-NAS, report training only; their bars do not establish a lifetime training-to-inference ratio. For high-traffic Archetype B deployments (**[[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/00 Introduction/01 Introduction/V2-01.07 The Fleet Stack and the Lighthouse Archetypes#^sec-introduction-archetype-b-dlrm-scale-fa6d|Archetype B (DLRM at Scale)]]**), both recurring serving and model updates belong in the account before deciding which phase offers the larger reduction.

### Training Energy Demands

^sec-sustainable-ai-training-energy-demands-1ff6

Even the training-only bars in the comparison above represent massive, concentrated power events: as early as 2020, the supercomputer Microsoft built for OpenAI combined 285,000 CPU cores and 10,000 GPUs ([[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/04 The Responsible Fleet/15 Sustainable AI/V2-15.15 Source References#^ref-microsoft2020openaiSupercomputer|Langston 2020]]) operating continuously for months, a dated reference point that subsequent frontier clusters have multiplied severalfold. Those intensive computational loads generate heat that cooling infrastructure must continuously remove, the facility overhead quantified in [[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/04 The Responsible Fleet/15 Sustainable AI/V2-15.07 Data Center Energy and Resource Consumption#^sec-sustainable-ai-cooling|section 15.6.3]], and reducing the draw requires co-optimizing hardware architecture, parallelism strategy, and algorithmic efficiency.

Every single-run training account in this chapter, from the GPT-3 run to the 70B model of notebook [15.6](https://mlsysbook.ai/vol2/15_sustainable_ai/15_sustainable_ai.html#nbk-sustainable-ai-lifecycle-carbon-estimation), also prices only the winning configuration. Development multiplies each run, by 5× for the sweep in [[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/04 The Responsible Fleet/15 Sustainable AI/V2-15.06 Carbon Footprint Calculation#^sec-sustainable-ai-comprehensive-carbon-accounting-methodologies-62fc|section 15.5.2]], while periodic retraining during service repeats part of that cost. Training energy is therefore paid once per completed run but recurs across the model’s development and update cycle.

#### Distributed Systems Energy Optimization

^sec-sustainable-ai-distributed-systems-energy-optimization-5e83

Distributing a training job across accelerators introduces coordination costs alongside the energy spent on computation. Distributed parallelism strategies exchange gradients, parameters, or activations while synchronization stalls leave idle accelerators drawing baseline power.[[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/04 The Responsible Fleet/15 Sustainable AI/V2-15.16 Footnotes#^fn16|¹⁶]] The resulting energy share cannot be inferred from cluster size alone because topology, collective algorithm, message volume, overlap, and utilization determine the account. Some AllToAll patterns also increase traffic faster than aggregate useful work as the participating set grows.

↩︎ Parallelism strategy selection and the communication cost of each strategy are established in **[[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/02 Distributed ML/06 Distributed Training/V2-06.03 The Parallelism Design Space#^sec-distributed-training-systems-systems-engineering-tradeoffs-selecting-parallelism-strategy-b344|Parallelism strategy selection and engineering trade-offs]]**.

The Fleet Law (**[[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/00 Introduction/01 Introduction/V2-01.06 The C³ Taxonomy and Foundations of Scale#^eq-fleet-law|equation 1.3]]**) turns this overhead into an energy account. If the fleet draws average power ${P_{\text{avg}}}$ while executing one step on ${N}$ accelerators, the energy of that step is approximately

$$
{E_{\text{step}}}\approx {P_{\text{avg}}}\times {T_{\text{step}}}({N}) = {P_{\text{avg}}}\left( \frac{{T_{\text{compute}}}}{{N}} + {T_{\text{comm}}}({N}) + {T_{\text{sync}}}({N}) - {T_{\text{overlap}}} \right) \tag{13}
$$

^eq-sustainable-ai-step-energy

The compute term accounts for local mathematical work; communication and synchronization make the distributed update possible. When these costs remain exposed, they extend the step while accelerators, hosts, and the fabric continue drawing power, adding powered time on the communication and coordination axes of C³. Their energy share depends on power during those intervals as well as on their duration. A quarter of the step spent in unhidden communication accounts for a quarter of its energy only if average fleet power during that interval equals the average over the whole step. Accelerators stalled at a collective can draw less than at full arithmetic load, so the share must be read from a power trace. Overlap, smaller messages, and fewer stragglers shorten the step, and they save energy to the extent that the shorter powered interval is not offset by higher average power or by extra work to reach the same model quality. The parallelism choices of **[[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/02 Distributed ML/06 Distributed Training/V2-06 Distributed Training#^sec-distributed-training-systems|Distributed Training]]** are therefore sustainability choices as well.

↩︎ The data-movement boundaries that set what each transferred byte costs, from on-package links through intra-node crossbars to inter-node switches, are established in **[[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/02 Distributed ML/05 Collective Communication/V2-05.03 The Physics of Data Movement#^sec-communication-collective-operations-physics-data-movement|The Physics of Data Movement]]**.

Beyond the communication term, coordinated resource management offers four levers with distinct effects on energy and emissions. At the cluster level, dynamic workload placement consolidates compatible jobs so unused hardware can enter lower-power sleep states. The savings must be verified at the facility meter rather than inferred from idle node counts. Carbon-aware scheduling ([[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/04 The Responsible Fleet/15 Sustainable AI/V2-15.10 Mitigation Strategies#^sec-sustainable-ai-carbonaware-scheduling-c2db|section 15.9.4]]) shifts flexible runs toward cleaner grid windows even when total energy use is unchanged, subject to data-movement overhead, completion deadlines, and reliability bounds. Inside active nodes, multi-tenant sharing raises accelerator compute and memory occupancy across concurrent tasks, though contention can lengthen execution and increase energy per completed job. Adaptive inference batching groups compatible requests to improve throughput within their latency limits. Changing training batch size instead requires checking the total work needed to reach the target model quality. Each lever must therefore be evaluated against completed useful work and its service constraints rather than occupancy alone, because energy spent on work that ML goodput discounts, the scheduling, runtime, and program losses of **[[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/03 The Fleet in Production/09 Fleet Efficiency/V2-09.03 ML Goodput#^sec-performance-engineering-measurement-scale|ML Goodput]]**, still enters the energy account.

Energy-aware training frameworks reduce step energy directly rather than shifting where it is spent. Zeus improved training energy efficiency by 15.3–75.8 percent across its evaluated workloads by automatically finding energy-performance trade-offs ([[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/04 The Responsible Fleet/15 Sustainable AI/V2-15.15 Source References#^ref-jie2023zeus|You et al. 2023]]), while Perseus reduced large-model training energy consumption by up to 30 percent by mitigating energy bloat, the energy spent running accelerators at full speed on work that must then wait for a slower pipeline stage or straggler ([[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/04 The Responsible Fleet/15 Sustainable AI/V2-15.15 Source References#^ref-chung2024|Chung et al. 2023]]). These tools make energy optimization available beyond hyperscale companies, yet all of these training costs stop accruing when the run stops. For high-volume services, the larger term is often the deployment that follows, as the crossover calculation in [[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/04 The Responsible Fleet/15 Sustainable AI/V2-15.08 Training Vs. Inference Energy Analysis#^sec-sustainable-ai-inference-energy-costs-aec9|section 15.7.3]] shows.

### Inference Energy Costs

^sec-sustainable-ai-inference-energy-costs-aec9

A model trained once can draw far more electricity over a year of high-volume serving than it ever consumed during training ([[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/04 The Responsible Fleet/15 Sustainable AI/V2-15.15 Source References#^ref-wu2022sustainable|Wu et al. 2022]]). Although a single inference request costs only a fraction of a watt-hour, serving executes every time the model answers a query, classifies an image, or scores a recommendation, scaling continuously with user traffic across search engines, recommendation systems, and generative AI services.

![[90 Assets/Volume II/15 Sustainable AI/Sustainable AI Inference Crossover 7cdf361481.svg]]

*Two lines over a model's service life: a flat gray line labeled training (once) and an orange line labeled inference (cumulative) that starts at zero, crosses the training line at a marked point, and ends far above it.*

*Cumulative inference emissions overtake the one-time training cost.*

Consider a model serving 10 million queries per day at 0.001 kWh of attributed facility electricity per query, including its share of idle capacity and support systems. At this serving rate, daily energy consumption reaches 10,000 kWh (10 million queries $\times$ 0.001 kWh), which accumulates to 3,650,000 kWh over a year of continuous service. Evaluated against the average US grid carbon intensity of 0.429 kg/kWh, this electricity demand produces 1,565.9 t (1,565,850 kg) of greenhouse gas emissions annually.

At this scale, cumulative inference emissions exceed the 5.7 t of the earlier 7B/64-A100 training example, the smallest of the chapter’s three training accounts and therefore the fastest to overtake, after approximately 1.3 days of deployment. The serving scenario and the training run describe different models, so the comparison does not trace one model’s lifecycle. It shows how quickly this serving volume would overtake a training run of that size. For larger training runs, the crossover can shift to weeks or months depending on query volume, per-query energy, and grid intensity. The lifecycle perspective therefore sets the priority: optimize inference efficiency for widely deployed models, and focus training efficiency efforts on models that undergo frequent retraining or experimental iteration.

Production measurements in [[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/04 The Responsible Fleet/15 Sustainable AI/V2-15.08 Training Vs. Inference Energy Analysis#^fig-meta-analysis|figure 11]] show why this crossover must remain workload-specific. Inference dominates the language-model account, while training and inference contribute roughly equally for the recommendation models. Training FLOPs alone omit serving costs, but the larger phase must be established from the service’s measured workload and observation period.

Inference workloads, like other software services, scale with demand. An always-available service consumes energy both while processing requests and while maintaining capacity for expected latency and availability. The sustainability account must include query volume, workload mix, and idle provisioned resources.

A production measurement shows how large that account is. Google instrumented the fleet serving its Gemini assistant and attributed energy to the median text prompt in May 2025 across every component of the serving stack ([[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/04 The Responsible Fleet/15 Sustainable AI/V2-15.15 Source References#^ref-elsworth2025measuring|Elsworth et al. 2025]]). The full-stack figure is 0.24 Wh per prompt ([[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/04 The Responsible Fleet/15 Sustainable AI/V2-15.08 Training Vs. Inference Energy Analysis#^fig-prompt-energy-boundary|figure 12]]), of which the active accelerators account for only 58 percent. The host processors and memory that feed them add 25 percent, and idle machines held for latency and availability together with facility overhead add another 17 percent. Counting only active accelerators on the most efficiently used machines, the boundary many published estimates use, yields 0.10 Wh, a 2.4× understatement of the same workload. That figure falls below even the accelerator share of the full-stack total because the full stack averages over every serving machine, including those running at lower utilization, while the narrow boundary keeps only the best-utilized ones. The measured prompt also sits at about a quarter of the 1 Wh per query assumed in the worked example above, a reminder that per-query energy is a property of a specific model, serving stack, and utilization rather than a constant. The same study reports that software and utilization improvements cut the median prompt’s energy 33× in a single year, so a per-query figure also carries a date.

**Checkpoint 15.4: The training-inference flip**

Consider a vision model where training requires 2,000 GPU-hours at an average accelerator power draw of 300 W. Once deployed, the model serves 1 million requests per day for 730 days, with each request occupying an accelerator for 50 ms at an average draw of 100 W. Assume requests do not overlap on an accelerator. This active-only account excludes idle capacity, host systems, networking, and facility overhead.

**Training energy**: calculate the accelerator energy accounted for during training.

**Inference energy**: calculate the active-request accelerator energy subtotal over the stated product lifespan.

**Optimization focus**: compute the ratio of these inference and training subtotals and identify which phase merits investigation. Explain why choosing the largest achievable lifecycle reduction also requires the omitted energy terms and the savings available from candidate interventions.

#### The Energy Inefficiency of the Decode Phase

^sec-sustainable-ai-energy-inefficiency-decode-phase-24da

Prefill and decode stress distinct memory and arithmetic pathways, so meaningful energy comparisons require a declared serving workload. Prefill amortizes loaded weights across all prompt positions simultaneously, whereas each autoregressive decode iteration reuses weights only across active sequence batch slots to emit one token at a time ([[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/04 The Responsible Fleet/15 Sustainable AI/V2-15.08 Training Vs. Inference Energy Analysis#^fig-prefill-decode-energy|figure 13]]). Batch size, context length, precision, and the latency target dictate whether memory bandwidth or arithmetic execution limits realized utilization.

↩︎ Why small-batch decode is bound by memory bandwidth, and how batching reuses each weight load, is established in **[[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/03 The Fleet in Production/10 Serving at Scale/V2-10.07 Batching Strategies at Scale#^sec-inference-scale-batching-differs-across-model-types-2026|Batching across model architectures]]**.

![[90 Assets/Volume II/15 Sustainable AI/Prefill Decode Energy eb7941ca5b.svg]]

Figure 13: **Reuse in Prefill and Decode**: Prefill reuses weights across prompt positions; a decode iteration can reuse weights across active sequences. At small batches, weight and KV-cache traffic can leave arithmetic units underused. These paths show mechanisms, not measured utilization or a universal energy-per-token ratio.

^fig-prefill-decode-energy

That split in dataflow governs per-token energy through data movement and arithmetic intensity. During prefill, parallel matrix operations reuse weights across prompt positions and can achieve high compute utilization when the workload exposes enough parallel work. During decode, each iteration produces one token per active sequence while accessing model parameters and an expanding KV cache. At small batches, this traffic can leave arithmetic units underused; batching improves weight reuse, subject to memory capacity and latency constraints.

The energy roofline of [[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/04 The Responsible Fleet/15 Sustainable AI/V2-15.05 Energy Measurement and Modeling#^sec-sustainable-ai-arithmetic-intensity-energy-roofline-6d13-roofline-6d13|section 15.4.2.3]] puts a number on that underuse. At batch size one, each decode step reads every FP16 weight (2 bytes) to perform two FLOPs with it, an arithmetic intensity of 1 FLOP/byte against an energy crossover of 16 FLOP/byte ([[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/04 The Responsible Fleet/15 Sustainable AI/V2-15.05 Energy Measurement and Modeling#^eq-ai-crossover|equation 7]]). Under the chapter’s modeled coefficients, moving each parameter’s bytes from DRAM costs 320 pJ while the arithmetic on it costs 20 pJ. Movement is therefore 16× the arithmetic and about 94 percent of the modeled dynamic energy per generated token, before any KV-cache traffic is counted. The coefficient is the table’s off-package DRAM value; the high-bandwidth memory that feeds an accelerator costs less per byte (**[[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/01 The Fleet/02 Compute Infrastructure/V2-02.04 The Memory Wall#^sec-compute-infrastructure-energy-cost-data-movement-3cd1|Energy cost of data movement]]**), which narrows the ratio without reversing it at batch one. Batching ${B}$ sequences reuses each weight load ${B}$ times and raises the intensity to ${B}$ FLOP/byte, so the two terms balance only near a batch of 16. Batching is therefore the first energy lever for decode, bounded by memory capacity for the KV cache and by the latency target.

Static power waste occurs when powered circuitry waits rather than producing useful outputs. Reducing that waiting can improve energy efficiency, but a comparison must meter the same hardware boundary and preserve output quality, context, latency, and serving load. Prompt processing and autoregressive generation perform different work; equal token counts alone do not establish a fair energy ratio.

### Edge Execution and the Fleet Energy Account

^sec-sustainable-ai-edge-ai-impact-6694

Moving inference from a cloud data center onto a billion phones or vehicles does not make its energy cost disappear; it shifts the draw into device batteries, local thermals, and handset manufacturing. Edge intelligence runs models on user devices or nearby computing nodes, avoiding raw-input transfers to a central facility while adding or extending computation at the edge. Local processing and duty cycling reduce total energy only when the communication and remote work they avoid exceed the device’s added computation and idle power. Device manufacturing joins that added cost when deployment requires new hardware. Its embodied carbon is allocated over the work the device serves ([[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/04 The Responsible Fleet/15 Sustainable AI/V2-15.06 Carbon Footprint Calculation#^sec-sustainable-ai-embodied-carbon-assessment-9de0|section 15.5.5]]), so a lightly used device can carry more manufacturing carbon per inference than the emissions of the transfers that local execution avoids. The comparison holds task quality and service availability fixed.

↩︎ The power, memory, and compute budgets of edge devices, and how on-device training amplifies them, are established in **[[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/03 The Fleet in Production/11 Edge Intelligence/V2-11.04 Design Constraints#^sec-edge-intelligence-design-constraints-c776|Design Constraints]]**.

In autonomous vehicles, time-critical perception and control must meet response deadlines despite variable network connectivity, so onboard computation remains part of the operating budget. Sudhakar et al. ([[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/04 The Responsible Fleet/15 Sustainable AI/V2-15.15 Source References#^ref-sudhakar2023|2023]]) model the potential emissions of a widely deployed autonomous-vehicle fleet, showing how device power and operating time can accumulate at global scale; their comparison concerns fleet-level scenarios, not an assertion that each vehicle consumes as much energy as a data center.

Smartphones, wearables, and sensors follow the same accounting. Aggregate operational energy depends on the number of devices, their power states, and the time spent in each state, so a small average draw per device can still produce a large fleet total. Attributing that total to AI requires separating the additional workload from the device’s other functions. Device power budgets constrain both local inference and learning, so the comparison must also account for duty cycling, selective cloud escalation, and the repeated computation and model transfers that federated training requires.

#### Edge and Mobile Power Budgets

^sec-sustainable-ai-edge-mobile-power-budgets-b3b2-b3b2

Edge devices operate within battery, thermal, and supply limits that differ from those of data center accelerators. These limits follow from the deployment rather than the processor’s instruction set. The engineering choice is to match each inference workload to a device that meets latency and accuracy requirements within its sustained power and energy budgets.

Power budgets reflect the physical constraints of battery capacity, thermal dissipation, and deployment environment. These constraints propagate differently across the edge AI power budget categories, from microcontrollers to vehicles ([[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/04 The Responsible Fleet/15 Sustainable AI/V2-15.08 Training Vs. Inference Energy Analysis#^tbl-edge-power-budgets|table 8]]). TinyML devices (microcontrollers that run small models in kilobytes of memory) must fit average demand to battery-life or harvesting targets while also supporting peak draw. Mobile devices must balance user experience with battery life, and automotive systems face thermal constraints within enclosed vehicle compartments despite having access to vehicle power.

#### TinyML Power State Dynamics

^sec-sustainable-ai-tinyml-power-state-dynamics-ceaf

TinyML devices are the smallest members of edge fleets, sitting below the 2–10 W phones of Archetype C (Federated MobileNet) in both memory and power. Their energy efficiency is a sustainability concern because billions of edge inference calls add up to a measurable carbon footprint at fleet scale. TinyML efficiency depends heavily on duty cycling, where devices alternate between deep sleep and active inference. The duty-cycle model of [[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/04 The Responsible Fleet/15 Sustainable AI/V2-15.05 Energy Measurement and Modeling#^eq-edge-duty-cycle|equation 8]] applies directly, with ${\alpha_{\text{duty}}} = {t_{\text{inference}}}/{T_{\text{period}}}$ and the deep-sleep draw in place of idle power.

↩︎ The same duty-cycled power budget, applied to on-device learning rather than inference, is established in **[[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/03 The Fleet in Production/11 Edge Intelligence/V2-11.11 Production Integration#^sec-edge-intelligence-continuous-training-orchestration-8974|Continuous training orchestration]]**.

Consider a keyword-spotting model on a Cortex-M4 microcontroller, an extreme case that obeys the same battery arithmetic as the Archetype C phones. The device draws 15 mW of active inference power for 20 ms in each detection cycle, falls to a deep-sleep draw of 10 microamps at 3.3 V (33 µW) between evaluations, and repeats on a 1 s detection period during continuous listening. Substituting these values into [[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/04 The Responsible Fleet/15 Sustainable AI/V2-15.05 Energy Measurement and Modeling#^eq-edge-duty-cycle|equation 8]] yields the average power draw:

$$
P_{\text{avg}} = 15\text{ mW} \times 0.02 + 0.033\text{ mW} \times 0.98 = 0.30 + 0.032 \approx 0.33\text{ mW}
$$

A 250 mAh coin cell at 3 V nominal stores 750 mWh, enough for approximately 2,260 hours, about 94 days, under this idealized budget. The estimate covers compute only. Continuous listening also requires powered sensing and input buffering while the microcontroller sleeps, and their energy, regulator losses, and battery derating must enter any deployment estimate.

#### On-Device Learning and the Battery Wall

^sec-sustainable-ai-edge-learning-battery

Gradient-based on-device learning costs more work and memory than inference alone. A dense gradient step needs roughly 2–3$\times$ the forward arithmetic, with activation, gradient, and optimizer-state memory budgeted separately. The battery, thermal, and foreground-latency test that decides when a phone may admit such an update is established in **[[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/03 The Fleet in Production/11 Edge Intelligence/V2-11.04 Design Constraints#^sec-edge-intelligence-energy-thermal-constraint-analysis-3133|Energy, heat, and background admission]]**. Its representative phone envelope (sustained processing within 2–3 W and bursts to 5–10 W for limited periods) bounds how long an on-device update can run alongside battery energy, memory, and the update deadline. Processor TDP alone does not establish feasibility. The sustainability account adds the fleet-scale question of how much energy the admitted updates consume in total.

**Napkin Math 15.8: The energy of learning**

**Problem**: Consider fine-tuning a small language model (1B parameters) on a user’s smartphone overnight. Is this feasible within a 5 percent battery budget?

**Math**:

1.  Phone battery: Typical capacity is approximately 15 Wh, or about 54,000 J.
2.  Budget: 5 percent of 54,000 J = 2,700 J.
3.  Training cost: this step uses illustrative per-parameter energy coefficients for a mobile accelerator (assumed for this scenario, not measured on a particular phone). The backward pass costs twice the forward pass because it computes gradients with respect to both activations and weights, so one training step costs about three forward passes:
    - Forward pass: $\approx$ 2 nJ/param.
    - Backward pass: $\approx$ 4 nJ/param.
    - Total per token: 6 nJ/param $\times 10^9$ params = 6 J/token.
4.  Capacity: 2,700 J / 6 J/token = 450 tokens.

**Systems insight**: The calculation establishes a token capacity, not whether the proposed adaptation fits. That decision also requires the token count needed to reach the quality target, the available memory, and sustained device power. If the required work exceeds the battery budget, parameter-efficient fine-tuning or sparse updates are candidates for reducing cost; their measured energy and achieved quality must establish whether they close the gap.

The local-processing decision compares the energy of the required computation with the communication and remote computation it avoids. Per-bit radio costs motivate the comparison, but the full job must also include repeated training rounds and model transfers.

**Systems Perspective 15.3: The energy hierarchy**

**Trade-off**: The architectural choice between processing data locally and sending it to the cloud is governed by an energy budget. The energy-to-communication ratio motivates comparing local work with transmission, but a decision requires the total energy of each alternative at the required quality and latency.

Energy cost per operation (approximate):

- 32-bit integer add: 0.1 pJ
- 32-bit float mult: 3.7 pJ
- Wireless transmit (1 bit): 100,000–500,000 pJ (Bluetooth/Wi-Fi)

**Systems insight**: Under these illustrative component costs, a transmitted bit consumes far more energy than one arithmetic operation. The radio cost is quoted per bit; per byte it reaches roughly 0.8 to 4 million pJ, about two orders of magnitude above the lower bound given for the wired network row of [[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/04 The Responsible Fleet/15 Sustainable AI/V2-15.05 Energy Measurement and Modeling#^tbl-energy-per-byte|table 2]]. This motivates local processing but does not establish an offloading threshold: include memory, radio startup and protocol overhead, idle power, and remote computation in the full edge-cloud comparison. Local processing wins only when those complete terms favor it.

#### Energy Harvesting for Autonomous Edge AI

^sec-sustainable-ai-energy-harvesting-autonomous-edge-ai-ac2b-autonomous-edge-ai-ac2b

TinyML can operate from harvested ambient energy when the available supply supports the full device workload. Sustained operation requires both an adequate long-term energy budget and a way to handle intervals when harvesting falls below demand, through storage, duty cycling, or suspended execution.

Ambient energy harvesting delivers from about a microwatt to about ten milliwatts per device, depending on the source ([[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/04 The Responsible Fleet/15 Sustainable AI/V2-15.08 Training Vs. Inference Energy Analysis#^tbl-energy-harvesting|table 9]]). A keyword spotting model optimized to 0.5 mW average power matches the nominal output of approximately 5 cm² of indoor solar harvesting only near the top of the listed illumination range, before conversion losses and other device loads. This average-power comparison does not establish uninterrupted operation. The design must cover sensing, storage losses, and periods of dim or absent light, or permit the workload to pause. Ambient supply removes the need for purchased operating electricity only when it covers that complete budget; manufacturing, storage, maintenance, and disposal still enter the lifecycle account.

|:-------------------------------|:-------------------:|:-------------------------------|
| **Indoor solar (1 cm²)**       |  10–100 microwatts  | Periodic sensor classification |
| **Outdoor solar (1 cm²)**      |   1–10 milliwatts   | Continuous keyword spotting    |
| **Thermoelectric (body heat)** |  10–100 microwatts  | Wearable gesture recognition   |

#### Cascade Inference Architecture

^sec-sustainable-ai-cascade-inference-architecture-c710

Beyond individual device efficiency, architectural patterns determine total system energy consumption across edge-cloud boundaries. A cascade architecture deploys a task-appropriate small edge model to filter inputs before cloud inference. [[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/04 The Responsible Fleet/15 Sustainable AI/V2-15.08 Training Vs. Inference Energy Analysis#^eq-cascade-energy|Equation 14]] expresses total energy as the sum of local processing plus probabilistically-triggered cloud costs:

$$
{E_{\text{cascade}}}= {E_{\text{local}}} + {p_{\text{escalate}}}\times ({E_{\text{transmit}}} + {E_{\text{cloud}}}) \tag{14}
$$

^eq-cascade-energy

where ${p_{\text{escalate}}}$ is the probability of requiring cloud inference (measured on the deployment input distribution).

Consider an illustrative visual inspection system where a lightweight edge model, such as MobileNetV3-Small, consumes 0.5 mJ per image classification, whereas a high-capacity cloud model, such as ResNet-152, requires 50 mJ per inference. Transmitting an image across a cellular uplink adds 10 mJ. If the edge model resolves routine cases locally and escalates only ambiguous inputs, for an escalation rate ${p_{\text{escalate}}}$ of 10 percent, substituting these parameters into [[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/04 The Responsible Fleet/15 Sustainable AI/V2-15.08 Training Vs. Inference Energy Analysis#^eq-cascade-energy|equation 14]] gives the average cascade energy:

$$
E_{\text{cascade}} = 0.5 + 0.10 \times (10 + 50) = 0.5 + 6.0 = 6.5 \text{ mJ/image}
$$

Compared to always-cloud inference at 60 mJ per image, this illustrative cascade reduces the modeled energy by 89 percent. Quality requires a separate test of local decisions, missed escalations, and the cloud model; the energy calculation says nothing about accuracy.

#### Wake-Word Triggered Systems

^sec-sustainable-ai-wakeword-triggered-systems-c878

Always-on systems can use hierarchical wake detection to reduce average power across stages of increasing capability and draw. In this illustrative model, an analog front end consumes 10 µW while monitoring for voice activity. A detected event activates a compact neural network wake detector drawing 100 µW. A positive wake decision then triggers full-model inference at 10 mW for 50 ms. The energy calculation uses those assumed state powers and event rates; a deployed system must also account for false activations, missed wake words, and transition costs when evaluating its energy and service quality.

↩︎ The always-on budget of wake-word detection, set by how often the detector evaluates over the listening period, is established in **[[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/03 The Fleet in Production/11 Edge Intelligence/V2-11.03 Why Learn on the Device#^sec-edge-intelligence-realworld-application-domains-c4e4|Where adaptation pays]]**.

For an illustrative speech-active fraction of 5 percent and a wake-event rate of 0.001 per second (one event per 1,000 s), each full-model event lasts 0.05 s. The front end draws continuously, the wake detector draws only during speech, and the full model contributes its event rate times its event energy:

$$
P_{\text{avg}} = 0.01 + 0.05 \times 0.1 + 0.001 \times 10 \times 0.05 = 0.0155\text{ mW}
$$

The hierarchical approach achieves 15.5 µW average power compared to 10 mW for always-active full inference, a reduction of roughly 645× within this modeled boundary. Battery lifetime also requires battery capacity, conversion losses, sensors, and other device loads.

#### Federated Learning Energy Analysis

^sec-sustainable-ai-federated-learning-energy-analysis-2c07

Training at the edge keeps raw data local but replaces its transfer with repeated model downloads and client-update uploads, one of each per participating client in every round of federated averaging. This is the regime of Archetype C (Federated MobileNet), where phones train a shared model within a sustained power budget of a few watts. [[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/04 The Responsible Fleet/15 Sustainable AI/V2-15.08 Training Vs. Inference Energy Analysis#^eq-federated-energy|Equation 15]] contrasts the energy trade-offs between federated and centralized approaches. Here ${R}$ is the number of rounds, and ${N_{\text{clients}}}$ is the number of participating clients per round. The term ${E_{\text{upload}}}$ is one client-update upload, ${E_{\text{raw}}}$ is one client’s raw-data upload, and ${E_{\text{cloud,train}}}$ is the energy of one centralized cloud training run:

$$
{E_{\text{federated}}}= {R}\left[{N_{\text{clients}}}\left({E_{\text{client}}}+ {E_{\text{download}}}+ {E_{\text{upload}}}\right) + {E_{\text{aggregation}}}\right]
$$

$$
{E_{\text{centralized}}}= {N_{\text{clients}}}\times {E_{\text{raw}}}+ {E_{\text{cloud,train}}} \tag{15}
$$

^eq-federated-energy

↩︎ Federated averaging, in which each round sends the model to participating clients and aggregates their updates, is established in **[[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/03 The Fleet in Production/11 Edge Intelligence/V2-11.07 Federated Learning Algorithms#^sec-edge-intelligence-federated-aggregation-protocols-5d37|Federated aggregation protocols]]**.

Federated learning becomes more energy-efficient only when repeated model communication, local training, and aggregation consume less energy than raw-data transfer and centralized training at comparable model quality. The comparison must cover the same client population and account for repeated participation across rounds. Data being larger than one model update is insufficient by itself because downloads and uploads recur. Privacy *and* energy benefits require separate evidence: keeping raw data local does not by itself prevent updates from revealing private information, and any protection mechanism adds its own costs to the energy account.

Training, serving, and edge deployment all draw operational energy, and none of these accounts includes the hardware that performs the work. That physical supply chain, from fabrication through disposal, is the lifecycle term that remains to be counted.
