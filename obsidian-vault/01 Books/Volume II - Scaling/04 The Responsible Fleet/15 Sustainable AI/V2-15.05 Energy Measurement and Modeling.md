# V2-15.05 Energy Measurement and Modeling

[[Home|Home]] · [[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/Volume 2 Overview|Volume Overview]] · [[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/04 The Responsible Fleet/15 Sustainable AI/V2-15 Sustainable AI|V2-15 Sustainable AI]]

^sec-sustainable-ai-part-ii-measurement-assessment-fb0b

A cluster drawing five megawatts during a large language model training run directs only a fraction of that power into matrix multiplications; host processors, memory, networking, power conversion, and cooling consume the rest. Engineers cannot optimize those losses from a single monthly utility bill.

Power delivery networks and cooling systems establish hard physical constraints on data center compute capacity. Systematic measurement turns these constraints into numbers an engineer can act on, provided the measurement fixes what it records and which lifecycle phase it targets before any power model or instrument is applied. Just as performance engineering requires profiling before optimization, sustainable AI engineering requires measurement before mitigation.

↩︎ The power-delivery path, thermal design power, and cooling limits of accelerators are established in **[[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/01 The Fleet/02 Compute Infrastructure/V2-02.07 The Rack#^sec-compute-power-wall|Power and thermal envelopes]]**.

The decision procedure is the same throughout the chapter: measure the dominant lifecycle term, identify the physical bottleneck behind it, choose the intervention that changes that term, and check whether rebound effects erase the gain. Operational electricity, embodied carbon, cooling overhead, water use, and e-waste each call for different levers. Moving a job changes its operational setting without undoing emissions already incurred in manufacturing. Keeping hardware longer changes future procurement needs but also extends its operating energy account. Sustainable design therefore traces each intervention through the terms it changes before comparing the full result.

A sustainability claim is comparable only when it satisfies a **sustainability measurement contract**, which extends the reproducible measurement contract of **[[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/03 The Fleet in Production/09 Fleet Efficiency/V2-09.04 Measuring Goodput#^sec-performance-engineering-measurement-contract|The measurement contract]]**. That contract already fixes the workload and its quality gate, the system state, the timing boundary, and a statistical report with its window and spread. A sustainability claim adds five fields that fix what the energy figure covers and how it becomes carbon. The functional unit names the work being charged, such as one completed training run or 1,000 served requests at a specified output quality. The system boundary states which compute, memory, network, facility, manufacturing, and end-of-life terms enter that account ([[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/04 The Responsible Fleet/15 Sustainable AI/V2-15.15 Source References#^ref-iso140402006lca|International Organization for Standardization 2006a]], [[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/04 The Responsible Fleet/15 Sustainable AI/V2-15.15 Source References#^ref-iso140442006lca|2006b]]). Because multiple workloads and hardware generations share a facility, an attribution rule must then allocate shared infrastructure and embodied emissions. A declared average or marginal carbon signal converts the resulting energy into emissions. The time and location of operation fix which hours and which grid that signal describes. The statistical report carried over from the base contract must then also state how sensitive the conclusion is to those assumptions. A result that omits any of these fields may still diagnose one component, but it cannot support an end-to-end comparison. Large models released after GPT-3 often lack comparable energy disclosures, so filling these fields requires workload telemetry rather than published model documentation alone.

This contract separates two questions that otherwise become conflated. Attributional accounting allocates an inventory of observed emissions to workloads for reporting, typically using average grid intensity and the attribution rule above to share common infrastructure. Consequential accounting estimates how a decision changes emissions, for example by modeling the generator affected when a scheduler moves one megawatt-hour of demand. That generator is the plant on the margin of the grid’s dispatch order, the plant whose output rises or falls when demand changes. A marginal emissions factor reflects that plant, which can burn fossil fuel even on a grid whose average mix is clean. Attributional accounting answers what share of an existing footprint belongs to a workload; consequential accounting answers whether changing the workload changes the footprint ([[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/04 The Responsible Fleet/15 Sustainable AI/V2-15.15 Source References#^ref-present2024carbonfactor|Present et al. 2024]]). Carbon-aware scheduling therefore needs a signal appropriate for consequential analysis even when the organization’s retrospective inventory uses attributional averages. The available marginal factors may be historical estimates, forecasts, or unavailable at the workload’s required time and location. The contract must therefore record the source, resolution, and uncertainty rather than treat “marginal” as a universally observable datum.

### Power Modeling Fundamentals

^sec-sustainable-ai-power-modeling-fundamentals-67cb-67cb

Lowering numerical precision or pruning half the weights of a model does not automatically cut the data center’s electricity bill in half, because static leakage, memory traffic, and facility cooling each take their own share before power reaches the arithmetic units. Tracing where a watt goes requires a model that climbs three levels. The CMOS power equation at the chip level explains why voltage, precision, and switching activity matter. Physical optimization mechanisms show how algorithms change those variables. Facility-level metrics reveal whether chip-level savings survive cooling and power-delivery overhead.

#### The CMOS Power Equation

^sec-sustainable-ai-cmos-power-equation-a33a-a33a

Every digital circuit consumes power through two fundamental mechanisms. **Dynamic power** arises from switching transistors between states, while **static power** results from leakage current that flows even when transistors are nominally off. [[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/04 The Responsible Fleet/15 Sustainable AI/V2-15.05 Energy Measurement and Modeling#^eq-cmos-power|Equation 1]] formalizes the total power consumption:

$$
{P_{\text{total}}}= {P_{\text{dynamic}}}+ {P_{\text{leakage}}} = {\alpha} {C} {V}^2 {f}+ {V} {I_{\text{leak}}} \tag{1}
$$

^eq-cmos-power

In the dynamic component ${P_{\text{dynamic}}}= {\alpha} {C} {V}^2 {f}$, the **switching activity factor** ${\alpha}$ summarizes capacitance-weighted switching per clock cycle and is not useful compute utilization. The term ${C}$ is the effective load capacitance of the circuits and interconnect being switched. Supply voltage ${V}$ enters quadratically, and clock frequency ${f}$ sets the rate of switching opportunities. Useful work per joule depends on how much productive computation those transitions accomplish.

The static power component ${P_{\text{leakage}}} = {V}\cdot {I_{\text{leak}}}$ depends on leakage current, whose temperature sensitivity varies with device technology, voltage, and operating point. Rising temperature can increase leakage, adding heat that further raises temperature. Cooling and power control must keep this feedback within a stable operating range. A leakage model therefore needs parameters appropriate to the device rather than a universal temperature-doubling rule ([[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/04 The Responsible Fleet/15 Sustainable AI/V2-15.15 Source References#^ref-dayarathna2016|Dayarathna et al. 2016]]).

The quadratic voltage dependence means that reducing voltage from 1 V to 0.8 V decreases dynamic power by 36 percent when activity, capacitance, and frequency stay fixed. The circuit must still complete its transitions within each clock period. A lower voltage may therefore require a lower frequency, which further reduces dynamic power but can lengthen execution. The energy benefit depends on both the resulting runtime and the static power consumed during it.

#### Physical Mechanisms of Energy Optimization

^sec-sustainable-ai-optimization-techniques-save-energy-832c

The power equation illuminates why specific optimization techniques can improve efficiency. Quantization reduces numerical precision, which can shrink arithmetic units and reduce bytes moved through the memory hierarchy. The realized energy change depends on the implemented kernels, packing and conversion overhead, memory traffic, utilization, and the original bottleneck ([[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/04 The Responsible Fleet/15 Sustainable AI/V2-15.15 Source References#^ref-gholami2022|Gholami et al. 2022]]). Lower precision does not by itself permit a lower supply voltage: voltage and frequency remain constrained by the physical implementation, process, and timing margins. Quantization therefore changes the operation and data-movement terms, but only measurement establishes the end-to-end reduction.

Pruning removes weights from neural networks, but it does not remove physical transistors from an existing accelerator. It saves dynamic energy when the resulting execution skips arithmetic or data movement. Structured pruning can map to smaller dense kernels; unstructured pruning needs an implementation that exploits the sparse pattern without spending the savings on indexing and irregular memory access. The power equation explains the benefit through reduced switching activity and powered execution time, not a change to the fabricated circuit capacitance ${C}$.

Specialized accelerators can improve useful work per joule by reducing instruction-control overhead, reusing data locally, and mapping operations to suitable datapaths. Keeping arithmetic units productive can amortize static and support power, but raising switching activity without raising useful output only raises power.

#### Facility-Level Power Metrics

^sec-sustainable-ai-facilitylevel-power-metrics-5558

The PUE ratio has entered every facility-energy estimate since the opening energy boundary. The chip-level model now explains what happens inside the IT term, so the ratio itself needs its precise definition and its blind spots. [[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/04 The Responsible Fleet/15 Sustainable AI/V2-15.05 Energy Measurement and Modeling#^eq-pue|Equation 2]] gives the ratio at this chapter’s facility boundary:

$$
\text{PUE} = \frac{{P_{\text{facility}}}}{{P_{\text{IT}}}} \tag{2}
$$

^eq-pue

**Definition 15.2: Power usage effectiveness (PUE)**

***Power usage effectiveness (PUE)*** is the data-center efficiency ratio ML system operators use to compare total facility power consumption against the power consumed specifically by IT equipment $({P_{\text{facility}}}/ {P_{\text{IT}}})$.

1.  **Significance**: It measures the infrastructure overhead of the data center. A PUE of 1.0 is the theoretical ideal; a PUE of 1.10 means that for every 100 watts consumed by IT equipment, an additional 10 watts are required for non-IT facility overhead, including cooling and facility-side power distribution.
2.  **Distinction**: Unlike computing efficiency (which focuses on FLOP/s per watt), PUE focuses on facility efficiency: it measures non-IT overhead relative to IT consumption. Idle equipment and power losses inside the IT measurement boundary remain in the denominator; PUE does not measure useful computation.
3.  **Common pitfall**: A frequent misconception is that a low PUE means a “green” data center. PUE measures only overhead efficiency, not the carbon intensity of the energy source; a coal-powered data center can have a better PUE than a solar-powered one while having a much higher environmental impact.

**Napkin Math 15.3: PUE: The cost of cooling**

**Problem**: A team operates a 2 MW cluster. If the facility can be optimized from a legacy air-cooled PUE (1.58) to state-of-the-art (1.10), how much energy and money does that save annually?

**Math**: Energy saved is the difference in infrastructure overhead $(\text{PUE}-1)$ across the IT load.

1.  **Overhead Reduction**: 1.58 - 1.10 = 0.48.
2.  **Annual energy savings**: 2 MW $\times$ 0.48 $\times$ 8760 h/year $\approx$ 8,409.6 MWh.
3.  **Financial Savings**: 8,409.6 MWh $\times$ \$70/MWh $\approx$ \$588,672. This facility-only saving leaves the model unchanged and scales with reducible non-IT overhead, so cooling optimization matters most in inefficient facilities.

A PUE of 1.0 would mean no measured non-IT overhead, not that all electricity produces useful computation. Idle servers and stalled accelerators remain inside the IT denominator. Surveyed data centers reported an average PUE near 1.55 in 2022, meaning that roughly 55 percent additional energy beyond IT consumption went to infrastructure, with older air-cooled sites higher still ([[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/04 The Responsible Fleet/15 Sustainable AI/V2-15.15 Source References#^ref-davis2022uptime|Uptime Institute 2022]]). Leading hyperscale facilities achieve PUE between 1.1 and 1.2 through advanced cooling techniques including free-air cooling in cold climates, liquid cooling for high-density GPU clusters, and optimized power distribution.

[[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/04 The Responsible Fleet/15 Sustainable AI/V2-15.05 Energy Measurement and Modeling#^eq-wue|Equation 3]] formalizes **water usage effectiveness (WUE)**, capturing the water consumption that evaporative cooling and other processes require:

$$
\text{WUE} = \frac{{W_{\text{water}}}}{{E_{\text{IT}}}} \tag{3}
$$

^eq-wue

The units are liters per kilowatt-hour, with typical values ranging from 0.5 to 2.0 L/kWh depending on climate and cooling technology. A data center with WUE of 1.8 L/kWh training a model requiring 10,000 MWh of IT energy would consume 18 million liters of water. That is equivalent to roughly 40–47 US household-years of water use under a 380,000–450,000 L/year household baseline. A facility-energy estimate must first be converted to the IT boundary before applying this WUE.

Serving has a water account too, and it is computed the same way. Google reports a fleet WUE of 1.15 L/kWh for the data centers that serve its Gemini assistant ([[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/04 The Responsible Fleet/15 Sustainable AI/V2-15.15 Source References#^ref-elsworth2025measuring|Elsworth et al. 2025]]). The same study measured the energy of the median Gemini text prompt across the serving stack ([[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/04 The Responsible Fleet/15 Sustainable AI/V2-15.08 Training Vs. Inference Energy Analysis#^sec-sustainable-ai-inference-energy-costs-aec9|section 15.7.3]] examines that measurement). Applied to that prompt’s IT energy, which excludes facility overhead, the WUE gives the 0.26 mL of water per prompt that the study reports. A single prompt’s water is negligible. A service answering billions of prompts a day turns the same arithmetic into a facility-scale demand, which is why the cooling choice, and the watershed it draws on, belongs in the serving design rather than only in the training budget. How a facility operates the cooling plant it already has also changes these facility-level metrics.

#### DeepMind Energy Efficiency Case Study

^sec-sustainable-ai-case-study-deepmind-energy-efficiency-84fd

Google’s cooling systems illustrate how operating settings affect PUE ([[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/04 The Responsible Fleet/15 Sustainable AI/V2-15.15 Source References#^ref-google2023cooling|2023]]). Server load, weather, and interactions among pumps and chillers complicate fixed operating heuristics ([[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/04 The Responsible Fleet/15 Sustainable AI/V2-15.15 Source References#^ref-buyya2010energyefficient|Buyya et al. 2010]]). Building on earlier work predicting PUE ([[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/04 The Responsible Fleet/15 Sustainable AI/V2-15.15 Source References#^ref-gao2014machine|Gao 2014]]), DeepMind trained neural networks from facility telemetry to predict future PUE and recommend settings. Additional models predicted temperature and pressure to check those recommendations against operating constraints.

In the reported live-site test, the system reduced cooling energy consumption by 40 percent and overall PUE overhead by 15 percent ([[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/04 The Responsible Fleet/15 Sustainable AI/V2-15.15 Source References#^ref-barroso2019|Barroso et al. 2019]]; [[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/04 The Responsible Fleet/15 Sustainable AI/V2-15.15 Source References#^ref-evans2016deepmind|Evans and Gao 2016]]). By [[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/04 The Responsible Fleet/15 Sustainable AI/V2-15.05 Energy Measurement and Modeling#^eq-pue|equation 2]], a 15 percent cut in overhead removes 15 percent of the non-IT term (PUE minus 1), not 15 percent of total facility energy. At equal IT energy, the same fractional overhead reduction saves more electricity where the initial overhead is larger. The reported result establishes a benefit for the tested facility, not an identical saving at every site.

The saving came from changing how the existing pumps, chillers, and cooling towers were operated rather than from installing new cooling equipment, which makes control software a lever available to a facility that cannot be rebuilt. The transferable lesson is to evaluate operating controls alongside equipment changes. Telemetry supports the prediction, operating limits constrain the recommendation, and metered energy establishes whether the change saves electricity.

### Systematic Energy Metrics

^sec-sustainable-ai-systematic-energy-metrics-5779-5779

Cutting a model’s FLOP count in half saves almost no energy when the workload spends nearly all of its joules shuttling weights between off-chip DRAM and the processor. Comparing hardware architectures and algorithmic changes therefore requires a systematic metric ladder that moves from energy per operation to energy per byte and then to the roofline relationship between arithmetic intensity and data movement.

#### Energy per Operation

^sec-sustainable-ai-energy-per-operation-776a-776a

A basic metric for computational energy efficiency is energy consumed per operation, typically measured in picojoules. State the operation type and precision: a floating-point multiply-accumulate (MAC) is conventionally two FLOPs, whereas an integer MAC is two integer arithmetic operations.

Hardware architecture changes which overheads can be reduced. Each architecture class carries an effective energy coefficient in this chapter’s models, paired with the measurement needed to test it ([[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/04 The Responsible Fleet/15 Sustainable AI/V2-15.05 Energy Measurement and Modeling#^tbl-energy-per-op|table 1]]). The coefficients span three orders of magnitude, from 100 pJ/FLOP for a general-purpose core, where instruction fetch and control dominate each operation, to 0.1 pJ/FLOP for a fixed-function datapath. They fold local operand delivery and control into each operation, which is why the GPU coefficient of 10 pJ/FLOP sits well above the roughly 3.7 pJ of a bare FP32 multiply at 45 nm ([[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/04 The Responsible Fleet/15 Sustainable AI/V2-15.15 Source References#^ref-horowitz2014|Horowitz 2014]]). They are order-of-magnitude modeling inputs, not a ranking of products.

| **Architecture**                            | **Effective energy (model)** | **Potential energy advantage**            | **Comparison must include**                           |
|:--------------------------------------------|:----------------------------:|:------------------------------------------|:------------------------------------------------------|
| **General-purpose CPU**                     |         100 pJ/FLOP          | Flexible execution for diverse workloads  | Instruction overhead and achieved utilization         |
| **GPU**                                     |          10 pJ/FLOP          | Parallel arithmetic and data reuse        | Precision, occupancy, and memory traffic              |
| **Systolic accelerator**                    |          1 pJ/FLOP           | Local reuse across a regular datapath     | Supported shapes, precision, and data movement        |
| **Edge accelerator or fixed-function ASIC** |         0.1 pJ/FLOP          | Specialized low-power execution           | Host, memory, peripherals, and duty cycle             |
| **Analog or in-memory compute**             |         Not modeled          | Reduced movement for supported operations | Conversion, peripheral circuits, and achieved quality |

Table 1: **Architecture and Energy Measurement**: The effective-energy column gives the order-of-magnitude coefficient this chapter’s energy models assign to each architecture class, including local operand delivery and control. Specialization can reduce execution overhead, but device specifications with different operation types and system boundaries do not establish an end-to-end energy ranking. Compare the same useful task at the required quality.

^tbl-energy-per-op

Precision affects energy per operation through implementation-specific arithmetic and data movement. Narrower formats can reduce circuit area, switched capacitance, and memory traffic, but bit width alone does not determine wall energy. The measurement contract requires a matched workload and quality target, with the same memory and supporting-system boundary.

#### Energy per Byte

^sec-sustainable-ai-energy-per-byte-5d87-5d87

In AI workloads with low arithmetic intensity, moving operands across storage and interconnect boundaries consumes far more energy than the arithmetic units themselves. An illustrative cost model across registers, caches, main memory, solid-state storage, and the network makes this hierarchy concrete; its coefficients teach the relative cost of movement across boundaries rather than fixed measurements of every platform.

![[90 Assets/Volume II/15 Sustainable AI/Sustainable AI Energy Per Byte Ladder b615d394c9.svg]]

*Two bars on one linear scale: a long blue bar for fetching one FP32 operand from DRAM at 640 pJ and a hairline orange bar for one GPU FLOP at 10 pJ, with a 64 times ratio marked on a leader between them.*

*Fetching an FP32 operand from DRAM costs 64× the FLOP that uses it.*

With the modeled coefficients of [[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/04 The Responsible Fleet/15 Sustainable AI/V2-15.05 Energy Measurement and Modeling#^tbl-energy-per-byte|table 2]], movement from DRAM can dominate the arithmetic it feeds. The rows trace the path from registers through L1 cache and L2 cache to DRAM, Non-Volatile Memory Express (NVMe), and network transfers. Under the GPU coefficient of 10 pJ/FLOP from [[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/04 The Responsible Fleet/15 Sustainable AI/V2-15.05 Energy Measurement and Modeling#^tbl-energy-per-op|table 1]], accessing one FP32 operand from DRAM (4 bytes $\times$ 160 pJ/byte = 640 pJ) costs 64× the energy of the operation that consumes it.

These coefficients agree with the movement costs priced per bit in **[[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/01 The Fleet/02 Compute Infrastructure/V2-02.04 The Memory Wall#^sec-compute-infrastructure-energy-cost-data-movement-3cd1|Energy cost of data movement]]** once the units match. That section’s off-package DRAM figure of about 20 pJ per bit is this table’s 160 pJ/byte, so one 16-bit operand fetched from DRAM costs 320 pJ, where this example fetches a 4-byte FP32 operand. The bare FP16 multiply-accumulate of about 1.5 pJ quoted there sits below the effective GPU coefficient here because that coefficient folds control and local operand delivery into each operation. Transfers across the network fabric are priced per bit in **[[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/02 Distributed ML/05 Collective Communication/V2-05.03 The Physics of Data Movement#^sec-communication-collective-operations-physics-data-movement|The Physics of Data Movement]]**, whose fabric-path figure of about 35 pJ/bit (280 pJ/byte) charges only the serializers, transceivers, and switches between endpoints. The network row here is a whole-system coefficient that also charges endpoint memory, host software, and idle network power, so the two differ by more than an order of magnitude.

^tbl-energy-per-byte

The resulting design levers target data movement as well as arithmetic. On-chip SRAM and registers support operand reuse, while systolic arrays pass operands and partial sums between neighboring processing elements instead of repeatedly returning to off-chip memory. Compression and sparse representations can reduce transferred data when saved payload exceeds metadata and conversion overhead. The energy benefit therefore depends on the implemented memory traffic, rather than solely on nominal model size or arithmetic throughput.

#### Arithmetic Intensity and Energy Roofline

^sec-sustainable-ai-arithmetic-intensity-energy-roofline-6d13-roofline-6d13

↩︎ The performance roofline and its ridge point are established in **[[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/01 The Fleet/02 Compute Infrastructure/V2-02.04 The Memory Wall#^sec-compute-roofline|Roofline model]]**.

Arithmetic intensity, the operations-per-byte ratio recalled in **[[05 Reference/Volume II - Scaling/V2 Single-Machine Foundations#^sec-appdx-dam-taxonomy-arithmetic-intensity-boundary|Arithmetic Intensity Boundary]]**, connects the operation count to the data-movement volume. Combined with energy per operation and per byte, it identifies which contribution dominates this energy model; it does not by itself identify the resource limiting execution speed. [[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/04 The Responsible Fleet/15 Sustainable AI/V2-15.05 Energy Measurement and Modeling#^eq-energy-arithmetic-intensity|Equation 4]] defines the workload ratio. In these equations, ${O}$ is the operation count in FLOPs, ${D_{\text{vol}}}$ is data volume in bytes, ${E_{\text{compute}}}$ is energy per FLOP, and ${E_{\text{move}}}$ is energy per byte moved:

$$
{I}= \frac{{O}}{{D_{\text{vol}}}} \tag{4}
$$

^eq-energy-arithmetic-intensity

At fixed energy coefficients, arithmetic intensity measured in FLOP/byte determines the relative size of the compute and movement terms. [[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/04 The Responsible Fleet/15 Sustainable AI/V2-15.05 Energy Measurement and Modeling#^eq-energy-total|Equation 5]] expresses modeled energy ${E_{\text{total}}}$ at the chip-and-memory boundary as their sum (the facility total ${E_{\text{facility}}}$ appears later in [[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/04 The Responsible Fleet/15 Sustainable AI/V2-15.05 Energy Measurement and Modeling#^eq-total-energy|equation 9]]), while [[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/04 The Responsible Fleet/15 Sustainable AI/V2-15.05 Energy Measurement and Modeling#^eq-energy-roofline|equation 6]] isolates the roofline-style dominant term. Static power and supporting systems require additional terms if the comparison includes them:

$$
{E_{\text{total}}} = {O}\times {E_{\text{compute}}} + {D_{\text{vol}}} \times {E_{\text{move}}} \tag{5}
$$

^eq-energy-total

$$
{E_{\text{dominant}}}= \max\left({O}\times {E_{\text{compute}}}, {D_{\text{vol}}} \times {E_{\text{move}}}\right) \tag{6}
$$

^eq-energy-roofline

The maximum identifies the larger energy contribution, not the sum. Near balance, omitting the other term substantially understates the modeled energy. [[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/04 The Responsible Fleet/15 Sustainable AI/V2-15.05 Energy Measurement and Modeling#^eq-ai-crossover|Equation 7]] defines the crossover arithmetic intensity where compute and memory energy balance:

$$
{I_{\text{crossover}}}= \frac{{E_{\text{move}}}}{{E_{\text{compute}}}} \tag{7}
$$

^eq-ai-crossover

For a GPU with ${E_{\text{compute}}}$ of 10 pJ/FLOP and ${E_{\text{move}}}$ of 160 pJ/byte (DRAM access):

$$
{I_{\text{crossover}}} = \frac{160 \text{ pJ/byte}}{10 \text{ pJ/FLOP}} = 16 \text{ FLOP/byte}
$$

The **energy roofline model** in [[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/04 The Responsible Fleet/15 Sustainable AI/V2-15.05 Energy Measurement and Modeling#^fig-energy-roofline|figure 5]] bounds energy efficiency (GFLOP/J) against arithmetic intensity (FLOP/byte), with a 16 FLOP/byte crossover under the stated component costs. Below this point, the larger energy contribution is memory movement (160 pJ/byte). Above it, arithmetic sets the horizontal upper bound of 100 GFLOP/J (10 pJ/FLOP). The piecewise bound keeps only the larger contribution, whereas actual energy includes both and any omitted system overhead. Its crossover therefore answers a different question from the performance ridge, which equals peak arithmetic throughput divided by memory bandwidth and often sits well above 16 FLOP/byte on modern accelerators. A kernel whose arithmetic intensity falls between the energy crossover and the performance ridge is still paced in execution time by memory bandwidth even though arithmetic already accounts for more of its modeled dynamic energy. Applying this crossover to matrix multiplication shows how problem dimension alone flips the dominant energy term.

The dashed curve uses the dominant energy term (blue where data movement dominates, orange where arithmetic dominates); the solid curve includes both compute and memory energy using the same coefficients as the worked example. At the crossover both terms contribute equally, so total-energy efficiency is half the dominant-term bound. Static and other system energy require additional terms.

**Napkin Math 15.4: MatMul energy analysis**

**Problem**: How do matrix dimensions ${N}$ dictate whether arithmetic energy ${E_{\text{compute}}}$ or memory energy ${E_{\text{move}}}$ dominates matrix multiplication ${\mathbf{C}}= {\mathbf{A}}{\mathbf{B}}$?

**Math**:

1.  Operation & byte count: $2{N}^3$ FLOPs and $12{N}^2$ bytes at FP32, assuming ideal reuse: read each input once and write the output once at the modeled memory boundary. Tiling and limited capacity can increase measured traffic.
2.  Arithmetic intensity: ${I}= \frac{{N}}{6}$ FLOP/byte, so the matrix sits exactly at the energy crossover when ${N}$ = 96 (${I} = 96/6 = 16$), the small-matrix row below.
3.  Regime comparison: small dense work sits at the crossover, large dense work is dominated by arithmetic, and element-wise work by memory movement ([[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/04 The Responsible Fleet/15 Sustainable AI/V2-15.05 Energy Measurement and Modeling#^tbl-matmul-energy-bottleneck-comparison|table 3]]).

| **Workload**                               | **Arithmetic intensity**             | **Compute energy**                                                                  | **Memory energy**                                                                             | **Optimization priority**           |
|:-------------------------------------------|:-------------------------------------|:------------------------------------------------------------------------------------|:----------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------|:------------------------------------|
| Small matrix (${N}=$ 96)                   | ${I} = 96/6 = 16$ FLOP/byte          | $2 \times 96^3 \times 10 \text{ pJ} = 17.69\,{\text{µ}}\text{J} = 0.0177\text{ mJ}$ | $3 \times 96^2 \times 4 \times 160 \text{ pJ} = 17.69\,{\text{µ}}\text{J} = 0.0177\text{ mJ}$ | Balanced at the crossover           |
| Large matrix (${N} = 1000$)                | ${I} = 1000/6 = 167$ FLOP/byte       | $2 \times 10^9 \times 10 \text{ pJ} = 20\text{ mJ}$                                 | $3 \times 10^6 \times 4 \times 160 \text{ pJ} = 1.92\text{ mJ}$                               | Improve compute efficiency          |
| Vector addition (${N_{\text{vec}}}= 1000$) | ${I} = 1000/12000 = 0.083$ FLOP/byte | $1000 \times 10 \text{ pJ} = 0.00001\text{ mJ}$                                     | $12000 \times 160 \text{ pJ} = 0.00192\text{ mJ}$                                             | Reduce data movement through fusion |

Table 3: **Energy Bottleneck Comparison**: The same energy model classifies small dense work, large dense work, and element-wise work by their dominant energy term.

^tbl-matmul-energy-bottleneck-comparison

**Systems insight**: Energy optimization follows the same bottleneck logic as latency optimization. Dense matrix multiply rewards efficient arithmetic; element-wise work rewards reducing memory movement.

The energy roofline model reveals why different optimization strategies suit different workloads. When arithmetic dominates the energy account, large dense matrix operations benefit from lower energy per executed operation. When data movement dominates, element-wise kernels benefit from data layout optimization, kernel fusion to reduce memory round-trips, and on-chip memory utilization. Faster execution alone does not establish either saving; average power and execution time must be measured together. This framework guides architectural and algorithmic choices for sustainable AI system design.

### Energy Measurement Techniques

^sec-sustainable-ai-energy-measurement-techniques-590e-techniques-590e

Reading `nvidia-smi` during a training step misses host DRAM, NVMe storage, network switches, and chiller pumps, whereas reading the substation utility meter lumps every co-located job into a single total. Quantifying AI system energy consumption therefore requires choosing measurement instruments across multiple levels of the hardware stack. On-chip counters provide fine attribution, mobile profilers expose platform-specific subsystems, external edge instruments capture duty-cycle behavior, and system-level tools connect component measurements to the facility overhead that device counters miss.

#### Hardware Power Counters

^sec-sustainable-ai-hardware-power-counters-0ec9

Modern processors expose manufacturer-provided power telemetry that software can query through device interfaces. These counters provide workload-correlated estimates within the rails and sampling windows the device reports; they are not universal ground truth for wall-plug energy, and their update rate, calibration, and component coverage vary by platform. External instruments remain necessary when a comparison requires traceable total energy or finer temporal resolution.

Intel’s **running average power limit (RAPL)** interface exposes cumulative energy counters for supported power domains, with coverage depending on the processor. A controlled measurement samples the relevant counter before and after the workload and divides the energy delta by elapsed time to recover average power. This makes RAPL useful for host-side work, provided the account identifies the domains actually reported and handles counter rollover. Package and subdomain counters must not be added when their coverage overlaps. RAPL does not measure discrete GPU energy, and access can require elevated permissions.

NVIDIA GPUs expose power information through the NVIDIA Management Library, accessible via the `nvidia-smi` command-line tool or programmatic bindings. GPU power monitoring must distinguish an instantaneous reading from a sensor-averaged value; the reported behavior depends on the device and query. Integrating samples requires a cadence and workload interval appropriate to that averaging window. Sampling an averaged signal more frequently does not recover short power spikes. Supported accumulated-energy counters can simplify interval accounting, but their scope and resolution still need checking, and neither method supplies full facility energy by itself.

Edge devices and microcontrollers often lack built-in power counters and require external instrumentation. Select current range, voltage measurement, sampling rate, and analog bandwidth to capture the workload’s active and sleep intervals. Board-level monitors can integrate inference energy; resolving shorter events requires adequate instrument bandwidth and careful treatment of filtering and aliasing. A fast sample rate alone cannot attribute energy to individual operations.

#### Mobile Platform Energy Profiling

^sec-sustainable-ai-mobile-platform-energy-profiling-7f9d

On mobile platforms, measurement depends on how much subsystem attribution the host operating system exposes. Some platforms report per-rail or per-component power for the CPU, GPU, neural processing unit, and radio, which lets a developer see which subsystem dominates total draw. Others expose only relative energy-impact scores or thermal states, which are useful for spotting regressions during development but cannot support an absolute energy claim. Vendor profilers that correlate power traces with kernel execution sit between these extremes. The design question is whether the reported quantity covers the electrical boundary the comparison needs, not which tool to use. When it cannot, external instrumentation is required. Edge power measurement instruments differ by measurement role, and each role requires its own checks ([[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/04 The Responsible Fleet/15 Sustainable AI/V2-15.05 Energy Measurement and Modeling#^tbl-edge-power-monitors|table 4]]).

| **Instrument class**             | **Measurement role**                | **Required checks**                                        |
|:---------------------------------|:------------------------------------|:-----------------------------------------------------------|
| **Board current monitor**        | Integrate energy on selected rails  | Voltage, sample interval, shunt losses, and event duration |
| **Multi-rail monitor**           | Attribute energy among components   | Channel synchronization and coverage                       |
| **Wide-range external analyzer** | Capture sleep and active intervals  | Bandwidth, range transitions, and calibration              |
| **Automated power analyzer**     | Repeat duty-cycle and battery tests | Triggering, integration window, and supply behavior        |

Table 4: **Edge Power Measurement Instruments**: Choose instrumentation by the event duration, dynamic range, and electrical boundary being measured. Verify configured specifications rather than assuming one device is universally sufficient.

^tbl-edge-power-monitors

#### Edge Measurement Methodology

^sec-sustainable-ai-edge-measurement-methodology-c02a

Edge energy measurements are useful only when they reflect the deployed duty cycle rather than an idealized active-only inference burst, so reproducible evaluation requires experimental controls matched to that workload. Simultaneous current and voltage logging must span both the active and quiescent states being measured. Logging voltage alongside current captures rail voltage fluctuations rather than inferring power from current alone. Thermal and frequency stabilization is necessary for sustained-load comparisons, whereas cold starts and periodic bursts must be measured across their actual state transitions. Because edge devices spend most of their deployment life waiting between events, realistic duty-cycle accounting records both peak inference draw and time-averaged power across typical idle intervals. Component attribution may isolate or subtract peripheral loads to expose accelerator consumption. A deployment estimate, however, must restore the radios, sensors, and other subsystems needed to deliver the service. For duty cycle accounting, [[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/04 The Responsible Fleet/15 Sustainable AI/V2-15.05 Energy Measurement and Modeling#^eq-edge-duty-cycle|equation 8]] expresses this relationship between active and idle power:

$$
{P_{\text{avg}}}= {P_{\text{active}}}\times {\alpha_{\text{duty}}} + {P_{\text{idle}}}\times (1 - {\alpha_{\text{duty}}}) \tag{8}
$$

^eq-edge-duty-cycle

where ${\alpha_{\text{duty}}}$ is the duty cycle (fraction of time performing inference).

#### System-Level Energy Profiling

^sec-sustainable-ai-systemlevel-energy-profiling-eecf

Comprehensive energy accounting requires combining chip-level measurements with infrastructure overhead. The component terms must be disjoint: GPU-board telemetry that already includes device memory must not be added to another measurement of that same memory. They must also cover the IT boundary, with storage and server support loads included in the relevant measured terms. [[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/04 The Responsible Fleet/15 Sustainable AI/V2-15.05 Energy Measurement and Modeling#^eq-total-energy|Equation 9]] formalizes total energy as the sum of those component contributions scaled by facility overhead:

$$
{E_{\text{facility}}}= ({E_{\text{CPU}}}+ {E_{\text{GPU}}}+ {E_{\text{memory}}}+ {E_{\text{network}}}) \times \text{PUE} \tag{9}
$$

^eq-total-energy

No single counter spans the full energy path, so system-level profilers like Intel VTune, NVIDIA Nsight Systems, and open-source tools such as PowerJoular aggregate measurements across components. On-chip counters estimate device power and may differ in sampling rate, coverage, and calibration; a rack power distribution unit (PDU) provides an independent measurement of the downstream IT load. A rack PDU does not measure central chillers, pumps, lighting, or upstream conversion losses, so facility energy must instead come from facility meters or from an explicitly time-matched PUE applied to the measured IT load. The same rule holds for power: facility power is the measured IT power, the sum of server, network, and storage equipment power, multiplied by the PUE of [[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/04 The Responsible Fleet/15 Sustainable AI/V2-15.05 Energy Measurement and Modeling#^eq-pue|equation 2]], so the overhead enters once at the facility boundary.

Development-time carbon tracking matters when it feeds placement and complexity decisions rather than becoming a report after the fact. Tools such as CarbonTracker ([[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/04 The Responsible Fleet/15 Sustainable AI/V2-15.15 Source References#^ref-anthony2020carbontracker|Anthony et al. 2020]]) and CodeCarbon ([[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/04 The Responsible Fleet/15 Sustainable AI/V2-15.15 Source References#^ref-schmidt2021codecarbon|Schmidt et al. 2021]]) wrap the workload boundary, estimate energy from hardware counters or utilization models, attach the local grid intensity, and record the resulting emissions alongside the experiment metadata. The important systems pattern is not the API call but the timing of the measurement: the estimate must appear while model size, training duration, region, and schedule are still adjustable, before the run has already consumed its energy. Even then, facility energy is still not a complete footprint, because converting it to emissions requires a grid factor and the hardware that drew it carries manufacturing emissions that no power meter records.
