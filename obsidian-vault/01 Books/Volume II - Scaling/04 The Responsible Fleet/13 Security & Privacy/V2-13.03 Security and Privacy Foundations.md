# V2-13.03 Security and Privacy Foundations

[[Home|Home]] · [[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/Volume 2 Overview|Volume Overview]] · [[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/04 The Responsible Fleet/13 Security & Privacy/V2-13 Security & Privacy|V2-13 Security & Privacy]]

^sec-security-privacy-foundations

Defense starts by separating two concerns that often share the same vocabulary. Security asks how an adversary can steal, manipulate, or disable the fleet; privacy asks how sensitive information can leak or be inferred even when the system behaves as designed. Keeping those questions separate determines which controls belong at each layer: authentication and isolation around adversarial pathways, provenance and monitoring across the release chain, and privacy accounting wherever learned parameters carry traces of the data that produced them.

### Security Defined

^sec-security-privacy-security-defined-1129

The first half of that separation is security, which defends model parameters, training pipelines, deployment infrastructure, and inference interfaces against intentional adversarial behavior. An attacker who crafts an adversarial patch on a stop sign, steals model weights from an unauthenticated bucket, or floods a generative endpoint with cost-maximizing queries compromises the system’s integrity, confidentiality, or availability.

**Definition 13.1: Security**

***Security*** is the set of system properties (confidentiality, integrity, and availability) that protect an ML system’s data, model weights, and inference pipeline from intentional adversarial actions, spanning both the infrastructure layer (network intrusion, credential theft) and the algorithmic layer (model extraction, prompt injection, adversarial examples).

1.  **Significance**: Security failures operate on both surfaces simultaneously. At the infrastructure layer, theft of a large proprietary model represents direct IP loss. At the algorithmic layer, model extraction via black-box queries can recover decision boundaries or distill a functional approximation of a deployed model at a small fraction of its training cost ([[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/04 The Responsible Fleet/13 Security & Privacy/V2-13.06 Model-Specific Attack Vectors#^sec-security-privacy-model-theft-1879|section 13.5.1]] quantifies the query budgets), bypassing the investment and competitive moat. Either failure transfers the value of the original training investment to the attacker without the attacker paying its compute cost.
2.  **Distinction**: Security specifies an intentional adversary and the boundaries that actor can cross. Robustness evaluates how model behavior changes under specified perturbations, including both accidental shifts and adversarial inputs; the two concerns overlap when the perturbation comes from an attacker.
3.  **Common pitfall**: A frequent misconception is that traditional IT security (firewalls, access controls, encryption) adequately secures ML systems. ML introduces an algorithmic attack surface orthogonal to infrastructure: a properly authenticated API request containing an adversarial input or a prompt injection bypasses all network-layer defenses and manipulates model behavior through the model’s own learned functions.

A facial recognition system deployed in public transit infrastructure, for example, may be targeted with adversarial inputs that cause it to misidentify individuals or fail entirely, representing a runtime security vulnerability that threatens both accuracy and system availability. The confidentiality-integrity-availability triad matters because each property breaks a different system contract. Confidentiality protects data and model access. Integrity protects the correctness of training and inference behavior. Availability protects the service from being disabled or degraded when it is needed.

### Privacy Defined

^sec-security-privacy-privacy-defined-da84

Whereas security addresses adversarial manipulation and unauthorized access, privacy limits the exposure and inference of sensitive information across training data, inference inputs, and model outputs. An authorized user querying a clinical classifier or a language model can extract a patient’s sensitive diagnosis or verbatim personal record without ever breaching a firewall or stealing a password. Privacy protections must therefore account for unintended disclosure during ordinary operation. They must also account for active adversaries who choose queries, combine outputs with auxiliary information, or exploit repeated releases to infer sensitive facts.

**Definition 13.2: Privacy**

***Privacy*** is the protection of sensitive information from unauthorized disclosure, inference, and misuse across the ML lifecycle.

1.  **Significance**: It limits the exposure risk of training data and user inputs. Differentially private training introduces a utility-privacy trade-off: tighter privacy bounds generally require more noise or data, which can increase the training compute, the ${T_{\text{compute}}}$ term of the Fleet Law (**[[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/00 Introduction/01 Introduction/V2-01.06 The C³ Taxonomy and Foundations of Scale#^eq-fleet-law|equation 1.3]]**), required to reach a target utility.
2.  **Distinction**: Unlike confidentiality (which focuses on access control), privacy in ML focuses on inference risks: the ability of a user, operator, or adversary to infer sensitive facts or reconstruct training samples from model outputs, weights, or repeated releases.
3.  **Common pitfall**: A frequent misconception is that removing names (de-identification) is sufficient for privacy. In reality, neural networks are correlation engines that can inadvertently memorize and leak unique snippets of sensitive data through high-dimensional patterns.

Privacy failures are not limited to raw records or explicit identifiers. Aggregated behavioral traces can reveal sensitive routines when the surrounding geography makes individuals and sites easy to infer. Differential privacy (DP) bounds how much the distribution of released results can change when one protected contribution changes. Mechanisms can achieve this guarantee through calibrated randomization, such as adding noise to a query result. The protected contribution may be one record or all records from one person; the guarantee must specify which. Its utility cost depends on the task, data, mechanism, and training procedure.

Differential privacy provides one mechanism-level guarantee; it does not provide lifecycle privacy by itself. A system must still minimize collection, establish an appropriate basis for use, enforce access controls, limit retention, honor deletion obligations, and prevent raw inputs or sensitive outputs from leaking through logs and caches. These controls answer questions that a formal privacy bound does not: whether the data should have been collected, who may use them, how long derived artifacts remain, and which copies must be removed. Production privacy therefore composes formal accounting with data-governance controls across collection, training, serving, telemetry, and retirement.

Before the formal treatment of differential privacy, the chapter needs only one knob, the privacy budget ${\epsilon}$. When one protected contribution changes, the probability of any released outcome changes by at most a factor of $e^{{\epsilon}}$, apart from a small additive slack the formal definition allows, so smaller values give a tighter bound, and ${\epsilon}$ is not an accuracy percentage. Because additional data-dependent DP releases about the same data spend more of that budget, the budget belongs to the history of releases rather than to any one server. Using a fixed DP-trained model does not spend additional training privacy budget. [[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/04 The Responsible Fleet/13 Security & Privacy/V2-13.10 Differential Privacy#^sec-security-privacy-differential-privacy-8c2b|Section 13.9]] states the formal definition, its additive slack term, and the accounting that tracks spending across releases, once the defense architectures that surround it are in place.

### Security Versus Privacy

^sec-security-privacy-security-vs-privacy-e0b8

Security and privacy intersect in controls such as encrypted storage, yet they differ in their objectives, threat models, and mitigation strategies. Encrypting a hospital’s training bucket stops an external attacker from stealing files, whereas the model trained on that bucket can still reveal whether a specific patient was in the cohort through an ordinary, authorized prediction query. These security-privacy distinctions matter because the two domains optimize for different failure modes. Their distinct goals carry through to typical concerns, example attacks, the defenses practitioners must consider, and regulatory standing ([[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/04 The Responsible Fleet/13 Security & Privacy/V2-13.03 Security and Privacy Foundations#^tbl-security-privacy-comparison|table 1]]).

| **Aspect**                  | **Security**                               | **Privacy**                                                      |
|:----------------------------|:-------------------------------------------|:-----------------------------------------------------------------|
| **Primary Goal**            | Prevent unauthorized access or disruption  | Limit exposure of sensitive information                          |
| **Threat Model**            | Adversarial actors (external or internal)  | Passive exposure and active inference with auxiliary information |
| **Typical Concerns**        | Model theft, poisoning, evasion attacks    | Data leakage, re-identification, memorization                    |
| **Example Attack**          | Adversarial inputs cause misclassification | Model inversion infers sensitive training-data properties        |
| **Representative Defenses** | Access control, adversarial training       | Differential privacy; federation with accounted privacy controls |
| **Relevance to Regulation** | Emphasized in cybersecurity standards      | Central to data protection laws (for example, GDPR)              |

Table 1: **Security-Privacy Distinctions**: Machine learning systems require distinct approaches to security and privacy; security mitigates adversarial threats targeting system functionality, while privacy protects sensitive information from both intentional and unintentional exposure through data leakage or re-identification. This table clarifies how differing goals and threat models shape the specific concerns and mitigation strategies for each domain.

**Systems Perspective 13.1: The privacy-utility trade-off**

Security and privacy are deeply interrelated but not interchangeable. A secure system helps maintain privacy by restricting unauthorized access to models and data. Privacy-preserving designs can improve security by reducing the attack surface; minimizing the retention of sensitive data reduces the risk of exposure if a system is compromised.

However, their controls can impose competing requirements. Differential privacy bounds information disclosed by a release but may lower model utility. Encryption restricts who can inspect protected data, so an audit that needs those data requires an authorized inspection path and appropriate key custody. The design must preserve useful evidence while limiting what that evidence reveals.

Security and privacy therefore constrain the same system through different boundaries. Access control, cryptography, trusted execution, and differential privacy address different failure modes; their combination must protect model assets, limit data exposure, and retain the evidence needed to investigate failures. The breach histories make those boundaries concrete by tracing how aggregate releases, compromised components, and excessive access can expose information or alter system behavior.
