# V2-13.02 The Expanded Attack Surface

[[Home|Home]] · [[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/Volume 2 Overview|Volume Overview]] · [[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/04 The Responsible Fleet/13 Security & Privacy/V2-13 Security & Privacy|V2-13 Security & Privacy]]

^sec-security-privacy-security-privacy-ml-systems-0b1e

A serving fleet can meet its latency target while an authenticated client collects enough responses to copy model behavior. A federated training job can converge while a participating device submits poisoned updates. The release gates and hierarchical monitoring that govern the fleet can report both systems healthy, because a copied model and a planted backdoor need not move any availability, latency, or aggregate-quality signal. Operational health alone therefore cannot establish security. In the fleet stack (**[[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/00 Introduction/01 Introduction/V2-01.03 From Single Node to Fleet#^fig-fleet-stack|The Fleet Stack]]**), the governance band begins with the Assurance row, which sits directly above Operations and which this chapter shares with **[[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/04 The Responsible Fleet/14 Robust AI/V2-14 Robust AI#^sec-robust-ai|Robust AI]]**. That row must preserve the confidentiality, integrity, and availability of the fleet across the same interfaces that make distributed training and serving possible.

Selecting a defense starts by identifying the protected asset, the actor who threatens it, the access that actor has, and the boundary that access must not cross. An API client can observe responses; a training participant can submit updates; a host administrator may inspect runtime state. Those capabilities call for different controls. The breach cases make these boundary failures concrete, the threat model turns them into explicit assumptions, and the later mechanisms enforce those assumptions at measurable compute, communication, and coordination cost.

An attacker can steal database records, alter them, or disrupt access to them. Machine learning adds a learned representation through which those failures can propagate. An adversary may probe a model for memorized training examples or poison its training data to introduce a backdoor that activates on a chosen trigger. Protecting the stored records therefore does not fully protect the behavior learned from them or the information exposed through predictions.

Learned-information exposure follows from the difference between *transient processing* and *persistent learning*. Many applications process sensitive data without encoding it into learned parameters; machine learning systems can preserve patterns from training data in their model parameters. Sensitive information may then remain in a model after the original processing has ended and become accessible through outputs or systematic interrogation. Queries to a healthcare model may expose memorized patient information, while queries to a proprietary model may reveal enough behavior to train a substitute. The first threatens training-data privacy ([[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/04 The Responsible Fleet/13 Security & Privacy/V2-13.06 Model-Specific Attack Vectors#^sec-security-privacy-leakage-authorized-outputs|section 13.5.2]]); the second threatens model confidentiality.

Operational platforms manage hundreds of models across distributed infrastructure, extending the attack surface across workers, devices, and administrative domains. In synchronous data-parallel training, one compromised worker contributes directly to the gradient that every replica applies, and the collective traffic carries that contribution across the training fabric. Its confidentiality depends on the fabric’s isolation and encryption controls. Federated aggregation creates another observation boundary because model updates can reveal information even when raw records remain on devices. Accelerators and caches shared among tenants introduce extraction and leakage risks that also exist on a single shared machine. [[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/04 The Responsible Fleet/13 Security & Privacy/V2-13.07 Fleet-Internal Trust Boundaries#^sec-security-privacy-fleet-trust-boundaries|Section 13.6]] develops these fleet-internal boundaries. Scaling the system requires identifying which actors can observe or alter each exchange.

↩︎ Multi-tenant platforms and the isolation they require are established in **[[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/03 The Fleet in Production/12 ML Operations at Scale/V2-12.09 Platform Engineering#^sec-ml-operations-scale-multitenancy-isolation-c3ff|Multi-tenancy and isolation]]**.

Architectural complexity compounds these challenges. A contemporary ML deployment spans data ingestion pipelines, distributed training infrastructure, model serving systems, and continuous monitoring frameworks, each introducing distinct vulnerabilities as [[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/04 The Responsible Fleet/13 Security & Privacy/V2-13.02 The Expanded Attack Surface#^fig-attack-surface|figure 1]] maps across the ML lifecycle. Continuous adaptation at edge nodes and federated coordination protocols add further entry points, each with its own trust assumptions.

![[90 Assets/Volume II/13 Security Privacy/Attack Surface Taxonomy 2d24d14429.svg]]

Figure 1: **ML System Attack Surface**: Red arrows mark where an adversary enters the system: the training data, the training job and its dependencies, the stored weights, the serving API, and the infrastructure beneath all three layers. Each entry point calls for a different control, so defense spans data collection, training and weights, the serving interface, and the provenance of firmware and hardware.

**Self-Check: Question**

1.  A security team deploys an enterprise ML inference service behind a web application firewall with strict mutual TLS (mTLS) authentication and rate limiting. Despite zero network-level intrusions, an external adversary reconstructs the model’s proprietary decision boundaries and extracts memorized training records. What fundamental characteristic of ML systems explains this vulnerability?

    1.  ML models act on and compress learned data, exposing an algorithmic attack surface through properly authenticated inputs and valid response signals.
    2.  mTLS certificates fail to encrypt floating-point tensors at the network transport layer.
    3.  Traditional firewalls cannot inspect JSON payloads formatted as numerical arrays.
    4.  Token-bucket rate limiting in standard firewalls is incapable of tracking HTTP/2 request streams.

2.  An ML platform engineer must classify two distinct failure modes: (1) a malicious actor submits crafted audio noise to cause an emergency dispatch classifier to miss distress calls, and (2) an observer examines a public salary prediction model and infers whether a specific executive was included in the training set. How should these two issues be categorized according to threat model and objective?

    1.  1.  is a privacy violation targeting confidentiality; (2) is a security breach targeting system availability.
    2.  1.  is an adversarial security attack targeting model integrity; (2) is a privacy leakage issue targeting participation inference.
    3.  Both (1) and (2) are pure infrastructure security incidents requiring network-layer firewall rule updates.
    4.  1.  is a stochastic robustness failure; (2) is an unauthorized access control violation requiring credential rotation.

3.  An inference service encrypts its stored training records and authenticates every caller, yet an authorized caller extracts memorized records from its predictions. Explain what the two controls protect and which privacy question remains.
