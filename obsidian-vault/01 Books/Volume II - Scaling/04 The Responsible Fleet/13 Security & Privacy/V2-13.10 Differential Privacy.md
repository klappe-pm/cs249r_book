# V2-13.10 Differential Privacy

[[Home|Home]] · [[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/Volume 2 Overview|Volume Overview]] · [[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/04 The Responsible Fleet/13 Security & Privacy/V2-13 Security & Privacy|V2-13 Security & Privacy]]

^sec-security-privacy-differential-privacy-8c2b

Suppose an adversary queries a medical diagnosis model using the exact attributes of a known patient and compares the answers with what a model trained without that patient would return. Encryption and access control cannot help here. The adversary is an authorized caller, and the query reaches only the trained model, never the stored records. A difference in one pair of predictions alone does not prove a privacy violation. The relevant question is how much the distribution of released outcomes permits an adversary to distinguish datasets with and without that patient, relative to a stated privacy bound.

Differential privacy constrains a randomized training or analysis mechanism so that its output distributions remain close on adjacent datasets. The adjacency definition specifies the protected contribution, such as one record or all records from one person. The information leakage invariant (principle [[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/04 The Responsible Fleet/V2 Responsible Fleet Principles#^pri-information-leakage|23]]) holds that any observable output can carry information about a protected training record, so a privacy claim must name the protected unit and the observations available to an adversary. Differential privacy answers that requirement by bounding the additional evidence a release supplies about the named contribution. For a fixed mechanism and workload, tighter privacy bounds require more noise or fewer data-dependent releases, and the first cost appears as lost utility. Recovering that utility with more data or more training steps raises the training compute, the ${T_{\text{compute}}}$ term of the Fleet Law, and per-example gradient clipping in differentially private stochastic gradient descent (DP-SGD) lowers throughput on its own. Whether a tighter bound costs accuracy, compute, or both is therefore a design choice, which places DP in the deployment architecture rather than only in a legal checklist.

**Definition 13.5: Differential privacy**

***Differential privacy*** is a mathematical guarantee that bounds changes in the output distribution of a randomized algorithm under a specified change to one protected contribution. The neighboring relation defines that contribution and the permitted change. Approximate differential privacy permits a multiplicative bound and additive slack, formalized by the $({\epsilon}, {\delta})$ bound $\Pr[{\mathcal{M}}({X}) \in {\mathcal{S}}] \leq e^{{\epsilon}} \Pr[{\mathcal{M}}({X'}) \in {\mathcal{S}}] + {\delta}$ for adjacent datasets ${X}$ and ${X'}$ and any measurable output set ${\mathcal{S}}$ ([[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/04 The Responsible Fleet/13 Security & Privacy/V2-13.15 Source References#^ref-dwork2006calibrating|Dwork et al. 2006]]; [[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/04 The Responsible Fleet/13 Security & Privacy/V2-13.15 Source References#^ref-dwork2014|Dwork and Roth 2014]]).

1.  **Significance**: The privacy budget ${\epsilon}$ converts an algorithmic property into an engineering currency that can be allocated, composed, and depleted. Production reports and benchmark studies show that privacy budgets are deployment-specific: Apple describes local-DP telemetry deployments with per-event ${\epsilon}$ values chosen by use case, while DP-SGD studies show accuracy and compute trade-offs across benchmark tasks ([[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/04 The Responsible Fleet/13 Security & Privacy/V2-13.15 Source References#^ref-apple2017learningprivacy|Apple Differential Privacy Team 2017]]; [[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/04 The Responsible Fleet/13 Security & Privacy/V2-13.15 Source References#^ref-abadi2016deep|Abadi et al. 2016]]; [[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/04 The Responsible Fleet/13 Security & Privacy/V2-13.15 Source References#^ref-bu2020gaussian|Bu et al. 2020]]; [[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/04 The Responsible Fleet/13 Security & Privacy/V2-13.15 Source References#^ref-de2022dp|De et al. 2022]]). Smaller ${\epsilon}$ raises the noise floor and degrades classification accuracy along the empirical frontier plotted in [[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/04 The Responsible Fleet/13 Security & Privacy/V2-13.10 Differential Privacy#^fig-privacy-utility-frontier|figure 26]], where CIFAR-10 suffers double-digit accuracy drops even at ${\epsilon}= 8$. DP-SGD can also reduce training throughput because per-sample gradient clipping works against the batch-level parallelism that makes accelerator training efficient.
2.  **Distinction**: Unlike de-identification, which removes identifiers from records and fails against re-identification through auxiliary data, and unlike statistical disclosure control, which depends on the attacker’s model, differential privacy is a property of the algorithm, not of the data. Its guarantee holds against any adversary, including one with arbitrary side information about other records in the dataset.
3.  **Common pitfall**: A frequent misconception is that an ${\epsilon}$ label applies independently to every data access. Sequential composition of ${k}$ DP mechanisms, each with privacy loss ${\epsilon}_0$, yields total loss at most ${k}{\epsilon}_0$ under basic composition, with tighter bounds available from mechanism-specific accountants ([[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/04 The Responsible Fleet/13 Security & Privacy/V2-13.10 Differential Privacy#^sec-security-privacy-privacy-budget-composition-edbe|section 13.9.2]]). A system that runs DP-SGD steps or answers interactive DP queries against sensitive data must therefore track cumulative loss and stop or redesign the release when the allocated budget is exhausted. Rotating cryptographic keys does not reset privacy loss, while querying a fixed DP-trained model is post-processing and incurs no additional training privacy loss.

A randomized mechanism ${\mathcal{M}}$ run on adjacent datasets ${X}$ and ${X'}$ produces two output densities that overlap without coinciding ([[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/04 The Responsible Fleet/13 Security & Privacy/V2-13.10 Differential Privacy#^fig-differential-privacy|figure 25]], blue and orange curves). Overlap means that either dataset can produce outcomes in the same region; it does not make the distributions identical. For a discrete outcome ${o}$ in $\text{Range}({\mathcal{M}})$, relative likelihood can be expressed as $\Pr[{\mathcal{M}}({X})={o}] / \Pr[{\mathcal{M}}({X'})={o}]$ when the denominator is positive. Continuous curves instead depict densities, whose ratio is not their vertical difference. The $({\epsilon}, {\delta})$ guarantee applies to probabilities of measurable output sets: it bounds one probability by $e^{{\epsilon}}$ times the other plus ${\delta}$. For a continuous output, those probabilities are areas under the curves over the selected set. The definition therefore constrains distributional change across all such sets, rather than declaring an overlapping region private or treating additive slack as a pointwise density-ratio allowance.

![[90 Assets/Volume II/13 Security Privacy/Cell 16 Output 1 2aaadf089e.png]]

Figure 25: **Differential Privacy and Output Distributions**: These illustrative densities show how outputs can overlap without having identical likelihoods. Differential privacy bounds how much the output distribution can change between adjacent datasets ${X}$ and ${X'}$. The privacy parameters limit, rather than eliminate, the information an observer can gain about any one individual’s inclusion.

^fig-differential-privacy

A randomized algorithm ${\mathcal{M}}$ is said to be ${\epsilon}$-differentially private if, for all adjacent datasets ${X}$ and ${X'}$ differing in one record, and for all measurable output sets ${\mathcal{S}} \subseteq \text{Range}({\mathcal{M}})$, the following holds ([[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/04 The Responsible Fleet/13 Security & Privacy/V2-13.15 Source References#^ref-dwork2006calibrating|Dwork et al. 2006]]; [[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/04 The Responsible Fleet/13 Security & Privacy/V2-13.15 Source References#^ref-dwork2014|Dwork and Roth 2014]]):

$$
\Pr[{\mathcal{M}}({X}) \in {\mathcal{S}}] \leq e^{{\epsilon}} \Pr[{\mathcal{M}}({X'}) \in {\mathcal{S}}]
$$

The parameter ${\epsilon}$ quantifies the privacy budget. In the pure-DP definition above, smaller values of ${\epsilon}$ impose a tighter likelihood-ratio bound; achieving that bound may require more noise or a different mechanism. No universal ${\epsilon}$ value denotes “strong,” “moderate,” or acceptable privacy: interpretation depends on adjacency, the additive slack ${\delta}$ in approximate DP, composition, and the consequences of inference. Under pure DP, the multiplicative factor $e^{{\epsilon}}$ bounds ratios of probabilities for the same output event on adjacent datasets. Approximate DP relaxes that event-probability inequality with additive slack, as developed below.

This bound limits how distinguishable the output distributions can be when a protected contribution changes; it does not make them identical or prevent inference of population-level patterns. Common DP mechanisms add calibrated noise to model updates or query responses, using distributions such as Laplace or Gaussian. Training techniques like DP-SGD[[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/04 The Responsible Fleet/13 Security & Privacy/V2-13.16 Footnotes#^fn22|²²]] combine contribution bounds with calibrated noise and composition accounting. Their guarantee applies to the specified contribution and release, which must be identified before selecting the mechanism.

### Mathematical Foundations and Privacy Parameters

^sec-security-privacy-dp-mathematical-foundations-7e4a

Attaching an ${\epsilon}$ label to a model without stating whether adjacency protects one record or one person, how many training steps composed, and what ${\delta}$ slack was allowed makes the privacy claim meaningless. Repeated training steps, retraining runs, or queries that access protected data may compose, while post-processing a fixed DP release incurs no additional privacy loss for that data. The mathematical foundations specify the guarantee, noise calibration, and accounting for those data-dependent operations so teams can define a valid accounting boundary before attaching an ${\epsilon}$ value to a deployed release.

#### The $({\epsilon}, {\delta})$-Differential Privacy Formulation

^sec-security-privacy-epsilon-deltadifferential-privacy-formulation-bbe9

The pure ${\epsilon}$-DP definition provides strong guarantees but can be overly restrictive for practical ML applications. The relaxed $({\epsilon}, {\delta})$-differential privacy of the definition callout adds slack ${\delta}$ to the probability bound controlled by ${\epsilon}$, and it is the starting point for noise calibration. Approximate DP is widely used with Gaussian perturbations in deep learning. Its role follows from the probability bound and noise calibration, not from unbounded support alone; Laplace noise also has unbounded support.

Formally, a randomized algorithm ${\mathcal{M}}$ satisfies $({\epsilon}, {\delta})$-differential privacy if for all adjacent datasets ${X}, {X'}$ and all measurable output sets ${\mathcal{S}}$:

$$
\Pr[{\mathcal{M}}({X}) \in {\mathcal{S}}] \leq e^{{\epsilon}} \Pr[{\mathcal{M}}({X'}) \in {\mathcal{S}}] + {\delta}
$$

The parameter ${\delta}$ is additive slack in this event-probability inequality, not generally the probability of a catastrophic disclosure. In practice, ${\delta}$ is commonly chosen smaller than the inverse dataset size, often ${\delta}< 1/{D}$ or smaller depending on policy and threat model; smaller ${\delta}$ tightens the guarantee at additional utility cost. For a dataset with 1 million records, ${\delta}= 10^{-12}$ specifies a small additive slack, but does not by itself assign a probability to every possible privacy failure.

#### Noise Mechanisms for Achieving Differential Privacy

^sec-security-privacy-noise-mechanisms-achieving-differential-privacy-f29c

ML systems implement differential privacy through carefully calibrated noise injection. The two primary mechanisms used in ML systems differ in their noise distributions and applicability. The first is the Laplace mechanism. For pure ${\epsilon}$-DP, it adds noise drawn from $\text{Lap}({\Delta} {f}/{\epsilon})$, where ${\Delta} {f}$ is the global sensitivity of the function (the maximum change in output when one record changes). For a query ${f}$, the privatized output is:

$$
{\tilde{f}}({X}) = {f}({X}) + \text{Lap}\left(\frac{{\Delta} {f}}{{\epsilon}}\right)
$$

The Laplace distribution has density ${p}({x}) = \frac{{\epsilon}}{2{\Delta} {f}}\exp\left(-\frac{{\epsilon}|{x}|}{{\Delta} {f}}\right)$, with scale parameter ${b}= {\Delta} {f}/{\epsilon}$. For a fixed query and adjacency relation, tightening the bound from ${\epsilon}= 1.0$ to ${\epsilon}= 0.1$ increases the noise scale by 10$\times$. Individual noise draws remain random; the scale controls their distribution rather than fixing their magnitude.

The Laplace derivation shows why the noise scale must match sensitivity. To prove that the Laplace mechanism achieves ${\epsilon}$-DP, consider the ratio of output densities on adjacent datasets ${X}$ and ${X'}$. Let ${f}({X}) = {v}$ and ${f}({X'}) = {v'}$, where $|{v}- {v'}| \leq {\Delta} {f}$ by the sensitivity bound. For any output ${o}$, the density ratio is:

$$
\frac{{p}({o} | {X})}{{p}({o} | {X'})} = \frac{\exp(-{\epsilon}|{o} - {v}|/{\Delta} {f})}{\exp(-{\epsilon}|{o} - {v'}|/{\Delta} {f})} = \exp\left(\frac{{\epsilon}(|{o} - {v'}| - |{o} - {v}|)}{{\Delta} {f}}\right)
$$

Using the triangle inequality, $|{o} - {v'}| - |{o} - {v}| \leq |{v}- {v'}| \leq {\Delta} {f}$. Therefore:

$$
\frac{{p}({o} | {X})}{{p}({o} | {X'})} \leq \exp\left(\frac{{\epsilon}\cdot {\Delta} {f}}{{\Delta} {f}}\right) = e^{{\epsilon}}
$$

This establishes that the Laplace mechanism satisfies ${\epsilon}$-differential privacy. The derivation reveals why sensitivity calibration is necessary, because the noise scale must match the maximum possible change in the query output to mask individual contributions.

**Napkin Math 13.3: The cost of differential privacy**

**Problem**: Consider computing the average salary of 1000 employees while guaranteeing privacy budget ${\epsilon}=$ 1. The salaries range from \$0 to \$200,000. Assume a fixed, public employee roster and neighboring datasets that differ in one employee’s salary, with both values inside these bounds. How much noise must the mechanism add?

**Math**:

1.  **Sensitivity** $({\Delta} {f})$: The maximum one person can change the sum is \$200,000.
2.  **Privacy Budget** $({\epsilon})$: 1.
3.  **Laplace Noise Scale** $({b})$: the Laplace mechanism sets its noise scale to the sensitivity divided by the privacy budget, so ${b}= {\Delta} {f}/ {\epsilon}=$ \$200,000 / 1 = \$200,000.
4.  Impact on Mean: The mean-estimation noise comes from noise added to the sum with magnitude approximately \$200,000.
    - Error in the mean = \$200,000 / 1000 = \$200. The mechanism draws noise once, for the sum; dividing by the group size spreads that single draw across the mean.

**Systems insight**: Under this fixed-roster contract, the published mean receives zero-mean Laplace noise with expected absolute magnitude \$200; individual draws can be smaller or larger. For a dataset of ${D}=$ 100, the expected absolute error grows to \$2,000. The noise needed to hide one person is fixed by the sensitivity, while the signal grows with the number of people, so small groups pay the largest relative cost and large datasets dilute it through the factor $1/{D}$.

The salary-average notebook above uses the central model of differential privacy. Each person supplies a bounded salary to a trusted analyst; the analyst computes the sum, adds one Laplace draw calibrated to the sum’s global sensitivity, and divides by the fixed, public group size. The example protects a salary replacement within a public roster. If participation itself is private, adjacency must be add/remove, meaning adjacent datasets differ by the presence or absence of one person’s records rather than by a change to one value. Under that relation the count becomes data dependent and needs its own privacy treatment, so division by that count is not automatically safe post-processing. This differs from local differential privacy, in which each person randomizes a value before submission and the analyst aggregates the randomized reports. Both models compare output distributions on adjacent inputs, but their mechanisms, trust assumptions, and utility are not interchangeable.

The Gaussian mechanism serves the ML case where the relaxed $({\epsilon}, {\delta})$ guarantee is acceptable. It adds noise with standard deviation ${\sigma_{\text{mech}}}$ calibrated based on ${\epsilon}, {\delta}$, and the $\ell_2$ sensitivity ${\Delta}_2 {f}$:

$$
{\tilde{f}}({X}) = {f}({X}) + {\mathcal{N}}\left({\mathbf{0}}, {\sigma_{\text{mech}}}^2 {\mathbf{I}}\right)
$$

Unlike the Laplace mechanism, which achieves pure ${\epsilon}$-DP, the Gaussian mechanism generally requires the relaxed $({\epsilon}, {\delta})$-DP formulation because shifted Gaussian densities can have arbitrarily large likelihood ratios in the tails. Bounding those tail events gives a sufficient way to establish the ${\delta}$ slack. The derivation works with the privacy loss, the log of the ratio between the two output densities at the observed output. Because the output is random, so is this log ratio, which is why it is called the privacy loss random variable.

For adjacent datasets with $\ell_2$ sensitivity ${\Delta}_2 {f}$, the privacy loss at output ${\mathbf{y}}$ follows:

$$
{\mathcal{L}_{\text{priv}}}({\mathbf{y}}) = \ln\frac{{p}({\mathbf{y}}|{X})}{{p}({\mathbf{y}}|{X'})} = \frac{\|{\mathbf{y}}- {f}({X'})\|_2^2 - \|{\mathbf{y}}- {f}({X})\|_2^2}{2{\sigma_{\text{mech}}}^2}
$$

When ${\mathbf{y}}= {f}({X}) + {\mathbf{z}}$ for noise ${\mathbf{z}}\sim {\mathcal{N}}({\mathbf{0}}, {\sigma_{\text{mech}}}^2 {\mathbf{I}})$, expanding the difference of squared norms cancels the quadratic term $\|{\mathbf{z}}\|_2^2$ and leaves a constant shift plus a linear inner product between ${\mathbf{z}}$ and the dataset difference ${f}({X}) - {f}({X'})$. Unlike the Laplace log ratio, which the triangle inequality caps at a constant across the entire output space, this linear function of ${\mathbf{z}}$ grows without bound along the direction of the dataset difference. For an adjacent pair attaining the sensitivity bound, the privacy loss is therefore a shifted Gaussian random variable with mean $\frac{{\Delta}_2 {f}^2}{2{\sigma_{\text{mech}}}^2}$ and variance $\frac{{\Delta}_2 {f}^2}{{\sigma_{\text{mech}}}^2}$, and ${\delta}$ bounds the probability that a noise draw lands far enough in the tail to push ${\mathcal{L}_{\text{priv}}}({\mathbf{y}})$ above ${\epsilon}$. For one application of the mechanism and $0<{\epsilon}<1$, a classic sufficient calibration is:

$$
{\sigma_{\text{mech}}}\geq \frac{{\Delta}_2 {f}}{{\epsilon}}\sqrt{2\ln\left(\frac{1.25}{{\delta}}\right)}
$$

This sufficient bound is a one-shot Gaussian-mechanism result within that parameter domain. Neither it nor the one-shot Laplace ratio ${\Delta} {f}/{\epsilon}$ of the salary example can set a DP-SGD noise multiplier, the ratio of the noise standard deviation to the clipping norm, by substituting the final training budget. DP-SGD clips sampled per-example gradients, adds Gaussian noise to their sum, and composes many such steps, so its budget requires a sampled-Gaussian accountant. Smaller ${\epsilon}$ or ${\delta}$ generally requires more noise, but the resulting accuracy change must be measured for the specific task and training procedure.

**Napkin Math 13.4: One-shot Gaussian mechanism**

**Problem**: Determine a sufficient noise standard deviation ${\sigma_{\text{mech}}}$ for the Gaussian mechanism to achieve $({\epsilon}, {\delta})$-differential privacy for a single vector-valued query.

**Variables**: $\ell_2$ sensitivity ${\Delta}_2 {f}=$ 1, target ${\epsilon}=$ 0.5, ${\delta}=$ $10^{-5}$.

**Math**: Classic sufficient bound requires ${\sigma_{\text{mech}}}\geq$ 9.7.

**Systems insight**: This calculation applies to a single Gaussian release. DP-SGD repeats a Gaussian release at every optimization step, so its budget must be composed across thousands of steps, and the accountants of [[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/04 The Responsible Fleet/13 Security & Privacy/V2-13.10 Differential Privacy#^sec-security-privacy-privacy-loss-random-variable-moments-accountant-cf17|section 13.9.2.1]] bound that composed total more tightly than adding up the per-step bounds would.

Gaussian noise fits the clipped-gradient mechanism because clipping explicitly bounds gradient norms in $\ell_2$ space, making sensitivity analysis tractable. Rényi differential privacy accounting can retain mechanism-specific information to improve on generic composition bounds; that advantage belongs to mechanisms like clipped-gradient training and does not make Gaussian noise the right choice for every private computation.

### Privacy Budget Composition

^sec-security-privacy-privacy-budget-composition-edbe

Releasing 10 models trained on the same patient cohort at ${\epsilon}=1$ each does not maintain ${\epsilon}=1$ protection; under basic composition the cumulative bound reaches ${\epsilon}=10$. Because a full training run or a sequence of model releases applies a mechanism many times, privacy loss accumulates across every additional data-dependent DP release about the same protected population. Basic composition bounds the total by adding the per-release bounds, which is valid but often loose, while mechanism-specific accountants retain more information about each mechanism to obtain tighter bounds across noisy stochastic gradient descent (SGD) steps before expressing the result as an $({\epsilon}, {\delta})$ budget. Repeated use of a fixed released DP model is post-processing and does not add to its training privacy loss, because a computation that reads only the release, never the protected data, cannot weaken the guarantee the release already satisfies.

![[90 Assets/Volume II/13 Security Privacy/Vol2 Security Privacy Margin 003 44b673fe59.svg]]

*A bar of ten equal gray segments labeled epsilon equals 1 per release, ending at a marker labeled basic bound, epsilon equals 10.*

*Ten ${\epsilon}=1$ releases add up to a basic bound of ${\epsilon}=10$.*

Privacy accounting depends on three primary composition tools. Simple composition bounds the cumulative privacy loss of ${k}$ pure-DP mechanisms with bounds ${\epsilon}_i$ by $\sum {\epsilon}_i$; for approximate DP, their additive slack parameters also accumulate. In suitable parameter regimes, advanced (strong) composition improves the bound using square-root and linear terms while allocating additional slack in the probability inequality. For iterative mechanisms like DP-SGD, the moments accountant and Rényi differential privacy developed next track the loss across steps before converting it to an explicit $({\epsilon}, {\delta})$ guarantee, and no accountant is uniformly tighter than every alternative. Organizations must maintain a privacy accounting log for the protected population and halt or redesign further data-dependent releases when the allocated bound would be exceeded.

#### Privacy Loss Random Variable and Moments Accountant

^sec-security-privacy-privacy-loss-random-variable-moments-accountant-cf17

A more refined approach to privacy analysis tracks the privacy loss random variable, which measures how strongly an observation favors one adjacent dataset over the other through a log-likelihood ratio. For adjacent datasets ${X}, {X'}$ and algorithm output ${o}$, the discrete-output expression is:

$$
{\mathcal{L}_{\text{priv},(X,X')}^{(o)}} = \ln\frac{\Pr[{\mathcal{M}}({X}) = {o}]}{\Pr[{\mathcal{M}}({X'}) = {o}]}
$$

The privacy loss random variable characterizes the log-likelihood ratio between outputs on adjacent datasets; for continuous outputs, the ratio uses densities rather than point probabilities. A sufficient condition for $({\epsilon}, {\delta})$-DP is that every ordered adjacent pair satisfies $\Pr[{\mathcal{L}_{\text{priv}}}> {\epsilon}] \leq {\delta}$, with the output sampled from the numerator distribution. This tail condition is stronger than the general definition, but it enables composition analysis through moment-generating functions.

The moments accountant technique, introduced for DP-SGD by Abadi et al. ([[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/04 The Responsible Fleet/13 Security & Privacy/V2-13.15 Source References#^ref-abadi2016deep|2016]]), bounds exponential moments of the privacy loss distribution. It retains more information about the mechanism than a single per-step privacy bound while still taking the worst case over adjacent datasets. For mechanism ${\mathcal{M}}$ with privacy loss ${\mathcal{L}_{\text{priv}}}$, the moments accountant computes:

$$
{\alpha_{\mathcal{M}}}({\lambda_{\text{mom}}}) = \max_{{X},{X'}} \ln \mathbb{E}_{{o}\sim {\mathcal{M}}({X})}\left[\left(\frac{\Pr[{\mathcal{M}}({X}) = {o}]}{\Pr[{\mathcal{M}}({X'}) = {o}]}\right)^{{\lambda_{\text{mom}}}}\right]
$$

for moment order ${\lambda_{\text{mom}}}$. Because the total privacy loss across steps with fresh noise draws is the sum of the per-step privacy losses, its exponential moment factors into the product of the per-step moments, and its logarithm adds across steps. If each of ${k}$ steps satisfies the same log-moment bound, composition gives an upper bound of ${k}\cdot {\alpha_{\mathcal{M}}}({\lambda_{\text{mom}}})$. Instead of converting every step into a separate tail cutoff and summing those cutoffs, the accountant accumulates the log-moments first and applies the Markov inequality once at the end to yield $({\epsilon}, {\delta})$ bounds:

$$
{\epsilon}({\delta}) = \min_{{\lambda_{\text{mom}}}>0} \left[\frac{{k}\cdot {\alpha_{\mathcal{M}}}({\lambda_{\text{mom}}}) + \ln(1/{\delta})}{{\lambda_{\text{mom}}}}\right]
$$

The numerical result depends on the sampling rule, sampling rate, noise multiplier, step count, adjacency relation, and accountant. In DP-SGD, random subsampling at rate ${q}$ also shrinks ${\alpha_{\mathcal{M}}}({\lambda_{\text{mom}}})$ at each step because a given record falls, on average, into only a ${q}$ fraction of lots, the randomly sampled groups of examples that DP-SGD processes at each step. For example, Abadi et al. ([[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/04 The Responsible Fleet/13 Security & Privacy/V2-13.15 Source References#^ref-abadi2016deep|2016]]) report that with sampling rate ${q}=0.01$, noise multiplier ${\sigma}=4$, and ${\delta}=10^{-5}$, their moments accountant gives ${\epsilon}=1.26$ after 100 epochs and ${\epsilon}=2.55$ after 400 epochs, whereas the corresponding advanced-composition bounds are 9.34 and 24.22. These values belong to that experiment and are not reusable constants for another DP-SGD run.

#### Renyi Differential Privacy and Composition

^sec-security-privacy-renyi-differential-privacy-composition-f69a

Rényi differential privacy (RDP), introduced by Mironov ([[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/04 The Responsible Fleet/13 Security & Privacy/V2-13.15 Source References#^ref-mironov2017|2017]]), provides a convenient representation for composition by tracking Rényi divergence. A mechanism ${\mathcal{M}}$ satisfies $({\alpha_{\text{R}}}, {\epsilon_{\text{R}}})\text{-RDP}$ if for all adjacent ${X}, {X'}$:

$$
{\mathcal{D}_{\alpha_{\text{R}}}}({\mathcal{M}}({X}) \lVert {\mathcal{M}}({X'})) = \frac{1}{{\alpha_{\text{R}}}-1}\ln \mathbb{E}_{{o} \sim {\mathcal{M}}({X'})}\left[\left(\frac{\Pr[{\mathcal{M}}({X}) = {o}]}{\Pr[{\mathcal{M}}({X'}) = {o}]}\right)^{{\alpha_{\text{R}}}}\right] \leq {\epsilon_{\text{R}}}
$$

where ${\alpha_{\text{R}}}> 1$ is the Rényi order. RDP composition is simple. If mechanism ${\mathcal{M}}_i$ satisfies $({\alpha_{\text{R}}}, {\epsilon_{\text{R},i}})\text{-RDP}$, then their composition satisfies $({\alpha_{\text{R}}}, \sum_i {\epsilon_{\text{R},i}})\text{-RDP}$. This representation avoids repeatedly applying a generic $({\epsilon}, {\delta})$ composition theorem to every training step.

For an unsampled Gaussian mechanism with unit $\ell_2$ sensitivity and noise standard deviation ${\sigma_{\text{mech}}}$, the RDP guarantee is:

$$
{\epsilon_{\text{R}}}({\alpha_{\text{R}}}) = \frac{{\alpha_{\text{R}}}}{2{\sigma_{\text{mech}}}^2}
$$

Subsampling changes this mechanism. In particular, ${q}^2{\alpha_{\text{R}}}/(2{\sigma_{\text{mech}}}^2)$ is only a leading-order approximation in restricted regimes, not a privacy guarantee that can certify a DP-SGD run. A sampled-Gaussian accountant must evaluate the specified sampling scheme and adjacency relation at multiple Rényi orders, compose the resulting per-step bounds across the actual number of steps, and then convert the result to $({\epsilon}, {\delta})$-DP. Production implementations should use a tested accountant such as Google DP Accounting, TensorFlow Privacy, or Opacus and record its version and sampling convention with the released model.

This RDP guarantee supplies a valid $({\epsilon}, {\delta})$-DP bound using the following conversion; the result is an upper bound on privacy loss, not generally its exact value:

$$
{\epsilon}= {\epsilon_{\text{R}}}+ \frac{\ln(1/{\delta})}{{\alpha_{\text{R}}}- 1}
$$

optimized over ${\alpha_{\text{R}}}$. The moments-accountant and RDP views are closely related; which implementation gives the tightest reported bound depends on the mechanism, orders, and numerical accountant rather than a universal multiplier.

#### DP-SGD Accountant Inputs for Image Classification

^sec-security-privacy-practical-privacy-budget-example-dpsgd-image-classification-2662

Consider training a CNN on 50,000 CIFAR-10 images with $({\epsilon}, {\delta}) = ($ 3 $, 10^{-5})$ target privacy. DP-SGD groups examples into **lots**, the randomly sampled sets whose clipped gradients are summed and share one noise draw per step; with Poisson sampling the lot size varies from step to step around its expected value. The run uses DP-SGD with:

- Poisson sampling with expected lot size ${B}=$ 4,000 (inclusion probability ${q}= {B}/{D} =$ 0.08)
- Per-example gradient clipping norm ${C}=$ 1
- Noise multiplier ${\sigma}=$ 1.3 (noise scale relative to clipping)
- Training for ${N_{\text{epochs}}}=$ 60 epochs (${k}= {N_{\text{epochs}}}\cdot {D}/{B}=$ 750 steps)

These inputs do not determine a privacy guarantee through the shortcut ${q}^2{\alpha_{\text{R}}}/(2{\sigma}^2)$. Before release, a tested sampled-Gaussian accountant must evaluate Poisson sampling with add/remove adjacency, compose all 750 steps, report the ${\epsilon}$ it certifies at the specified ${\delta}$, and verify whether that value meets target ${\epsilon}=$ 3. The release record must pin the accountant library and version, sampling convention, adjacency relation, and evaluated Rényi orders.

The adjacency relation also decides what the bound protects. Per-example clipping bounds the contribution of one record, so when one person contributes many records, as a repeat customer does to a review corpus, a record-level guarantee does not protect that person as a unit. Person-level protection requires bounding each person’s total contribution and accounting under person-level adjacency.

Training for ${N_{\text{epochs}}}=$ 25 reduces the number of composed steps, while reducing the expected lot size to ${B}=$ 2,000 changes the Poisson inclusion probability to ${q}=$ 0.04. The accountant must be rerun for either change; neither direction should be assigned a numerical ${\epsilon}$ through the leading-order shortcut. If the accountant reports a budget above the target, the operator raises the noise multiplier, trains for fewer epochs, or lowers the sampling rate, and then reruns it. Each change has a utility and compute cost that must be measured. The guarantee itself comes from clipping, calibrated noise, and the privacy accountant; [[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/04 The Responsible Fleet/13 Security & Privacy/V2-13.10 Differential Privacy#^lst-security-privacy-dp-sgd-step|listing 1]] shows the mechanism sequence without implementing an accountant.

``` text
fix a public normalization B for the expected lot size under the sampling design
check prospectively that the accountant permits the next step within the budget
independently sample each record with probability q (Poisson sampling)
clip each sampled per-example gradient to norm C
sum clipped gradients; use the zero vector for an empty lot
add noise N(0, sigma^2 C^2 I), then divide by fixed B
take one optimizer step with the privatized gradient
record the step in the matching privacy accountant
stop before a step that would exceed the release budget
account for private-data-dependent selection; retuning does not erase prior releases
```

Listing 1: **DP-SGD Training Step**: Per-step clipping, noise addition, and accountant update that together enforce the privacy budget.

### Quantifying the Privacy-Utility Trade-Off

^sec-security-privacy-privacy-utility-tradeoff-b3e7

Tightening differential privacy to single-digit ${\epsilon}$ keeps MNIST within 3.3 percentage points of its 98.3 percent nonprivate baseline, yet drops CIFAR-10 trained from scratch by roughly 14 to 22 percentage points even at ${\epsilon} =$ 8 ([[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/04 The Responsible Fleet/13 Security & Privacy/V2-13.15 Source References#^ref-abadi2016deep|Abadi et al. 2016]]; [[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/04 The Responsible Fleet/13 Security & Privacy/V2-13.15 Source References#^ref-bu2020gaussian|Bu et al. 2020]]; [[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/04 The Responsible Fleet/13 Security & Privacy/V2-13.15 Source References#^ref-de2022dp|De et al. 2022]]). That gap shows how strongly the utility cost of differential privacy depends on task complexity, architecture, privacy accountant, pretraining, and whether extra public data are allowed.

[[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/04 The Responsible Fleet/13 Security & Privacy/V2-13.10 Differential Privacy#^fig-privacy-utility-frontier|Figure 26]] collects those published MNIST and CIFAR-10 results on a logarithmic axis ([[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/04 The Responsible Fleet/13 Security & Privacy/V2-13.15 Source References#^ref-abadi2016deep|Abadi et al. 2016]]; [[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/04 The Responsible Fleet/13 Security & Privacy/V2-13.15 Source References#^ref-bu2020gaussian|Bu et al. 2020]]; [[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/04 The Responsible Fleet/13 Security & Privacy/V2-13.15 Source References#^ref-de2022dp|De et al. 2022]]). These comparisons show setting-specific utility costs; differences in architectures and training procedures prevent isolating task complexity alone. The plotted MNIST results stay within 3.3 percentage points of the 98.3 percent nonprivate baseline at every plotted single-digit budget, the smallest of which is ${\epsilon} =$ 2. For CIFAR-10 without extra data, the plotted results reach 73 percent in Abadi et al. ([[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/04 The Responsible Fleet/13 Security & Privacy/V2-13.15 Source References#^ref-abadi2016deep|2016]]) and 81.4 percent in De et al. ([[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/04 The Responsible Fleet/13 Security & Privacy/V2-13.15 Source References#^ref-de2022dp|2022]]) at ${\epsilon}=$ 8, roughly 14 to 22 points below the displayed 95.0 percent nonprivate reference. These CIFAR-10 settings therefore pay several times the MNIST accuracy cost at a budget four times looser. That reference is a comparison point, not a ceiling on achievable accuracy. Selecting a budget therefore requires the full training regime, adjacency relation, additive slack, accountant, and utility requirements rather than the horizontal coordinate alone.

These empirical results show why a privacy budget alone does not predict utility. The original DP-SGD experiments achieved higher accuracy on MNIST than on CIFAR-10, while later training methods improved the CIFAR-10 result under the same budget. Public pretraining changes the learning problem again. With an NFNet-F3 pretrained on JFT-4B, De et al. ([[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/04 The Responsible Fleet/13 Security & Privacy/V2-13.15 Source References#^ref-de2022dp|2022]]) report 86.7 percent ImageNet accuracy at $(8, 8 \times 10^{-7})$-DP, so comparisons must state which data receive protection and which data support pretraining. The figure supplies selected operating points rather than a complete privacy-accuracy curve. A deployment needs its own sweep over ${\epsilon}$ with the other privacy assumptions and acceptance criteria held explicit.

### Decision Framework for Differential Privacy Deployment Trade-Offs

^sec-security-privacy-dp-decision-framework-c4a8

Committing to ${\epsilon} = 1$ before checking rare-subgroup accuracy or 8-GPU training budgets can leave a clinical classifier too inaccurate or too expensive to ship. The quantified trade-offs turn differential privacy from a mathematical guarantee into a deployment decision: specify the required protection first, then test whether a mechanism can deliver it at acceptable utility and systems cost. A mandatory privacy bound is a feasibility constraint; an accuracy improvement does not by itself justify exceeding it.

Start with the regulatory and legal requirement. DP becomes especially valuable when policy, contracts, audits, or risk assessments require demonstrable privacy bounds. Regulations rarely mandate DP by name, but GDPR’s data-protection-by-design principle ([[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/04 The Responsible Fleet/13 Security & Privacy/V2-13.15 Source References#^ref-gdpr2016|European Parliament and Council of the European Union 2016]]) and privacy-by-design framing ([[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/04 The Responsible Fleet/13 Security & Privacy/V2-13.15 Source References#^ref-cavoukian2012|Cavoukian 2012]]) motivate privacy-preserving system design. HIPAA’s technical safeguards, in turn, require controls such as access control, audit controls, integrity protections, authentication, and transmission security ([[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/04 The Responsible Fleet/13 Security & Privacy/V2-13.15 Source References#^ref-hhs2005hipaasecurity|U.S. Department of Health and Human Services 2005]]). DP can complement those controls by documenting a mathematical privacy-loss bound, but it does not replace HIPAA safeguard implementation. Organizations handling EU health data, US medical records, or California personal data subject to California state privacy laws (CCPA/CPRA) should evaluate DP as one possible compliance-supporting mechanism.

Next, match the threat model to the mechanism. DP bounds how much any one record can change the released distribution, even when an adversary chooses queries adaptively and combines outputs with auxiliary information. This property can limit membership inference and record-level extraction, but only under the stated adjacency relation and composed budget. When the primary threats are data breaches of stored data rather than inference from a release, encryption and access controls address the relevant boundary; DP does not substitute for them.

The dataset and task then determine whether the guarantee is usable. Effective DP training depends on whether the clipped signal remains learnable after adding calibrated noise. Effective sample size, the number of independent examples that actually inform learning for a class or subgroup, captures why repeated observations, class imbalance, and subgroup rarity can limit learning even in a large table. Task complexity also matters, but class count alone does not determine noise tolerance; representation, label quality, and the training regime affect the result. The data sensitivity distribution raises a separate question about whose errors carry the greatest consequences; sensitivity here means the cost of an error, not the mathematical sensitivity used to calibrate noise. Rare subgroups may suffer disproportionate utility loss even when the same formal privacy bound covers every protected contribution. As the salary notebook showed, the noise that hides one contribution is the same for every group, while a small group supplies little signal to outweigh it.

The utility budget must also be explicit. Before deployment, the team must define task-specific acceptance criteria for overall performance, rare subgroups, calibration, and safety-relevant errors. An average accuracy change that appears small can still be unacceptable if DP noise concentrates errors on a protected subgroup or a high-cost clinical class. Published results supply comparison points on the privacy-utility frontier ([[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/04 The Responsible Fleet/13 Security & Privacy/V2-13.10 Differential Privacy#^fig-privacy-utility-frontier|figure 26]]), but the release decision requires an experiment on the actual model and data.

Finally, the computational budget decides whether the design can ship. DP-SGD adds clipping, noise generation, and accounting work, while attaining acceptable private utility can change the batch size and training duration. The resulting runtime and memory cost depends on the model and implementation. Deployment planning needs measured time to the required utility under the privacy budget, rather than a universal compute multiplier.

#### Decision Matrix Summary

^sec-security-privacy-decision-matrix-summary-6c3e

Use DP when a formal participation or contribution bound addresses the stated threat model, a tested accountant covers the relevant data-dependent releases, measured utility satisfies the task’s acceptance criteria, and the training system can afford the mechanism’s compute and memory cost. Use different controls when the primary boundary is unauthorized access to stored data. If a required DP bound cannot coexist with acceptable utility, redesign the mechanism, task, or data strategy, or withhold the release. Federated learning, encryption, access control, and audit logging do not substitute for a required release guarantee merely because they are easier to deploy.

**Checkpoint 13.4: Applying the DP decision framework**

A wearable cardiac monitor classifies arrhythmias from raw ECG signals collected on-device for 5,000 enrolled patients. The product is sold in the United States and is HIPAA-regulated; the training pipeline runs on a cloud cluster of 8 GPUs, and a competing vendor has incentive to extract the model. Apply each of the five decision criteria to determine whether DP-SGD belongs in the training pipeline.

**Criterion 1 (regulatory)**: decide whether HIPAA supports a DP guarantee here and identify the documentation DP supplies that ad-hoc de-identification cannot.

**Criterion 2 (threat model)**: identify the adversary populations that matter for a cardiac-monitor model and evaluate membership inference as a credible attack.

**Criterion 3 (dataset characteristics)**: evaluate effective sample size, class imbalance, repeated measurements, and rare clinical subgroups before selecting a mechanism.

**Criterion 4 (acceptable utility loss)**: define safety-critical error, calibration, and subgroup acceptance criteria, then measure whether the private model satisfies them.

**Criterion 5 (compute)**: estimate the runtime and memory cost of private training on the stated cloud allocation, measure utility under the selected privacy budget, and identify the binding deployment constraint.

This framework treats DP as one tool in a privacy-preserving toolkit rather than a universal solution. Production deployments often combine an accounted DP mechanism with data minimization, federated execution where raw-data centralization is out of scope, secure aggregation for contribution confidentiality, and access controls for deployment security.

Returning to the data-layer techniques of [[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/04 The Responsible Fleet/13 Security & Privacy/V2-13.09 Comprehensive Defense Architectures#^sec-security-privacy-privacypreserving-data-techniques-64f8|section 13.8.5]], differential privacy sits beside federated learning, homomorphic encryption, secure multiparty computation, and synthetic data generation, and each strikes its own balance among privacy strength, accuracy, and computational cost ([[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/04 The Responsible Fleet/13 Security & Privacy/V2-13.10 Differential Privacy#^tbl-privacy-technique-comparison|table 8]]). The maturity labels are a deployment snapshot for the regimes discussed here, not a permanent ranking of the underlying techniques. Cryptographic guarantees depend on key custody and the stated adversary model; they do not prevent an authorized output from revealing sensitive information. Differential privacy instead bounds disclosure through the released result under a specified adjacency relation.

Increasing the noise to reduce ${\epsilon}$ may degrade model accuracy, especially in low-data regimes or fine-grained classification tasks. Consequently, DP is often applied selectively (either during training on sensitive datasets or at inference when returning aggregate statistics) to balance privacy with performance goals ([[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/04 The Responsible Fleet/13 Security & Privacy/V2-13.15 Source References#^ref-dwork2014|Dwork and Roth 2014]]).

Differential privacy completes the data layer of the defense stack. With the mechanisms of every layer now in view, the remaining engineering questions are which of them a given deployment needs and in what order to adopt them.
