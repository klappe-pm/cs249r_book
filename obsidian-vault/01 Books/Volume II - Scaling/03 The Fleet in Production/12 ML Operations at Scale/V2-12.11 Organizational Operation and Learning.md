# V2-12.11 Organizational Operation and Learning

[[Home|Home]] · [[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/Volume 2 Overview|Volume Overview]] · [[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/03 The Fleet in Production/12 ML Operations at Scale/V2-12 ML Operations at Scale|V2-12 ML Operations at Scale]]

^sec-ml-operations-scale-organizational-patterns-e2e8

A shared feature change may pass platform checks yet still require approval from several model owners. The control plane becomes durable only when those technical interfaces align with organizational authority. Team structure determines who can approve the change, stop a rollout, or fund the repair when a shared dependency fails.

The central question is where an organization places invariants. Deployment safety, observability, compliance, cost control, and feature consistency can either be enforced once by a platform team or rediscovered differently by every model team.

### Centralized Platform Team

^sec-ml-operations-scale-centralized-platform-team-16fc

When eight product teams each build their own deployment scripts, feature pipelines, and alert dashboards, the same stale-feature or rollback outage repeats across the company. A centralized ML platform team eliminates that duplication by building and maintaining shared infrastructure while model teams own model development and behavior. Three organizational patterns trade consistency against local responsiveness ([[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/03 The Fleet in Production/12 ML Operations at Scale/V2-12.11 Organizational Operation and Learning#^fig-ml-org-models|figure 15]]). A shared request queue (Panel A) centralizes prioritization but can become a bottleneck; uniform standards depend on enforced contracts, not the queue itself. Embedded tooling (Panel B) favors local responsiveness while risking duplication. The hybrid pattern (Panel C) places dedicated ML engineers near product teams while retaining a common platform. Self-service interfaces can preserve central standards without routing every routine action through a human queue.

![[90 Assets/Volume II/12 Ops Scale/ML Org Models 39a2ce9cca.svg]]

Figure 15: **Organizational Patterns for ML**: (A) A centralized platform team gives every model team the same infrastructure, but all requests pass through one shared queue, which can become the bottleneck. (B) Embedded teams each build their own tooling, which is fast locally but duplicates infrastructure and lets stacks diverge. (C) A hybrid keeps a core platform and places ML engineers (MLE) inside model teams as liaisons, balancing standardization with responsiveness.

^fig-ml-org-models

Centralization works when the same operational mistakes would otherwise recur across the organization. A shared team can enforce consistent deployment, monitoring, and governance practices; invest once in training, serving, and observability infrastructure; and concentrate deep ML systems expertise that would be hard to replicate across many product teams. It also gives infrastructure engineers a visible career path rather than scattering them as one-person support functions.

The same structure can become a queue. If every platform request routes through one team, model teams wait on prioritization decisions they do not control. Distance from product context can also produce abstractions that are technically elegant but poorly matched to the consuming team’s constraints. Centralization is therefore strongest when common invariants dominate local variation, and weakest when model teams need rapid domain-specific changes.

### Embedded ML Engineers

^sec-ml-operations-scale-embedded-ml-engineers-35e2

When a fraud detection team needs a custom streaming feature adapter before tomorrow’s attack window, waiting two weeks in a central platform queue is unacceptable. The embedded pattern chooses proximity over uniformity by placing ML infrastructure expertise directly inside model teams, with coordination through communities of practice rather than a single reporting line. The benefit is responsiveness: the infrastructure engineer sees the team’s data, model behavior, release pressure, and incident history firsthand, so local changes do not wait for cross-team scheduling.

The cost appears later, when each team has solved deployment, monitoring, and feature management slightly differently. Fragmentation wastes engineering effort, complicates integration, and makes platform quality depend on the skill and bandwidth of whoever happens to be embedded with a given team. Embedded teams therefore work best when local speed matters more than cross-team consistency, and worst when every team is reimplementing the same platform substrate.

### Hybrid Models

^sec-ml-operations-scale-hybrid-models-237b

An organization running 80 production models across recommendation, search, fraud, and ads cannot afford four separate serving stacks, nor can one central queue design domain-specific evaluation gates for all four workloads. Hybrid approaches resolve this tension by combining shared infrastructure ownership with domain-specific expertise. In this pattern, the core platform team owns compute, storage, networking, common training and serving systems, monitoring, and shared security, compliance, and cost controls. Domain platform engineers stay close to recommendation, language, vision, fraud, or search teams. Explicit boundaries distinguish changes those teams can make locally from changes that require coordination because they affect shared interfaces or operating guarantees.

A federated version of the same idea distributes implementation work without giving up architectural control. Major contributing teams help maintain platform components, but standards, ownership boundaries, and prioritization are coordinated explicitly. This works only when governance has real authority: otherwise federation degenerates into embedding with more meetings.

### Organizational Pattern Selection

^sec-ml-operations-scale-organizational-pattern-selection-56a9

A 15-model startup iterating on disparate prototypes stalls if every change must clear a central governance queue, whereas a 100-model regulated financial institution faces audit failures and repeated outages if every team improvises its own deployment path. The appropriate organizational pattern depends on where standardization creates more value than local autonomy. Higher model counts, stricter regulatory requirements, and immature infrastructure favor centralized platforms, while heterogeneous model portfolios and smaller organizations may benefit from distributed expertise ([[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/03 The Fleet in Production/12 ML Operations at Scale/V2-12.11 Organizational Operation and Learning#^tbl-ops-scale-org-factors|table 38]]).

| **Factor**                  | **Favors centralized** | **Favors distributed** |
|:----------------------------|:----------------------:|:----------------------:|
| **Model count**             |     Higher (100+)      |     Lower (10–20)      |
| **Model similarity**        |      Homogeneous       |     Heterogeneous      |
| **Organization size**       |         Larger         |        Smaller         |
| **Regulatory requirements** |        Stricter        |        Lighter         |
| **Infrastructure maturity** |     Earlier stage      |      Later stage       |

Table 38: **Organizational Pattern Decision Factors**: Five criteria for choosing between centralized and distributed ML platform teams. Higher model counts (100+), stricter regulatory requirements, and earlier infrastructure maturity favor centralized platforms; heterogeneous model portfolios and smaller organizations may benefit from distributed expertise with coordination through communities of practice.

^tbl-ops-scale-org-factors

The infrastructure-maturity row can look inverted, because a young organization seems the natural home for local improvisation. The reasoning runs the other way. Early on, no shared substrate exists, and a central team is the cheapest way to build one before every model team writes its own deployment and monitoring path. Once that substrate matures into self-service interfaces, distributed teams can consume it directly, and the central queue matters less.

The selection logic becomes clearer in a concrete scenario. A technology company with 50 ML engineers across 8 teams operates 80 production models across recommendation, fraud, search, and ads. Each team maintains its own deployment and monitoring, creating duplicated work and inconsistent integration practices. That duplication motivates evaluating a shared platform; the model count alone does not establish its return. Domain diversity also motivates retaining expertise close to each workload rather than expecting one central team to make every modeling and operational decision.

One candidate design is hybrid. In this scenario, a central platform team of roughly 12–15 engineers owns core infrastructure, while domain-specific platform leads remain embedded with the largest model teams. That staffing assumption must be tested against the platform’s scope, support burden, and service commitments. A community of practice coordinates standards, and a shared contribution model lets domain teams upstream reusable components instead of maintaining permanent forks. The same platform engineering, feature management, and governance trade-offs become more concrete in the hyper-scale systems built by large technology companies.
