# V2-12.09 Platform Engineering

[[Home|Home]] · [[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/Volume 2 Overview|Volume Overview]] · [[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/03 The Fleet in Production/12 ML Operations at Scale/V2-12 ML Operations at Scale|V2-12 ML Operations at Scale]]

^sec-ml-operations-scale-platform-engineering-17be

Retiring the recurring toil surfaced by on-call rotations requires turning ad-hoc operational fixes into shared infrastructure. Organizations where every data science team independently provisions GPU clusters, configures model registries, and wires up alerting dashboards duplicate that infrastructure work across the fleet. **Platform engineering** addresses this by treating the ML infrastructure itself as an internal product, providing supported paths that absorb recurring operational complexity while exposing the controls model teams need. Product teams still own model behavior and workload-specific decisions, while the platform balances self-service capabilities that accelerate development against governance requirements that enforce common release and reliability policies.

### Abstraction Levels

^sec-ml-operations-scale-abstraction-levels-6e15

Handing ten model teams raw Kubernetes namespaces forces each team to rebuild its own training loop, serving stack, and monitoring dashboard, whereas locking every team into a rigid end-to-end wizard blocks the first workload that needs a custom distributed topology. The abstraction-level decision is how much operational judgment the platform should centralize on behalf of model teams. The four levels in [[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/03 The Fleet in Production/12 ML Operations at Scale/V2-12.09 Platform Engineering#^tbl-ops-scale-platform-abstraction-levels|table 27]] sit on that flexibility-vs.-convenience curve, distinguished by which concerns the platform owns and which it leaves to the model team.

| **Level** | **Platform owns**                                                                                                  | **Model team still owns**                                                       | **Best fit and risk**                                                                                                      |
|:----------|:-------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------|:--------------------------------------------------------------------------------|:---------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------|
| Level 1   | GPU capacity, storage volumes, network connectivity, and basic orchestration such as Kubernetes namespaces         | Training code, serving stack, monitoring, and deployment path                   | Fits unusual workloads with strong infrastructure teams; becomes costly when many teams repeat the same operational work   |
| Level 2   | Standardized containers, ML-aware Kubernetes integration, persistent volumes, and basic service-mesh support       | Experiment structure, training jobs, serving releases, and monitoring workflows | Reduces packaging duplication; operational correctness still depends heavily on each team                                  |
| Level 3   | ML-specific control loops such as topology-aware scheduling, hyperparameter tuning, distributed training, serving  | Modeling judgment and workload-specific trade-offs                              | Absorbs failure modes that recur across teams; systems such as Kubeflow and Ray often sit near this level                  |
| Level 4   | Full lifecycle product: IDEs, feature stores, experiment tracking, registries, CI/CD, monitoring, cost, governance | Higher-level product and modeling intent, with less direct low-level control    | Maximizes speed and consistency; the platform team accepts responsibility for policy, reliability, and economic visibility |

Table 27: **Platform Abstraction Levels**: Platform engineering centralizes different parts of the ML lifecycle at each abstraction level. The right level depends on whether an organization values workload-specific control or shared operational invariants more.

^tbl-ops-scale-platform-abstraction-levels

Managed services such as Vertex AI and SageMaker, and lifecycle tooling such as TensorFlow Extended (TFX)[[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/03 The Fleet in Production/12 ML Operations at Scale/V2-12.17 Footnotes#^fn14|¹⁴]] at Google ([[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/03 The Fleet in Production/12 ML Operations at Scale/V2-12.16 Source References#^ref-baylor2017|Baylor et al. 2017]]) and MLflow ([[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/03 The Fleet in Production/12 ML Operations at Scale/V2-12.16 Source References#^ref-zaharia2018accelerating|Zaharia et al. 2018]]), support different parts of this spectrum. A product name does not establish the organization’s abstraction level. The decisive boundary is which lifecycle responsibilities the assembled platform actually owns, which controls model teams retain, and who supplies policy, reliability, and economic visibility for the gaps.

### Platform Lifecycle and Adoption

^sec-ops-scale-platform-lifecycle-adoption

When a platform team treats its internal stack as a one-time infrastructure build, every breaking upgrade forces product teams into a synchronized migration that stalls launches, or leaves behind “shelfware” with high signup counts where teams still maintain custom shadow pipelines. An internal ML platform is a versioned product whose interfaces, supported workflows, reliability targets, and ownership boundaries must evolve without halting model delivery. Each platform release therefore needs compatibility guarantees, a migration path, telemetry that distinguishes old and new paths, and a retirement date for deprecated behavior. The platform team also needs its own service-level objectives because a registry, feature service, or deployment controller outage can block every product team at once.

Adoption measures whether the platform removes work rather than merely centralizing it. Useful indicators include the share of production models on supported paths, time from validated artifact to bounded production traffic, exception rate, migration completion, support load, and repeated toil outside the paved road. A high signup count with persistent custom pipelines signals nominal adoption but weak product fit. Conversely, mandatory migration can raise the adoption percentage while increasing lead time and operational risk. The platform roadmap should therefore combine fleet outcomes with user evidence: incident reduction and cost attribution matter alongside time-to-first-deployment and the fraction of teams that can complete routine changes without platform intervention.

Lifecycle decisions must preserve an escape hatch for workloads whose constraints do not fit the shared path. Exceptions should be explicit, time-bounded contracts with named owners and integration obligations, not permanent forks. When exception volume grows around the same missing capability, the pattern becomes product evidence for the next platform version. When a capability no longer earns its maintenance and migration cost, the platform should deprecate it through measured dual-running, compatibility testing, and a final removal gate. This product discipline keeps the shared control plane from becoming the next source of technical debt.

### Self-Service Model Deployment

^sec-ml-operations-scale-selfservice-model-deployment-4753

If every model release requires a ticket to the infrastructure team, deployments stall in a central queue; if model teams push directly to production without guardrails, a single unvalidated config can exhaust cluster GPU quotas or bypass canary gates. Self-service deployment works only when the platform lets model teams move quickly while keeping risky choices inside governed boundaries. The design question is therefore which deployment decisions remain team-owned and which invariants the platform enforces before a model receives production traffic.

A well-designed deployment API abstracts operational complexity by making the model team’s intent declarative. The platform lesson is not the shape of a particular YAML file; it is which invariants the platform must capture before it turns a model version into fleet traffic. The invariants in [[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/03 The Fleet in Production/12 ML Operations at Scale/V2-12.09 Platform Engineering#^tbl-self-service-deployment-invariants|table 28]] are the platform contract behind that declarative interface. Prometheus[[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/03 The Fleet in Production/12 ML Operations at Scale/V2-12.17 Footnotes#^fn15|¹⁵]] is one common operational sink for the telemetry side of that contract.

| **Declared intent**        | **Platform invariant**                                                                                                |
|:---------------------------|:----------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------|
| Model artifact and version | The serving system loads exactly the validated model from the registry.                                               |
| Resource envelope          | GPU, memory, and replica requests stay inside quota and scheduling constraints.                                       |
| Traffic policy             | Canary, shadow, and rollback controls bound blast radius before full promotion.                                       |
| Quality gates              | Error, latency, drift, and task-quality thresholds block unsafe promotion.                                            |
| Telemetry sink             | Operational metrics flow into operational monitoring, while model-quality signals flow into drift and slice monitors. |
| Approval boundary          | Sensitive data access, budget overruns, and high-risk release windows require review.                                 |

Table 28: **Self-Service Deployment Invariants**: A deployment API should encode the controls that keep local team autonomy from creating fleet-wide risk. The concrete syntax can vary, but the invariants must survive across platforms.

^tbl-self-service-deployment-invariants

TensorFlow Serving provides model-serving lifecycle capabilities for loading, versioning, canarying, and rolling back models ([[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/03 The Fleet in Production/12 ML Operations at Scale/V2-12.16 Source References#^ref-olston2017tensorflow|Olston et al. 2017]]). A self-service platform adds the policy layer around those capabilities: model teams specify intent, and the platform translates it into scheduling, traffic routing, telemetry, and approval controls.

### Resource Management

^sec-ml-operations-scale-resource-management-5550

Packing batch training jobs onto a shared cluster to hit 95 percent GPU utilization leaves co-located inference endpoints without headroom when user traffic spikes, while preempting a 72-hour training job that lacks a recent checkpoint wipes out days of compute. Training jobs can trade completion time for utilization within their deadlines; serving needs headroom to protect tail latency as requests arrive. A shared scheduler can manage both, provided its policy represents those distinct constraints rather than maximizing utilization alone.

Training resource policy must expose which jobs may wait or be preempted,[[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/03 The Fleet in Production/12 ML Operations at Scale/V2-12.17 Footnotes#^fn16|¹⁶]] which deadlines are binding, and who accepts the recovery cost. Scheduler mechanisms enforce those choices. The platform records checkpoint readiness, budget, priority, and accountable owner with each job so a utilization improvement does not silently transfer deadline or lost-work risk to a model team.

↩︎ Scheduler mechanisms for queueing, priority preemption, and elastic resizing of training jobs are established in **[[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/02 Distributed ML/08 Fleet Orchestration/V2-08 Fleet Orchestration#^sec-fleet-orchestration|Fleet Orchestration]]**.

Serving binds on latency rather than batch flexibility, because a live request cannot be preempted without user impact. Its control loop therefore starts from the latency SLO and the capacity that must be online before requests arrive. Autoscalers translate request load and queue pressure into capacity decisions; operations owns their policy envelope: the latency SLO, warm rollback capacity, tenant limits, budget, and authority to override normal scaling during an incident. Those commitments must remain visible in the release record so a cheaper allocation cannot silently remove the headroom on which validation relied.

↩︎ Autoscaling controllers that convert queue growth and token arrival rates into replica counts are established in **[[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/03 The Fleet in Production/10 Serving at Scale/V2-10.14 Autoscaling and Capacity Control#^sec-inference-scale-autoscaling-capacity-control|Autoscaling and Capacity Control]]**.

#### Platform Utilization Metrics

^sec-ml-operations-scale-platform-utilization-metrics-ed31

Once training and serving controls are separated, the shared platform still needs an aggregate measure of whether capacity is being converted into useful work. [[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/03 The Fleet in Production/12 ML Operations at Scale/V2-12.09 Platform Engineering#^eq-platform-utilization|Equation 11]] defines platform efficiency as the capacity-weighted average utilization across all resources:

$$
{U_{\text{platform}}}= \frac{\sum_{{i}} {U}_{{i}} \times {\text{Cap}}_{{i}}}{\sum_{{i}} {\text{Cap}}_{{i}}} \tag{11}
$$

^eq-platform-utilization

where ${U}_{{i}}$ is the utilization of resource ${i}$ and ${\text{Cap}}_{{i}}$ is its capacity in a common unit over the same interval. Heterogeneous accelerators require a declared workload normalization; CPU cores, device counts, and bytes cannot be added directly. Weighting by capacity keeps the metric honest about where stranded work lives. An idle single GPU and an idle eight-GPU node are not equally wasteful, so a small underused resource should not drag the platform average down as hard as a large idle pool.

However, raw utilization is incomplete. The scheduler already separates allocated, compute, and productive utilization for a single cluster; the platform metric needs the same distinction. Effective utilization must also weigh utilization quality, whether busy GPUs are doing productive work or waiting on data; utilization fairness, whether each team receives capacity in line with its agreed allocation; and utilization cost, the cost per unit of ML output.

↩︎ The allocated, compute, and productive utilization tiers are established in **[[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/02 Distributed ML/08 Fleet Orchestration/V2-08.13 Debugging Cluster Utilization#^sec-fleet-orchestration-resource-accounting|Allocated, active, and productive utilization]]**.

#### GPU Cluster Efficiency

^sec-ml-operations-scale-worked-example-gpu-cluster-efficiency-87b0

A platform operates a 100-GPU training cluster with average GPU utilization of 65 percent, memory utilization of 80 percent, four-hour average queue waits, and an internal chargeback rate of \$2.50/GPU-hour, the price the platform bills teams for each GPU-hour. Memory occupancy and compute utilization alone do not identify the bottleneck. Profiling must separate input stalls, communication, kernel inefficiency, and scheduling gaps, and queue waits may reflect burst arrivals or placement constraints rather than insufficient aggregate capacity. Busy time is also not useful work. The cluster costs $100 \times 24 \times \$2.50 = \$6,000/\text{day}$ at the chargeback rate, and its busy share, $100 \times 24 \times 0.65 \times \$2.50 = \$3,900/\text{day}$, is only an upper bound on the value of its useful output. Busy accelerators can still run jobs that later fail, repeat work lost after a checkpoint, or stall inside a step. The goodput accounting of **[[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/03 The Fleet in Production/09 Fleet Efficiency/V2-09.03 ML Goodput#^sec-performance-engineering-goodput-boundaries|Accounting boundaries]]** charges each of those losses to the layer that caused it. Suppose profiling attributes the gap to input stalls and a data-loading repair raises busy time to 80 percent, $100 \times 24 \times 0.80 \times \$2.50 = \$4,800/\text{day}$, with throughput validated at the same quality target. The fixed cluster bill does not change; whether the recovered capacity becomes cash, by avoiding a purchase, or value, by serving queued work, follows the utilization valuation of **[[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/02 Distributed ML/08 Fleet Orchestration/V2-08.09 Cost Optimization#^sec-fleet-orchestration-cost-optimization|Cost Optimization]]**. Scheduling and capacity planning then address the remaining queue pressure.

**Checkpoint 12.4: The annual cost of GPU cluster underutilization**

Use daily cluster spend (\$6,000/day) and busy-capacity value, utilization times spend and an upper bound on useful work (\$3,900/day at 65 percent utilization), to extend the analysis to annual scale and compare against the platform ROI argument.

**Underutilization cost**: calculate the annual cost of underutilization for the 100-GPU cluster at 65 percent utilization, the cost after optimization to 80 percent, and the annual value of recovered capacity. State when that value would become cash savings.

**Queue dynamics**: Little’s law (theorem [10.1](https://mlsysbook.ai/vol2/10_inference/10_inference.html#thrm-inference-littles-law-inference)) holds for any stable queue, rather than serving requests alone. Applied to the training-job queue, the average number of jobs waiting equals the job arrival rate times the average queue wait. Calculate the job arrival rate for a 4-hour average wait with 100 jobs in queue and explain what additional job-size, service-time, and arrival-pattern measurements are needed to choose expansion or utilization optimization.

**Savings vs. platform**: compare the annual recovered-capacity value from cluster optimization to the \$2M/year platform investment scenario. Holding the utilization gain and chargeback rate fixed, determine the GPU count where annual recovered-capacity value equals that investment, and state what must happen for the capacity value to become cash savings.

### ML Productivity Goodput Across the Portfolio

^sec-ml-operations-scale-advanced-fleet-metrics-ml-productivity-goodput-mpg-fabf

A GPU spinning at 100 percent utilization on a hyperparameter tuning job that eventually fails from a configuration error looks efficient on a hardware dashboard while producing zero engineering value. Raw utilization metrics capture device busyness, not useful progress; ML productivity goodput (MPG) turns that distinction into a fleet metric ([[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/03 The Fleet in Production/12 ML Operations at Scale/V2-12.16 Source References#^ref-wongpanich2025fleet|Wongpanich et al. 2025]]).

↩︎ ML productivity goodput, its three factors, and the boundary at which each one counts a loss are established in **[[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/03 The Fleet in Production/09 Fleet Efficiency/V2-09.03 ML Goodput#^sec-performance-engineering-measurement-scale|ML Goodput]]**.

MPG multiplies scheduling, runtime, and program goodput, which measure how much capacity survives each stage of a job’s life: holding a complete allocation, retaining progress while it holds one, and running its retained steps at the ideal rate. Each lost hour is charged to exactly one factor, so the product says which layer limits useful output as well as how much output was lost. The platform’s job is to compute the three factors per team and workload class from scheduler records, job timelines, and step traces. A portfolio dashboard then shows which layer limits each team and which owner, orchestration, the runtime, or the program, must act.

### Multi-Tenancy and Isolation

^sec-ml-operations-scale-multitenancy-isolation-c3ff

When a batch training job claims all free HBM on a shared node and starves co-located production inference, or when a shared cluster generates a monthly GPU bill that no team can attribute to its own workloads, multi-tenancy fails before its efficiency savings can materialize. The scheduler’s quotas and fair-share policies and the serving system’s isolation mechanisms, established in **[[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/03 The Fleet in Production/10 Serving at Scale/V2-10.13 Multi-Tenancy and Isolation#^sec-inference-scale-multitenancy-isolation-688c|Multi-Tenancy and Isolation]]**, already bound interference within one cluster or one inference service. What the platform adds is one namespace hierarchy across training and serving, plus cost isolation that follows each tenant’s spend, and neither the scheduler nor the serving system owns those alone. Multi-tenancy is therefore an isolation problem before it is an efficiency problem, and the platform must bound performance interference, data exposure, and cost spillover at the same time.

↩︎ Quotas, hierarchical fair-share, and burst borrowing are established in **[[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/02 Distributed ML/08 Fleet Orchestration/V2-08.12 Multi-Tenancy and Quotas#^sec-fleet-orchestration-multi-tenancy|Multi-Tenancy and Quotas]]**.

The platform’s added obligations are hierarchy and attribution. A typical design gives each team its own development, staging, and production namespaces with resource quotas, and places shared services such as the feature store, model registry, and monitoring in common namespaces with access controls, network segmentation, and encryption at the tenant boundary. Cost isolation then combines metering with declared allocation rules for shared services and reserved capacity, because not every shared cost can be assigned to an individual request. Without these boundaries, one team’s demanding workload can degrade performance for the others, the noisy neighbor problem, which on an ML platform crosses the boundary between training and serving whenever an unthrottled training job contends with co-located inference. The request limits, rate limits, priority classes, and burst budgets that enforce fairness in the data plane are the serving and scheduling mechanisms already cited; the platform’s job is to apply them consistently across every tenant’s namespaces.

↩︎ Noisy-neighbor interference and the device-sharing mechanisms that bound it are established in **[[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/02 Distributed ML/08 Fleet Orchestration/V2-08.11 Serving Capacity in the Shared Fleet#^sec-fleet-orchestration-resource-isolation|Resource isolation]]**.

Isolation makes sharing safe, but it does not make sharing pay. Cost isolation meters each tenant’s consumption; whether the shared fleet is worth its capital, how its spend reaches the teams that caused it, and when a workload’s cost justifies optimization are economic questions that the platform must answer with the same rigor it applies to releases.

    2.  Which layer (orchestration, the runtime, or the program) limits each team’s useful output, and therefore which owner must act
    3.  The capacity-weighted average utilization across heterogeneous accelerators
    4.  The number of models a platform needs before its benefit-cost ratio passes one

2.  Which shared-resource path can allow a training tenant to violate the latency SLO of a co-located inference tenant?

    1.  Competition for finite GPU High Bandwidth Memory (HBM) or shared RDMA fabric bandwidth can stall the inference tenant even when host CPU resources are isolated.
    2.  Linux kernel cgroups and container namespaces cannot be instantiated on systems containing GPU accelerators.
    3.  CPUs cannot run multi-tenant virtualization or container runtimes.
    4.  Multi-tenant GPU clusters require all tenant models to share the exact same weights in memory.
