# V2-12.10 Fleet Economics and Utilization

[[Home|Home]] · [[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/Volume 2 Overview|Volume Overview]] · [[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/03 The Fleet in Production/12 ML Operations at Scale/V2-12 ML Operations at Scale|V2-12 ML Operations at Scale]]

^sec-ops-scale-fleet-economics

A provisioned GPU incurs capacity costs whether it serves predictions, waits between training bursts, or remains reserved for rollback. Those idle periods have different meanings: a scheduling bottleneck strands capacity, while a justified reserve buys recovery capability. The operational machinery developed so far decides how models change safely; it does not decide whether the capacity underneath them is worth what it costs. Fleet economics therefore distinguishes productive use, deliberate headroom, and avoidable waste before treating higher utilization as an improvement.

Three economic levels keep the cost analysis from collapsing unlike decisions into one number. *Platform economics* ([[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/03 The Fleet in Production/12 ML Operations at Scale/V2-12.03 The N-Models Problem#^sec-ml-operations-scale-quantifying-platform-economics-2026|section 12.2.3]]) asks whether common control-plane capabilities repay their fixed development and maintenance cost across the portfolio. *Fleet economics* asks whether shared hardware, facilities, and capacity commitments produce useful work at acceptable utilization and risk. *Workload economics* asks whether one training run, serving endpoint, or experiment earns its marginal compute, data, and engineering cost. A gain at one level does not establish value at the others. Faster jobs do not remove an existing capacity commitment, and high fleet utilization does not establish that a platform repays its adoption and maintenance costs. The placement and scheduling mechanisms in **[[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/02 Distributed ML/08 Fleet Orchestration/V2-08 Fleet Orchestration#^sec-fleet-orchestration|Fleet Orchestration]]** operate within this capacity envelope. Operations owns the commitments around them: capital planning, portfolio return, lifecycle total cost of ownership (TCO), and cost attribution across teams.

**Definition 12.1: ML systems TCO**

***Total cost of ownership (TCO) for ML systems*** is the complete economic accounting of developing, deploying, and operating machine learning capabilities across their full lifecycle.

1.  **Significance**: It captures the cost inversion of scale: training incurs a cost per run, while inference accumulates with served work. The lifecycle example in [[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/03 The Fleet in Production/12 ML Operations at Scale/V2-12.10 Fleet Economics and Utilization#^sec-ml-operations-scale-tco-total-value-ownership-de10|section 12.9.2.2]] prices that inversion for a high-traffic service, where cheaper serving repays additional training spend.
2.  **Distinction**: This lifecycle view includes serving efficiency alongside hardware, data, development, and recurring training costs. The energy and compute cost of each prediction depends on achieved hardware efficiency ${\eta_{\text{hw}}}$ as well as batching and model size.
3.  **Common pitfall**: A frequent misconception is that the initial training bill is the primary financial risk. In reality, the data debt and maintenance overhead are the “silent interest rates” that can make an accurate model economically unsustainable in production.

Platform economics was priced earlier in the chapter, so the analysis here follows the remaining levels downward. It prices the fleet first, then the workload, and closes with financial operations (FinOps), the controls that attribute those costs to teams and assign the authority to act on them.

Engineering constraints shape the feasible economic choices. Infrastructure must first meet quality, latency, reliability, and capacity requirements; useful computation per dollar then helps compare feasible designs over the system’s lifetime. At an assumed \$350,000 per 8-GPU DGX H100 node, a 10,000-GPU cluster represents about \$437.5M in node hardware capital expenditure before networking, facility, staffing, and maintenance costs. The purchase price, however, is only the beginning. Over the three-year planning horizon used here, those operating and supporting costs determine the full commitment. TCO[[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/03 The Fleet in Production/12 ML Operations at Scale/V2-12.17 Footnotes#^fn17|¹⁷]] connects that commitment to useful output and provides the basis for the broader ML-lifecycle accounting developed in [[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/03 The Fleet in Production/12 ML Operations at Scale/V2-12.10 Fleet Economics and Utilization#^sec-ml-operations-scale-ml-systems-tco-framework-b980|section 12.9.6]].

An accelerator fleet concentrates a large commitment in resources that stand idle whenever data, communication, or scheduling falls behind, so the economic target is useful output within the headroom that failures, rollbacks, and traffic spikes require, not maximum device busyness.

Two reference prices anchor the arithmetic that follows. The internal fleet rate of \$2/GPU-hour approximates what an owned H100 fleet operated at high utilization costs its own teams, and the on-demand cloud rate of \$4/GPU-hour is the rental reference for build-vs.-buy comparisons. Other rates in the chapter, such as the internal chargeback price in the GPU cluster efficiency example, the FinOps spending scenario, or the non-energy accelerator-hour rate in the training cost example, are scenario choices and are labeled as such where they occur. Cluster sizes follow the same rule. The running fleet is the Archetype A reference cluster of **[[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/00 Introduction/01 Introduction/V2-01.07 The Fleet Stack and the Lighthouse Archetypes#^sec-introduction-archetype-gpt4llama3-b02f|Archetype A (GPT-4/Llama-3)]]**, the H100 cluster that trains the GPT-3-class reference instance; every later analysis prices it unless it names a separate scenario.

### Cost Centers as Operating Constraints

^sec-ml-operations-scale-tco-breakdown

Standing up the running 1,024-GPU H100 cluster (128 nodes) commits \$44.80M in server hardware before a single job runs, plus \$5 million for the InfiniBand fabric, \$10 million for facility buildout, and \$1.5 million each year in power and staffing. The total cost of an ML cluster decomposes into two broad categories. **Capital expenditure (CapEx)** covers the one-time costs of building the infrastructure: accelerators, servers, networking equipment, facility construction, and installation. **Operational expenditure (OpEx)** covers the recurring costs of running it: electricity, cooling, network bandwidth, staffing, maintenance, and software licenses. For a large on-premises cluster, [[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/03 The Fleet in Production/12 ML Operations at Scale/V2-12.10 Fleet Economics and Utilization#^tbl-frontier-training-cost-stack|table 29]] separates the cost stack into capital and operating centers, each with different utilization implications.

| **Cost center**              | **Cost class** | **Typical share** | **Included components and implications**                                                                                                                        |
|:-----------------------------|:--------------:|:------------------|:----------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------|
| **Accelerators and servers** |     CapEx      | 50–60% of CapEx   | GPUs or TPUs, host servers, baseboard management controllers, and local storage; this category dominates upfront purchase and depreciation.                     |
| **Networking**               |     CapEx      | 10–15% of CapEx   | InfiniBand switches, HCAs, cables, and optical transceivers; the running example budgets about \$5 million for a two-tier fat-tree serving 1,024 GPUs.          |
| **Facilities and cooling**   |     CapEx      | 15–25% of CapEx   | Building construction or retrofit, transformers, UPS systems, PDUs, and cooling plant; liquid cooling adds 10–15% to facility costs but reduces long-term OpEx. |
| **Electricity**              |      OpEx      | 60–70% of OpEx    | At \$0.07/kWh, a 1,024-GPU H100 cluster consuming about 1.1 MW including cooling at power usage effectiveness (PUE) 1.1 costs about \$665,000 per year.         |
| **Staffing and maintenance** |      OpEx      | 20–30% of OpEx    | System administrators, hardware technicians, replacement parts, and software license fees.                                                                      |

Table 29: **Frontier Training Cost Stack**: Capital costs are dominated by accelerators, networking, and facilities, while operating costs are dominated by electricity and specialized staffing. The shares are broad planning ranges; in the running 1,024-GPU example, node hardware is about 75 percent of CapEx and electricity about 44 percent of OpEx.

^tbl-frontier-training-cost-stack

The running cost example is Archetype A’s reference instance, the 175B-parameter GPT-3-class model, trained on the reference cluster of 1,024 H100 GPUs spread across 128 nodes. For the illustrative training plan developed below, assume a 2–4-week run. Parameter count alone does not determine this duration; the later planning example states its token and throughput budget.

Over a three-year lifecycle, whether owning or renting the cluster costs less depends on utilization. The hardware CapEx of \$44.80M (\$350,000 per node), the \$5 million two-tier InfiniBand fat-tree network, the \$10 million facility allocation, and \$1.5 million per year in electricity (at \$0.07/kWh with a PUE of 1.1) and specialized staffing bring the three-year total to roughly \$64 million. If this dedicated cluster only trains six large models per year, the effective cost per run is \$3.6 million. Renting the same 1,024-GPU allocation in the public cloud at \$4/GPU-hour costs about \$1.4M–2.8M per run (1,024 GPUs $\times$ 336–672 hours $\times$ \$4), or \$25M–50M over three years for six runs per year. Cloud rental is cheaper for this bursty scenario when idle allocations can be released. At the opposite extreme, *continuous utilization* spreads the owned cluster’s stated costs over every available hour, reducing the cost to approximately \$2.39/GPU-hour, below the cloud reference rate. Ownership need not require continuous use; the actual break-even lies where useful demand repays the fixed commitment under matched cost boundaries.

↪ Power usage effectiveness as a facility metric, and what it omits, is developed in **[[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/04 The Responsible Fleet/15 Sustainable AI/V2-15.05 Energy Measurement and Modeling#^sec-sustainable-ai-facilitylevel-power-metrics-5558|Facility-level power metrics]]**.

### Utilization as the Economic Invariant

^sec-ml-operations-scale-build-vs-buy

An organization that buys a dedicated cluster for six bursty training runs a year pays more per run than public cloud rental, whereas keeping that same cluster busy above its break-even utilization turns upfront capital into a substantial per-GPU-hour savings. The most consequential economic question is whether the fleet can sustain enough useful load to amortize fixed capacity. Build-vs.-buy is one expression of that question, but the same invariant appears inside a deployed platform: reserved capacity, fallback pools, regional replicas, and canary headroom all become economical only when their reliability value justifies their idle cost. Ownership pays a large CapEx step up front and then climbs a shallow operating slope, while cloud rental starts at zero and rises steadily. Under sustained high utilization the rental line overtakes the owned cluster within a 30-month horizon ([[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/03 The Fleet in Production/12 ML Operations at Scale/V2-12.10 Fleet Economics and Utilization#^fig-tco-build-vs-buy|figure 13]]).

![[90 Assets/Volume II/12 Ops Scale/Cell 33 Output 1 18aef631ff.png]]

Figure 13: **Build vs. Buy TCO**: The curves compare cumulative cost over a 30-month horizon for a high-utilization H100 reference cluster. On-premises deployment begins with a CapEx step plus monthly OpEx, whereas cloud rental cost rises linearly with utilization. Over a 3-year service life, owning pays off only above 59.7 percent sustained utilization; below that, renting stays cheaper.

^fig-tco-build-vs-buy

The plotted reference uses 1,024 H100 GPUs across 128 nodes at 80 percent sustained utilization. The on-premises curve starts at \$59.80M in upfront CapEx and adds about \$1.5M per year, while cloud rental at \$4/GPU-hour grows by about \$2.39M per month. Over 30 months, the cloud line reaches \$71.8M, and the on-premises line crosses it around 26.4 months.

Cloud providers charge by the GPU-hour. At the on-demand reference rate of \$4 per H100-hour, a single 8-GPU node rented only during 80 percent of the year costs \$224,256 per year. An on-premises DGX H100 node costs approximately \$350,000 to purchase. Amortized over three years and combined with electricity costs of \$4,519 per node per year, the total annual on-premises cost is approximately \$121,186 per node. This assumes idle rental periods can be released; an allocated but idle GPU still incurs a bill. Solving the break-even equation with electricity scaling at the same allocation fraction, on-premises infrastructure becomes favorable when sustained utilization exceeds roughly 42.5 percent. This narrow comparison includes node acquisition and utilization-proportional electricity; it excludes idle power, network and facility capital, staffing, maintenance, and financing. It does not use the wider owned-cost boundary of the cluster comparison in [[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/03 The Fleet in Production/12 ML Operations at Scale/V2-12.10 Fleet Economics and Utilization#^fig-tco-build-vs-buy|figure 13]].

The same calculation explains why cloud pricing cannot be read as a simple price list. The scenario’s reserved discount of 40–60 percent converts optionality into commitment. Its spot or preemptible discount of 60–90 percent introduces interruption risk. The relevant comparison is cost per completed workload under the deadline or service contract. Checkpoint recovery, replacement capacity, and traffic fallback offer different ways to manage that risk; no single combination is required for every workload.

Owned capacity moves a different set of risks into the platform. Facility construction, networking, staffing, maintenance, and hardware obsolescence continue to accrue even when the GPUs are idle; their cost depends on site, power delivery, cooling design, and accounting boundary. A three-year-old GPU may be economically stranded if the next generation delivers 3$\times$ more performance per watt. The engineering question is therefore not whether one purchasing channel is universally cheaper; it is whether the workload mix can keep fixed capacity useful enough to compensate for its lifecycle and operating risks.

Hybrid designs follow from the same invariant. Owned infrastructure can carry predictable baseline work, while cloud allocations absorb peak demand, early hardware access, or bursty training campaigns. The hybrid split is not a compromise between two vendor categories; it is a scheduling policy over capital risk, utilization, and deadline risk.

For the 175B model, workload cadence changes the ownership decision. Consider a dedicated training fleet used only 15–20 percent of the time because its annual training campaign leaves long gaps. A portfolio of experiments, hyperparameter sweeps, fine-tuning jobs, and model variants could instead sustain 70–80 percent utilization on the same hardware, provided those jobs fit its capacity and scheduling constraints. Serving can also fill gaps when the hardware and isolation policy support it. These illustrative utilization levels describe workload mixes, not consequences of training a particular model. A hybrid design can keep predictable baseline work on owned capacity and rent the temporary expansion, such as the 5–10$\times$ baseline allocation assumed here for large training runs. The same invariant governs the comparison at a larger fleet scale, provided each estimate states which costs it includes.

**Napkin Math 12.9: The 10,000-GPU cluster**

Consider a cluster of 1,250 DGX H100 nodes (10,000 GPUs) for training the 175B model.

**On-premises (3-year lifecycle)**:

- **Hardware CapEx**: 1,250 $\times$ \$350,000 = \$437.5M
- **Network CapEx**: ~\$25M (InfiniBand fat-tree fabric)
- **Facility CapEx**: ~\$75M (liquid-cooled data center hall)
- **Annual Electricity**: Simplified GPU-only estimate: 10,000 GPUs $\times$ 700 W $\times$ PUE 1.1 $\times$ 8,760 h/year $\times$ \$0.07/kWh = \$4.7M/year
- **Annual Staffing**: ~\$5M/year
- **3-year total**: \$537.5M + 3 years $\times$ \$9.7M = ~\$566.7M

**Cloud rental** (3-year rental at 80 percent utilization):

- **Annual Cost**: 10,000 GPUs $\times$ \$4/GPU-hour $\times$ 8,760 h/year $\times$ 0.80 = \$280.3M/year
- **3-year total**: ~\$841.0M

**Systems insight**: With the stated prices and cost boundary, on-premises saves approximately \$274.3M over three years at 80 percent utilization, but the savings disappear below roughly 53.9 percent. Owned hardware still incurs facility and staffing costs regardless of load. This is a scenario comparison, not a procurement quote: its GPU-only electricity estimate omits host and network power, and omitted maintenance or financing costs would also change the break-even point.

**Systems Perspective 12.3: Sustained demand and unit cost**

The 10,000-GPU scenario illustrates how sustained demand can support ownership. A portfolio that keeps compatible capacity usefully occupied at 70–90 percent utilization spreads fixed costs over more work than one operating at 20–30 percent. These are illustrative demand regimes, not utilization guarantees based on organizational size. Continuous training pipelines, model refreshes, and compatible inference workloads can support the higher regime; sporadic campaigns favor the option to release rented capacity. When a broad workload portfolio lowers unit cost enough to fund further investment, the advantage compounds, but it depends on useful demand and operating discipline, not fleet size alone.

#### Operational Complexity

^sec-ml-operations-scale-operational-complexity-c325

A TCO estimate must include the operational work that sustains the fleet. Hardware maintenance must diagnose failed GPUs, links, network adapters, and cooling components before a local fault becomes a fleet-wide slowdown. The five-hour fleet failure interval in the scenario of [[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/03 The Fleet in Production/12 ML Operations at Scale/V2-12.07 Monitoring at Scale#^sec-ops-scale-fleet-telemetry|section 12.6.1]] informs staffing, but does not determine headcount by itself. Staffing also depends on diagnosis and repair time, automation, concurrent incidents, and coverage requirements while the rest of the fleet keeps working.

The software side creates the same coupling. CUDA, GPU drivers, InfiniBand drivers, container runtimes, schedulers, monitoring systems, and training frameworks all participate in the achieved utilization number. A driver/toolkit incompatibility can silently degrade performance, while a scheduler or monitoring gap can strand accelerators behind failed jobs. Staffing depends on automation, service commitments, and operating scope because performance optimization is continuous: as models, frameworks, and hardware configurations change, communication patterns, memory pressure, and parallelism strategies change with them. **[[05 Reference/Volume II - Scaling/V2 The C³ Taxonomy#^sec-appdx-c3-taxonomy-case-1|Case 1 on the underutilized compute fleet]]** works an underutilized fleet through the C³ diagnosis, ruling out communication before isolating the compute axis, which shows the performance engineer how to locate where the lost utilization goes rather than tuning blindly.

↪ The C³ telemetry diagnostic decision tree is developed in **[[05 Reference/Volume II - Scaling/V2 The C³ Taxonomy#^sec-appdx-c3-diagnostic-tree|C^3 Infrastructure Diagnostic Decision Tree and Fleet Diagnostic Scorecard]]**.

Cloud providers charge a margin above raw hardware and electricity partly because they absorb this operating burden. The point is not that cloud is simpler in every technical sense; it exposes a different interface. The platform either pays for internal expertise in GPU kernel behavior, InfiniBand operations, distributed-systems debugging, liquid cooling, and power infrastructure, or it pays a provider to hide some of that machinery behind a service contract. In both cases, the economics are still governed by useful work per dollar.

#### From TCO to Total Value of Ownership

^sec-ml-operations-scale-tco-total-value-ownership-de10

A more complete framework than TCO is total value of ownership, which includes the value generated by infrastructure rather than only its cost. Two clusters with identical TCO may create different outcomes if one reaches a result two weeks earlier, sustains higher scaling efficiency, starts jobs faster, checkpoints more cheaply, or serves the resulting model at lower cost per token. The value terms are hard to price exactly, but they are not optional in systems reasoning. Time-to-result affects product deadlines. Scaling efficiency affects final model quality under a fixed training window. Experimentation velocity affects the number of hypotheses the team can test. Inference efficiency can dominate training cost over a multi-year deployment.

This value-oriented perspective changes the infrastructure decision from cost minimization to constraint management. A cheaper cluster that slows the research cycle or produces a model with higher serving cost may be more expensive over the model lifecycle. Conversely, a more expensive training platform can pay for itself if it enables a smaller or more efficient deployed model.

The inference dimension of total value of ownership (TVO) connects a training investment to the service it supports. **[[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/03 The Fleet in Production/10 Serving at Scale/V2-10.05 Serving Cost Dominance in Production#^sec-inference-scale-serving-cost-dominates-training-cost-576a|Serving Cost Dominance in Production]]** establishes why recurring serving cost overtakes a one-time training bill (principle [[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/03 The Fleet in Production/V2 Fleet-In-Production Principles#^pri-serving-cost-dominance|17]]); the fleet accounting asks the converse question, whether additional training spend repays itself through cheaper serving. Consider a service of the 175B model that handles 10 million queries per day, each generating an average of 500 tokens, for 5 billion tokens daily. At an assumed \$2 per million tokens, serving costs approximately \$3.6M per year, held constant over a three-year deployment. If an additional \$2M training investment produces a validated 20 percent reduction in serving cost at matched quality and traffic, the three-year gross savings are \$2.2M, enough to recover that investment before any additional maintenance costs. A lifecycle forecast must still add retraining and changing demand when they occur.

### Depreciation and Lifecycle

^sec-ml-operations-scale-depreciation

A three-year-old accelerator fleet that still passes every hardware diagnostic can become uneconomic for frontier pretraining when the next GPU generation delivers 3–4$\times$ the performance per watt. It can also lose tens of millions of dollars in hardware value while sitting idle between two annual training runs. ML accelerators depreciate as newer devices change the cost of useful work: a physically functioning accelerator can become uneconomic for a particular training workload while remaining useful for another. The peak arithmetic efficiencies in **[[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/01 The Fleet/02 Compute Infrastructure/V2-02.03 The Accelerator#^tbl-power-efficiency|Power Efficiency across GPU Generations]]** provide one screening measure, but depreciation and refresh decisions require sustained throughput at matched quality, memory capacity, energy consumption, and the cost of replacing otherwise usable equipment.

Resale value depends on supply, demand, memory capacity, software support, and the workload a buyer intends to run. A refresh budget should therefore test a range of resale assumptions rather than extrapolating a fixed historical depreciation rate across generations.

Refresh policy connects lifecycle planning to scheduling. The three-year horizon used in these examples is a planning assumption, not a universal accounting life or a required replacement date. Replacing the entire fleet at once creates a large CapEx spike, while staggered refresh spreads expenditure but leaves the scheduler with mixed-generation hardware. Placement must then account for different compute capabilities, memory capacities, and communication bandwidths. Keeping generations in separate job pools is one option; jobs that span generations need explicit support for their performance differences.

The same lifecycle logic determines where older accelerators remain useful. A GPU that is inefficient for the largest training runs may still serve smaller models, fine-tuning jobs, or memory-bound inference economically if its capacity, bandwidth, and energy cost meet those workloads’ requirements. A newer device’s peak arithmetic advantage alone does not settle that comparison. Resale, repurposing, and capacity-sharing arrangements preserve value only when the platform can route useful work to the appropriate generation without hiding performance cliffs from users.

Depreciation is most punitive for bursty workloads. If a team trains one large model per year, the GPUs lose value during the idle months even though they perform no useful computation. Cloud capacity amortizes the same depreciation across many customers, while owned capacity must amortize it across the owner’s own workload mix. That is why the utilization invariant appears again: the hardware must either stay busy or be cheap enough to strand.

Isolating hardware cost shows how much depreciation alone charges each run of the 175B model. Under the \$350,000-per-8-GPU-node assumption, the 1,024-GPU (128-node) fleet costs \$44.8M before network and facility costs. Assume a resale value of \$7M–10M after three years in a market where newer accelerators offer 3–4$\times$ the performance per watt. That performance assumption does not determine resale value by itself. The assumed residual value yields net hardware depreciation of \$34.8M–37.8M. Allocated to only two large training runs, it contributes \$17.4M–18.9M per run before electricity, staffing, or facility costs. At 80 percent useful utilization over three years, it instead contributes approximately \$1.62–1.76 per used GPU-hour. This is the hardware component of owned cost, not a complete rate directly comparable with cloud rental. Utilization spreads that commitment across output; the full cost accounting determines economic viability.

#### Refresh Screening

^sec-ml-operations-scale-power-efficiency

Each accelerator generation has raised peak arithmetic per watt, more slowly than raw throughput because device power rose as well. A refresh pays only when the workload realizes enough of the higher ceiling to lower energy and cost per completed run at the same quality target. Memory-bound serving and communication-bound training can therefore justify different refresh choices for the same fleet.

↩︎ Accelerator generations, their peak rates, the power each draws, and their peak arithmetic per watt are established in **[[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/01 The Fleet/02 Compute Infrastructure/V2-02.03 The Accelerator#^sec-compute-infrastructure-evolution-gpu-architectures-566b|Evolution of GPU architectures]]**.

A refresh comparison can hold either the arithmetic ceiling or the accelerator power budget fixed, and the two framings answer different questions. Consider replacing a V100 fleet of the reference cluster’s size, 1,024 devices with a combined accelerator thermal design power (TDP) of 307.2 kW. On the dense FP16 basis of **[[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/01 The Fleet/02 Compute Infrastructure/V2-02.03 The Accelerator#^tbl-power-efficiency|Power Efficiency across GPU Generations]]**, 130 H100s match that fleet’s arithmetic ceiling at about 91 kW of combined TDP. Held at the same 307.2 kW, the budget instead accommodates 438 H100s with 3.4× the original ceiling. The first comparison screens for a lower power envelope at matched peak arithmetic; the second screens for more arithmetic capacity within a fixed power envelope. Neither establishes achieved throughput or measured power.

The fleet matched by arithmetic ceiling is not automatically usable. The original fleet has 7.9× as many devices, so, with the same port count per device, the replacement offers proportionally fewer network ports, and its aggregate HBM falls from 35.2 TB to 11.2 TB. A replacement plan must pass memory-fit, topology, and latency checks before the arithmetic equivalence counts as capacity, and both framings compare peak ceilings rather than energy per completed run at matched quality. The hybrid split described in [[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/03 The Fleet in Production/12 ML Operations at Scale/V2-12.10 Fleet Economics and Utilization#^sec-ml-operations-scale-build-vs-buy|section 12.9.2]] also hedges refresh timing, because cloud bursts provide access to newer hardware before a large purchase commits the fleet to it.

The screening numbers bound the refresh decision for the 175B model without settling it. The H100’s 700 W TDP is more than twice the V100’s 300 W, yet run energy can still fall, because energy integrates measured power over elapsed time and the faster device finishes sooner. The dollar saving and payback period therefore come from a matched training configuration: measured or validated runtime, accelerator and host power, fabric and storage consumption, and the facility overhead applied to that IT total.

### Cloud Capacity as an Operations Constraint

^sec-ml-operations-scale-cloud-options

Renting 1,024 cloud GPUs avoids upfront capital expenditure, but a synchronous training job whose tensor-parallel ranks straddle a cloud placement-group boundary stalls on inter-instance network latency at every layer. A spot preemption without checkpoint discipline can erase a 70 percent price discount in a single lost afternoon. For organizations that choose the cloud path, the provider absorbs hardware ownership while exposing capacity, topology, and interruption risk as operating constraints. Instance types and pricing change frequently, so the durable distinction is not vendor branding; it is how the cloud allocation affects communication, scheduling, and fault tolerance.

Three properties of a cloud allocation matter most. The interconnect fabric decides whether distributed jobs keep the scaling assumptions used in design, through fixed-latency and per-byte terms that **[[05 Reference/Volume II - Scaling/V2 Communication Foundations#^sec-appdx-comm-foundations-alpha-beta|The α-β Communication Model]]** separates. Provisioning granularity decides how much topology the platform controls, from individual instances it must assemble itself to preconnected pods that limit composition. Custom accelerators repeat the generality tax (principle [[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/01 The Fleet/V2 Fleet Principles#^pri-generality-tax|1]]): supported operations get cheaper, but end-to-end savings depend on workload mapping, software support, and migration effort. The on-demand, reserved, and spot pricing compared in [[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/03 The Fleet in Production/12 ML Operations at Scale/V2-12.10 Fleet Economics and Utilization#^sec-ml-operations-scale-build-vs-buy|section 12.9.2]] trade optionality, commitment, and interruption risk alongside those physical properties, so the purchasing choice is also a fault-tolerance decision.

**[[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/02 Distributed ML/08 Fleet Orchestration/V2-08.09 Cost Optimization#^sec-fleet-orchestration-spot-instances|Spot instances and preemptible VMs]]** develops spot economics from the scheduler’s side, where **[[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/02 Distributed ML/08 Fleet Orchestration/V2-08.09 Cost Optimization#^eq-fleet-orchestration-spot-cost|equation 8.3]]** inflates the spot price by the ratio of total to productive time. The operations view applies that relation to a capacity budget. At a 70 percent discount, a scenario choice within the 60–90 percent range that section cites, spot H100 instances cost approximately \$1.20/GPU-hour instead of \$4. For the 175B model, the 1,024-GPU, 2–4 week workload consumes approximately 344,000–688,000 allocated GPU-hours. The on-demand cost is therefore about \$1.4M–2.8M, while spot pricing would reduce the bill to roughly \$0.4M–0.8M before interruptions. Interruptions, restarts, and lost work can erase that discount or push the job past its deadline, so completion cost and elapsed time both belong in the comparison. Checkpoint frequency, instance diversification, and on-demand fallback improve that comparison within the limits that **[[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/02 Distributed ML/08 Fleet Orchestration/V2-08.09 Cost Optimization#^sec-fleet-orchestration-spot-strategies|Spot instance strategies for ML training]]** sets out, and serving can use interruptible capacity only when remaining replicas, routing, and replacement readiness preserve its service contract.

Renting capacity for the 175B model also requires an allocation whose placement contract matches the parallelism plan. Cloud instances share the provider’s fabric with other tenants, and placement groups or dedicated fabrics reserve high-bandwidth connectivity only within a group, so a synchronous job whose ranks straddle a group boundary pays that boundary’s latency and bandwidth on every step. The working rule is to keep tensor- and pipeline-parallel groups, which exchange data within every layer or micro-batch, inside one placement group, and let only the less frequent data-parallel gradient traffic cross the boundary. Representative collectives must be benchmarked on the actual allocation before nominal GPU availability counts as usable training capacity.

**Checkpoint 12.5: TCO decision framework**

An organization needs to train 10 models/year, each requiring 1,000 GPU-hours on H100s, evaluating an on-premises cluster with 8 GPUs against cloud instances at \$4/GPU-hour.

**Cloud cost estimate**: calculate the annual cloud cost from the training volume and hourly rate.

**On-premises TCO**: calculate the annualized on-premises cost, assuming \$350,000 per 8-GPU node, \$0.07/kWh electricity at PUE 1.1, and 700 W per GPU.

**Break-even volume**: solve for the number of annual training runs at which on-premises becomes cheaper.

### Capacity Planning as a Reliability Control

^sec-ml-operations-scale-planning

Sizing a training cluster from parameter count alone can starve the job of memory and fabric bandwidth. Ordering 1,024 H100s that arrive six months before the substation and liquid-cooled racks can power them leaves tens of millions of dollars of silicon sitting in crates while the launch window closes. Infrastructure planning begins when a workload requirement becomes a facility requirement. Model architecture, precision, optimizer state, and batch configuration establish memory needs; the training algorithm and token budget establish work; the deadline establishes required sustained throughput. Parallelization then determines the communication that the fabric must support. Accelerator TDP contributes to the node power envelope, which also includes hosts, memory, and networking, and that envelope constrains rack density and cooling. Planning follows this causal chain from useful work to physical capacity and total cost of ownership.

The first step is workload characterization. Memory sizing follows activations, optimizer state, temporary buffers, and the sharding or offload policy rather than parameter count alone. The token budget fixes total work, and the deadline fixes sustained throughput. That budget is a compute-optimal choice under the scaling laws of **[[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/00 Introduction/01 Introduction/V2-01.04 AI Scaling Laws#^sec-vol2-intro-ai-scaling-laws-a043|AI Scaling Laws]]** (principle [[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/02 Distributed ML/V2 Distributed ML Principles#^pri-universal-scaling|9]]), and the choice holds only within the breakdown conditions of **[[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/00 Introduction/01 Introduction/V2-01.04 AI Scaling Laws#^sec-vol2-intro-scaling-law-breakdown-conditions-1f8c|When scaling laws break down]]**. Bottom-up sizing then works from the accelerator outward, using roofline analysis to tell whether each training or serving phase binds on computation or memory bandwidth and the parallelization plan to set the AllReduce, AllToAll, or point-to-point traffic the fabric must carry.

↩︎ The Roofline Model and its ridge point are established in **[[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/01 The Fleet/02 Compute Infrastructure/V2-02.04 The Memory Wall#^sec-compute-roofline|Roofline model]]**.

Each layer of physical sizing then applies a method taught in an earlier chapter. Per-device training state sets node memory and the sharding or offload policy (**[[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/01 The Fleet/02 Compute Infrastructure/V2-02.06 The Node#^sec-compute-node-memory|Node memory partitioning]]**, **[[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/02 Distributed ML/06 Distributed Training/V2-06.04 Data Parallelism#^sec-distributed-training-systems-systems-memoryefficient-data-parallelism-zero-fsdp-0e69|Memory-efficient data parallelism with ZeRO and FSDP]]**). The primary collective sets the fabric topology and its congestion-control requirements (**[[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/01 The Fleet/03 Network Fabrics/V2-03.06 Level 3 Switch and Topology#^sec-network-fabrics-fat-tree|Fat-tree (Clos) networks]]**, **[[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/01 The Fleet/03 Network Fabrics/V2-03.07 Level 4 Fabric Behavior, Congestion, and Routing#^sec-network-fabrics-congestion-control|End-to-end congestion control with DCQCN and HPCC]]**). Node power sets rack density and cooling (**[[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/01 The Fleet/02 Compute Infrastructure/V2-02.07 The Rack#^sec-compute-power-wall|Power and thermal envelopes]]**). The four-node DGX H100 rack assumed for the 175B plan is roughly a 33.5 kW design point once host, network, conversion, and cooling overhead are included. TCO and schedule remain design variables because the cluster must arrive in time to matter, and GPU supply or electrical work often dominates lead time.

For the 175B training plan, the pod-sizing method of **[[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/01 The Fleet/02 Compute Infrastructure/V2-02.08 The Pod#^sec-compute-pod|The Pod]]** turns the run’s 3.15 × 10²³ FLOPs budget into a cluster. On a BF16/FP16 planning basis rather than the FP8 headline peak, each H100 sustains an assumed 445.1 TFLOP/s at 45 percent model FLOPs utilization (MFU), so 1,024 GPUs complete the work in about 8 days. That token budget is the GPT-3 operating point, which is undertrained relative to compute-optimal allocation (**[[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/00 Introduction/01 Introduction/V2-01.04 AI Scaling Laws#^sec-vol2-intro-computeoptimal-resource-allocation-541a|Compute-optimal resource allocation]]**). A plan that followed the compute-optimal ratio of tokens to parameters at the same model size would multiply the token count, and with it the cluster-days, so the scaling-law choice moves the capacity plan before any hardware question arises. A 2–4-week calendar allowance and a 5–10 percent spares pool then cover delays and recovery outside the MFU measurement window; stalls already reflected in MFU must not be counted again.

The plan’s 32 racks draw approximately 1.1 MW under the rack accounting boundary of **[[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/01 The Fleet/02 Compute Infrastructure/V2-02.07 The Rack#^sec-compute-power-wall|Power and thermal envelopes]]**, and its three-year cost and cloud alternative are those of the running cluster in [[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/03 The Fleet in Production/12 ML Operations at Scale/V2-12.10 Fleet Economics and Utilization#^sec-ml-operations-scale-tco-breakdown|section 12.9.1]], where ownership pays only if utilization stays high beyond the initial training run. Procurement and facility schedules must be confirmed against available capacity. A three-month initial deployment is feasible only if its hardware and facility resources are already available within that window; it cannot be inferred from a longer procurement lead time.

#### Capacity Lead Time as Operational Risk

^sec-ml-operations-scale-procurement

The economics of ML infrastructure are not purely about hardware specifications and electricity rates. Capacity lead time is itself an operational risk: a platform that cannot acquire, reserve, or free capacity quickly enough may miss model launches, delay rollback-safe migrations, or run without the headroom needed for incident response.

The GPU supply chain adds a correlated risk, because processors, memory, packaging, and network equipment that must arrive together can depend on the same upstream capacity, so orders placed with several vendors do not by themselves establish independent supply.

Capacity commitments can take much longer to change than model traffic, which can shift within days of a launch or product change. For planning, consider a large deployment (1,000+ GPUs) with a 6–12-month interval from purchase decision to usable capacity; the actual interval requires supplier and facility confirmation. That delay changes operations. The platform must forecast model growth, reserve refresh capacity, and maintain fallbacks before demand arrives rather than after dashboards turn red.

Facility readiness can lag silicon delivery. In a planning scenario, substation, building-shell, and facility schedules of 12–30 months run concurrently with 6–12-month GPU deliveries, and the longest prerequisite sets the critical path. Site power, data-residency rules, and connection queues can also rule out an otherwise economical location before any accelerator is purchased. The physical design of power delivery and cooling belongs to **[[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/01 The Fleet/02 Compute Infrastructure/V2-02.07 The Rack#^sec-compute-power-wall|Power and thermal envelopes]]**; the operational point is that its schedule becomes the fleet’s schedule.

Mismatched schedules are expensive in both directions. Hardware delivered before the facility is ready ties up capital without producing useful work, as does a completed facility waiting for accelerators. Phased deployment can reduce the mismatch by commissioning usable capacity in waves while construction continues. An early phase lets the software team validate the distributed-training stack and tune collective kernels on the deployed topology while operators test power distribution and cooling; success at that scale does not establish readiness at full load. The economic value of making useful capacity available earlier can justify premiums for modular halls or temporary colocation when the expected benefit exceeds their additional cost. Projected monthly model value is an input to that comparison, not a guaranteed loss for every month of delay: the estimate must account for launch readiness, demand, operating costs, and whether delayed value can be recovered later.

Hardware access becomes part of the deployment schedule for the 175B model as well. An illustrative 1,024-H100 allocation represents approximately \$45 million in node hardware under the \$350,000-per-8-GPU-node assumption established in [[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/03 The Fleet in Production/12 ML Operations at Scale/V2-12.10 Fleet Economics and Utilization#^sec-ml-operations-scale-build-vs-buy|section 12.9.2]], but the operational risk extends beyond the purchase price alone. If the allocation arrives a quarter late, the platform may lack capacity for fine-tuning, shadow traffic, canary expansion, or rollback-safe serving during the model launch window. Capacity planning is therefore a reliability control rather than a procurement function alone.

### ML Systems TCO Framework

^sec-ml-operations-scale-ml-systems-tco-framework-b980

A budget that prices only visible GPU compute and object storage misses the engineering labor, pipeline maintenance, continuous retraining, monitoring, and incident response that routinely make up the other half of total cost. Fleet cost accounting prices physical capacity, but deciding which workloads deserve to consume that capacity requires a TCO framework covering the full ML lifecycle alongside the value each workload creates. The lifecycle definition of TCO that opened the fleet analysis (definition [12.1](https://mlsysbook.ai/vol2/12_ops_scale/12_ops_scale.html#dfn-ops-scale-ml-systems-tco)) now applies workload by workload, widening the hardware lens to training and inference costs, with data and iteration completing a four-component model. Their relative weights depend on workload, traffic, retraining cadence, and operating requirements rather than a fixed distinction between ML and traditional software.

#### The TCO Equation

^sec-ml-operations-scale-tco-equation-169d

[[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/03 The Fleet in Production/12 ML Operations at Scale/V2-12.10 Fleet Economics and Utilization#^eq-tco-ml|Equation 12]] expresses the total cost of ownership as the sum of four distinct cost components, each with different scaling characteristics and optimization levers:

$$
\text{TCO}_{\text{ML}} = {C_{\text{train}}} + {C_{\text{infer}}} + {C_{\text{total}}} + {C_{\text{iter}}} \tag{12}
$$

^eq-tco-ml

Here, ${C_{\text{train}}}$ is the initial training and evaluation cost, ${C_{\text{infer}}}$ is recurring serving cost, ${C_{\text{total}}}$ covers data acquisition, storage, cleaning, and governance, and ${C_{\text{iter}}}$ captures subsequent experimentation and maintenance. The categories require a common accounting horizon and an explicit allocation rule. If retraining compute is charged to ${C_{\text{train}}}$, it must be excluded from ${C_{\text{iter}}}$; shared hardware depreciation and energy likewise enter these workload costs once rather than being added again as a separate fleet bill.

In the illustrative budget of [[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/03 The Fleet in Production/12 ML Operations at Scale/V2-12.10 Fleet Economics and Utilization#^fig-tco-iceberg|figure 14]], engineering labor (20 percent), pipeline maintenance (10 percent), continuous retraining, monitoring, incident response, and governance together equal the visible infrastructure spend. The example shows what an incomplete budget can miss; its proportions are not universal cost shares. Each organization must measure these obligations within the same accounting horizon as its infrastructure costs.

![[90 Assets/Volume II/12 Ops Scale/Cell 56 Output 1 82749a680e.png]]

Figure 14: **The TCO Iceberg**: The analysis separates visible infrastructure costs from operational costs that are easier to omit. In this illustrative budget, the hidden operational costs (engineering labor, pipeline maintenance, retraining, monitoring, incident response, and compliance) together constitute half of total cost.

^fig-tco-iceberg

The decomposition shows why optimization must follow the measured cost structure. An effort centered on experimentation may spend most on ${C_{\text{iter}}}$, a training campaign on ${C_{\text{train}}}$, and a high-traffic deployment on ${C_{\text{infer}}}$. These are workload regimes, not mandatory stages of organizational maturity. The cost breakdown identifies which component merits attention under the current workload mix.

#### Training Cost Model

^sec-ml-operations-scale-training-cost-model-9e15

Training cost begins with the price of the GPU-hour. A 30-day run on a 256-node H100 cluster (2,048 GPUs) at the internal fleet rate of \$2/GPU-hour is a direct investment of roughly \$2.95M. At this scale the cost per training run $({C_{\text{train}}})$ becomes a design lever that sizes the fleet. Faster time-to-market pushes the planner toward more GPUs, but larger jobs also suffer diminishing parallel efficiency and a higher probability of hardware interruption. Total cost is therefore not merely a function of compute time; it must account for data staging, checkpointing, and the cost of recovery from interruptions.

Organizations must decide when to buy additional permanent capacity and when to recover useful throughput through better orchestration or model optimization. The decision also has an energy dimension. Less electricity consumption reduces the energy bill at a fixed tariff, but emissions depend on the energy source and time of use; a lower price alone does not establish lower carbon impact. The cost model below accounts for purchased energy, while an emissions comparison requires the corresponding carbon intensity.

[[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/03 The Fleet in Production/12 ML Operations at Scale/V2-12.10 Fleet Economics and Utilization#^eq-training-cost|Equation 13]] formalizes training cost as a function of GPU count, training duration, non-energy accelerator-hour rates, data center energy overhead, and failure overhead:

$$
{C_{\text{train}}} = {N_{\text{GPU}}} \times {T_{\text{hours}}}\times \left({R_{\text{GPU/hr}}}+ {P_{\text{device}}} \times {R_{\$/\text{kWh}}}\times \text{PUE}\right) \times (1 + {F_{\text{fail}}}) \tag{13}
$$

^eq-training-cost

where:

- ${N_{\text{GPU}}}$ is the number of GPUs allocated
- ${T_{\text{hours}}}$ is the baseline training duration in hours before the additional failure overhead
- ${R_{\text{GPU/hr}}}$ is the non-energy accelerator-hour cost
- ${P_{\text{device}}}$ is per-GPU power draw in kW
- ${R_{\$/\text{kWh}}}$ is the electricity price
- $\text{PUE}$ is the power usage effectiveness multiplier applied to the energy term
- ${F_{\text{fail}}}$ is additional time from failures and recovery as a fraction of the baseline duration

The PUE factor captures facility energy overhead, the facility metric of **[[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/04 The Responsible Fleet/15 Sustainable AI/V2-15.05 Energy Measurement and Modeling#^sec-sustainable-ai-facilitylevel-power-metrics-5558|Facility-level power metrics]]**. A PUE of 1.1 means 10 percent additional energy relative to IT energy. It applies to energy consumption, not to the whole accelerator-hour rate. This equation uses GPU power as a simplified IT boundary; a full facility estimate must also include host, network, and storage power. The failure overhead ${F_{\text{fail}}}$ adds recovery time beyond the baseline. Checkpointing or restart time already included in measured baseline duration must not be charged again through this factor.

↩︎ Cluster mean time between failures and the checkpoint interval it implies are established in **[[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/02 Distributed ML/07 Fault Tolerance/V2-07.02 Failure Analysis at Scale#^sec-fault-tolerance-reliability-reliability-failure-analysis-scale-6b4b|Failure Analysis at Scale]]**.

#### Training Cost Calculation

^sec-ml-operations-scale-worked-example-training-cost-calculation-6535

Consider training a large language model:

- Configuration: 256 H100 GPUs for 14 days
- Base non-energy accelerator-hour rate (scenario choice): \$3.50/GPU-hour
- Energy adder: 700 W at \$0.07/kWh and PUE 1.1 adds \$0.05/GPU-hour
- Effective rate: \$3.55/GPU-hour
- Failure overhead: 10 percent (scenario assumption relative to baseline duration)

${C_{\text{train}}} = 256 \times (14 \times 24) \times (\$3.50 + \$0.05) \times 1.1 \approx \$335,892$

If this model requires quarterly retraining, annual training cost reaches approximately \$1.3M. However, training cost often represents a small fraction of total TCO for production systems serving millions of users.

#### Inference Cost Model

^sec-ml-operations-scale-inference-cost-model-63ed

![[90 Assets/Volume II/12 Ops Scale/Ops Scale Tco Dominance e6509e155d.svg]]

*Horizontal bar split into four segments with illustrative widths, labeled Train, Infer, Data, and Iter. Infer is the widest segment and is orange; the others are gray, and Train is the narrowest.*

*Serving can outgrow the training bill.*

Inference costs can dominate when cumulative serving demand outweighs training and iteration costs. [[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/03 The Fleet in Production/12 ML Operations at Scale/V2-12.10 Fleet Economics and Utilization#^eq-ops-scale-inference-cost|Equation 14]] estimates continuously provisioned capacity from average daily demand and sustained per-GPU throughput measured under representative batching, the target latency SLO, and operating headroom. Daily volume alone does not size traffic peaks; the estimate requires a separate check against the arrival pattern, workload mix, and scaling response:

$$
{N_{\text{GPU}}} = \left\lceil \frac{{Q_{\text{day}}}}{86{,}400 \times {R_{\text{GPU,target}}}} \right\rceil, \qquad {C_{\text{infer}}} = {N_{\text{GPU}}} \times 24 \times 365 \times {R_{\text{GPU/hr}}} \tag{14}
$$

^eq-ops-scale-inference-cost

where:

- ${Q_{\text{day}}}$ is the daily query volume
- ${R_{\text{GPU,target}}}$ is sustained queries per second per GPU at the target latency SLO, including batching and operating headroom
- ${N_{\text{GPU}}}$ is the integer number of continuously provisioned GPUs
- ${R_{\text{GPU/hr}}}$ is the hourly GPU cost

Inference cost therefore grows with query volume in discrete capacity steps. Utilization and batching reduce cost only when they improve measured throughput at the target latency SLO; they should not be applied again as independent divisors.

For capacity planning, the same continuous-provisioning bill can be expressed in terms of allocated GPU-seconds per query:

$$
{C_{\text{infer}}} = {Q_{\text{annual}}}\times {S_{\text{GPU/query}}}\times \frac{{R_{\text{GPU/hr}}}}{3600}
$$

where ${S_{\text{GPU/query}}} = ({N_{\text{GPU}}} \times 86{,}400) / {Q_{\text{day}}}$ divides the total GPU-seconds provisioned across the day by the completed queries over the same accounting period. Because the numerator counts every provisioned GPU-second, including off-peak traffic troughs and latency-SLO headroom, substituting ${Q_{\text{annual}}} = 365 \times {Q_{\text{day}}}$ into this expression reproduces the continuous-provisioning bill in [[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/03 The Fleet in Production/12 ML Operations at Scale/V2-12.10 Fleet Economics and Utilization#^eq-ops-scale-inference-cost|equation 14]]. Plugging in profiler-measured active kernel time per query instead measures raw device work and excludes idle headroom and diurnal overprovisioning, which substantially understates the serving bill unless a multi-tenant scheduler bills only for active execution slices.

#### Data Cost Model

^sec-ml-operations-scale-data-cost-model-c51a

Data costs can grow faster than the user base when per-user history, transfer volume, and feature processing all rise together, even though each term below is linear in its own volume. [[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/03 The Fleet in Production/12 ML Operations at Scale/V2-12.10 Fleet Economics and Utilization#^eq-data-cost|Equation 15]] decomposes data cost into three components that scale differently: storage cost ${C_{\text{store}}}$, egress cost ${C_{\text{egress}}}$ for moving data out over the network, and processing cost ${C_{\text{process}}}$:

$$
{C_{\text{total}}} = {C_{\text{store}}} + {C_{\text{egress}}}+ {C_{\text{process}}} \tag{15}
$$

^eq-data-cost

Storage costs grow with data retention requirements:

$$
{C_{\text{store}}} = {D_{\text{vol,store}}} \times {c_{\text{storage}}} \times {T_{\text{months}}}
$$

where ${D_{\text{vol,store}}}$ is a fixed data volume in GB, ${c_{\text{storage}}}$ is the monthly storage rate, and ${T_{\text{months}}}$ is its retention period in months. This product prices one volume retained at a constant rate; a growing dataset requires accounting for each period’s stored volume rather than multiplying the final volume by the entire history.

Egress costs scale with data transfer volume:

$$
{C_{\text{egress}}}= {D_{\text{vol,transfer}}}\times {R_{\text{egress/GB}}}
$$

At the scenario egress rate of \$0.09/GB, repeated transfers can become a significant cost driver for multi-region deployments and training data distribution. The applicable rate depends on the source, destination, and service contract; the estimate must distinguish billable transfers from included traffic.

Processing costs scale with compute requirements for extract, transform, load, feature engineering, and data validation:

$$
{C_{\text{process}}}= {D_{\text{vol,processed}}}\times {R_{\text{process/GB}}}
$$

Data processing costs often surprise organizations: a feature engineering pipeline processing 10 TB daily at \$0.02/GB costs \$73,000 annually.

#### Iteration Cost Model

^sec-ml-operations-scale-iteration-cost-model-50a9

Development costs capture the engineering investment in experimentation and model improvement. [[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/03 The Fleet in Production/12 ML Operations at Scale/V2-12.10 Fleet Economics and Utilization#^eq-iteration-cost|Equation 16]] formalizes this as the experiment count multiplied by the combined per-experiment engineering and compute costs:

$$
{C_{\text{iter}}}= {N_{\text{exp}}}\times ({C_{\text{engineer,per-exp}}}+ {C_{\text{compute,per-exp}}}) \tag{16}
$$

^eq-iteration-cost

where:

- ${N_{\text{exp}}}$ is the number of experiments conducted
- ${C_{\text{engineer,per-exp}}}$ is the engineering cost per experiment
- ${C_{\text{compute,per-exp}}}$ is the compute cost per experiment

Experiments that do not improve production metrics still incur costs. If 90 percent produce no improvement and every experiment has the same cost, expenditure per improving experiment is 10$\times$ the per-experiment cost. A negative result can nevertheless rule out an unproductive direction or expose a risk. Experiment infrastructure should reduce avoidable engineering and compute work while preserving that learning value, rather than optimize the fraction of positive results alone.

#### TCO Sensitivity Analysis

^sec-ml-operations-scale-tco-sensitivity-analysis-01e8

Sensitivity analysis starts with the dollar cost breakdown and changes one workload assumption at a time. Each change touches only some cost terms, and the platform must update specific measurements before it can estimate a new total. More daily users, for example, requires new figures for requests per user, peak concurrency, retained history, and SLO headroom ([[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/03 The Fleet in Production/12 ML Operations at Scale/V2-12.10 Fleet Economics and Utilization#^tbl-tco-sensitivity|table 32]]). The same increase in user count can have different consequences for a cached recommendation service and a long-context language-model service.

| **Workload change**          | **Cost terms affected**                       | **Measurements needed**                                                                             |
|:-----------------------------|:----------------------------------------------|:----------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------|
| **More daily users**         | Inference, data, and capacity operations      | Requests per user, peak concurrency, history retained per user, and headroom needed to meet the SLO |
| **More models**              | Training, inference, data, and iteration      | Refresh cadence, traffic per model, shared feature reuse, and engineering effort per release        |
| **More queries per user**    | Inference and request-related data processing | Request mix, batching, cache hit rate, bytes logged, and provisioned capacity at the target SLO     |
| **Larger models**            | Training, inference, and iteration            | Live-state memory, sustained throughput, parallelism, and time spent validating each candidate      |
| **More frequent retraining** | Training, data preparation, and iteration     | Cost per run, incremental data processing, evaluation effort, and any measured serving benefit      |

Table 32: **TCO Sensitivity Drivers**: Each workload change maps to the cost terms it affects and the measurements needed to update them. Shared infrastructure, capacity steps, and changing engineering effort can make the response nonlinear; the complete cost breakdown determines the total.

^tbl-tco-sensitivity

User scaling changes requests and stored history; model size scaling changes arithmetic and memory demand. The analysis recomputes those components in dollars over the same period, retaining unchanged components and including iteration and platform overhead, then sums the revised components and compares the total with the baseline. The resulting change identifies where an optimization can create enough recurring savings to repay its implementation cost.

#### Decision Framework for Speed Versus Efficiency

^sec-ml-operations-scale-decision-framework-speed-vs-efficiency-baf4

TCO analysis enables principled decisions about when to optimize for development speed vs. operational efficiency. [[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/03 The Fleet in Production/12 ML Operations at Scale/V2-12.10 Fleet Economics and Utilization#^eq-optimization-breakeven|Equation 17]] calculates the breakeven point for optimization investments:

$$
{T_{\text{breakeven}}}= \frac{{C_{\text{optimization}}}}{{C_{\text{save,monthly}}}} \tag{17}
$$

^eq-optimization-breakeven

where ${C_{\text{optimization}}}$ is the one-time implementation and validation cost and ${C_{\text{save,monthly}}}$ is positive monthly savings net of added operating and maintenance costs. This simple payback assumes savings remain constant over the interval; recovered capacity counts as cash savings only when it reduces expenditure or avoids a planned purchase.

The breakeven equation helps compare specific investments once their implementation costs and recurring savings are estimated. The dominant cost component suggests where to look first, but does not rule out a valuable smaller intervention elsewhere. [[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/03 The Fleet in Production/12 ML Operations at Scale/V2-12.10 Fleet Economics and Utilization#^tbl-tco-cost-structure-decisions|Table 33]] offers investigation starting points and illustrative policy thresholds; actual investment criteria also depend on service risk, expected workload lifetime, and available engineering capacity.

| **Dominant cost structure**                                   | **Operating context**       | **Optimization posture and risk to evaluate**                                                                                                                                                     | **Payback threshold**                                                                              |
|:--------------------------------------------------------------|:----------------------------|:--------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------|:---------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------|
| Iteration costs dominate (${C_{\text{iter}}} > 50\%$ of TCO)  | Experiment-heavy workload   | Investigate experiment infrastructure and developer tooling that shorten iteration time. Risk: engineering effort that does not repay through faster iteration or realized savings.               | Evaluate candidate value; iteration cost share alone does not set a payback bar.                   |
| Training costs dominate (${C_{\text{train}}} > 40\%$ of TCO)  | Repeated training campaigns | Investigate mixed precision, gradient checkpointing, checkpoint-and-resume on spot instances, and architecture changes. | Inference costs dominate (${C_{\text{infer}}} > 40\%$ of TCO) | High-volume serving         | Investigate quantization, batching, caching, and distillation where repeated queries can realize savings. A reusable training pipeline may support a longer horizon, while a one-off training campaign may not. For either workload, the comparison sets net savings over the candidate’s expected useful lifetime against implementation cost and risk; a dominant cost category does not guarantee that its next optimization has the highest value.

#### Optimization Investment Decisions

^sec-ml-operations-scale-worked-example-optimization-investment-decision-cc81

A production company with an inference bill of \$400K/month evaluates INT8 quantization:

- Implementation cost: \$80,000 (engineering time + validation)
- Expected inference cost reduction: 40 percent
- Monthly savings: \$400,000 $\times$ 0.40 = \$160,000

${T_{\text{breakeven}}} = \frac{\$80,000}{\$160,000/month} = 0.5 \text{ months}$

Breakeven in about 2 weeks makes this investment attractive if validation confirms acceptable quality and latency and the expected cost reduction can be realized. For comparison, the same company, with a training bill of \$150K/month, evaluates a training optimization:

- Implementation cost: \$80,000
- Expected training cost reduction: 30 percent
- Monthly savings: \$150,000 $\times$ 0.30 = \$45,000

${T_{\text{breakeven}}} = \frac{\$80,000}{\$45,000/month} = 1.8 \text{ months}$

The training optimization has longer payback and smaller absolute savings in this scenario. Those results favor the inference investment if both satisfy their quality and reliability requirements and compete for the same engineering capacity.

#### TCO-Driven Architecture Decisions

^sec-ml-operations-scale-tcodriven-architecture-decisions-2fea

TCO analysis should inform architectural choices alongside operational optimization. The build-vs.-buy analysis in [[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/03 The Fleet in Production/12 ML Operations at Scale/V2-12.10 Fleet Economics and Utilization#^sec-ml-operations-scale-build-vs-buy|section 12.9.2]] also applies to individual workloads, accounting for any capacity they share with the rest of the portfolio. Model architecture selection changes the cost breakdown because a model requiring 2$\times$ training cost but 0.5$\times$ inference cost may have lower lifecycle cost at sufficient serving volume. Retraining frequency adds to ${C_{\text{train}}}$ and ${C_{\text{total}}}$; it can reduce emergency work charged to ${C_{\text{iter}}}$ only when evaluation and release controls produce timely, useful updates. Monitoring detects the need to investigate drift; retraining alone does not. Feature complexity can likewise increase ${C_{\text{total}}}$ and ${C_{\text{train}}}$, while its effect on ${C_{\text{iter}}}$ depends on whether better predictions outweigh added integration and maintenance. Knowledge placement in language-model services illustrates the same trade-off.

#### Retrieval-Augmented Generation Vs. Fine-Tuning

^sec-ml-operations-scale-rag-vs-finetuning-knowledge-operations-tradeoff-77f3

Domain knowledge can live in a retrievable index, in model weights, or in adapter weights, and each placement creates a different operating cost surface. **Retrieval-augmented generation (RAG)** retrieves documents at inference time and places them in the prompt ([[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/03 The Fleet in Production/12 ML Operations at Scale/V2-12.16 Source References#^ref-lewis2020retrieval|Lewis et al. 2020]]). Fine-tuning stores adaptation in model weights or adapter weights: supervised fine-tuning trains on curated input-output examples, while low-rank adaptation (LoRA) stores small adapter-weight deltas ([[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/03 The Fleet in Production/12 ML Operations at Scale/V2-12.16 Source References#^ref-hu2021lora|Hu et al. 2021]]). Both approaches can improve task behavior, but they move cost and failure risk to different terms of [[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/03 The Fleet in Production/12 ML Operations at Scale/V2-12.10 Fleet Economics and Utilization#^eq-tco-ml|equation 12]]. RAG charges knowledge to ${C_{\text{infer}}}$, through retrieval and the extra prompt tokens every request must prefill, and to ${C_{\text{total}}}$, through the index it must build and refresh. Fine-tuning charges it to ${C_{\text{train}}}$ and ${C_{\text{iter}}}$, through curation, training runs, and re-evaluation whenever the knowledge changes. Query volume therefore decides which cost grows faster.

RAG makes knowledge updates possible through the document index without retraining the model. Retrieved passages provide evidence against which generated claims can be checked, but retrieval alone does not ensure that an answer uses or cites that evidence correctly. Retrieved context also consumes serving resources, through prefill work that grows faster than linearly with sequence length and KV-cache memory that grows with it (**[[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/03 The Fleet in Production/10 Serving at Scale/V2-10.08 Memory and Decode-Time Management#^sec-inference-scale-kv-cache-wall|The KV cache wall and memory capacity]]**). The cost comparison therefore depends on retrieval quality, context selection, and the serving configuration; excessive context can also make relevant evidence harder for the model to use.

Fine-tuning moves adaptation work before deployment. The platform pays for data curation, training, and evaluation; successful adaptation can shorten prompts when it replaces instructions or examples that would otherwise accompany each request. Lower serving cost is a benefit to measure, not an automatic consequence of changing weights. Adapter-based fine-tuning also creates a fleet-management obligation when many customer-specific LoRA adapters must be versioned, loaded, and isolated, while changing facts embedded in weights requires a different update process from replacing documents in an index.

The decision depends on knowledge volatility, attribution requirements, latency budget, and adapter-management complexity. RAG provides an update path for changing facts and a source trail for review, subject to retrieval and generation quality. Fine-tuning can adapt domain language or stable behavior, subject to training cost and validation. Neither method implies a fixed context length or query-volume limit. A combined design can fine-tune how the model uses tools and documents while retrieving facts that must remain fresh, and evaluation must establish whether those benefits justify the combined lifecycle cost. Because each of these architecture choices is a forecast of lifecycle cost, once a choice ships the fleet needs controls that measure whether spending follows the forecast and give someone the authority to act when it does not.

### FinOps for ML Platforms

^sec-ml-operations-scale-finops-ml-platforms-ffd6

A cost dashboard can identify a failed training sweep without giving anyone authority to stop it. FinOps closes that gap by connecting fleet cost accounting and anomaly signals to budgets, owners, and intervention policies. The platform, fleet, and workload analyses above decide whether capacity and platform investment are worthwhile; FinOps decides how teams spend that capacity and respond when cost or value departs from the plan.

**Definition 12.2: FinOps for ML**

***FinOps for ML*** is the practice of treating compute cost as a first-class engineering constraint (measured in real-time per experiment and model, and optimized jointly with model accuracy and latency) rather than accounting for it retrospectively through annual budget reconciliation.

1.  **Significance**: ML compute costs scale steeply with experimentation volume. A team running 1,000 GPU-hours/day at the fleet reference rate of \$2/GPU-hour spends \$2,000/day (\$730K/year) on training alone. With per-experiment cost visibility, an engineer can identify that 30 percent of runs are terminated early due to divergence (a reason to investigate the training configuration and failure causes) and that the team’s fault-tolerant jobs could run on spot capacity at 60–90 percent discounts, provided recovery and interruption costs preserve the required quality and completion target.
2.  **Distinction**: Unlike traditional IT budgeting (which allocates fixed annual compute budgets across departments), FinOps for ML operates at the per-run and per-model level with real-time feedback, enabling decisions like early stopping a \$50K training run at step 1,000 when loss curves signal divergence, rather than discovering the waste after the full run completes.
3.  **Common pitfall**: A frequent misconception is that FinOps means minimizing compute spend. The goal is useful value across the full lifecycle within quality, latency, and reliability requirements: a \$500K training run producing a model serving 10B inferences at \$0.0001/query may be far more cost-efficient than a \$50K run producing a model that requires 10$\times$ the inference compute to achieve the same accuracy at scale.

#### Cost Visibility and Attribution

^sec-ml-operations-scale-cost-visibility-attribution-3f3a

Cost optimization requires visibility into spending and ownership. Attribution policy also shapes incentives. Direct metering charges for measured consumption, but shared services and idle reserves still require allocation rules; measured activity does not uniquely determine every team’s cost. Charging only for consumption can encourage under-provisioning when teams optimize their bill instead of service health. Allocation-based attribution charges for reserved capacity, making commitments visible even when that capacity is idle. Hybrid attribution combines a base allocation charge with a variable charge for excess usage. Each policy must preserve the headroom and shared services on which the reliability contract depends. Attribution itself starts from metadata. Tag-based allocation assigns each resource’s cost to a team, service, environment, and cost center, as [[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/03 The Fleet in Production/12 ML Operations at Scale/V2-12.10 Fleet Economics and Utilization#^lst-cost-attribution|listing 7]] shows.

``` yaml
# Resource tagging schema for cost attribution
cost_allocation:
  dimensions:
    - team: "recommendation"      # Organizational owner
    - service: "ranking-model"    # Specific service
    - environment: "production"   # prod/staging/dev
    - model_type: "inference"     # training/inference
    - cost_center: "CC-4521"      # Finance tracking

  shared_cost_distribution:
    # Platform infrastructure costs distributed by usage
    - resource: "shared-gpu-cluster"
      method: "proportional_gpu_hours"
    - resource: "feature-store"
      method: "proportional_query_volume"
    - resource: "monitoring-infrastructure"
      method: "equal_split"
```

Listing 7: **Cost Attribution Schema**: Resource tagging dimensions and shared-cost distribution policies for allocating ML infrastructure expenses to teams and services.

^lst-cost-attribution

Shared infrastructure needs its own accounting policy because no single team owns it. Platforms typically adopt one of three models. Proportional allocation distributes shared cluster expenses according to measured consumption metrics such as GPU-hours, storage capacity, or API query volume. Equal split divides common baseline expenses evenly across consuming teams, which is well suited for fixed foundational tooling. Marginal cost charges tenant teams strictly for the incremental compute and storage their workloads introduce. The choice of allocation policy ultimately determines whether operational dashboards can drive organizational action rather than merely describing past spend.

For serving workloads, **cost per inference** provides a unit economic metric. [[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/03 The Fleet in Production/12 ML Operations at Scale/V2-12.10 Fleet Economics and Utilization#^eq-cost-per-inference|Equation 18]] divides total serving cost by inference count over the same accounting period. Comparisons require matched request mix, quality and latency targets, and cost boundaries; a short classification request and a long generated response are not equivalent units of work:

$$
\text{Cost per inference} = \frac{\text{Total serving cost}}{\text{Total inferences served}} \tag{18}
$$

^eq-cost-per-inference

Cost per inference then becomes the bridge from infrastructure telemetry to product decisions. Teams can compare model versions by asking whether an accuracy gain justifies a 2$\times$ inference cost, evaluate optimization work by measuring whether quantization reduced cost per inference by 40 percent, and forecast monthly serving cost under projected traffic. Tracking the metric by model, customer segment, and request type reveals which workload deserves optimization effort.

Cost dashboards reuse the attention-routing hierarchy of the reliability views in [[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/03 The Fleet in Production/12 ML Operations at Scale/V2-12.07 Monitoring at Scale#^tbl-ops-scale-dashboard-views|table 21]]. At the top of the hierarchy, the executive view tracks total ML infrastructure cost, its month-over-month trend, budget against actual, and efficiency metrics such as cost per prediction and cost per active user. When that top-level spend shifts, the portfolio view breaks cost down by domain and model type so the platform team can see which domain is driving the change. The service view exposes per-service cost, cost per inference, GPU utilization efficiency, and budget comparison for the responsible team. Finally, the investigation view adds deployment markers and attribution detail so responders can tie an anomaly to a specific change rather than dismissing it as accounting noise.

Chargeback becomes actionable only when fine-grained metering, the attribution rules above, budget forecasts, and anomaly detection feed continuous cost monitoring across four complementary signals. Cost per inference tracks unit spending under a declared request mix, whereas cost per active user relates spending to the population served, though cross-service comparisons still require comparable activity and product scope. To connect those unit metrics to hardware and calendar limits, GPU utilization efficiency measures how allocated accelerators are used (distinct from revenue or engagement per accelerator-hour, which also depends on product demand and model quality). Budget burn rate tracks spending velocity against the allocation so owners can investigate projected overruns before the budget is exhausted.

#### Cost Anomaly Detection

^sec-ml-operations-scale-cost-monitoring-anomaly-detection-1976

Cost anomaly detection must account for the fleet’s allocation and lifecycle behavior because several distinct operational events can masquerade as a simple spending spike. Adding a serving replica creates an immediate cost step when that replica occupies multiple accelerators, whereas training sweeps and repeated recovery attempts accumulate GPU-hours gradually without producing useful artifacts. Zombie models ([[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/03 The Fleet in Production/12 ML Operations at Scale/V2-12.04 Governance of State and Change#^sec-ml-operations-scale-model-lifecycle-management-f112|section 12.3.3]]) hold onto serving capacity long after their traffic has disappeared. Because traffic spikes, shifting request mixes, and cloud price changes can also raise the bill, detection must connect any cost change to allocation, workload, and lifecycle evidence rather than inferring a cause from dollar magnitude alone. An undetected anomaly keeps accruing charges until someone reviews the bill, so cost anomaly detection must balance sensitivity against false positive rates as deliberately as reliability alerting does.

The foundation of cost monitoring is the statistical process control of [[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/03 The Fleet in Production/12 ML Operations at Scale/V2-12.07 Monitoring at Scale#^sec-ml-operations-scale-statistical-process-control-c95d|section 12.6.4.1]] applied to spend. For a cost time series with historical mean ${\mu}$ and standard deviation ${\sigma}$, the Z-score quantifies how unusual a current observation is:

$$
{Z}= \frac{{C_{\text{current}}}- {\mu}}{{\sigma}}
$$

where ${C_{\text{current}}}$ is the observed cost for the current period.

Complementing Z-score analysis, percentage change detection captures sudden shifts regardless of historical variance, which makes it useful for catching step-function increases such as a misconfigured autoscaler that doubles instance count overnight:

$$
{\Delta}\% = \frac{{C_{\text{current}}}- {C_{\text{previous}}}}{{C_{\text{previous}}}} \times 100
$$

Effective alerting requires calibrating thresholds to balance detection sensitivity against operational burden. Two illustrative configurations are a Z-score threshold, which alerts when $|{Z}| > 3$, and a percentage change threshold, which alerts when $|{\Delta}\%| > 50\%$ day over day. Seasonal demand and planned jobs must be represented in the baseline before ordinary variation can be interpreted as unusual spending. Under the calibrated Gaussian null of the statistical-process-control rule, a daily $|{Z}| > 3$ check raises about one raw false alert per year and a 2-sigma check about 16, and [[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/03 The Fleet in Production/12 ML Operations at Scale/V2-12.07 Monitoring at Scale#^sec-ml-operations-scale-alert-fatigue-problem-038c|section 12.6.2]] shows how such crossings compound across many monitored series. Choosing between thresholds requires measuring detection power and the cost of missed events as well as alert burden.

For percentage-based alerts, false positive rates depend on the underlying volatility of costs. Services with naturally variable demand may require higher thresholds (75 percent or 100 percent) to avoid excessive alerts, while stable baseline services can use tighter thresholds (25 percent or 30 percent).

A threshold crossing identifies an unusual observation under the monitoring baseline, not a confirmed defect or cause. [[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/03 The Fleet in Production/12 ML Operations at Scale/V2-12.10 Fleet Economics and Utilization#^tbl-cost-anomaly-categories|Table 35]] groups candidate cost anomaly root causes into five classes, each with a distinct investigation path.

| **Category**              | **Indicators**                          | **Investigation path**                                              |
|:--------------------------|:----------------------------------------|:--------------------------------------------------------------------|
| **Traffic increase**      | QPS proportional to cost                | Check upstream services, marketing campaigns, viral events          |
| **Efficiency regression** | Cost up, QPS unchanged, latency up      | Review recent deployments, model updates, infrastructure changes    |
| **Resource leak**         | Gradual cost growth, utilization stable | Check for orphaned resources, failed cleanup jobs, zombie processes |
| **Pricing change**        | Cost up, all metrics stable             | Verify cloud provider pricing, reserved instance expiration         |
| **Configuration error**   | Step-function cost increase             | Audit autoscaling rules, instance types, replica counts             |

Table 35: **Cost Anomaly Root Cause Categories**: Five primary categories of cost anomalies with their characteristic indicators and investigation approaches. Traffic increases show proportional QPS growth, while efficiency regressions pair rising latency with stable traffic.

^tbl-cost-anomaly-categories

#### Detecting an Inference Cost Spike

^sec-ml-operations-scale-worked-example-detecting-inference-cost-spike-9069

A recommendation service typically costs \$100 per day for inference compute. The operations team receives an alert: today’s cost has reached \$250 by end of day.

Historical data shows mean daily cost ${\mu}= \$100$ with standard deviation ${\sigma}= \$15$, so the first check is whether the alert is statistically plausible under normal operations: ${Z} = \frac{\$250 - \$100}{\$15} = \frac{\$150}{\$15} = 10$. A Z-score of 10 is far outside the assumed Gaussian baseline, but the team first verifies billing completeness, duplicate records, and pipeline correctness. Query volume is unchanged; the investigation must also check request mix and arrival peaks before ruling out demand. In this scenario, those remain comparable while p99 latency rises from 50 ms to 125 ms. A larger ranking model deployed at 2:00 AM adds features, GPU utilization stays near 95 percent, and measured throughput per GPU falls 60 percent. At matched demand and operating targets, that throughput loss implies 2.5× the capacity requirement. The team confirms the allocation increase and unchanged rates before attributing the rise from \$100/day to \$250/day to the release. It then evaluates whether the measured quality benefit justifies the cost or calls for a leaner serving configuration.

#### Cost Optimization Strategies

^sec-ml-operations-scale-cost-optimization-strategies-d1c8

Cost optimization is a risk-allocation decision, not a catalog of discounts. Interruptible compute pays off only when checkpointing bounds the work lost to preemption, serving autoscaling only within the latency budget, and instance right-sizing only against measured cost per step or per inference. Each optimization lever trades a specific risk for a specific saving.

Interruptible training capacity and its checkpoint discipline were priced in [[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/03 The Fleet in Production/12 ML Operations at Scale/V2-12.10 Fleet Economics and Utilization#^sec-ml-operations-scale-cloud-options|section 12.9.4]]. Serving allocates risk differently. Model load time, lumpy accelerator capacity steps, and the batching trade-off between throughput and latency budget all constrain how far autoscaling can cut serving cost, and **[[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/03 The Fleet in Production/10 Serving at Scale/V2-10.14 Autoscaling and Capacity Control#^sec-inference-scale-autoscaling-capacity-control|Autoscaling and Capacity Control]]** develops those controllers. FinOps asks a narrower question, whether the warm spare capacity and fallback paths that absorb demand while new replicas start are priced as deliberate headroom rather than flagged as waste.

Instance selection then starts from the binding constraint rather than the newest instance type. Memory-bound training with large embedding tables prioritizes GPU memory capacity and bandwidth, and compute-bound dense training maximizes sustained FLOP/s per dollar. Latency-sensitive serving minimizes cold start and single-request latency, while throughput-oriented serving maximizes requests per dollar through batching. Selection is therefore benchmark-driven, comparing cost per training step or cost per inference across instance types instead of assuming larger hardware is automatically more economical.

#### Budget-Aware Development

^sec-ml-operations-scale-budgetaware-development-5ab2

FinOps extends beyond infrastructure optimization because cost feedback changes which experiments teams run and which models they ship. Unconstrained experimentation leads to runaway costs, but budget controls should make trade-offs visible before they block useful work. Per-experiment limits cap individual training runs at a cost threshold, team budgets allocate monthly compute budgets with visibility into consumption, and approval workflows require review for experiments that exceed cost thresholds. Controls should make the budget boundary and exception path explicit. An authorized owner may raise a limit for a valuable experiment; a job cannot silently exceed it.

Model selection should explicitly consider cost alongside accuracy, and a cost-quality trade-off analysis exposes diminishing returns ([[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/03 The Fleet in Production/12 ML Operations at Scale/V2-12.10 Fleet Economics and Utilization#^tbl-ops-scale-cost-quality|table 36]]). Moving from small to medium model yields a 3 percentage-point accuracy gain for 10$\times$ training cost increase, while medium to large yields only 1 additional percentage point for another 10$\times$ cost, a pattern that should inform deployment decisions.

| **Model**  | **Accuracy** | **Training cost** | **Serving cost/1K** | **Value judgment**                         |
|:-----------|:------------:|:-----------------:|:-------------------:|:-------------------------------------------|
| **Small**  |     92%      |       \$500       |       \$0.10        | Baseline                                   |
| **Medium** |     95%      |      \$5,000      |       \$0.50        | 3 percentage points for 10× cost           |
| **Large**  |     96%      |     \$50,000      |       \$2.00        | Additional 1 percentage point for 10× more |

Table 36: **Cost-Quality Trade-Off Analysis**: Diminishing returns in model scaling. Small-to-medium model transition yields a 3 percentage-point accuracy gain for 10$\times$ cost increase; medium-to-large yields only 1 additional percentage point for another 10$\times$ cost. This pattern demonstrates why explicit cost-quality analysis should inform model selection rather than defaulting to larger architectures.

^tbl-ops-scale-cost-quality

The marginal accuracy gain must be valued in the application context. An additional percentage point can matter greatly if it reduces costly errors on an important population, but aggregate accuracy alone does not establish that value. Comparing error consequences, quality by slice, and lifecycle cost prevents defaulting to the largest available model.

Efficiency metrics should be reviewed alongside model quality to identify spending that warrants investigation. Low utilization can reflect required reserve capacity, and experiments without a deployed model can still resolve useful questions. Each efficiency metric therefore prompts an investigation rather than delivering an automatic verdict of waste ([[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/03 The Fleet in Production/12 ML Operations at Scale/V2-12.10 Fleet Economics and Utilization#^tbl-ops-scale-efficiency-metric-actions|table 37]]).

| **Metric**                       | **Waste signal**                                      | **Typical action**                                                    |
|:---------------------------------|:------------------------------------------------------|:----------------------------------------------------------------------|
| Cost per accuracy point          | Accuracy gains are bought with disproportionate spend | Revisit model size, feature set, or serving target before scaling up. |
| Experiments per production model | Many runs produce little deployable value             | Improve experiment design, stopping rules, and promotion criteria.    |
| GPU utilization                  | Capacity is reserved but not doing useful work        | Right-size instances, improve batching, or profile inefficient code.  |
| Spot utilization rate            | Eligible workloads use expensive on-demand capacity   | Move fault-tolerant jobs to spot instances with checkpoint support.   |

Taken together, the three economic levels and the FinOps controls make platform investment accountable to measured lifecycle outcomes. Platform return decides whether shared tooling pays, fleet utilization decides whether capacity pays, workload TCO decides where optimization pays, and attribution with anomaly detection keeps each team answerable for its share. A technically sound shared service still needs an owner for its budget, compatibility promises, and retirement decisions. Organizational design determines whether those responsibilities support model teams or leave every cross-team change waiting in a central queue.
