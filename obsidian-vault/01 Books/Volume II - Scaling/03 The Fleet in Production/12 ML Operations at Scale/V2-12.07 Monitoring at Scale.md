# V2-12.07 Monitoring at Scale

[[Home|Home]] · [[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/Volume 2 Overview|Volume Overview]] · [[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/03 The Fleet in Production/12 ML Operations at Scale/V2-12 ML Operations at Scale|V2-12 ML Operations at Scale]]

^sec-ml-operations-scale-monitoring-scale-73c5

Preserving a release contract after promotion requires knowing whether a production regression comes from shifting data, an upstream dependency, or the hardware underneath the model. Because application-layer drift and latency checks depend on a healthy physical substrate, where a degraded NVLink connection or a thermally throttling GPU can masquerade as a software timeout, fleet monitoring starts bottom-up with hardware telemetry and climbs through alert aggregation, model-quality signals, and dashboards so operators can route each symptom to the layer that can repair it.

### Fleet Telemetry and Hardware Observability

^sec-ops-scale-fleet-telemetry

Under an illustrative independent-failure model, a 50,000-hour per-GPU planning interval gives a 10,000-GPU cluster a hardware failure roughly every five hours, and the full-system interval of **[[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/02 Distributed ML/07 Fault Tolerance/V2-07.03 The Mathematics of Inevitable Failure#^sec-fault-tolerance-reliability-reliability-worked-example-cluster-mtbf-calculation-9255|Cluster MTBF calculation]]** is shorter still. Because failure is routine at pod scale, fleet monitoring and hardware observability are prerequisites for productive training. Recovering in 10 minutes rather than hours depends on detection, usable recovery state, replacement capacity, and restart procedures, so the monitoring system is itself a distributed service collecting telemetry across devices, switches, and cooling components.

The telemetry spans three physical levels. At the GPU level, junction temperature tracks cooling degradation, and error-correcting code (ECC) counts in high-bandwidth memory (HBM) and SRAM track memory health. A rising rate of correctable errors can precede an uncorrectable, job-ending error. Streaming multiprocessor (SM) utilization shows whether the device is doing work. None alone identifies the cause: low utilization can reflect input starvation, synchronization, or a device problem. At the network level, retry counters on individual ports can point to a failing cable or switch; at the facility level, coolant temperature deviations can trigger load shedding before thermal limits force throttling. Correlating these signals with workload and topology lets responders narrow the failure domain before deciding whether to reduce load, quarantine hardware, or investigate software.

Correlation analysis helps locate the scope of a shared disturbance. Elevated temperature on one GPU motivates local inspection; simultaneous changes across 8 GPUs in a node suggest checking shared cooling and workload changes. A rack-wide pattern adds the coolant distribution unit (CDU) and rack-level load to the investigation, while a multi-rack pattern raises facility-level hypotheses. These patterns guide diagnosis rather than prove a cause. Grouping related alerts preserves that context and avoids presenting one shared event as hundreds of unrelated failures.

Telemetry capacity follows the series count, sample rate, bytes per observation, retention, and indexing policy. Sampling one metric every second across 10,000 GPUs produces 864 million observations per day before other metrics or labels are added. A daily byte total or monitoring-budget share cannot be inferred without the encoding and retention assumptions. The platform therefore bounds label cardinality and provisions ingestion, storage, and queries as an explicit platform workload.

Before allocating a long training job, a scheduler can use automated preflight checks to screen its nodes. General matrix multiply (GEMM) benchmarks exercise arithmetic throughput, NCCL AllReduce tests exercise collective paths, and memory stress tests look for errors under load. Results must be compared with baselines for the same hardware and configuration before a node is quarantined. Preflight consumes startup time and cannot reveal every fault; its value depends on the failed work it avoids and the cost of unnecessary exclusions.

![[90 Assets/Volume II/12 Ops Scale/Vol2 Ops Scale Margin 005 09b9559852.svg]]

*One red square labeled slow with gray arrows fanning out to six identical blue peer nodes, which sit before a dashed vertical line labeled barrier.*

*One slow accelerator stalls every peer at the barrier.*

A gray failure leaves a component responsive while degrading its useful performance. In synchronous data-parallel training, a persistently slow participant can delay collective completion even when every device passes an availability check. Per-rank timing and hardware telemetry help localize the slowdown; controlled microbenchmarks can then compare suspected nodes with healthy peers. An illustrative policy flags GEMM throughput below 90 percent of a matched fleet median or NVLink bandwidth below 85 percent. Those thresholds require calibration for hardware generation, software, load, and measurement variability. A low result warrants investigation rather than proving a hardware defect, because configuration and contention can produce similar symptoms.

↩︎ Gray failures and the straggler detection that exposes them are established in **[[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/02 Distributed ML/07 Fault Tolerance/V2-07.10 Failure Detection and Recovery#^sec-fault-tolerance-reliability-reliability-distinguishing-stragglers-failures-285d|Stragglers versus fail-stop failures]]**.

In multi-tenant clusters, shared links can also couple otherwise separate workloads, so fabric telemetry must attribute congestion to the tenant that caused it. The isolation mechanisms that bound such interference, along with their limits, are established in **[[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/01 The Fleet/03 Network Fabrics/V2-03.09 Network Virtualization#^sec-network-fabrics-qos|Traffic isolation and quality of service]]**.

Checkpoint cadence is the point where hardware telemetry becomes visible to ML operators rather than only to data-center technicians. A degraded node that forces frequent checkpoint recovery, or a storage path that lengthens checkpoint writes, turns directly into lost training throughput. **[[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/02 Distributed ML/07 Fault Tolerance/V2-07.09 Checkpointing and Progress Preservation#^sec-fault-tolerance-young-daly|The Young-Daly checkpoint interval]]** derives the checkpoint-compute trade-off and the Young-Daly interval; the operations responsibility here is to expose the telemetry those formulas depend on: write time, failure rate, straggler behavior, and recovery duration. Without that visibility, a platform may report that a job is “running” while useful learning has quietly collapsed behind retries and slow checkpoints.

With the physical substrate accounted for, monitoring returns to the deployed model fleet. Validation and canary evidence cover a candidate under observed conditions; production can later change abruptly or drift over weeks and months. Monitoring must therefore retain acute-failure controls while detecting gradual loss of quality. Independently paging on every model metric can multiply notifications from a shared event and create alert fatigue. Fleet-level aggregation connects those symptoms while preserving urgent local signals that require immediate action.

### The Alert Fatigue Problem

^sec-ml-operations-scale-alert-fatigue-problem-038c

Joining hardware counters with per-model quality, drift, and latency metrics across a portfolio multiplies the number of time series evaluated on every check interval. Even when each metric uses a well-calibrated statistical threshold with a tiny per-check false-positive rate, evaluating thousands of checks every few minutes produces a continuous stream of random threshold crossings whose operational cost depends on the alerting policy.

**Napkin Math 12.7: False alarms across many checks**

**Problem**: Consider a system monitoring 100 models. Each model has 10 metrics (latency, accuracy, drift, and others). Alert thresholds are set at 3-sigma (99.7 percent specificity), and a control script re-evaluates every metric on a fixed interval. Assume calibrated stable Gaussian null behavior and count raw threshold crossings before grouping or suppression; those crossings are not necessarily distinct incidents.

**Math**:

1.  **Total Monitors**: 100 models $\times$ 10 metrics = 1,000 monitors.
2.  **False Positive Rate**: $1 - 0.997 = 0.003$ (0.3 percent).
3.  **Checks per day**: Assume checks every 5 minutes (288 checks/day).
4.  **Daily false threshold crossings**: $1,000 \times 288 \times 0.003$ $\approx$ 864 alerts/day.

**Systems insight**: Even with high-specificity (3-sigma) alerts, scale becomes problematic. Aggregation, deduplication, persistence windows, and calibrated thresholds reduce repeated notifications. Specific safety or hardware-failure alerts must still trigger independently when their contracts require immediate action.

![[90 Assets/Volume II/12 Ops Scale/Vol2 Ops Scale Margin 003 5593a987e4.svg]]

*Red saturating curve rising from a gray baseline toward a gray horizontal line at 1, labeled near 1, with the horizontal axis labeled N tests.*

*False alerts become near-certain as checks multiply.*

The multiple-testing problem from concurrent experiments ([[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/03 The Fleet in Production/12 ML Operations at Scale/V2-12.06 CI CD for ML at Scale#^sec-ml-operations-scale-multiple-testing-correction-023f|section 12.5.3.7]]) also appears in monitoring. For independent checks under the null, each with false positive rate ${\alpha_{\text{fp}}}$, [[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/03 The Fleet in Production/12 ML Operations at Scale/V2-12.07 Monitoring at Scale#^eq-false-alert-rate|equation 9]] shows that the probability of at least one false alert approaches certainty as the number of tests ${N_{\text{tests}}}$ grows, because the probability of no false alert decays exponentially:

$$
\Pr(\text{at least one false alert}) = 1 - (1 - {\alpha_{\text{fp}}})^{{N_{\text{tests}}}} \tag{9}
$$

^eq-false-alert-rate

The equation holds at any per-check rate, and teams under pressure to catch regressions sooner often loosen thresholds. Loosening the notebook’s 3-sigma thresholds to 2 sigma raises the per-check rate from 0.3 percent to roughly ${\alpha_{\text{fp}}}= 0.05$. With that rate and ${N_{\text{tests}}}= 1000$ (100 models $\times$ 10 metrics):

$$
\Pr(\text{false alert}) = 1 - (1 - 0.05)^{1000} = 1 - 0.95^{1000} \approx 1.0
$$

The probability is essentially 100 percent for this collection of independent checks. This result establishes the likelihood of at least one false crossing, not the fraction of alerts that are false. Repeated checks compound the operational burden when every crossing generates a page, and frequent nonactionable pages can train operators to discount the alert stream, making genuine incidents harder to recognize. For the notebook’s fleet of 100 models with 10 metrics checked every 5 minutes, the 5 percent rate raises the daily raw crossings far above the 3-sigma count.

Expected daily false alerts: $\text{Daily false alerts} = 100 \times 10 \times 0.05 \times \frac{24 \times 60}{5} = 14,400$.

Even if 99 percent of these crossings are deduplicated or auto-resolved, the remaining 144 alerts daily would impose a substantial interruption burden if each became a page. Comprehensive telemetry therefore needs a policy that separates diagnostic records from notifications requiring immediate action.

### Hierarchical Monitoring Architecture

^sec-ml-operations-scale-hierarchical-monitoring-architecture-407d

When a shared feature store stalls, paging twenty downstream model teams simultaneously floods on-call rotations without telling anyone that the root cause sits in shared infrastructure. To prevent uncoordinated alarm floods, hierarchical monitoring arranges telemetry as a four-tier triage pyramid that maps operational scope from component health at the base to fleet-wide business impact at the apex ([[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/03 The Fleet in Production/12 ML Operations at Scale/V2-12.07 Monitoring at Scale#^fig-monitoring-pyramid|figure 11]]). Broad signals reveal fleet impairment, portfolio signals route incidents to the owning model family, and granular model or infrastructure telemetry supports local diagnosis. Routine fluctuations remain diagnostic records, while urgent local failures still page their owners without waiting for a fleet-wide signal. The hierarchy provides context for routing and grouping alerts while preserving those immediate response paths. It routes evidence to the owner of the affected level and does not decide the remedy; whether a model must change remains its owner’s decision.

Figure 11: **Hierarchical Monitoring Architecture**: To prevent alert fatigue, monitoring operates at four levels, each with its own owner. Business and portfolio metrics raise the few alarms that indicate broad impairment and route each incident to the owning model family, while model and infrastructure metrics serve primarily for diagnosis and root-cause analysis.

^fig-monitoring-pyramid

The hierarchy works because each level answers a different routing question and has a different owner, from the incident lead who watches business impairment to the platform team that repairs capacity, networking, and storage failures ([[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/03 The Fleet in Production/12 ML Operations at Scale/V2-12.07 Monitoring at Scale#^tbl-hierarchical-monitoring-levels|table 17]]).

#### Multidimensional Reliability Budgets

^sec-ops-scale-multidimensional-reliability-budgets

A fleet cannot summarize reliability with availability alone. An endpoint may return HTTP 200 while serving stale features, missing its latency target, or producing predictions below the quality floor. Each service therefore needs an SLO vector

$$
{\mathbf{S}}= ({S_{\text{availability}}}, {S_{\text{latency}}}, {S_{\text{freshness}}}, {S_{\text{quality}}}),
$$

with one indicator, target, measurement window, and owner for every dimension. For dimension ${i}$, the normalized budget consumption over a window is

$$
{B}_{{i}} = \frac{{V}_{{i}}}{{V^{\text{allow}}}_{{i}}},
$$

where ${V}_{{i}}$ is the fraction of eligible observations that violate target ${i}$ and ${V^{\text{allow}}}_{{i}}$ is the allowed violating fraction for that same target and window. The numerator and denominator have identical units within each dimension: failed-request fraction for availability, late-request fraction for latency, stale-prediction fraction for freshness, and below-floor evaluation fraction for model quality. The quality contract must define its evaluation units, label delay, and coverage; unlabeled predictions cannot silently count as passing observations. A value ${B}_{{i}}=1$ exhausts that dimension’s budget; ${B}_{{i}}>1$ exceeds it. Ratios may be compared after this normalization, but raw latency, freshness, and quality magnitudes are not commensurable. The fleet remains inside its measured reliability envelope only when $\max_{{i}} {B}_{{i}} \leq 1$, the reliability condition of the evaluation contract (principle [[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/03 The Fleet in Production/V2 Fleet-In-Production Principles#^pri-evaluation-contract|21]]). Averaging would hide a severe regression behind healthy dimensions. A service whose freshness budget is overspent can still report a comfortable mean consumption, yet the maximum stops its promotion ([[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/03 The Fleet in Production/12 ML Operations at Scale/V2-12.07 Monitoring at Scale#^fig-ops-scale-budget-vector|figure 12]]). Missing or stale evidence requires its own release policy rather than an assumed pass.

![[90 Assets/Volume II/12 Ops Scale/Ops Scale Budget Vector 5df8427d58.svg]]

Figure 12: **Reliability as a Vector of Budgets**: Each bar is one dimension’s budget consumption, its observed violating fraction divided by the allowed fraction, with the owner of that dimension beside it. The values are illustrative. Availability, latency, and quality sit inside their budgets and the four-dimension average is 0.70, but freshness has spent 1.30 of its budget; because the maximum, not the average, governs, the service stops promotion until the data owner restores freshness.

^fig-ops-scale-budget-vector

Burn rate converts this vector into an operating control. For dimension ${i}$, the **burn rate** is the observed violating fraction over a short recent interval divided by the allowed fraction ${V^{\text{allow}}}_{{i}}$, so it measures how fast the budget is being spent relative to the pace that would exhaust it exactly at the end of the window. A burn rate of 1 spends the budget on schedule; a burn rate of 10 would exhaust the whole window’s budget in a tenth of the window. A fast burn in any dimension pages the owning team and stops promotion. Sustained moderate burn reduces rollout velocity and requires remediation. An exhausted budget freezes nonessential releases until the responsible indicator returns inside its recovery policy.

The platform owns indicator computation, common windows, alert routing, and enforcement. Model teams own task-quality thresholds and remediation for model behavior; data owners own freshness and validity; serving owners own latency and availability. This division turns monitoring into an explicit contract between evidence and authority rather than a dashboard that everyone can see but no one must act on. Each exhausted dimension triggers a release consequence its own owner carries out ([[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/03 The Fleet in Production/12 ML Operations at Scale/V2-12.07 Monitoring at Scale#^tbl-ops-scale-reliability-budget-vector|table 18]]).

| **Budget dimension** | **Representative indicator**                    | **Primary owner** | **Release consequence**                                    |
|:---------------------|:------------------------------------------------|:------------------|:-----------------------------------------------------------|
| Availability         | Successful eligible requests/total requests     | Serving           | Halt rollout or fail over when fast burn exceeds policy    |
| Latency              | Requests meeting the tail-latency objective     | Serving/platform  | Reduce traffic, capacity pressure, or candidate complexity |
| Freshness            | Predictions using features inside freshness SLO | Data              | Block promotion and fall back to a safe feature path       |
| Model quality        | Slice-aware task metric above its quality floor | Model/domain      | Roll back, abstain, or route to a validated baseline       |

Table 18: **ML Reliability Budget Vector**: Fleet reliability joins service health, data freshness, and model behavior. Any exhausted dimension can stop a release because success in one dimension cannot compensate for failure in another.

### Anomaly Detection Across the Fleet

^sec-ml-operations-scale-anomaly-detection-across-fleet-e38f

Protecting those multidimensional reliability budgets requires detecting correlated failures and input shifts before a single upstream fault exhausts every downstream model’s error allowance. When 60 of 100 models cross their control limits at the same timestamp because a shared feature pipeline broke, or when user input distributions shift days before ground-truth labels arrive, per-model static thresholds cannot distinguish a local bug from a fleet-wide event. Fleet-wide anomaly detection adds portfolio context to individual metric thresholds. The detection stack proceeds from local statistical process control, to correlation across model families, to drift scores that characterize changes in data distributions. Each stage narrows the investigation, but none establishes a cause by itself. Grouping related signals can reduce duplicate pages while retaining the evidence needed to route an incident to the right owner.

#### Statistical Process Control

^sec-ml-operations-scale-statistical-process-control-c95d

Statistical process control originated in industrial quality control ([[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/03 The Fleet in Production/12 ML Operations at Scale/V2-12.16 Source References#^ref-shewhart1931economic|Shewhart 1931]]). When adapted for ML monitoring, control charts[[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/03 The Fleet in Production/12 ML Operations at Scale/V2-12.17 Footnotes#^fn10|¹⁰]] track whether metric distributions remain stable over time and complement drift-detection methods that watch for changing data distributions ([[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/03 The Fleet in Production/12 ML Operations at Scale/V2-12.16 Source References#^ref-gama2014|Gama et al. 2014]]). The core idea is distinguishing common cause variation (normal fluctuation) from special cause variation (genuine anomalies).

For a metric ${X}$ with established mean ${\mu}$ and standard deviation ${\sigma}$, the upper control limit is $\text{UCL} = {\mu}+ 3{\sigma}$ and the lower control limit is $\text{LCL} = {\mu}- 3{\sigma}$. Points outside control limits or systematic patterns, such as 7 consecutive points above or below the mean, trigger investigation. Adding pattern rules changes the combined false-alarm rate; the single-check 3-sigma rate no longer describes the entire policy. Calibration must also account for seasonality and temporal dependence in the monitored metric.

#### Fleet-Wide Correlation

^sec-ml-operations-scale-fleetwide-correlation-d928

When multiple models show similar anomalies simultaneously, shared infrastructure or an upstream data pipeline becomes a candidate cause. Correlation analysis across the fleet supports three operational tasks: identifying shared dependencies to investigate, grouping related alerts into an incident, and prioritizing triage based on the breadth of downstream impact. As shown in [[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/03 The Fleet in Production/12 ML Operations at Scale/V2-12.07 Monitoring at Scale#^lst-anomaly-attribution|listing 6]], the fraction of anomalous models can guide escalation from model-by-model debugging to a coordinated investigation. Crossing that threshold does not establish a shared failure; dependency records, deployment timing, and component telemetry must support the attribution.

``` python
def detect_fleet_anomaly(model_metrics, threshold=0.6):
    """
    Detect correlated anomalies across model fleet.

    Returns list of (timestamp, affected_models, likely_cause) tuples.
    """
    anomalies = []

    for timestamp in model_metrics.timestamps:
        # Identify models with anomalous metrics at this time
        anomalous_models = []
        for model in model_metrics.models:
            if is_anomalous(model_metrics[model][timestamp]):
                anomalous_models.append(model)

        # Check if anomaly fraction exceeds correlation threshold
        if (
            len(anomalous_models) / len(model_metrics.models)
            > threshold
        ):
            # Many models affected -> likely shared cause
            cause = attribute_to_shared_cause(
                timestamp, anomalous_models
            )
            anomalies.append((timestamp, anomalous_models, cause))

    return anomalies
```

Listing 6: **Fleet Anomaly Attribution**: Detecting correlated anomalies across a model fleet and attributing them to shared infrastructure or data causes.

^lst-anomaly-attribution

The threshold controls when the monitor escalates to a fleet-wide investigation. A low threshold increases sensitivity and investigation load; a high threshold can miss failures affecting a smaller but critical subset of models. Above the threshold, the incident coordinator uses shared dependencies to identify candidate owners, while affected model teams retain responsibility for local containment.

#### Drift Detection

^sec-ml-operations-scale-drift-detection-793f

Distribution changes can invalidate the population assumptions behind a release even while service health remains normal. Monitoring must distinguish a change in inputs from a change in the input-output relationship; neither an input alarm nor a healthy input histogram alone establishes model quality.

Covariate shift moves the input distribution ${p}({x})$ while the input-output relationship holds, so label-free input monitoring can detect the move but cannot confirm that the relationship stayed fixed. Concept drift changes the relationship ${p}({y}\mid {x})$ itself, so detecting it requires ground-truth labels that often arrive minutes, days, or weeks late.

↪ The full shift taxonomy, including label shift, and the evidence that distinguishes one kind of drift from another are developed in **[[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/04 The Responsible Fleet/14 Robust AI/V2-14.05 Environmental Shifts#^sec-robust-ai-distribution-shift-concept-drift-55e2|Distribution shift and concept drift]]**.

Because labels are often delayed, most real-time monitoring systems use input drift as a leading indicator of possible performance degradation. A cheap drift score is valuable even when it is not a complete theory of robustness because it tells the operator where to investigate before outcome labels arrive.

For continuous features, the population stability index (PSI)[[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/03 The Fleet in Production/12 ML Operations at Scale/V2-12.17 Footnotes#^fn11|¹¹]] quantifies distribution shift ([[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/03 The Fleet in Production/12 ML Operations at Scale/V2-12.16 Source References#^ref-yurdakul2020|Yurdakul and Naranjo 2020]]). [[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/03 The Fleet in Production/12 ML Operations at Scale/V2-12.07 Monitoring at Scale#^eq-psi-monitoring|Equation 10]] computes PSI as the sum of log-ratio weighted differences between actual and expected bucket proportions. In platform operations, PSI plays a narrow role as an inexpensive alerting signal.

$$
\text{PSI} = \sum_{{b}=1}^{{n_{\text{bins}}}} ({A}_{{b}} - {E}_{{b}}) \times \ln\left(\frac{{A}_{{b}}}{{E}_{{b}}}\right) \tag{10}
$$

^eq-psi-monitoring

where ${A}_{{b}}$ is the proportion in bucket ${b}$ of the actual (current) distribution, ${E}_{{b}}$ is the proportion in bucket ${b}$ of the expected (reference) distribution, and ${n_{\text{bins}}}$ is the number of buckets.

An illustrative policy uses these coarse bands to route investigation. PSI below 0.1 leaves the model in the normal monitoring path; values between 0.1 and 0.25 create an investigation ticket; values at or above 0.25 escalate review of the shifted inputs and affected predictions. These actions depend on local calibration. A high PSI does not establish degraded model quality, and a low PSI does not establish that quality remains acceptable.

**Napkin Math 12.8: Time-to-detection: Monitoring lag**

**Problem**: A production model has a baseline accuracy of 95 percent. A data drift event causes accuracy to fall to 93 percent. If labeled outcomes arrive at 1000 samples/hour, what collection time supports the stated detection-power calculation, assuming an independent baseline sample of the same size and immediately available labels?

**Math**: Detection requires enough samples to distinguish the signal (the 2-percentage-point drop) from the noise (random variance). The sizing logic of [[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/03 The Fleet in Production/12 ML Operations at Scale/V2-12.06 CI CD for ML at Scale#^eq-ops-scale-ab-sample-size|equation 4]] applies, in its unpooled form because the baseline and degraded accuracies (${a}_1$, ${a}_2$) differ, with the same confidence and power targets (${z_{\alpha/2}}$, ${z_{\beta}}$):

$$
{n}\approx \frac{({z_{\alpha/2}} + {z_{\beta}})^2 \left[ {a}_1(1 - {a}_1) + {a}_2(1 - {a}_2) \right]}{({a}_1 - {a}_2)^2}
$$

1.  **Samples Required**: At 95 percent confidence and 80 percent power, the standard normal quantiles are ${z_{\alpha/2}} = 1.96,\ {z_{\beta}} = 0.84$, and plugging in gives ${n} \approx (1.96 + 0.84)^2 \times \left[ 0.95(1 - 0.95) + 0.93(1 - 0.93) \right] / (0.95 - 0.93)^2 \approx 2,207$ samples.
2.  **Detection Latency**: 2,207 samples / 1000 samples/hour $\approx$ 2.2 hours.

**Systems insight**: Monitoring is not instantaneous. The computed duration is a planned collection time at 80 percent power, not a guaranteed detection deadline. It assumes an independent baseline sample, the stated post-change rate, and immediately available labels. Delayed labels, dependence, and repeated testing change the detection contract. Input drift can provide an earlier investigation signal, but it does not establish a quality regression by itself.

Fleet-wide drift monitoring extends that signal across the portfolio. A PSI spike in one noncritical feature may be a local investigation, while simultaneous drift across shared features or dependent models warrants a broader investigation. A shared data pipeline failure is one candidate cause; a genuine change in the population served can produce the same pattern. Pipeline checks and outcome evidence distinguish a data defect from a change to which the models must adapt. Whether a confirmed shift calls for retraining, adaptation, or no change is a model decision developed in **[[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/04 The Responsible Fleet/14 Robust AI/V2-14.05 Environmental Shifts#^sec-robust-ai-retraining-decision-framework-68e3|Retraining decision framework]]**; the fleet monitor’s responsibility ends when calibrated evidence reaches the owner who makes that decision.

### Model-Type Specific Monitoring

^sec-ml-operations-scale-modeltype-specific-monitoring-b901

A fraud detector watching for active card-testing attacks, a recommendation ranker watching real-time click-through rates, and an LLM assistant watching for toxic or hallucinated generations cannot share one monitoring metric or polling interval. Monitoring cadence follows failure cost, label delay, and traffic volume. The same workload families that set release cadence in [[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/03 The Fleet in Production/12 ML Operations at Scale/V2-12.03 The N-Models Problem#^tbl-ops-scale-model-types|table 2]] and validation focus in [[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/03 The Fleet in Production/12 ML Operations at Scale/V2-12.06 CI CD for ML at Scale#^tbl-ops-scale-cicd-patterns|table 16]] now need a post-promotion counterpart: which metric each watches and how often. The illustrative parameters in [[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/03 The Fleet in Production/12 ML Operations at Scale/V2-12.07 Monitoring at Scale#^tbl-ops-scale-monitoring-types|table 19]] must be calibrated to the service, because a frequently updated metric is useful only if enough observations support the alert decision.

|:--------------------|:------------------------------:|:---------------------:|:------------------------:|
| **Recommendation**  |      CTR, engagement lift      |   5% relative drop    |        Real-time         |
| **Fraud Detection** | Precision, recall, fraud rate  |    1% degradation     |        Real-time         |
| **LLM**             | Quality scores, safety metrics | Per-model calibration |          Hourly          |
| **Vision**          |       Accuracy by class        |   Dataset-specific    |          Daily           |
| **Search Ranking**  |      NDCG, click position      |    2% degradation     |        Real-time         |

^tbl-ops-scale-monitoring-types

Recommendation monitoring must respond quickly to user impact, but each metric becomes informative on its own observation timescale. Click-through rate, dwell time, and conversion rate should be compared against time-matched historical baselines, control traffic when available, and the previous model version after a release. Conversions can arrive later than clicks, and sparse traffic can delay a reliable comparison. Engagement metrics alone also leave gaps; diversity, catalog coverage, and filter-bubble indicators track longer-term concerns, while revenue attribution and promotional-inventory metrics connect recommendation quality to business outcomes.

Fraud monitoring binds on adversarial urgency. Missed fraud creates immediate financial loss, but excessive false positives create customer friction and manual-review load. The monitoring surface therefore has to pair detection rate, prevented dollar amount, and detection latency with false-positive rate, blocked-legitimate-transaction events, manual-review volume, and adversarial indicators such as probing patterns or sudden shifts in fraudulent behavior.

LLM monitoring binds on semantic quality, which is difficult to measure from service metrics alone. Latency, token generation rate, error rate, and safety-classifier scores establish operational health; user satisfaction signals such as thumbs-up/thumbs-down rates, regeneration rate, and task-completion proxies add delayed quality evidence. Safety metrics such as toxicity detections, refusal rate, and hallucination indicators remain partial, so they should trigger review rather than claim complete coverage.

Service-health metrics alone cannot detect semantic safety failures such as persuasive misinformation or skilled manipulation. Here, red teaming supplies an additional monitoring channel: human evaluators or automated probes attempt jailbreaks before deployment, and a smaller continuous probe set checks production behavior against known failure cases. Sampling outputs for delayed human review adds evidence about failures that automated metrics miss. Neither a passing probe set nor a reviewed sample establishes complete safety coverage.

### Observability Architecture

^sec-ml-operations-scale-observability-architecture-ba87

When a single user request traverses ten inference services and suffers a 50 ms tail-latency spike or a silent accuracy regression, independent service counters cannot tell investigators which stage stalled. Unsampled logs from 10,000 nodes can flood the telemetry backend during the outage. Effective monitoring therefore requires observability infrastructure that preserves enough context to route an alert from symptom to owner. The metrics, logs, and traces that make up that infrastructure were introduced for debugging a single distributed job; at fleet scale the same channels must route evidence across many models and owners. Metrics show that a service property moved outside its envelope, traces show where a request spent time, logs identify which component emitted the abnormal event, and prediction logs connect system health back to model behavior. Distributed tracing,[[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/03 The Fleet in Production/12 ML Operations at Scale/V2-12.17 Footnotes#^fn12|¹²]] exemplified by Google’s Dapper system ([[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/03 The Fleet in Production/12 ML Operations at Scale/V2-12.16 Source References#^ref-sigelman2010dapper|Sigelman et al. 2010]]), preserves a request’s path across inference services so investigators can locate latency contributions. Taken together, the channels form an evidence map rather than a set of disconnected telemetry tools, with each channel matched to the operational question it answers ([[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/03 The Fleet in Production/12 ML Operations at Scale/V2-12.07 Monitoring at Scale#^tbl-ops-scale-observability-signals|table 20]]). The channels join into one map only when their records share identifiers, such as the request, the model version, and the host, so that a metric, a trace, and a log from the same incident can be matched. [[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/03 The Fleet in Production/12 ML Operations at Scale/V2-12.08 Production Debugging and Incident Response#^sec-ml-operations-scale-debugging-distributed-ml-systems-a2ea|Section 12.7.5]] specifies that identifier contract for distributed jobs.

| **Evidence channel** | **Primary question**                        | **What it preserves**                                                  | **Typical failure revealed**                         |
|:---------------------|:--------------------------------------------|:-----------------------------------------------------------------------|:-----------------------------------------------------|
| Metrics              | Which service property moved out of bounds? | Real-time streams for alerting and aggregated time series for trends   | Drift, saturation, error-rate spikes, cost anomalies |
| Traces               | Where did this request spend time?          | Request IDs propagated across model invocations, timing, and resources | Cross-model latency, fan-out failures, hot stages    |
| Logs                 | Which component emitted the abnormal event? | Structured events with consistent schemas and searchable indexes       | New error classes, dependency failures, bad releases |
| Prediction logs      | Did system health preserve model behavior?  | Sampled inputs, outputs, features, and delayed labels                  | Accuracy regressions, bad cohorts, data gaps         |

Table 20: **Observability Signals by Operational Question**: Observability architecture is an evidence-routing system. Metrics trigger attention, traces localize the request path, logs explain component behavior, and prediction logs connect operational symptoms to model quality.

^tbl-ops-scale-observability-signals

↩︎ The observability pillars for a single distributed job are established in **[[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/02 Distributed ML/07 Fault Tolerance/V2-07.14 Distributed Debugging and Observability#^sec-fault-tolerance-reliability-reliability-observability-pillars-0e45|Observability pillars]]**.

The channels also grow at different rates, which decides what the platform can afford to collect continuously. Metrics stay compact only while the number of distinct time series is bounded. Each label combination creates another series, so a request identifier or an unbounded user label can overwhelm monitoring even when each sample is small. Logs grow with recorded events and traces with spans, so both are sampled or aggregated at fleet scale. When 10,000 edge nodes or hundreds of microservices emit at once, unsampled telemetry can become a thundering herd, a simultaneous burst of writes that perturbs the production system it is meant to observe. Effective platforms therefore present high-level metric dashboards by default and open lower levels only when an anomaly warrants investigation.

Prediction logging can be expensive because it records model-facing data rather than compact service counters. Production systems therefore sample it deliberately, for example logging 1 percent of predictions or retaining additional examples from cohorts under investigation. The sample must serve the intended analysis; a debugging sample concentrated on failures does not directly estimate fleet-wide accuracy. Recording the selection policy alongside inputs, outputs, and eventual labels preserves the context needed to interpret the evidence without making every prediction a permanent storage record.

### Dashboard Design

^sec-ml-operations-scale-dashboard-design-ffcc

Sampling metrics, logs, and traces keeps telemetry storage bounded, yet a single all-purpose dashboard still fails both ends of an outage. An executive checking whether the business is impaired gets lost in per-GPU trace spans, while an on-call responder diagnosing a tail-latency spike is stuck staring at a high-level portfolio summary. A dashboard is the human attention surface of the monitoring hierarchy, and it should answer routing questions without forcing every reader into the same debugging console. Executives need to know whether the business is impaired, domain owners need to know which portfolio is failing, model owners need the evidence for their model, and incident responders need the trace from symptom to root cause. Each view therefore answers one question at one level of detail ([[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/03 The Fleet in Production/12 ML Operations at Scale/V2-12.07 Monitoring at Scale#^tbl-ops-scale-dashboard-views|table 21]]).

| **View**      | **Question answered**                                   | **Signals to surface**                                                                 |
|:--------------|:--------------------------------------------------------|:---------------------------------------------------------------------------------------|
| Executive     | Is the business impaired?                               | Platform health, business impact, active incidents, key trends                         |
| Portfolio     | Which domain or model family is driving the impairment? | Model inventory, portfolio metrics, recent deployments, resource utilization and cost  |
| Model         | What changed for the responsible model?                 | Current metrics vs. baselines, deployment history, drift indicators, cost attribution  |
| Investigation | Why did the change occur?                               | Cross-model correlations, time-series overlays, log search, request-level trace detail |

Table 21: **Dashboard Views by Operational Question**: Dashboard hierarchy should route attention from business impact to root cause. Each view reveals the amount of detail needed for the decision at that level, preventing executive views from becoming debugging consoles and investigation views from becoming portfolio summaries.

^tbl-ops-scale-dashboard-views

The same views carry cost as one more monitored dimension: the portfolio and model views surface spend beside quality and latency, and [[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/03 The Fleet in Production/12 ML Operations at Scale/V2-12.10 Fleet Economics and Utilization#^sec-ml-operations-scale-cost-monitoring-anomaly-detection-1976|section 12.9.7.3]] develops the anomaly detection that watches that spend. Even a well-instrumented fleet does not prevent failures. The monitoring hierarchy detects them; what remains is the response, which must classify the symptom, contain its impact, and route it to an owner with the authority to act.
