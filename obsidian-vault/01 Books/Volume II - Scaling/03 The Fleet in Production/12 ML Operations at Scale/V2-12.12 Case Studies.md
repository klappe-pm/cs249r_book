# V2-12.12 Case Studies

[[Home|Home]] · [[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/Volume 2 Overview|Volume Overview]] · [[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/03 The Fleet in Production/12 ML Operations at Scale/V2-12 ML Operations at Scale|V2-12 ML Operations at Scale]]

^sec-ml-operations-scale-case-studies-8bfd

When hundreds of engineers train and deploy thousands of models against shared data streams and strict latency budgets, bespoke per-team pipelines collapse under silent feature skew, duplicated glue code, and uncoordinated rollouts. The five platform cases that follow instantiate the operations trade-offs developed earlier in this chapter: standardization vs. team-local velocity, abstraction vs. control, freshness vs. cost, and governance vs. autonomy. Each case shows a different way to keep a multi-model fleet repeatable without turning every model into bespoke infrastructure.

### Uber Michelangelo

^sec-ml-operations-scale-uber-michelangelo-37ff

Uber’s Michelangelo platform shows why repeatability becomes a platform requirement at company scale ([[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/03 The Fleet in Production/12 ML Operations at Scale/V2-12.16 Source References#^ref-hermann2017michelangelo|Hermann and Del Balso 2017]]). Uber’s 2017 account describes data scientists using varied modeling tools while engineering teams built custom production systems, with no common deployment path or reproducible data pipelines. Michelangelo standardized one workflow from data management through training, evaluation, deployment, and prediction monitoring, and shared feature definitions let teams reuse platform-managed pipelines. Its delivery-time model combined historical and near-real-time restaurant preparation features, and such features raise the consistency problem of [[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/03 The Fleet in Production/12 ML Operations at Scale/V2-12.05 Feature Store Operations#^sec-ml-operations-scale-feature-store-operations-2a36|section 12.4]], because one feature definition must produce compatible values for offline training sets and online requests. The standardization trade-off is the durable lesson. Common data and deployment paths remove repeated infrastructure work, while product teams still own model behavior and business-specific validation.

### Meta FBLearner Flow

^sec-ml-operations-scale-meta-ml-platform

Meta’s FBLearner Flow shows how shared ML infrastructure can hide resource management while preserving workflow structure ([[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/03 The Fleet in Production/12 ML Operations at Scale/V2-12.16 Source References#^ref-facebook2016fblearner|Engineering 2016]]). In its 2016 account, Facebook reported use by more than 25 percent of its engineering team and more than one million models trained. Engineers defined operators with typed inputs and outputs, and compilation formed a DAG whose dependencies controlled execution; operators declared CPU, GPU, and memory needs, and the platform allocated resources and moved data between steps. Feed ranking adds freshness pressure, because the value of a signal such as a recent like decays quickly. Shadow mode (or dark launching) and canary releases then verify a candidate’s latency, memory, and error behavior on live traffic before its outputs reach users. The durable lesson is the interface between workflow intent and managed execution.

### Netflix ML Infrastructure

^sec-ml-operations-scale-netflix-ml-infrastructure

Netflix’s ML infrastructure shows how personalization platforms manage fan-out cost under a strict latency budget ([[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/03 The Fleet in Production/12 ML Operations at Scale/V2-12.16 Source References#^ref-gomez-uribe2015|Gomez-Uribe and Hunt 2015]]). The recommendation system combines several ranking and personalization algorithms for page generation, row selection, search, and artwork, and every additional online ranker or feature dependency consumes latency budget. Production systems therefore separate candidate generation, offline training, online ranking, and caching, so the homepage stays personalized without executing an unbounded number of models per request. New members and new titles raise the cold-start problem; exploration policies such as bandits, which route some traffic to options whose value is still uncertain, are one option, and they need explicit exploration budgets, policy versions, and feedback logging. Netflix’s interleaving work merges competing ranked lists for the same user and credits each click to its source ranker ([[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/03 The Fleet in Production/12 ML Operations at Scale/V2-12.16 Source References#^ref-netflix2017interleaving|Netflix Technology Blog 2017]]), so faster experiments require additional serving and logging capability.

### Google Vertex AI

^sec-ml-operations-scale-google-vertex-ai

Google Vertex AI illustrates the managed-platform abstraction problem, which is to reduce the glue-code burden described by Sculley et al. ([[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/03 The Fleet in Production/12 ML Operations at Scale/V2-12.16 Source References#^ref-sculley2015hidden|2015]]) while preserving control over custom workloads. Offering AutoML, automated search over model and training configurations, alongside custom training makes that boundary visible, because such search, of which neural architecture search is one method, trades compute for engineering effort and needs bounded cost and concurrency. A managed service also carries contracts that its consumers must verify. A container separates a model’s software environment but does not isolate accelerator memory bandwidth, shared networks, or backing services, and a managed feature service must respect when information was available to the predictor, not merely when the event occurred. Managed infrastructure transfers implementation work, not responsibility for leakage, quality, or tail latency under the intended load. These mechanisms are general design options, not claims about Vertex AI’s internal architecture.

### Spotify ML Platform

^sec-ml-operations-scale-spotify-ml-platform

Spotify’s ML platform places shared infrastructure in a personalization setting where immediate response and longer-term listener value need not align, since optimizing clicks alone can overlook diversity and discovery (the concern about filter bubbles). Separating candidate generators, such as collaborative filtering models ([[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/03 The Fleet in Production/12 ML Operations at Scale/V2-12.16 Source References#^ref-koren2009|Koren et al. 2009]]), which recommend what similar listeners chose, or editorial selections, from rankers lets teams change retrieval strategies while preserving the ranking contract. Spotify’s documented contribution is a paved road for model orchestration built around TensorFlow Extended, Kubeflow Pipelines, and metadata tracking ([[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/03 The Fleet in Production/12 ML Operations at Scale/V2-12.16 Source References#^ref-spotify2019mlinfra|Engineering 2019]]). Its operational lesson is lineage, which lets an engineer trace a silent regression back to the data or workflow state that produced it. The same discipline supports mixed freshness. In an illustrative split, a weekly playlist can be computed in batch, while a responsive home screen ranks precomputed candidates with recent interaction features online, so only decisions that need current context pay for the live path.

### Comparing Platform Control Planes

^sec-ops-scale-case-study-synthesis

Across Uber, Meta, Netflix, Google, and Spotify, teams started from different bottlenecks (inconsistent delivery-time features, duplicated DAG plumbing, homepage fan-out latency, multi-tenant cloud glue code, and unrepeatable personalization experiments) yet converged on the same division of control-plane responsibilities. No single implementation is the template; the durable pattern is that shared state, release evidence, resource policy, and ownership become platform interfaces, while each domain team keeps a responsibility that no platform can absorb ([[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/03 The Fleet in Production/12 ML Operations at Scale/V2-12.12 Case Studies#^tbl-ops-scale-case-study-synthesis|table 39]]).

| **Case**       | **Primary pressure**                   | **Control-plane emphasis**                     | **Local responsibility retained**                 |
|:---------------|:---------------------------------------|:-----------------------------------------------|:--------------------------------------------------|
| Michelangelo   | Repeated feature and deployment work   | Shared features and repeatable workflows       | Product-specific model behavior and validation    |
| FBLearner Flow | Many teams and heterogeneous pipelines | DAG orchestration and resource abstraction     | Workflow intent and domain evaluation             |
| Netflix        | Fan-out under strict latency budgets   | Composition, precomputation, experimentation   | Ranking objectives and user-experience trade-offs |
| Vertex AI      | Multi-tenant managed infrastructure    | Isolation, policy, and lifecycle integration   | Workload configuration and acceptance criteria    |
| Spotify        | Fresh personalization with mixed paths | Lineage, paved roads, and hybrid data movement | Domain policy and exploration strategy            |

Table 39: **Comparative Platform Control Planes**: The five cases allocate implementation differently but retain the same systems contract: the platform owns shared invariants and evidence, while domain teams retain workload intent and acceptance criteria.

^tbl-ops-scale-case-study-synthesis

The comparison also exposes the adoption constraint. A platform succeeds when its common path captures recurring invariants without erasing domain judgment. Too little platform ownership recreates bespoke infrastructure; too much converts every exceptional workload into a central-team queue. Versioned interfaces, explicit exceptions, and outcome-based adoption metrics keep that boundary adjustable as the portfolio changes. The same boundary choices, misjudged, produce the misconceptions that recur when teams scale ML operations.
