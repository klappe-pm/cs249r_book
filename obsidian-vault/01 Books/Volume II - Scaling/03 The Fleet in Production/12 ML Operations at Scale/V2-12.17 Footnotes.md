# V2-12.17 Footnotes

[[Home|Home]] · [[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/Volume 2 Overview|Volume Overview]] · [[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/03 The Fleet in Production/12 ML Operations at Scale/V2-12 ML Operations at Scale|V2-12 ML Operations at Scale]]

^footnotes

<sup>1</sup> **Feature store**: A centralized repository that manages the computation, storage, and serving of ML features. The core systems problem it solves is training-serving skew; [[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/03 The Fleet in Production/12 ML Operations at Scale/V2-12.05 Feature Store Operations#^sec-ml-operations-scale-feature-store-operations-2a36|section 12.4]] develops that failure mode and the platform invariant that contains it.

^fn1

<sup>2</sup> **Zombie models**: Production models that continue serving despite being obsolete or superseded. Even a low-traffic model consumes registry, monitoring, dependency-management, and on-call capacity until it is deliberately retired. The operational cost therefore extends beyond compute: zombie models enlarge the dependency graph, making platform-wide upgrades and security patches slower and riskier.

^fn2

<sup>3</sup> **Blue-green deployment**: A release pattern that prepares the new version in a parallel environment before switching production traffic. Fast rollback requires the previous environment to remain ready and its dependencies to remain compatible. Holding both versions during the transition adds serving capacity and cost $({C_{\text{infer}}})$; the duplication depends on which resources the environments can safely share.

^fn3

<sup>4</sup> **SLO**: A target for a declared service indicator and measurement window. Under a time-based definition, “three nines” (99.9 percent) annual availability allows about 8.8 hours of downtime; a request-based definition instead counts failed eligible requests. A p99 latency target of 200 ms requires at least 99 percent of eligible requests to complete within that bound over the specified window.

^fn4

<sup>5</sup> **Data leakage**: Future information can inflate offline metrics when a model learns signals unavailable at prediction time. Shared feature infrastructure should enforce historical availability boundaries, but model and data owners must still specify the prediction moment and verify how features were computed. A timestamped join cannot remove future information already embedded inside an incorrectly constructed feature.

^fn5

<sup>6</sup> **Kubeflow**: Kubeflow Pipelines connects workflow dependencies to Kubernetes execution and resource requests. Declared requests let scheduling account for resources needed by a step, but do not eliminate contention on shared storage, networks, or other services. Platform policy must therefore combine admission and resource allocation with measurements of actual execution behavior.

^fn6

<sup>7</sup> **Interleaving experiments**: Originally developed for search engine evaluation, interleaving merges results from two rankers into one list shown to each user, then credits clicks to the originating ranker ([[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/03 The Fleet in Production/12 ML Operations at Scale/V2-12.16 Source References#^ref-chapelle2012|Chapelle et al. 2012]]). For fleet-scale recommendation systems iterating on many model variants, the sample efficiency of that within-user comparison translates directly into faster experiment cycles and lower opportunity cost from suboptimal models in production.

^fn7

<sup>8</sup> **A/B testing power**: The probability that an experiment correctly detects a true effect ($1-{\beta_{\text{stat}}}$, typically set to 0.8). At fleet scale, the systems cost of power is exposure time. If the expected improvement is small (for example, 0.1 percent), the experiment may require millions of samples to reach statistical significance, consuming substantial serving resources $({C_{\text{infer}}})$ for weeks.

^fn8

<sup>9</sup> **Canary deployment**: Named after the coal-mining practice of using canaries to detect toxic gases. In an ML release, a limited traffic cohort supplies early evidence while bounding initial exposure. Quality regressions can leave health checks green, so the canary needs model and product metrics as well as service-health signals; delayed outcomes can still postpone detection.

^fn9

<sup>10</sup> **Statistical process control (SPC)**: Shewhart ([[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/03 The Fleet in Production/12 ML Operations at Scale/V2-12.16 Source References#^ref-shewhart1931economic|1931]]) established the control-chart framework for manufacturing quality control. Under a normal approximation, a 3-sigma control limit produces a false-alarm probability of only 0.27 percent for a stable process. For ML fleet monitoring, even small per-test false-alarm rates compound across many models and metrics, making alert-fatigue management an inherent constraint of applying SPC at platform scale.

^fn10

<sup>11</sup> **Population stability index (PSI)**: Bands such as $\text{PSI} < 0.1$ for routine monitoring and $\text{PSI} \geq 0.25$ for investigation are heuristics, not guarantees of stability or model failure. Once histograms are available, the comparison uses bucket proportions rather than individual records. Histogram construction, sample size, binning, and treatment of empty buckets still affect cost and interpretation, so the platform must calibrate the signal on its own data.

^fn11

<sup>12</sup> **Distributed tracing**: Google’s 2010 Dapper paper describes propagating trace context across service boundaries while controlling collection overhead through sampling and instrumentation in common libraries. In a multi-model pipeline, the resulting trace connects stage timings to the same request rather than requiring investigators to infer the path from independent service summaries. Without it, a 50 ms tail-latency spike in a 10-model pipeline must be investigated in all 10 models separately. Sampling and instrumentation coverage determine which requests and stages remain visible.

^fn12

<sup>13</sup> **SRE**: Founded by Ben Treynor Sloss at Google in 2003, SRE introduced error budgets and SLOs as quantitative reliability contracts. For ML systems, classical SRE must be extended. Traditional services can fail semantically as well as become unavailable, and models add statistical quality degradation that an uptime check cannot detect. This demands drift-aware SLOs that treat prediction quality alongside uptime as a reliability metric.

^fn13

<sup>14</sup> **TensorFlow Extended (TFX)**: Google’s production ML pipeline framework includes TensorFlow Data Validation (TFDV), which checks data against schema expectations and configured drift and skew comparisons. Connecting those checks to a release policy allows a pipeline to stop before spending training resources on detected data defects. The checks cover declared expectations; a passing result does not establish that every value is correct or that a distribution change is harmless.

^fn14

<sup>15</sup> **Prometheus**: An open-source monitoring system that scrapes and stores labeled time series. A fleet can export operational metrics and computed drift scores through the same metrics interface while retaining detailed prediction records elsewhere for analysis. The distinction is between compact alerting signals and the evidence needed to investigate them; it does not require every platform to adopt the same two-tier implementation.

^fn15

<sup>16</sup> **Job preemption**: The ability to terminate lower-priority workloads to free resources for urgent work. Preemptible capacity is also how cloud providers sell deep discounts, which [[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/03 The Fleet in Production/12 ML Operations at Scale/V2-12.10 Fleet Economics and Utilization#^sec-ml-operations-scale-cloud-options|section 12.9.4]] prices. Without a usable checkpoint, a preempted 72-hour run may need to restart from scratch, potentially erasing the discount. Checkpoint frequency trades I/O overhead against wasted-compute risk; the right policy depends on job duration, interruption frequency, and recovery cost.

^fn16

<sup>17</sup> **TCO boundary**: A fleet comparison must declare what its total cost of ownership counts. The boundary must include the supporting power, cooling, networking, facility, and staffing commitments. Moving from 20 percent to 80 percent useful utilization spreads fixed costs over more output, but whether that changes the ownership decision depends on the rental alternative, workload fit, and costs included in each option.

^fn17
