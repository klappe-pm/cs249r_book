# V2-09.02 Where Fleet Capacity Goes

[[Home|Home]] · [[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/Volume 2 Overview|Volume Overview]] · [[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/03 The Fleet in Production/09 Fleet Efficiency/V2-09 Fleet Efficiency|V2-09 Fleet Efficiency]]

^sec-performance-engineering-memory-wall

A fleet can be fully allocated and visibly busy while most of its capacity never becomes training progress or completed service. When thousands of accelerators run a single workload, losses accumulate before a job starts, between the steps it executes, and inside the steps it keeps. The size of that gap is clearest in a run whose engineering is not in doubt.

**Lighthouse 9.1: Archetype A (GPT-4/Llama-3): Held capacity versus progress**

Meta’s pretraining of Llama 3 405B on 16,384 H100 GPUs is one of the best-documented large training runs, and its two most quoted efficiency numbers measure different things. Across a 54-day snapshot, the job kept more than 90 percent of its elapsed time as effective training time (time spent on useful training) despite hundreds of unexpected interruptions, as **[[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/02 Distributed ML/07 Fault Tolerance/V2-07.10 Failure Detection and Recovery#^sec-fault-tolerance-reliability-recovery-automation-pipeline|Recovery automation pipeline]]** records. In the steps it kept, its arithmetic achieved a model FLOPs utilization (MFU) of about 41 percent of the accelerators’ peak rate ([[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/03 The Fleet in Production/09 Fleet Efficiency/V2-09.14 Source References#^ref-dubey2024llama|Dubey et al. 2024]]). Multiplied, the two figures show the run turning roughly 37 percent of the peak-rate capacity it held into training progress. Because the two figures come from different measurement windows, the product is only an estimate. Yet it shows one of the most carefully engineered runs on record losing about two-thirds of the capacity it held, mostly to steps that ran below the hardware’s rate rather than to the failures that dominate its reputation. Its two figures therefore illustrate what a well-run job of this archetype loses during execution.

Even that one-third figure overstates what a shared cluster delivers, because the Llama 3 numbers begin only after the job holds its accelerators. On a multi-tenant fleet, the setting of **[[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/02 Distributed ML/08 Fleet Orchestration/V2-08 Fleet Orchestration#^sec-fleet-orchestration|Fleet Orchestration]]**, capacity is also lost before a job starts. Accelerators sit unallocated when no queued job fits the free pool, or a job holds part of its gang (the set of accelerators that must all start together) while waiting for the remaining members and cannot execute a single step. The orchestrator’s accounting separates allocated capacity from productive work, yet it cannot see where inside a running job the missing work went. Allocated capacity is where fleet-efficiency accounting begins, not evidence that the fleet used those accelerators well.

↩︎ The allocated, active, and productive tiers of fleet resource accounting are established in **[[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/02 Distributed ML/08 Fleet Orchestration/V2-08.13 Debugging Cluster Utilization#^sec-fleet-orchestration-resource-accounting|Allocated, active, and productive utilization]]**.

Earlier chapters examined the mechanisms of a fleet one layer at a time: data delivery (**[[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/01 The Fleet/04 Data Storage/V2-04 Data Storage#^sec-data-storage|Data Storage]]**), collective communication (**[[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/02 Distributed ML/05 Collective Communication/V2-05 Collective Communication#^sec-collective-communication|Collective Communication]]**), parallel work partitioning (**[[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/02 Distributed ML/06 Distributed Training/V2-06 Distributed Training#^sec-distributed-training-systems|Distributed Training]]**), fault recovery (**[[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/02 Distributed ML/07 Fault Tolerance/V2-07 Fault Tolerance#^sec-fault-tolerance-reliability|Fault Tolerance]]**), and cluster scheduling (**[[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/02 Distributed ML/08 Fleet Orchestration/V2-08 Fleet Orchestration#^sec-fleet-orchestration|Fleet Orchestration]]**). Each subsystem reports performance in its own local unit—input stall ratio, link bandwidth, scaling efficiency, availability, or cluster occupancy—and none of those isolated metrics tells an organization what fraction of its fleet investment becomes useful progress. Worse, when subsystem reports are read separately, their denominators overlap in some places and leave gaps in others. An hour lost to a restart can be penalized in two reports at once, while accelerators held idle by an incomplete gang vanish between the scheduler’s log and the job’s step timer. Charging each lost hour exactly once requires a single end-to-end accounting framework that follows every accelerator-hour from offered hardware to useful output and ranks every optimization lever across the stack on one common scale. That framework opens the Serving band of **[[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/00 Introduction/01 Introduction/V2-01.03 From Single Node to Fleet#^fig-fleet-stack|The Fleet Stack]]** by asking how much of the infrastructure built in the lower bands becomes useful work.

↩︎ The Fleet Law, where these multiplicative efficiency factors are first previewed, is established in **[[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/00 Introduction/01 Introduction/V2-01.06 The C³ Taxonomy and Foundations of Scale#^sec-vol2-introduction-fleet-law|The Fleet Law]]**.

To make that accounting concrete, one recurring training job carries the numbers through this chapter: pretraining Llama 2 70B, a smaller Archetype A model than the archetype’s 175B reference, on 128 GPUs (16 nodes of eight GPUs each) in a shared cluster. The job uses 8-way tensor parallelism within each node and data parallelism across nodes. Because it competes with other teams for complete gang allocations, absorbs cluster failures and preemptions, and executes steps whose exposed communication grows with node count, every stage of fleet loss appears in a single workload on a single pool. The job is a constructed illustration rather than a measured run, so its numbers are modeled from stated assumptions.

↩︎ Archetype A and its 175B-parameter reference model are established in **[[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/00 Introduction/01 Introduction/V2-01.07 The Fleet Stack and the Lighthouse Archetypes#^sec-introduction-archetype-gpt4llama3-b02f|Archetype A (GPT-4/Llama-3)]]**.

A serving replica of the same model provides the running example’s inference twin. The replica spans one node of eight H100 GPUs, so a single tensor-parallel node forms one complete serving unit. Because a dense Archetype A transformer cannot fit on one accelerator, the eight GPUs exchange activations in every layer. The cluster scheduler must place the replica as an indivisible gang, the replica must load weights and warm up before taking traffic, and its decode steps can leave most of the node’s arithmetic idle while weights stream from high-bandwidth memory (HBM). Its capacity losses therefore arise at the same stages as those of the training job.

The serving twin also marks where fleet-efficiency accounting stops and request-level serving design begins. In production serving, requests share execution across dynamically formed batches, occupy key-value cache memory, and must satisfy tail-latency targets; **[[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/03 The Fleet in Production/10 Serving at Scale/V2-10 Serving at Scale#^sec-inference-scale|Serving at Scale]]** develops how a serving engine manages uneven arrivals under those constraints. Here, the boundary test is whether a loss would persist even under a steady, uncontended stream of work. A replica held off-line while loading weights, or a decode step that reads weights more slowly than the node’s memory bandwidth allows, fails to use held hardware even without arrival bursts and is counted here over windows of hours to weeks. By contrast, a request delayed behind an oversized batch is a queueing phenomenon caused by uneven arrivals and belongs to serving analysis. Headroom that a replica holds in reserve to absorb traffic spikes appears in goodput accounting as ready capacity running below its ideal rate, whereas choosing how much headroom a latency target requires is a serving decision.

This chapter builds the measurement and optimization discipline that turns those scattered losses into one engineering budget. We begin by defining *ML goodput* ([[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/03 The Fleet in Production/09 Fleet Efficiency/V2-09.03 ML Goodput#^sec-performance-engineering-measurement-scale|section 9.2]]), which factors useful fleet output into scheduling, runtime, and program goodput on non-overlapping boundaries. We then establish the reproducible measurement contract and hierarchical fleet profiling needed to localize which factor moved without drowning the cluster in trace data ([[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/03 The Fleet in Production/09 Fleet Efficiency/V2-09.04 Measuring Goodput#^sec-performance-engineering-measuring-goodput|section 9.3]]). From there, we trace the three factors in chronological order. We attribute scheduling losses ([[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/03 The Fleet in Production/09 Fleet Efficiency/V2-09.05 Scheduling Goodput#^sec-performance-engineering-scheduling-goodput|section 9.4]]) and runtime losses ([[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/03 The Fleet in Production/09 Fleet Efficiency/V2-09.06 Runtime Goodput#^sec-performance-engineering-runtime-goodput|section 9.5]]) to the orchestration and fault-tolerance mechanisms developed in earlier chapters, and then diagnose ([[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/03 The Fleet in Production/09 Fleet Efficiency/V2-09.07 Program Goodput#^sec-performance-engineering-profiling|section 9.6]]) and recover ([[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/03 The Fleet in Production/09 Fleet Efficiency/V2-09.08 Recovering Program Goodput#^sec-performance-engineering-program-goodput|section 9.7]]) the program-goodput losses that this chapter owns directly. Finally, we compose all three factors into a whole-fleet waterfall and cost per useful accelerator-hour ([[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/03 The Fleet in Production/09 Fleet Efficiency/V2-09.09 The Whole Fleet#^sec-performance-engineering-whole-fleet|section 9.8]]) and evaluate optimizations that trade one goodput factor against another along the fleet’s efficiency frontier ([[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/03 The Fleet in Production/09 Fleet Efficiency/V2-09.10 Trading Between Factors#^sec-performance-engineering-frontier-objective|section 9.9]]).

**Self-Check: Question**

1.  Meta’s Llama 3 405B run kept more than 90% of its elapsed time as effective training time and ran its kept steps at about 41% MFU. What does multiplying the two figures show about where its held capacity went?

    1.  Roughly 90% became progress, because MFU describes kernel quality and does not reduce useful output
    2.  Roughly 41% became progress, because effective training time already includes the MFU loss
    3.  Roughly 37% became progress, and most of the loss came from the hundreds of unexpected interruptions the job survived
    4.  Roughly 37% of the peak-rate capacity it held became training progress, and most of the loss came from kept steps running below the hardware’s rate rather than from interruptions

2.  Name the three places a fleet job can lose capacity, in the order the job meets them, and explain why a dashboard that counts allocated accelerators as used cannot see most of them.

3.  The chapter counts a serving replica’s loss in its accounting only if the loss would remain with the request stream switched off. Which loss belongs to the serving analysis instead?

    1.  Time the replica spends loading weights and warming up before it serves
    2.  A decode step that streams weights more slowly than the node’s memory bandwidth allows
    3.  A request that waits behind a long batch because requests arrive unevenly
    4.  Capacity the replica holds in reserve for bursts, running below its ideal rate

4.  True or False: A shared fleet whose accelerators are fully allocated is, by that fact, using its capacity well, so fleet efficiency accounting can start from the busy count.

5.  Order the following events in the life of the running example’s training job, from first to last: (1) its kept steps run below the hardware’s rate because collectives are exposed, (2) its group of 16 nodes becomes a complete allocation, (3) it waits in the queue while other teams’ jobs hold the capacity, (4) it starts up, compiles, and loads its initial state before the first step.
