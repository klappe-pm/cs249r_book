# V2-09.10 Trading Between Factors

[[Home|Home]] · [[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/Volume 2 Overview|Volume Overview]] · [[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/03 The Fleet in Production/09 Fleet Efficiency/V2-09 Fleet Efficiency|V2-09 Fleet Efficiency]]

^sec-performance-engineering-frontier-objective

Waiting for a compact node placement, running a longer autotuning compilation before the first step, and enforcing a tighter preemption policy each raise one goodput factor by shifting loss to another boundary. Evaluating any cross-boundary change on the single factor it was built to improve ignores the capacity lost at the boundary that paid for it, so the change must be judged by the goodput product it leaves behind. This trade-off is the fleet-level manifestation of the displacement of overhead (principle [[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/02 Distributed ML/V2 Distributed ML Principles#^pri-conservation-overhead|14]]), in which an optimization relocates overhead among compute, communication, and coordination rather than eliminating it outright. Because every factor multiplies the same goodput product, and one point of any factor is worth the product of the other two ([[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/03 The Fleet in Production/09 Fleet Efficiency/V2-09.09 The Whole Fleet#^sec-performance-engineering-whole-fleet-decomposition|section 9.8.1]]), a loss displaced to one boundary can be weighed in a common currency against the gain it purchases at another.

### Judging a Trade by the Product

^sec-performance-engineering-trade-product-rule

When a compiler team enables an autotuning pass that speeds up steady-state steps while adding minutes to startup, the compiler team sees only the faster step trace and the reliability team sees only the longer launch in the job timeline. Deciding whether that trade helped the fleet requires combining both teams’ measurements without rebuilding the whole-fleet waterfall from scratch. A change that touches more than one boundary multiplies MPG ([[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/03 The Fleet in Production/09 Fleet Efficiency/V2-09.03 ML Goodput#^eq-perf-ml-productivity-goodput|equation 1]]) by the product of each factor’s before-and-after ratio, with every ratio measured on the same boundary and over the same time window. The change improves fleet efficiency only when that product of ratios exceeds one, regardless of which individual factor the lever was designed to raise.

Expressing the rule in ratios allows separate engineering teams to evaluate a trade from their own telemetry: the reliability team measures what a restart policy does to runtime goodput, the compiler team measures what an autotuned build does to program goodput, and the two relative ratios multiply directly. Because a 1 percent relative gain in any factor produces a 1 percent gain in the overall product, small changes reduce to adding percentage gains and subtracting percentage losses. The marginal value of one absolute percentage point derived in [[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/03 The Fleet in Production/09 Fleet Efficiency/V2-09.09 The Whole Fleet#^sec-performance-engineering-whole-fleet-decomposition|section 9.8.1]], the product of the other two factors, is the exact same rule expressed in percentage points. A point added to the lowest factor therefore represents the largest relative change and carries the highest marginal value.

Even so, every factor’s ratio must be re-measured after the same change, because a lever aimed at one factor mechanically shifts the others across their shared boundaries. Productive step time is both the denominator of program goodput and the numerator of runtime goodput, while held allocation time is both the denominator of runtime goodput and the numerator of scheduling goodput. Shortening the time spent on one side of a boundary therefore alters the ratio on the other side. The fleet-goodput study measured this coupling on production clusters, and [[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/03 The Fleet in Production/09 Fleet Efficiency/V2-09.10 Trading Between Factors#^tbl-performance-engineering-factor-coupling|table 9]] summarizes the directional effects ([[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/03 The Fleet in Production/09 Fleet Efficiency/V2-09.14 Source References#^ref-wongpanich2025fleet|Wongpanich et al. 2025]]).

A compiler optimization that shortens on-device execution per step raises program goodput, yet it simultaneously lowers runtime goodput because the job now spends fewer productive device hours relative to its fixed startup, checkpoint, and recovery durations. When the job is device-bound, total wall-clock step time falls and the run finishes earlier. Releasing the allocation sooner shortens the job’s held time, but the gap between one job’s release and the next job’s complete gang does not shrink with it. When each job holds its devices for less time, those fixed gaps take a slightly larger share of the pool’s calendar window, so scheduling goodput dips slightly even as total MPG rises. Eliminating runtime waste likewise finishes the job earlier, raising runtime goodput while slightly lowering scheduling goodput, and only shortening partial-gang holding raises scheduling goodput in isolation.

When the job is host-bound and paced by its input pipeline, faster on-device execution does not shorten wall-clock step time at all. Every millisecond saved on the device becomes an extra millisecond spent waiting for the next input batch, so the rise in program goodput and the drop in runtime goodput cancel out and leave MPG unchanged. Consequently, a monitoring dashboard that shows one factor dipping after an optimization does not indicate a regression until the full product has been evaluated.

|:---------------------------------------|:--------------------|:--------------------|:--------------------------------------|:--------------------------------------|
| **Compiler shortens on-device step**   | Rises               | Falls               | Falls if device-bound; else no change | Rises if device-bound; else no change |
| **Runtime removes idle or lost time**  | No change           | Rises               | Falls                                 | Rises                                 |

^tbl-performance-engineering-factor-coupling

This ratio rule is strongly asymmetric when the factors start at different levels. A program optimization that lifted the running example’s program goodput from 0.48 to its single-node ceiling of 0.65 would increase the product by 35 percent. It would therefore still yield a net gain even if it sacrificed up to 26 percent of the job’s runtime goodput ([[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/03 The Fleet in Production/09 Fleet Efficiency/V2-09.10 Trading Between Factors#^fig-performance-engineering-iso-goodput|figure 13]]). A runtime lever has no comparable margin to spend: even if every runtime loss were eliminated, a runtime goodput of 0.90 could rise by at most 11 percent. Because a factor already near one has little relative headroom left to offer, a trade that spends runtime goodput to purchase program goodput has a wide margin before it lowers the product, although it still needs measuring, whereas a trade in the opposite direction has little margin and warrants immediate skepticism.

Two conditions determine whose product and which measurement window the product rule requires. First, a lever confined to a single job’s allocation—such as a compiler flag or a local checkpoint strategy—is measured on that job’s own product (${\eta_{\text{runtime}}} \times {\eta_{\text{program}}}$). By contrast, a lever that moves capacity between jobs—such as priority preemption, a placement wait, or an elastic resize that returns devices to the pool—also alters other jobs’ factors. It must therefore be judged on the pool’s MPG, the only quantity that captures both the urgent job’s scheduling gain and the preempted victim’s runtime loss. Second, one-time costs such as a compilation build, a mesh rescale, or a restart purchase gains across every subsequent step, so a ratio measured over one hour and the same ratio measured over one week can fall on opposite sides of unity. For that reason, the measurement contract of [[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/03 The Fleet in Production/09 Fleet Efficiency/V2-09.04 Measuring Goodput#^sec-performance-engineering-measurement-contract|section 9.3.1]] requires the observation window to be reported alongside the result.

The goodput product itself does not measure wall-clock completion time: two configurations with the exact same product can finish a job days apart. The product rule evaluates capacity efficiency for the fleet, whereas [[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/03 The Fleet in Production/09 Fleet Efficiency/V2-09.10 Trading Between Factors#^sec-performance-engineering-frontier|section 9.9.4]] introduces the additional dimensions on which a job’s owner chooses an operating point.

The elastic scaling decision analyzed in **[[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/02 Distributed ML/08 Fleet Orchestration/V2-08.08 Elastic Scheduling#^sec-fleet-orchestration-elastic-policies|Elastic scaling policies]]** illustrates this trade-off across boundaries. Consider an elastic job, separate from the running example, whose target batch is designed for 512 GPUs under three strategies. The first starts immediately on 128 GPUs and remains at that size. The second waits 4 hours in the queue for the full 512 GPUs allocation. The last starts immediately on 128 GPUs and steps up to 256 GPUs when capacity frees, leaving the remaining devices to queued jobs. Because the global batch size is held fixed across all three strategies, each epoch delivers identical statistical progress. Program goodput separates the three options most strongly: the scaling efficiencies—90 percent at 256 GPUs and 80 percent at 512 GPUs—are the exact ratios of each larger allocation’s program goodput to that of the 128 GPUs baseline. Runtime goodput separates them by a single non-productive interval: the 10-minute pause required to checkpoint, reshard, and restart during the step-up transition ([[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/03 The Fleet in Production/09 Fleet Efficiency/V2-09.06 Runtime Goodput#^sec-performance-engineering-runtime-goodput-counts|section 9.5.1]]). The step-up strategy retains its throughput advantage over the immediate small run unless that rescale pause exceeds roughly 9 hours.

Scheduling goodput—which belongs to the shared pool—determines which strategy serves the cluster best. The 256 GPUs that the step-up strategy leaves in the pool are worth whatever the queue can do with them. If waiting jobs form complete allocations on those devices immediately, the pool’s MPG rises. If those devices sit in unallocated fragments that no queued gang can use, they become a scheduling loss, and giving the full allocation to the first job would have been more productive. Wall-clock completion time pulls in the opposite direction: the step-up strategy advances at nearly 1.7× the rate of the immediate small run yet still trails the full allocation by roughly 38 percent over the window. Consequently, a team facing a hard deadline prefers waiting for the full allocation, whereas a fleet operator managing a deep queue prefers the elastic step-up.

### Cross-Boundary Trades Across the Fleet Stack

^sec-performance-engineering-priced-trades

Earlier chapters in this book priced these mechanisms in the local units of their own subsystems, such as restart minutes, cascade multipliers, or storage bandwidth. Three structural patterns recur across [[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/03 The Fleet in Production/09 Fleet Efficiency/V2-09.10 Trading Between Factors#^tbl-performance-engineering-priced-trades|table 10]]. First, any lever that transfers capacity between jobs—such as priority preemption or quota lending—must be evaluated at the pool level because its gain lands on one job while its cost lands on another. The orchestrator’s preemption budget sets the rate at which one is exchanged for the other. Second, waiting in the queue costs scheduling goodput only when capacity is held idle to assemble the target allocation. If other jobs run on those devices in the meantime, the wait costs only wall-clock time to result, which is why placement waits and elastic resizing depend on the state of the pool’s queue rather than on the job alone. Third, an elastic resize changes the device mesh, forcing a compiler-partitioned program to recompile for the new topology ([[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/03 The Fleet in Production/09 Fleet Efficiency/V2-09.08 Recovering Program Goodput#^sec-performance-engineering-gspmd|section 9.7.1]]) and adding that build time to the rescale pause.

Asynchronous checkpoint staging is the one trade in the table whose program-goodput cost must be measured empirically on the target system. Staging copies a state snapshot to host memory or local flash and flushes it to durable storage in the background while training continues (**[[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/02 Distributed ML/07 Fault Tolerance/V2-07.09 Checkpointing and Progress Preservation#^sec-fault-tolerance-reliability-reliability-synchronous-vs-asynchronous-checkpointing-ecc1|Synchronous vs. asynchronous checkpointing]]**), removing most of the synchronous pause counted by the checkpoint term of [[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/03 The Fleet in Production/09 Fleet Efficiency/V2-09.06 Runtime Goodput#^eq-perf-runtime-loss-composition|equation 4]]. However, the background flush still contends with active training steps for pinned host memory, PCIe transfers, network links, and storage bandwidth, so its overhead surfaces as slightly longer retained steps—a program goodput loss. Staging also delays the durability point: until every shard reaches persistent storage, a failure rolls the job back to the previous checkpoint generation, increasing runtime rework if a fault occurs during the flush. Whether asynchronous staging raises the overall product therefore depends on two measurements under the contract: the retained-step duration with and without a background flush in flight, and the time required to commit a checkpoint generation.

By contrast, checkpoint cadence trades two losses within a single factor—the time spent writing checkpoints versus the steps lost when an interruption occurs—so the Young-Daly interval (**[[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/02 Distributed ML/07 Fault Tolerance/V2-07.09 Checkpointing and Progress Preservation#^eq-ft-young-daly-interval|equation 7.7]]**) minimizes total runtime loss rather than balancing two separate goodput factors. It couples to the orchestration rows through the total interruption rate, which is why including budgeted preemptions shortened the running example’s optimal checkpoint interval in [[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/03 The Fleet in Production/09 Fleet Efficiency/V2-09.06 Runtime Goodput#^sec-performance-engineering-runtime-composition|section 9.5.2]]. Graceful preemption, which saves state upon preemption notice rather than discarding the steps since the last periodic checkpoint, similarly trades rework against a synchronous save within runtime goodput.

### Compilation Builds

^sec-performance-engineering-compilation-overhead

An autotuning compiler that searches for many minutes before the first step shortens every subsequent step by fusing operations, planning memory layouts, and selecting faster kernels, yet the accelerators sit allocated without retaining a single step while that search runs. Compilation is the cross-factor trade owned directly by performance engineering because both of its effects land inside the job’s own allocation. The faster compiled steps raise program goodput ([[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/03 The Fleet in Production/09 Fleet Efficiency/V2-09.08 Recovering Program Goodput#^sec-performance-engineering-kernel-levers|section 9.7.3]]), whereas the build and warmup that precede them are held time without retained progress, which counts as startup and lowers runtime goodput ([[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/03 The Fleet in Production/09 Fleet Efficiency/V2-09.06 Runtime Goodput#^sec-performance-engineering-runtime-goodput-counts|section 9.5.1]]). A compilation build pays off when the cumulative time it saves across all executions that reuse the compiled artifact exceeds the build time itself; the break-even point therefore depends on the number of executions that share the artifact rather than on wall-clock deployment duration alone.

The boundary rule determines where a recompilation is charged. A build that runs at job launch, after a failure restart, or after an elastic resize to a new mesh occurs once per launch and is charged to runtime goodput along with the rest of startup. Conversely, a recompilation triggered repeatedly during steady-state execution—because input shapes vary or the captured graph keeps breaking—recurs inside the job’s fault-free step loop and lowers program goodput instead. A dynamic shape distribution that forces a recompile every few hundred steps therefore manifests as slow retained steps rather than as startup overhead, and its remedy is a shape-polymorphic compilation strategy rather than a faster compiler build.

**Napkin Math 9.6: When a compilation build pays for itself**

**Problem**: A 13B-parameter model is small enough that one H100 holds it, so one accelerator forms a complete replica, here serving INT4 weights. That replica decodes at 120 tokens/s without compilation, and an autotuning compilation of its graph takes about 15 min to build. How many generated tokens repay that build on one replica, and how does the answer change when 128 replicas share one compiled artifact versus when each replica builds its own?

**Variables**:

- The uncompiled step takes 8.33 ms per token, of which matrix multiplications account for 0.50, element-wise kernels account for 0.35, and exposed launch overhead accounts for 0.15.
- Compilation leaves the matrix multiplications unchanged, removes 70 percent of the element-wise time through fusion, and removes 80 percent of the launch overhead.
- A default-mode build takes about 1 min; the autotuning search takes about 15 min.

**Math**:

- Compiled step: 0.50 + 0.35 $\times$ 0.30 + 0.15 $\times$ 0.20 = 0.635 of the uncompiled step, about 5.3 ms per token, a 1.58× speedup that saves about 3.0 ms per token.
- One replica: 15 min $\div$ 3.0 ms $\approx$ 300,000 tokens, about 26 min of single-stream generation. The default build would need only about 20,000 tokens if it captured the same saving.
- Shared artifact: 26 min $\div$ 128 replicas $\approx$ 12 seconds of the pool’s generation.
- Per-replica builds: 128 replicas $\times$ 15 min $\approx$ 32 GPU-hours per rollout, about 25 percent of the held time of a replica added for one burst hour.

**Systems insight**: A compilation build is amortized over executions rather than over elapsed time, so the same autotuning search that the pool repays in seconds when its artifact is shared consumes a quarter of a short-lived replica’s held time when every replica repeats it independently.

A stored compilation artifact—or the autotuning cache that produced it—is built once for each combination of model version, shape range, and accelerator type, and its step-time savings accrue across every replica that loads it. By contrast, a per-replica build imposes a runtime loss on every replica, sized against the replica’s lifetime in the same manner as the weight load and warmup analyzed in [[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/03 The Fleet in Production/09 Fleet Efficiency/V2-09.06 Runtime Goodput#^sec-performance-engineering-runtime-evidence|section 9.5.3]]. Because replicas added for a traffic burst have the shortest lifetimes, per-replica compilation makes the exact capacity a fleet adds under urgency both slower to come online and less productive while it runs.

Training jobs face the same trade-off across relaunches rather than across serving replicas. Unless compiled artifacts are cached persistently, a training job recompiles after every failure restart, every preemption, and every elastic resize to a new mesh, embedding compilation overhead into three terms of [[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/03 The Fleet in Production/09 Fleet Efficiency/V2-09.06 Runtime Goodput#^eq-perf-runtime-loss-composition|equation 4]] at once: initial startup, failure recovery warmup, and preemption warmup. Whereas startup is negligible for a week-long pretraining run, it represents a substantial fraction of held time for hour-long jobs ([[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/03 The Fleet in Production/09 Fleet Efficiency/V2-09.06 Runtime Goodput#^sec-performance-engineering-runtime-composition|section 9.5.2]])—and production fleets run large numbers of short jobs, including evaluations, debugging runs, and hyperparameter sweeps. A persistent compilation cache[[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/03 The Fleet in Production/09 Fleet Efficiency/V2-09.15 Footnotes#^fn9|⁹]] shared across launches turns a recurring per-launch runtime loss into a single build per program revision. Because the cache is keyed by the model revision, input shapes, device mesh, accelerator model, and compiler version, a compiler or hardware upgrade invalidates cached artifacts and pays every build anew, a cost caught by the startup regression checks of [[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/03 The Fleet in Production/09 Fleet Efficiency/V2-09.04 Measuring Goodput#^tbl-performance-engineering-scaling-regression-tests|table 4]].

Production compilation stacks differ in build cost, in the shape variations a compiled artifact tolerates, and in how broadly artifacts can be reused across launches ([[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/03 The Fleet in Production/09 Fleet Efficiency/V2-09.10 Trading Between Factors#^tbl-performance-engineering-compiler-tradeoffs|table 11]]). Deeper hardware specialization pays off only when the model revision and its shape distribution remain stable long enough for many executions to amortize a single build.

| **Compilation path**           | **Build cost**                    | **Shape flexibility**                           | **Specialization depth**                       | **Reused across jobs by**                                                     | **Typical fit**                       |
|:-------------------------------|:----------------------------------|:------------------------------------------------|:-----------------------------------------------|:------------------------------------------------------------------------------|:--------------------------------------|

^tbl-performance-engineering-compiler-tradeoffs

Graph breaks and shape specialization express the same trade-off at a finer granularity. A graph break—where dynamic control flow or unsupported host operations prevent the compiler from tracing a single graph—partitions the regions over which the compiler can fuse kernels and plan memory, and it can trigger recompilation when surrounding code paths change. Similarly, specializing to a narrow shape profile yields faster kernels but triggers a rebuild whenever a new shape arrives, whereas symbolic dimensions accept variable batch sizes and sequence lengths without rebuilding. Under the boundary rule, a poorly matched specialization strategy shows up either as slow retained steps (when a narrowly specialized artifact recompiles repeatedly during steady-state operation) or as inflated startup overhead (when an expensive autotuning search runs anew at every launch).

### The Efficiency Frontier

^sec-performance-engineering-frontier

For the elastic job of [[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/03 The Fleet in Production/09 Fleet Efficiency/V2-09.10 Trading Between Factors#^sec-performance-engineering-trade-product-rule|section 9.9.1]], holding the job to 128 GPUs rather than 512 gives the higher per-device program goodput, whereas expanding it to 512 GPUs lowers program goodput yet finishes the run in a fraction of the wall-clock time. Because a workload’s owner cares when the result finishes and what it costs as well as how efficiently the cluster runs, a cross-factor trade that raises the goodput product is still only part of an operational decision. The broader engineering objective is to choose a position on the **efficiency frontier**: the Pareto-optimal subset of feasible configurations for a stated workload and resource budget on which no measured dimension can improve without degrading another. As scaling laws drive rapid growth in the computation required for model quality improvements ([[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/03 The Fleet in Production/09 Fleet Efficiency/V2-09.14 Source References#^ref-thompson2021|Thompson et al. 2021]]), fleet efficiency determines how much of that purchased compute becomes useful progress, making a target quality reachable sooner or at lower cost.

↩︎ The power-law relation between compute and model quality that drives fleet growth is established in **[[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/00 Introduction/01 Introduction/V2-01.04 AI Scaling Laws#^sec-vol2-intro-ai-scaling-laws-a043|AI Scaling Laws]]**.

Three measured dimensions span the efficiency frontier of a training fleet. First, goodput is the pool’s MPG—or the job’s product ${\eta_{\text{runtime}}} \times {\eta_{\text{program}}}$—measured under the contract. Second, cost per useful unit is the raw capacity price divided by that goodput ([[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/03 The Fleet in Production/09 Fleet Efficiency/V2-09.09 The Whole Fleet#^eq-perf-cost-per-useful-unit|equation 6]]). Third, time to result is the wall-clock duration from job submission until the workload reaches its target quality. With the job’s queue wait ${W}$, its allocated device count ${N}$, and the ideal accelerator-time ${T_{\text{ideal}}}$ required to complete the work at the ideal rate, the time to result ${T_{\text{result}}}$ follows directly from the job’s goodput factors ([[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/03 The Fleet in Production/09 Fleet Efficiency/V2-09.10 Trading Between Factors#^eq-perf-time-to-result|equation 7]]):

$$
{T_{\text{result}}} = {W} + \frac{{T_{\text{ideal}}}}{{N} \times {\eta_{\text{runtime}}} \times {\eta_{\text{program}}}} \tag{7}
$$

^eq-perf-time-to-result

Scheduling goodput does not appear explicitly in the denominator of [[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/03 The Fleet in Production/09 Fleet Efficiency/V2-09.10 Trading Between Factors#^eq-perf-time-to-result|equation 7]] because unallocated capacity elsewhere in the pool does not slow a job that already holds its gang. The pool’s contention enters the job’s completion time solely through the initial queue wait ${W}$. The allocated device count ${N}$ does appear in the denominator, but program goodput ${\eta_{\text{program}}}$ declines as ${N}$ grows according to the layout’s scaling efficiency. Adding devices therefore shortens held execution time only while the product ${N} \times {\eta_{\text{program}}}$ continues to rise, and each additional device lowers per-device program goodput. Consequently, a deadline-driven job purchases shorter completion time by holding more devices at lower program goodput, whereas a heavily loaded fleet maximizes total useful output by holding that job to a smaller, more efficient allocation. The three elastic scaling strategies analyzed in [[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/03 The Fleet in Production/09 Fleet Efficiency/V2-09.10 Trading Between Factors#^sec-performance-engineering-trade-product-rule|section 9.9.1]] occupy three distinct points along this trade-off curve, and none of them Pareto-dominates the others.

On a homogeneous fleet with a single capacity price, goodput and cost per useful unit are reciprocals of each other, reducing the frontier to a two-dimensional trade-off between goodput and time to result. The two dimensions separate whenever capacity tiers carry different prices. Preemptible spot capacity is discounted relative to reserved capacity but experiences more frequent interruptions, lowering runtime goodput alongside the hourly price; the spot-cost equation (**[[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/02 Distributed ML/08 Fleet Orchestration/V2-08.09 Cost Optimization#^eq-fleet-orchestration-spot-cost|equation 8.3]]**) determines whether the price discount outweighs the runtime rework. Under the elastic scaling parameters of **[[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/02 Distributed ML/08 Fleet Orchestration/V2-08.08 Elastic Scheduling#^sec-fleet-orchestration-elastic-policies|Elastic scaling policies]]** and the spot interruption profile of **[[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/02 Distributed ML/08 Fleet Orchestration/V2-08.14 Fallacies and Pitfalls#^sec-fleet-orchestration-fallacies|Fallacies and Pitfalls]]**, the price discount more than compensates for the interruptions, placing spot configurations on every point of the cost-time frontier except the single fastest completion time ([[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/03 The Fleet in Production/09 Fleet Efficiency/V2-09.10 Trading Between Factors#^fig-performance-engineering-efficiency-frontier|figure 14]]). Choosing between reserved and on-demand instances, retaining older accelerator generations at a lower internal chargeback rate, or introducing a second accelerator family all pose the exact same trade-off, and each option must be evaluated by cost per useful unit rather than by raw price per accelerator-hour.

Model quality and device memory bound the set of feasible configurations rather than ranking the points within it ([[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/03 The Fleet in Production/09 Fleet Efficiency/V2-09.10 Trading Between Factors#^tbl-performance-engineering-frontier-dimensions|table 12]]). A configuration that runs faster by lowering numerical precision, enlarging the global batch size, or relaxing gradient synchronization is valid only if it still reaches the measurement contract’s quality target, which is why time to result is always measured to a fixed quality criterion ([[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/03 The Fleet in Production/09 Fleet Efficiency/V2-09.11 Fallacies and Pitfalls#^sec-performance-engineering-fallacies|section 9.10]]). Similarly, a parallelism layout, microbatch size, or sequence length that exceeds device memory does not lie on the feasible frontier at all. The mechanisms that make it fit, such as recomputing activations or sharding model state more finely, purchase feasibility at the expense of program goodput.

Because different stakeholders value different points along the same frontier, an engineering proposal should state which frontier dimension it targets before being evaluated. A flagship training run facing a hard release deadline accepts a higher cost per useful unit to shorten its time to result, holding a larger allocation and waiting for a compact placement. A research sweep comprising hundreds of short jobs optimizes for cost per useful unit, favoring smaller allocations, preemptible spot capacity, and a shared compilation cache. The cluster infrastructure team optimizes for the pool’s overall MPG and favors policies that raise the fleet-wide product even when they lengthen individual jobs. The product rule determines whether a change moves the system toward the efficiency frontier, while the stakeholder’s operational priority determines where along that frontier to operate.

Any move along the frontier is verified using the same discipline as a single-step optimization. Engineers measure every affected goodput factor before and after the move under the measurement contract, over an observation window that captures both one-time setup costs and recurring per-step gains. They report the change in time to result alongside the ratio of the goodput product.

**Checkpoint 9.4: Trading between factors**

A lever that raises one goodput factor and lowers another is judged by the product of the factors’ ratios, and the efficiency frontier places the result against cost and time to result.

**Judging the trade**

**Ratios, not points**: A change raises program goodput by a tenth of its value and lowers runtime goodput by a twelfth of its value. Can you decide whether the product rose without knowing scheduling goodput, and explain why?

**Whose product**: Why must a preemption be judged on the pool’s product rather than on the product of the job it admits?

**Within one factor**: Why is the checkpoint interval a trade inside runtime goodput, and how does a preemption budget change it?

**Compilation and the frontier**

**Compile amortization**: When does a shared compiled artifact repay its build, and when does per-replica or per-launch compilation lower runtime goodput more than the compiled speed raises program goodput?

**Time and goodput**: Using the time-to-result equation, explain why holding a job to fewer devices can raise the fleet’s goodput while lengthening that job’s time to result.
