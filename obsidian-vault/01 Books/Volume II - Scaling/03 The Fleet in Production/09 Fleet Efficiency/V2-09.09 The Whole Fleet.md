# V2-09.09 The Whole Fleet

[[Home|Home]] · [[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/Volume 2 Overview|Volume Overview]] · [[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/03 The Fleet in Production/09 Fleet Efficiency/V2-09 Fleet Efficiency|V2-09 Fleet Efficiency]]

^sec-performance-engineering-whole-fleet

The Llama 2 70B job keeps 0.90 of its held time as retained steps, runs those steps at 0.48 of the ideal rate, and draws its nodes from a pool that holds 0.65 of its capacity in complete allocations. Each figure is accurate on its own boundary, yet none states alone how much of the fleet’s capacity becomes training progress or which engineering lever yields the largest return. Those answers emerge only from the product of [[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/03 The Fleet in Production/09 Fleet Efficiency/V2-09.03 ML Goodput#^eq-perf-ml-productivity-goodput|equation 1]], which also converts a fraction of fleet capacity into the unit cost of useful work.

### Decomposing the Running Example

^sec-performance-engineering-whole-fleet-decomposition

Evaluated solely by its runtime and program goodput inside its own allocation, the Llama 2 70B job appears nearly half efficient, yet the shared pool that supplies its nodes loses roughly another third of its capacity before any job holds a complete gang. Composing all three factors for one job on one fleet reveals where the entire efficiency gap goes across the cluster.

**Napkin Math 9.5: Where the running example's fleet capacity went**

**Problem**: The Llama 2 70B job holds 128 GPUs drawn from a shared pool. What share of the accelerator-hours the job holds becomes progress at the ideal rate, what share of the capacity the pool offers becomes progress, and at which boundary is the rest lost?

**Variables**:

- Scheduling goodput ${\eta_{\text{scheduling}}}$ = 0.65, the pool’s value from notebook [9.2](https://mlsysbook.ai/vol2/09_performance_engineering/09_performance_engineering.html#nbk-performance-engineering-shared-pool-scheduling-goodput).
- Runtime goodput ${\eta_{\text{runtime}}}$ = 0.90, composed from five owners’ terms in notebook [9.3](https://mlsysbook.ai/vol2/09_performance_engineering/09_performance_engineering.html#nbk-performance-engineering-runtime-loss-composition).
- Program goodput ${\eta_{\text{program}}}$ = 0.48, the per-microbatch fleet MFU of the retained steps from notebook [9.4](https://mlsysbook.ai/vol2/09_performance_engineering/09_performance_engineering.html#nbk-performance-engineering-scaling-tax); over a full optimizer step the amortized gradient reduction lowers it to about 0.456 ([[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/03 The Fleet in Production/09 Fleet Efficiency/V2-09.08 Recovering Program Goodput#^sec-performance-engineering-overlap|section 9.7.2]]).

**Math**:

- Job product, ${\eta_{\text{runtime}}}{\eta_{\text{program}}}$: 0.90 $\times$ 0.48 $\approx$ 0.43.
- Pool product, ${\text{MPG}} = {\eta_{\text{scheduling}}}{\eta_{\text{runtime}}}{\eta_{\text{program}}}$: 0.65 $\times$ 0.43 $\approx$ 0.28.
- Loss shares by [[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/03 The Fleet in Production/09 Fleet Efficiency/V2-09.03 ML Goodput#^eq-perf-goodput-loss-shares|equation 2]]: scheduling 1 $-$ 0.65 = 0.35; runtime 0.65 $\times$ 0.10 $\approx$ 0.06; program 0.59 $\times$ 0.52 $\approx$ 0.31, where 0.59 is ${\eta_{\text{scheduling}}}{\eta_{\text{runtime}}}$. With the 0.28 that became progress, the four parts add to one.
- Calibration: Llama 3 405B’s product of about 0.37 on its dedicated cluster (notebook [9.1](https://mlsysbook.ai/vol2/09_performance_engineering/09_performance_engineering.html#nbk-performance-engineering-llama3-loss-split)) becomes 0.65 $\times$ 0.37 $\approx$ 0.24 on this pool.

**Result**: The job turns about 43 percent of the accelerator-hours it holds into ideal-rate progress, and the pool turns about 28 percent of its offered capacity into progress. Of that offered capacity, 35 percent is lost before any job holds it, 6 percent is held without producing a retained step, and 31 percent is spent on retained steps that run below the ideal rate.

**Systems insight**: The scheduling layer multiplies every job’s product by the same 0.65, so even the best-documented large run on record, moved onto this pool, would deliver only about a quarter of the pool’s capacity as progress. No engineering inside a job recovers a loss that occurs before the job holds its devices.

Both products are meaningful ([[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/03 The Fleet in Production/09 Fleet Efficiency/V2-09.09 The Whole Fleet#^tbl-performance-engineering-goodput-decomposition|table 8]]), and they answer different questions. The job’s product (${\eta_{\text{runtime}}} \times {\eta_{\text{program}}}$) measures what the team running the job can improve directly: runtime and program losses occur inside its allocation, and a higher job product means fewer held hours per retained step and an earlier result. The pool product (${\text{MPG}}$) measures what the fleet’s owner pays for: capacity that never forms a complete allocation is still powered, depreciated, and billed. Raising the pool’s scheduling goodput does little for an individual job that already holds its allocation, because the capacity it recovers becomes complete allocations for other jobs waiting in the queue. Reporting only the job’s product hides the pool’s largest loss from the infrastructure team, whereas reporting only the pool’s product charges the workload team for a scheduling loss it cannot control.

The pool product also rests on an assumption that the job product does not require. Because it multiplies the pool’s scheduling goodput by this job’s runtime and program goodput, it describes a pool in which every workload runs like the running example. The pool-level runtime losses identified in [[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/03 The Fleet in Production/09 Fleet Efficiency/V2-09.06 Runtime Goodput#^sec-performance-engineering-runtime-composition|section 9.5.2]]—reserved blocks that no job uses and zombie allocations—lower the pool’s effective runtime goodput further, making the pool figure an upper bound.

Read as a map of where the fleet’s capacity went, [[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/03 The Fleet in Production/09 Fleet Efficiency/V2-09.09 The Whole Fleet#^tbl-performance-engineering-goodput-decomposition|table 8]] shows that two boundaries carry almost all of the loss. The capacity lost before allocation and the capacity lost inside slow steps are each several times larger than the capacity lost to startup, input waits, checkpoints, failures, and preemptions combined ([[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/03 The Fleet in Production/09 Fleet Efficiency/V2-09.09 The Whole Fleet#^fig-performance-engineering-goodput-waterfall|figure 12]]). Those runtime interruptions are nevertheless the losses that operators see most directly in job logs and restart alerts.

Comparing the running example against the Llama 3 405B column highlights how cluster scale shifts the balance between factors. The running example’s program goodput sits above the Llama 3 reference by construction, reflecting the expectation that a job on 16 nodes exposes less communication per step than a run across 16,384 H100 GPUs. Its runtime goodput matches the reference, although a different mix of losses produces it, as [[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/03 The Fleet in Production/09 Fleet Efficiency/V2-09.06 Runtime Goodput#^sec-performance-engineering-runtime-evidence|section 9.5.3]] analyzes.

Ranked by the share of the pool’s capacity lost at each boundary, scheduling comes first, program goodput comes second, and runtime goodput comes a distant third. However, a boundary’s loss share is not what an engineering lever recovers, because capacity recovered at an early boundary must still survive the later boundaries.

Instead, the product equation gives the marginal value of a lever directly. One percentage point of any factor is worth the product of the other two factors, because raising one factor by a small increment increases the overall product by that increment times the remaining two factors. For the running example, one point of scheduling goodput is worth 0.43 of a point of MPG, one point of runtime goodput is worth 0.31, and one point of program goodput is worth 0.59. A point of program goodput is worth the most because its multiplier is the product of the two highest factors, whereas a point of runtime goodput is worth the least because its multiplier is the product of the two lowest factors. Consequently, every factor’s lever is worth more on a fleet whose other two factors are already high.

The gains a lever can achieve are also bounded by its own structural ceiling, and those ceilings reinforce the ranking. Removing every partial-gang hold and filling every freed device raises scheduling goodput to at most 0.80 ([[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/03 The Fleet in Production/09 Fleet Efficiency/V2-09.05 Scheduling Goodput#^sec-performance-engineering-scheduling-evidence|section 9.4.3]]). Eliminating input waits—the largest runtime term in [[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/03 The Fleet in Production/09 Fleet Efficiency/V2-09.06 Runtime Goodput#^sec-performance-engineering-runtime-composition|section 9.5.2]]—raises runtime goodput to 0.98. Eliminating every loss the fleet adds beyond a single node’s baseline—mostly exposed communication ([[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/03 The Fleet in Production/09 Fleet Efficiency/V2-09.07 Program Goodput#^sec-performance-engineering-exposed-communication|section 9.6.2]])—raises program goodput to the single-node baseline of 0.65. Because each lever multiplies MPG by its factor’s relative gain,[[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/03 The Fleet in Production/09 Fleet Efficiency/V2-09.15 Footnotes#^fn8|⁸]] reaching those ceilings raises MPG by 23 percent (to 0.35) for the scheduling lever, by 9 percent (to 0.31) for the input pipeline, and by 35 percent (to 0.38) for the program lever. For this workload, the program lever yields the largest gain even though scheduling loses a larger share of the pool’s total capacity, and the input pipeline yields the smallest gain of the three. Although these ceilings are optimistic—no communication overlap hides all exposed transfers and no packer fills every freed device—the priority order holds unless one team’s practical reach falls far short of the others’. Still, this ranking is a property of the workload’s factors, here constructed, rather than a universal law of the accounting; a fleet running a different model archetype will arrive at a different priority order.

**Lighthouse 9.2: Archetype B (DLRM at Scale): Where a recommendation fleet loses goodput**

A recommendation model stores embedding tables that far exceed accelerator memory in host DRAM or across remote shards (**[[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/01 The Fleet/04 Data Storage/V2-04.04 The ML Storage Hierarchy#^sec-storage-tier1-dram|Tier 1 host DRAM]]**). When the embedding rows required by a batch are fetched ahead of the training step, they belong to the input path; if that path cannot keep pace with the step rate, the accelerators stall waiting for their batch. The input-wait rule of [[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/03 The Fleet in Production/09 Fleet Efficiency/V2-09.03 ML Goodput#^sec-performance-engineering-goodput-boundaries|section 9.2.3]] charges that stall to runtime goodput, and the data stall ratio of **[[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/01 The Fleet/04 Data Storage/V2-04.05 The Data Pipeline Equation#^sec-storage-pipeline-equation|The Data Pipeline Equation]]** sizes it. Inside a retained step, the gathered embedding vectors travel to their destination ranks through an AllToAll collective whose completion time is governed by the slowest destination under shard skew or fabric congestion (**[[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/02 Distributed ML/05 Collective Communication/V2-05.09 AllToAll Exchanges at Scale#^sec-communication-alltoall|AllToAll Exchanges at Scale]]**). Whatever portion of that exchange is not hidden by dense computation lengthens the retained step and is charged to program goodput. Because a recommendation step is bounded more by memory and network bytes moved than by dense arithmetic, its program goodput must define its ideal denominator with the same care as a bandwidth-bound serving replica. The engineering levers shift accordingly: runtime goodput rises with prefetching and hot-row caching, whereas program goodput rises with shard load balancing and message coalescing (**[[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/02 Distributed ML/06 Distributed Training/V2-06.06 Model Parallelism#^sec-distributed-training-systems-systems-parameter-servers-embedding-sharding-8821|Sparse embedding sharding]]**). The marginal value of one point of any factor remains the product of the other two factors, but the lever ranking must be computed from the recommendation fleet’s own measured numbers.

### Cost per Useful Unit

^sec-performance-engineering-cost-per-useful-unit

An organization pays for fleet capacity by the accelerator-hour regardless of whether a complete allocation holds it, whether a retained step uses it, or whether that step runs at the ideal rate. Every factor of the goodput decomposition therefore divides directly into the cost of useful work. Specifically, the price of one accelerator-hour of ideal-rate progress equals the raw capacity price divided by MPG. With the capacity price per accelerator-hour ${R_{\text{GPU/hr}}}$ and the cost of one useful accelerator-hour ${C_{\text{useful}}}$, [[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/03 The Fleet in Production/09 Fleet Efficiency/V2-09.09 The Whole Fleet#^eq-perf-cost-per-useful-unit|equation 6]] states this relationship:

$$
{C_{\text{useful}}} = \frac{{R_{\text{GPU/hr}}}}{{\text{MPG}}} = \frac{{R_{\text{GPU/hr}}}}{{\eta_{\text{scheduling}}} \times {\eta_{\text{runtime}}} \times {\eta_{\text{program}}}} \tag{6}
$$

^eq-perf-cost-per-useful-unit

This relation turns each goodput factor into an inverse cost multiplier: a loss at any boundary raises the price of every unit of useful output in exact proportion. Using the internal fleet reference rate of \$2/GPU-hour from **[[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/03 The Fleet in Production/12 ML Operations at Scale/V2-12.10 Fleet Economics and Utilization#^sec-ops-scale-fleet-economics|Fleet Economics and Utilization]]** as the cost of one in-service accelerator-hour, dividing by scheduling goodput charges the scheduling loss once, consistent with the in-service denominator defined in [[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/03 The Fleet in Production/09 Fleet Efficiency/V2-09.05 Scheduling Goodput#^sec-performance-engineering-scheduling-goodput-counts|section 9.4.1]]. Under this accounting, the running example’s pool pays roughly \$7.09/GPU-hour for each accelerator-hour of ideal-rate progress, or 3.5× the raw capacity price. Because any unit of useful output—whether a training token or a served token—requires a fixed number of ideal-rate accelerator-hours, its unit cost carries the exact same multiplier. The spot-instance cost equation (**[[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/02 Distributed ML/08 Fleet Orchestration/V2-08.09 Cost Optimization#^eq-fleet-orchestration-spot-cost|equation 8.3]]**) is the special case that divides the instance price by runtime goodput alone.

A chargeback policy that bills each workload team only for the accelerator-hours its jobs hold charges the running example 2.3× the raw rate per useful accelerator-hour and leaves the pool’s scheduling loss unbilled. How an organization assigns that unallocated pool cost is a matter of cost-attribution policy (**[[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/03 The Fleet in Production/12 ML Operations at Scale/V2-12.10 Fleet Economics and Utilization#^sec-ml-operations-scale-cost-visibility-attribution-3f3a|Cost visibility and attribution]]**).

Raising any goodput factor lowers the cost of every useful unit in proportion. Lifting the running example’s program goodput to its single-node ceiling would reduce the cost of useful work by 26 percent without purchasing a single additional accelerator. However, the cost multiplier alone does not dictate which improvement to pursue, because levers that raise one factor frequently lower another; [[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/03 The Fleet in Production/09 Fleet Efficiency/V2-09.10 Trading Between Factors#^sec-performance-engineering-frontier-objective|section 9.9]] evaluates those cross-boundary trade-offs using the full goodput product.
