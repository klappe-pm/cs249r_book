# V2-09.07 Program Goodput

[[Home|Home]] · [[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/Volume 2 Overview|Volume Overview]] · [[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/03 The Fleet in Production/09 Fleet Efficiency/V2-09 Fleet Efficiency|V2-09 Fleet Efficiency]]

^sec-performance-engineering-profiling

Even when a job holds a complete allocation and retains every step it executes, it can still waste most of its capacity if each retained step runs far below the speed its hardware allows. Within the held time of our Llama 2 70B training job, program goodput is the single largest source of loss—even though scheduling losses consume a larger share of the shared pool as a whole ([[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/03 The Fleet in Production/09 Fleet Efficiency/V2-09.09 The Whole Fleet#^sec-performance-engineering-whole-fleet-decomposition|section 9.8.1]]). Because the job’s retained steps run at a fleet MFU of 48 percent, its program goodput is about 0.48, meaning that 52 percent of the time spent inside retained steps produces no progress at the ideal rate.

Program goodput is also the only factor whose losses this chapter both diagnoses and recovers directly by changing how the step executes. Diagnosing a program goodput loss requires two complementary instruments: the iron law and the Fleet Law identify which analytical term of a step a loss lengthens, while a hierarchical step trace—read from the fleet metric down to individual hardware counters—shows which term is actually exposed on the critical path.

↩︎ Partitioning layouts and their communication patterns, which set most of a large job’s step structure, are established in **[[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/02 Distributed ML/06 Distributed Training/V2-06 Distributed Training#^sec-distributed-training-systems|Distributed Training]]**.

### The Iron Law and the Fleet Law Terms

^sec-performance-engineering-iron-law

Speeding up a kernel on a single accelerator often yields much less benefit—or none at all—once a job scales across a fleet, because the step also spends time moving data across memory hierarchies, waiting at synchronization barriers, and exchanging tensors over the network fabric. Separating what a local kernel change can reach from what requires a fleet-level change starts with the single-machine iron law ([[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/03 The Fleet in Production/09 Fleet Efficiency/V2-09.07 Program Goodput#^eq-iron-law-perf|equation 5]]). The iron law decomposes the local execution of each device into the data volume crossing a chosen memory boundary, the arithmetic operations performed, and the latency overhead that neither term explains:

$$
{T}= \frac{{D_{\text{vol}}}}{{\text{BW}}} + \frac{{O}}{{R_{\text{peak}}} \cdot {\eta_{\text{hw}}}} + {L_{\text{lat}}} \tag{5}
$$

^eq-iron-law-perf

Here ${D_{\text{vol}}}$ is the data volume crossing the chosen memory boundary, ${\text{BW}}$ is that boundary’s bandwidth, ${O}$ is the operation count, and ${R_{\text{peak}}} \cdot {\eta_{\text{hw}}}$ is the realized compute rate. At fleet scale, the overhead term ${L_{\text{lat}}}$ splits into two parts with distinct owners: host dispatch (the kernel launches that keep each accelerator fed) belongs to single-device execution, whereas exposed communication and synchronization exist only because the step spans multiple devices. **[[05 Reference/Volume II - Scaling/V2 Single-Machine Foundations#^sec-appdx-dam-taxonomy-iron-law-mapping|Iron Law Mapping]]** reviews these terms and their overlapped form.

The Fleet Law (**[[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/00 Introduction/01 Introduction/V2-01.06 The C³ Taxonomy and Foundations of Scale#^eq-fleet-law|equation 1.3]]**) models a distributed step as each device’s share of local work plus communication time plus synchronization time, minus the portion of communication and synchronization hidden behind computation. The single-machine iron law connects to the fleet through these terms. The data-movement and compute terms, together with the host-dispatch portion of ${L_{\text{lat}}}$, constitute local execution, whose unsharded reference time is ${T_{\text{compute}}}$ and whose per-device share across ${N}$ devices is ${T_{\text{compute}}}/{N}$. Meanwhile, the communication component of ${L_{\text{lat}}}$ becomes the Fleet Law’s ${T_{\text{comm}}}({N})$, the synchronization component becomes ${T_{\text{sync}}}({N})$, and any communication or synchronization hidden behind useful computation is credited back through ${T_{\text{overlap}}}$. Profiling one device of an already-partitioned job measures the per-device share of local execution directly, because partitioning has already divided the local workload, so the measured time must not be divided by ${N}$ a second time. Throughout the remainder of this chapter, “overhead” refers to host dispatch unless communication is named explicitly. A local kernel optimization therefore improves fleet performance only through the specific term it shortens, and it shortens the overall step only by the portion of that term that was exposed on the critical path.

Every program goodput loss cataloged in [[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/03 The Fleet in Production/09 Fleet Efficiency/V2-09.03 ML Goodput#^tbl-performance-engineering-goodput-boundaries|table 1]] lengthens one of these Fleet Law terms. Memory stalls, sub-peak arithmetic, and slow host dispatch all lengthen local execution, whereas exposed collectives lengthen ${T_{\text{comm}}}({N}) - {T_{\text{overlap}}}$. Stragglers and pipeline bubbles both manifest in ${T_{\text{sync}}}({N})$ as healthy devices wait at a barrier or stage boundary for upstream work to finish, whereas activation recomputation inflates ${O}$ beyond the work required by the model itself. Each mechanism leaves a distinct signature in a step trace and answers to its own engineering lever ([[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/03 The Fleet in Production/09 Fleet Efficiency/V2-09.07 Program Goodput#^tbl-performance-engineering-program-loss-terms|table 6]]).

^tbl-performance-engineering-program-loss-terms

Although the coordination term of the Fleet Law also encompasses checkpoint writes, failure detection, and failure recovery, those intervals occur *between* retained steps and are charged to runtime goodput ([[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/03 The Fleet in Production/09 Fleet Efficiency/V2-09.06 Runtime Goodput#^sec-performance-engineering-runtime-goodput|section 9.5]]). The Fleet Law’s coordination term therefore straddles two goodput factors: only the barrier and pipeline-stage waits *inside* a retained step lower program goodput. Put simply, the Fleet Law term identifies the physical mechanism and its engineering lever, while the goodput boundary identifies which factor pays the cost.

This mapping also bounds how much a single-device speedup can help at scale. Because a kernel optimization shortens only ${T_{\text{compute}}}/{N}$, at a fixed global batch its share of the total step shrinks as ${N}$ grows and ${T_{\text{compute}}}$ is divided across more devices, while communication and synchronization do not shrink in proportion. Under weak scaling, as in the running example, each device’s share of local work stays constant while communication and synchronization grow, so the kernel’s share shrinks in that regime too. Consequently, the very same kernel speedup delivers a smaller end-to-end gain on a larger job. Optimizations can also shift cost from one term to another rather than eliminating it: recomputing activations to free HBM capacity adds floating-point operations to ${O}$, illustrating that the displacement of overhead (principle [[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/02 Distributed ML/V2 Distributed ML Principles#^pri-conservation-overhead|14]]) operates within a single step just as it does across the C$^3$ dimensions.

The serving replica applies the exact same decomposition to a single decode step. Because each of its eight devices holds one shard of the 70B model’s weights, a small-batch decode step must stream its entire weight shard from HBM to perform only a handful of operations per weight, making the data-movement term ${D_{\text{vol}}}/{\text{BW}}$ dominant. Meanwhile, the tensor-parallel activation exchange in every transformer layer forms its communication term. The replica’s program goodput is therefore governed by how closely its decode step approaches the minimum time required to stream its weights—a relationship that the roofline model makes concrete in [[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/03 The Fleet in Production/09 Fleet Efficiency/V2-09.07 Program Goodput#^sec-performance-engineering-roofline|section 9.6.3]].

**Checkpoint 9.2: The iron law and the Fleet Law terms**

Program goodput falls only through the terms of a step, and a change helps only through the term it shortens.

**Iron-law estimate**: For a step that is 60 percent data movement, 30 percent compute, and 10 percent overhead, can you estimate which single change buys the most wall-clock time, and what the iron law predicts once that dominant term is halved?

**Fleet Law term**: Can you name the Fleet Law term a fused kernel shortens, and explain why its fleet gain shrinks as ${N}$ grows?

**Barrier waits**: Can you explain why a rank that waits at a barrier for a slow peer is charged to program goodput, while a job that waits for a failed node to be replaced is charged to runtime goodput?

### Exposed Communication at Fleet Scale

^sec-performance-engineering-exposed-communication

A job distributed across many nodes must exchange data over the inter-node network fabric, which is slower than intra-node links, shared with competing jobs, and traversed by collectives whose volume and latency grow with participant count. Whatever portion of that exchange the execution schedule cannot hide behind useful computation lengthens every retained step. That exposed communication interval is what separates a multi-node fleet job from the same workload on a single node, and because the job holds a complete allocation and retains every slowed step, the loss belongs entirely to program goodput.

↩︎ How much communication a schedule can hide, and why the hidden fraction falls as collectives grow relative to the compute window, are established in **[[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/02 Distributed ML/05 Collective Communication/V2-05.11 Communication-Computation Overlap#^sec-communication-overlap-limits|Overlap limits and exposed communication]]**.

Our running example isolates this multi-node penalty in a single comparison, with constructed step times standing in for measured ones. Because its single-node baseline and its 128-GPU fleet configuration train the same model with the same per-device workload, the ratio of their MFUs estimates what scaling across the fabric costs the program. It is an estimate rather than an exact measure because the two configurations can also differ in other ways, such as the node baseline’s offload of optimizer state to host memory.

**Napkin Math 9.4: From one node to 128 GPUs**

**Problem**: The 70B parameter training job of the running example achieves a node baseline of 65 percent MFU on one 8 GPUs node, with optimizer state offloaded to host memory because the full training state of a 70B model exceeds one node’s HBM. Scaled to 128 GPUs with the same work on each device, its constructed microbatch-scale step time rises from 180 ms to 245 ms. What program goodput does the job keep at fleet scale, and how much of the node’s efficiency does scale cost?

**Math**: Both MFU figures apply [[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/03 The Fleet in Production/09 Fleet Efficiency/V2-09.03 ML Goodput#^eq-perf-program-goodput-mfu|equation 3]], the useful FLOPs of a step divided by the FLOPs the devices could execute at peak in that step’s wall time.

Step 1: Fleet MFU. A step performs 14.9 PFLOP of useful work, while 128 GPUs $\times$ 989 TFLOP/s per GPU $\times$ 245 ms could execute 31 PFLOP at peak, so fleet MFU is 14.9 PFLOP ÷ 31 PFLOP $\approx$ 48 percent. The same substitution on the node gives 65 percent.

Step 2: Efficiency relative to the node. Because each device does the same work in both configurations, the ratio of the two MFUs is the ratio of the two step times, 180 ms ÷ 245 ms $\approx$ 74 percent.

Step 3: Time beyond the node. Each fleet step spends 180 ms on the node’s work plus 65 ms that the node step did not, about 27 percent of the fleet step.

**Result**: The job keeps program goodput of about 0.48 and 74 percent of the node’s efficiency, losing 26 percent of it to scale.

**Systems insight**: The 65 ms per step beyond the node baseline bounds what exposed communication and synchronization can claim, but it does not by itself isolate the fabric, since offload, kernel shapes, and barrier waits can also change between the two configurations. The bound becomes an attribution only when a step trace shows which intervals fill the added time.

The ratio computed in the notebook is the scaling efficiency of **[[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/02 Distributed ML/06 Distributed Training/V2-06.05 Scaling Efficiency and Convergence#^eq-distributed-training-scaling-efficiency|equation 6.1]]**—referenced here to a single node rather than to a single device as in **[[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/02 Distributed ML/06 Distributed Training/V2-06.05 Scaling Efficiency and Convergence#^sec-distributed-training-systems-systems-physics-scaling-amdahls-law-communication-4d7f|Scaling physics and Amdahl's Law with communication]]**—and it completes the metric reconciliation begun in [[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/03 The Fleet in Production/09 Fleet Efficiency/V2-09.03 ML Goodput#^sec-performance-engineering-fleet-efficiency-metric-dff4|section 9.2.2]]. A job’s fleet MFU equals its single-node baseline MFU multiplied by its scaling efficiency from that node (65 percent $\times$ 0.74 $\approx$ 48 percent), and that product, evaluated over retained steps, is its program goodput. An engineering report may quote the fleet MFU directly, or it may quote the single-node MFU alongside the scaling efficiency, but it must never multiply the fleet MFU by the scaling efficiency a second time, which would double-count the multi-node penalty. Because the single-node baseline already includes intra-node NVLink collectives and host-offload traffic, factoring fleet MFU at the node boundary bounds the cross-node scaling cost against a baseline that engineers can easily reproduce.

In the language of the Fleet Law, the additional 65 ms per step reflects growth in ${T_{\text{comm}}}({N}) + {T_{\text{sync}}}({N}) - {T_{\text{overlap}}}$ alongside any configuration difference in local execution, and only a step trace can separate those contributions. In the constructed diagnosis that [[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/03 The Fleet in Production/09 Fleet Efficiency/V2-09.04 Measuring Goodput#^sec-performance-engineering-localize-factor|section 9.3.2]] walked through, the trace showed no rank consistently lagging its peers but did show exposed cross-node synchronization and collective waits on every microbatch step. Had the trace instead shown fifteen nodes waiting at a barrier for one slow node, the very same step-time increase would have belonged to ${T_{\text{sync}}}({N})$—yielding the same program goodput, but pointing to node replacement rather than to the communication terms as the place to act.

The Llama 3 405B training run provides a production reference point: it sustained an MFU of about 41 percent across 16,384 GPUs[[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/03 The Fleet in Production/09 Fleet Efficiency/V2-09.15 Footnotes#^fn7|⁷]] under a parallelism layout tuned by the team that built the cluster ([[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/03 The Fleet in Production/09 Fleet Efficiency/V2-09.14 Source References#^ref-dubey2024llama|Dubey et al. 2024]]). The 128-GPU running example’s slightly higher program goodput is a constructed value, not a measured comparison. It was set above Llama 3’s figure to reflect the expectation that a job spanning far fewer devices spends a smaller fraction of each step in cross-node collectives, and it says nothing about the quality of either run’s kernels. Both runs surrender roughly half of their retained step time relative to the peak arithmetic ceiling; the task of performance diagnosis is to determine which specific Fleet Law terms hold that lost half.

### Drilling into the Step

^sec-performance-engineering-roofline

Inspecting hardware counters on an arbitrary kernel before knowing which rank or stream delayed the step wastes engineering effort on operations that may not even lie on the critical path. Once a loss has been localized to program goodput, the step trace becomes the primary evidence, and it must be read from the top down. Diagnosis descends through four hierarchical levels, and the lower three expand the step-trace rung of [[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/03 The Fleet in Production/09 Fleet Efficiency/V2-09.04 Measuring Goodput#^sec-performance-engineering-localize-factor|section 9.3.2]] ([[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/03 The Fleet in Production/09 Fleet Efficiency/V2-09.07 Program Goodput#^fig-optimization-hierarchy|figure 6]]):

1.  **Job and fleet metrics** (step time against plan, MFU of retained steps, and per-rank step timers) detect that a program goodput loss exists.
2.  **Framework and distributed traces** correlate step time with model operations across ranks, revealing whether a lagging rank or an exposed collective is lengthening the step.
3.  **Kernel timelines** expose host launch gaps and the stream-level overlap between computation and communication on a single rank.
4.  **Hardware performance counters** measure achieved memory bandwidth, SM occupancy, and instruction mix for an individual kernel.

Each lower level is collected only for the specific ranks and time windows flagged by the level above it. **[[05 Reference/Volume II - Scaling/V2 Single-Machine Foundations#^sec-appdx-dam-taxonomy-tooling-map|Tooling Map]]** maps the single-device profiling tools used at each rung.

![[90 Assets/Volume II/09 Performance Engineering/Profiling Hierarchy c8443640ea.svg]]

Figure 6: **The Profiling Hierarchy**: Diagnosis of a program goodput loss descends from the fleet measure that detected it to the hardware root cause. Each rung names the evidence that level exposes and a representative tool; the navy label under each tool gives the scope and time scale one sample covers, and each highlighted window expands into the trace below it. Each lower level trades breadth for the detail needed to isolate a root cause, and the three lower levels together are the step-trace rung of factor localization.

^fig-optimization-hierarchy

The constructed diagnosis of our running example descends three of the four levels. First, distributed traces across a data-parallel group show that no rank lags consistently behind its peers, which rules out a hardware straggler, and they reveal collective intervals that widen with device count. Next, a kernel timeline on a representative rank shows those collectives executing on the communication stream with no concurrent kernels on the compute stream during the latter part of the backward pass. That timeline establishes that those collectives are exposed, but not why. Separating a poor collective schedule from a transfer too long for the backward compute window requires comparing the collective’s transfer time with that window (**[[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/02 Distributed ML/05 Collective Communication/V2-05.11 Communication-Computation Overlap#^sec-communication-overlap-limits|Overlap limits and exposed communication]]**). For the job’s data-axis gradient reduction the comparison is decisive, because that transfer takes longer than the entire microbatch step. No schedule can hide it within one step, so the job runs it once per optimizer step after accumulating gradients over many microbatches ([[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/03 The Fleet in Production/09 Fleet Efficiency/V2-09.08 Recovering Program Goodput#^sec-performance-engineering-overlap|section 9.7.2]]). The exposed intervals on every microbatch step are, by assumption of the constructed example, per-microbatch synchronization across the nodes and the collective waits it causes, not a transfer the example sizes. Hardware counters, the lowest level, are unnecessary because the compute kernels flanking the exposed collectives already run at their expected rates, and the descent stops as soon as the responsible Fleet Law term is identified.

**Systems Perspective 9.2: The cost of tracing a step**

Tracing adds work to the step it measures. Recording every kernel launch, memory allocation, and collective consumes host cycles and memory and can add synchronization, so a traced step can run slower, or with a different overlap between communication and computation, than the untraced steps it is meant to explain. The perturbation grows with the events and options collected. A trace is therefore evidence about the structure of a step, while the step’s duration is measured from the untraced job timeline, and comparing an instrumented with an uninstrumented window of the same job shows how much the trace changed.

The window must also describe the job’s steady state. A profiler’s own warmup steps let the profiler start up before it records, but they do not ensure that the application has finished compiling, allocating memory, and initializing its devices, so the window is chosen from step times that have stopped changing. Startup that falls inside the trace belongs to runtime goodput and is measured from the timeline, not from the trace.

A first-order bound on program goodput needs no trace at all. The diagnostic from **[[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/02 Distributed ML/08 Fleet Orchestration/V2-08.13 Debugging Cluster Utilization#^sec-fleet-orchestration-anti-patterns|Common utilization anti-patterns]]** compares total step duration against compute-only step time (the forward and backward passes alone). Because ideal hardware step time cannot exceed compute-only time, the ratio of compute-only time to actual step time is an immediate upper bound on program goodput.

Systematic triage of a slow step begins outside the accelerator kernels. If a step misses its duration target while accelerator utilization is low, the bottleneck lies in input stalls, host preprocessing, or exposed communication—because optimizing a kernel cannot speed up a device that is waiting for work to arrive. Only when the accelerator is actively executing kernels does diagnosis turn to whether those kernels are bound by arithmetic throughput or memory bandwidth, evaluated against the roofline’s ridge point (the operational intensity where the memory-bandwidth slope meets the compute ceiling). Organized in order of application, these checks form a diagnostic decision tree ([[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/03 The Fleet in Production/09 Fleet Efficiency/V2-09.07 Program Goodput#^fig-iron-law-flowchart|figure 7]]) whose initial branches exit program goodput altogether. A device waiting for its next batch or for host-side preprocessing is losing runtime goodput at the input boundary, which returns the investigation to [[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/03 The Fleet in Production/09 Fleet Efficiency/V2-09.06 Runtime Goodput#^sec-performance-engineering-runtime-goodput|section 9.5]].

Low accelerator activity sends the diagnosis right, toward waits, and high activity sends it down to the roofline. Every path returns to re-profiling.

Every path in the flowchart concludes by re-profiling the workload, because an optimization counts only if the measured step becomes shorter under the contract of [[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/03 The Fleet in Production/09 Fleet Efficiency/V2-09.04 Measuring Goodput#^sec-performance-engineering-measurement-contract|section 9.3.1]]. Removing one exposed interval frequently uncovers the next, shifting the critical path and restarting the diagnostic cycle.

Once the flowchart confirms that an accelerator is busy, its kernels are evaluated against the roofline model. A kernel with arithmetic intensity ${I}$ can achieve at most $\min({R_{\text{peak}}}, {I}\times {\text{BW}})$, and the ridge point ${I_{\text{ridge}}} = {R_{\text{peak}}}/{\text{BW}}$ divides the memory-bound slope of that envelope from its compute-bound plateau ([[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/03 The Fleet in Production/09 Fleet Efficiency/V2-09.14 Source References#^ref-williams2009|Williams et al. 2009]]). Whereas **[[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/01 The Fleet/02 Compute Infrastructure/V2-02.04 The Memory Wall#^sec-compute-roofline|Roofline model]]** introduced the Roofline Model from hardware specifications and algorithmic bounds, performance engineering places each kernel on the roofline using the operations and memory traffic measured during the actual step that lost program goodput.

**Definition 9.4: Measured arithmetic intensity**

***Measured arithmetic intensity*** is the ratio of the floating-point operations a kernel actually executes to the bytes observed crossing a named memory boundary, such as HBM, during that execution (FLOP per byte).

1.  **Significance**: It places a profiled kernel on the same roofline as the bandwidth (${\text{BW}}$) and compute (${R_{\text{peak}}}$) ceilings, so its distance below the algorithmic estimate exposes traffic the algorithm did not require.
2.  **Distinction**: Unlike *algorithmic intensity*, which counts the data movement an idealized algorithm requires, measured intensity includes tiling, cache behavior, fusion, padding, and redundant transfers.
3.  **Common pitfall**: A roofline point is interpretable only when its FLOP convention and memory boundary match the ceilings on the same plot.

Placing a profiled kernel on the roofline proceeds in three steps. First, hardware counters for the kernel report its achieved FLOP rate and its achieved bandwidth across a named memory boundary. Second, dividing the achieved FLOP rate by the achieved bandwidth yields the kernel’s measured arithmetic intensity, which reveals excess memory traffic whenever it falls below the algorithmic estimate. Third, comparing the achieved FLOP rate against the roofline ceiling at that measured intensity classifies the kernel into one of three regimes:

1.  **Far below both ceilings.** The kernel suffers from an execution inefficiency rather than a hardware resource limit—such as serial dependencies, poor instruction mix, or insufficient parallelism—and the remedy lies in how the kernel is written or selected.
2.  **Near the bandwidth ceiling.** The kernel is saturating memory bandwidth; only transferring fewer bytes (through operator fusion, tiling, or a narrower numeric representation) can speed it up.
3.  **Near the compute ceiling.** The kernel is saturating the arithmetic units; only performing fewer operations or switching to a faster supported numeric format or accelerator can reduce its duration.

Whereas the roofline narrows the set of candidate levers for an individual kernel, the step trace determines whether that kernel occupies enough of the critical path to warrant optimization.

A normalization kernel illustrates this three-step placement. Suppose hardware counters for a LayerNorm kernel on an H100 report 5 TFLOP/s of achieved compute and 2.8 TB/s of achieved HBM bandwidth. Its measured arithmetic intensity is 5 TFLOP/s ÷ 2.8 TB/s $\approx$ 1.8 FLOP/byte—more than two orders of magnitude below the H100’s FP16 ridge point of about 295 FLOP/byte. The kernel therefore sits squarely on the memory-bandwidth slope, and its achieved bandwidth is already 83.6 percent of the 3.35 TB/s hardware peak. Faster arithmetic units cannot speed it up at all, and tuning its memory access pattern can recover at most the remaining sixth of headroom to the ceiling. Any substantial speedup must come from reducing ${D_{\text{vol}}}$: either by fusing the normalization into the preceding operator so the intermediate tensor never round-trips through HBM, or by adopting a narrower numeric format.

The kernels of our 70B training job divide along the same line. Large matrix multiplications in the attention projections and feed-forward blocks operate above the ridge point near the compute ceiling. By contrast, normalization, activation functions, softmax, residual additions, and the optimizer update sit far down the memory-bandwidth slope just like LayerNorm, executing a tiny fraction of the step’s useful FLOPs while consuming a disproportionate share of local execution time. Comparing the time spent in matrix multiplications against the time spent in all other kernels therefore provides a rapid check on local program goodput losses. A step dominated by fast matrix multiplications loses its program goodput primarily to exposed communication and synchronization (as in our running example). A step spending substantial time in non-GEMM kernels, by contrast, has a local memory-traffic loss that operator fusion can recover.

The serving replica’s decode step sits even farther down the bandwidth slope. At batch size one, each of its eight accelerators must stream its entire weight shard from HBM to emit a single token. That minimum streaming time, about 5.2 ms per token (**[[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/01 The Fleet/02 Compute Infrastructure/V2-02.04 The Memory Wall#^sec-compute-infrastructure-memory-bandwidth-token-latency-1b38|Memory bandwidth and token latency]]**), serves as the named ideal for the replica’s program goodput. Even at that ideal floor, the H100 tensor cores run at only 0.3 percent of their FP16 peak, rendering MFU uninformative. Instead, the replica’s program goodput is measured by MBU: the ratio of the weight-streaming floor time to the measured step time ([[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/03 The Fleet in Production/09 Fleet Efficiency/V2-09.03 ML Goodput#^sec-performance-engineering-fleet-efficiency-metric-dff4|section 9.2.2]]).

Because the weight-streaming floor counts only model parameters, a measured decode step always takes longer. Reading the key-value cache, executing the per-layer tensor-parallel AllReduce, and dispatching dozens of small kernels per layer all lengthen the step and lower MBU, appearing as program goodput losses of the replica. How large a batch the replica can accumulate to climb the roofline slope toward the ridge point is governed by its latency service-level objective in **[[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/03 The Fleet in Production/10 Serving at Scale/V2-10 Serving at Scale#^sec-inference-scale|Serving at Scale]]**.

### Common Bottleneck Patterns

^sec-performance-engineering-bottleneck-patterns

An accelerator that idles *between* steps waiting for its next input batch shows the same drop in utilization as an accelerator that idles *within* a step waiting for a collective to finish. Yet the first is losing runtime goodput and answers to the data pipeline, whereas the second is losing program goodput and answers to communication overlap. Recognizing recurring trace signatures provides fast diagnostic hypotheses ([[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/03 The Fleet in Production/09 Fleet Efficiency/V2-09.07 Program Goodput#^tbl-performance-engineering-bottleneck-patterns|table 7]]), and identifying which goodput factor a signature lowers is just as important as identifying its mechanism, since not every slow-step symptom is a program loss.

**Self-Check: Question**

1.  A training step’s wall-clock time is decomposed with the iron law ($T = D_{\text{vol}}/\text{BW} + O/(R_{\text{peak}} \cdot \eta_{\text{hw}}) + L_{\text{lat}}$) into 60% memory traffic, 30% compute, and 10% dispatch and latency overhead. A hardware upgrade doubles peak compute $R_{\text{peak}}$ while leaving memory bandwidth and overhead unchanged. What is the maximum speedup this upgrade can achieve on this workload?

    1.  $2.00\times$, because doubling peak compute halves the execution time of the matrix multiplications
    2.  $1.67\times$, because the compute and overhead fractions are both accelerated by the faster arithmetic
    3.  About $1.18\times$, because doubling compute only reduces the 30% compute slice to 15% while the 70% non-compute time remains
    4.  $1.43\times$, because the memory-bound portion is compressed by the faster arithmetic units

2.  The running example reaches 65% MFU on one 8-GPU node and 48% fleet MFU on 128 GPUs with the same work on each device, its step time rising from 180 ms to 245 ms. How much of the node’s efficiency does scale cost, and how should a report state the job’s program goodput?

    1.  17%, the direct difference in MFU points, and program goodput is $0.65 \times 0.74$ multiplied again by the fleet MFU
    2.  About 26% (a scaling efficiency of about 74%), and program goodput is either the fleet MFU of 0.48 or the node MFU times the scaling efficiency, never the fleet MFU times the scaling efficiency
    3.  About 52%, the unused share of peak, all of which is exposed communication
    4.  About 26%, and program goodput is $0.48 \times 0.74 \approx 0.36$ because the scale loss must be charged separately

3.  Synchronized per-rank traces record the same collective in otherwise matched steps. In the baseline, every rank enters at 8 ms and all finish at 10 ms. In case A, all ranks but one enter at 8 ms, the last enters at 12 ms, and all finish at 14 ms. In case B, every rank enters at 8 ms and all finish at 14 ms. Which Fleet Law term does each case lengthen, and why do both lower the same goodput factor while pointing to different lever owners?

4.  Describe the top-down drill-down from a fleet measure to a hardware root cause for a program goodput loss, and explain why the step’s duration is read from the untraced job timeline rather than from the trace.

5.  Counters for a LayerNorm kernel on an H100 report 5 TFLOP/s of achieved arithmetic and 2.8 TB/s of achieved memory bandwidth, against a peak bandwidth of 3.35 TB/s and an FP16 ridge of about 295 FLOP/byte. Which lever can deliver a material gain?

    1.  Faster arithmetic units, because the kernel achieves only a tiny fraction of peak FLOP/s
    2.  A better memory schedule inside the kernel, because achieved bandwidth is far below peak
    3.  Graph replay, because a low FLOP rate always indicates host dispatch overhead
    4.  Moving fewer bytes, by fusing the normalization with the operation that produces its input or by a supported smaller representation
