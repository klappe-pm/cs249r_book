# V2-09.03 ML Goodput

[[Home|Home]] · [[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/Volume 2 Overview|Volume Overview]] · [[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/03 The Fleet in Production/09 Fleet Efficiency/V2-09 Fleet Efficiency|V2-09 Fleet Efficiency]]

^sec-performance-engineering-measurement-scale

Across a fleet, capacity disappears at three successive stages in the life of a job. First, a job must acquire a complete set of devices: an accelerator produces no progress while it sits unallocated, while cluster fragmentation strands it in a shape no queued gang can use, or while a partially allocated gang holds it idle waiting for the rest of its peers. Second, once a job holds a complete allocation, its execution must produce durable progress: initialization, input stalls, synchronous checkpoint pauses, failure recovery, and steps re-executed after a rollback all consume held device-hours without advancing the last saved checkpoint. Third, the steps that do survive in a checkpoint must execute near the rate the hardware can sustain. A step that exposes collective communication, waits on a straggling rank or a pipeline bubble, stalls on memory traffic, or dispatches long chains of tiny kernels occupies healthy accelerators while extracting only a fraction of their capability.

### The Three Goodput Factors

^sec-performance-engineering-goodput-factors

Run-level goodput, introduced with the Coordination dimension in **[[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/00 Introduction/01 Introduction/V2-01.06 The C³ Taxonomy and Foundations of Scale#^sec-vol2-introduction-dim-coord|Orchestration coordination layer]]**, tracks a single job across its execution and discounts time lost to failures and replayed steps. Because that measure starts only after the job holds its accelerators and stops at the boundary of a completed step, it misses both the capacity the job never received and the execution efficiency of the steps the job kept. Google’s fleet-efficiency framework extends the measurement window backward to the capacity offered by the fleet and forward to the ideal execution time of each retained step (the time its required work would take at the peak rate of the hardware resource that limits it), factoring useful fleet output into three multiplicative terms ([[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/03 The Fleet in Production/09 Fleet Efficiency/V2-09.14 Source References#^ref-wongpanich2025fleet|Wongpanich et al. 2025]]).[[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/03 The Fleet in Production/09 Fleet Efficiency/V2-09.15 Footnotes#^fn1|¹]]

**Definition 9.1: ML productivity goodput (MPG)**

***ML productivity goodput (MPG)*** is the fraction of a fleet’s capacity-time that becomes useful work at the hardware’s ideal rate, expressed as the product of scheduling, runtime, and program goodput, each measured on a fixed accounting boundary so that every lost interval is charged to exactly one factor ([[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/03 The Fleet in Production/09 Fleet Efficiency/V2-09.14 Source References#^ref-wongpanich2025fleet|Wongpanich et al. 2025]]).

1.  **Significance**: It converts the losses that scheduling, recovery, and execution each report in their own units into one fraction of fleet capacity, so a loss at one boundary can be compared directly with an equal loss at another, and an accelerator-hour’s price divided by it is the cost of one ideal-rate hour of useful work.
2.  **Distinction**: Unlike utilization, which counts an accelerator as used whenever it is allocated or busy, it counts only retained work at its ideal rate. Unlike run-level goodput, it also charges the capacity a job never received and the rate at which its kept steps ran.
3.  **Common pitfall**: A frequent mistake is to multiply factors measured over different windows or against overlapping denominators, such as a run-averaged MFU that already includes restart time and a runtime factor that charges the same restarts again. The product is meaningful only when each factor divides by exactly what the previous one passed.

The three factors follow a job chronologically, and each factor takes as its denominator the capacity-time that survived the stage before it. **Scheduling goodput** (${\eta_{\text{scheduling}}}$) is the fraction of fleet capacity-time held by complete allocations—intervals in which a job holds every resource its synchronous execution requires, which the fleet-efficiency literature calls all-allocated time ([[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/03 The Fleet in Production/09 Fleet Efficiency/V2-09.14 Source References#^ref-wongpanich2025fleet|Wongpanich et al. 2025]]). **Runtime goodput** (${\eta_{\text{runtime}}}$) is the fraction of that all-allocated time spent on steps that a checkpoint retains, excluding initialization, input stalls, synchronous checkpoint pauses, failure recovery, and work discarded after the last checkpoint; its numerator is productive time. **Program goodput** (${\eta_{\text{program}}}$) is the ratio of the predicted ideal execution time of those retained steps to their actual productive time, capturing the efficiency of code, compiler, and communication against a named hardware ideal. In the Llama 3 405B run, effective training time serves as runtime goodput and MFU serves as program goodput; on a shared multi-tenant cluster, a scheduling factor below one multiplies both. [[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/03 The Fleet in Production/09 Fleet Efficiency/V2-09.03 ML Goodput#^eq-perf-ml-productivity-goodput|Equation 1]] combines the three factors into MPG:

$$
{\text{MPG}} = {\eta_{\text{scheduling}}} \times {\eta_{\text{runtime}}} \times {\eta_{\text{program}}} \tag{1}
$$

^eq-perf-ml-productivity-goodput

The three factors multiply cleanly because each factor’s denominator is the preceding factor’s numerator. Scheduling goodput divides held all-allocated time by offered capacity-time, runtime goodput divides retained productive step time by all-allocated time, and program goodput divides predicted ideal time by productive step time. Adjacent numerators and denominators cancel, leaving the ideal-rate time of useful work divided by the total capacity-time the fleet offered ([[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/03 The Fleet in Production/09 Fleet Efficiency/V2-09.03 ML Goodput#^fig-performance-engineering-goodput-boundaries|figure 1]]). MPG is therefore a dimensionless fraction of fleet capacity rather than a raw throughput rate: useful fleet output equals peak capacity multiplied by MPG, and the effective cost per unit of useful work equals the cost of raw capacity divided by MPG. That telescoping cancellation also defines when the product is valid. If two factors include the same lost interval in their denominators, that loss is counted twice; if a gap falls between one factor’s numerator and the next factor’s denominator, the loss goes uncounted in both.

Each factor captures the losses from one stage of a job’s lifecycle ([[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/03 The Fleet in Production/09 Fleet Efficiency/V2-09.02 Where Fleet Capacity Goes#^sec-performance-engineering-memory-wall|section 9.1]]), and the three goodput boundaries cut across the C$^3$ taxonomy rather than mapping one-to-one onto its axes. Hiding collective communication behind computation raises program goodput, whereas checkpointing and failure recovery—which the Fleet Law places under Coordination—reduce runtime goodput. Goodput factors identify *when* in a job’s execution capacity was lost, while the C$^3$ dimensions identify *which physical mechanism* caused the loss; diagnosing a fleet bottleneck therefore names the goodput factor first and the C$^3$ mechanism inside it second.

Because the three factors multiply, the share of total fleet capacity lost at any boundary equals that boundary’s fractional loss weighted by the capacity share that reached it. [[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/03 The Fleet in Production/09 Fleet Efficiency/V2-09.03 ML Goodput#^eq-perf-goodput-loss-shares|Equation 2]] partitions the total efficiency gap $1 - {\eta_{\text{scheduling}}}{\eta_{\text{runtime}}}{\eta_{\text{program}}}$ into three additive shares—the scheduling loss on offered capacity, the runtime loss on held capacity, and the program loss on retained step time:

$$
\begin{aligned} 1 - {\eta_{\text{scheduling}}}{\eta_{\text{runtime}}}{\eta_{\text{program}}} &= (1-{\eta_{\text{scheduling}}}) \\ &\quad + {\eta_{\text{scheduling}}}(1-{\eta_{\text{runtime}}}) \\ &\quad + {\eta_{\text{scheduling}}}{\eta_{\text{runtime}}}(1-{\eta_{\text{program}}}) \end{aligned} \tag{2}
$$

^eq-perf-goodput-loss-shares

The three right-hand terms sum to the total gap, reflecting the rule that every lost device-hour is charged at exactly one boundary. Although the product ${\eta_{\text{scheduling}}}{\eta_{\text{runtime}}}{\eta_{\text{program}}}$ is commutative, the additive share attributed to each stage depends on the order in which capacity passes through the boundaries. The decomposition therefore follows the chronological order in which a workload encounters the three losses. Applied to the published Llama 3 405B telemetry, [[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/03 The Fleet in Production/09 Fleet Efficiency/V2-09.03 ML Goodput#^eq-perf-goodput-loss-shares|equation 2]] shows immediately which boundary consumed most of the cluster’s held capacity.

**Napkin Math 9.1: Where Llama 3's held capacity went**

**Problem**: Llama 3 405B trained on a dedicated cluster of 16,384 GPUs, kept more than 90 percent of its elapsed time as effective training time, and ran its kept steps at about 41 percent MFU ([[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/03 The Fleet in Production/09 Fleet Efficiency/V2-09.14 Source References#^ref-dubey2024llama|Dubey et al. 2024]]). What share of the capacity it held was lost at the runtime boundary, what share at the program boundary, and what share became progress at the ideal rate?

**Variables**:

- Scheduling goodput ${\eta_{\text{scheduling}}}$ is one, because the cluster was dedicated to the run, so the first term of [[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/03 The Fleet in Production/09 Fleet Efficiency/V2-09.03 ML Goodput#^eq-perf-goodput-loss-shares|equation 2]] vanishes.
- Runtime goodput ${\eta_{\text{runtime}}}$ is about 0.90, from effective training time (a stated lower bound).
- Program goodput ${\eta_{\text{program}}}$ is about 0.41, from MFU at full scale.

**Math**:

- Runtime boundary, $1-{\eta_{\text{runtime}}}$: 1 $-$ 0.90 = 0.10.
- Program boundary, ${\eta_{\text{runtime}}}(1-{\eta_{\text{program}}})$: 0.90 $\times$ 0.59 $\approx$ 0.53.
- Useful work, ${\eta_{\text{runtime}}}{\eta_{\text{program}}}$: 0.90 $\times$ 0.41 $\approx$ 0.37.

**Result**: About 10 percent of the held capacity was lost at the runtime boundary, about 53 percent at the program boundary, and about 37 percent became progress at the ideal rate.

**Systems insight**: Removing every interruption would have recovered at most a tenth of the capacity the run held, while the steps it kept left more than half unused, so for one of the most carefully engineered runs on record the program boundary, not reliability, held most of the gap.

This calculation treats Meta’s reported effective training time as runtime goodput.[[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/03 The Fleet in Production/09 Fleet Efficiency/V2-09.15 Footnotes#^fn2|²]] That identification holds when the job retained its full allocation throughout the elapsed window and when input stalls were already excluded from effective time. The calculation’s second identification, using MFU as program goodput, holds when the MFU was measured over those same retained steps, whereas Meta’s MFU comes from a different measurement window, so the notebook’s shares are estimates.

### Program Goodput and Its Measure

^sec-performance-engineering-fleet-efficiency-metric-dff4

Hardware activity counters cannot measure how efficiently a retained step ran, because an accelerator registers as busy whether it is waiting on a collective, recomputing dropped activations, or executing arithmetic far below its peak rate. Measuring program goodput instead requires comparing actual step time against a named hardware ideal for the resource that limits the workload. For training steps and for the prefill pass over a prompt, arithmetic dominates, so the ideal time of a step is the time the accelerators would need to execute the model’s required mathematical operations at their peak rate. MFU measures against that ideal by counting only the operations the model architecture requires.[[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/03 The Fleet in Production/09 Fleet Efficiency/V2-09.15 Footnotes#^fn3|³]]

In Google’s fleet-efficiency methodology, the ideal operation count is fixed before compilation from the program as written—before the compiler fuses kernels or rematerializes activations—so that a compiler optimization that shortens the step raises program goodput instead of shifting its own baseline ([[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/03 The Fleet in Production/09 Fleet Efficiency/V2-09.14 Source References#^ref-wongpanich2025fleet|Wongpanich et al. 2025]]). Program goodput is not measured against a per-operation roofline either, because a per-operation roofline rewards kernels that run near their local ceiling and would therefore penalize a rewrite that executes fewer operations at lower utilization while finishing the step sooner. The roofline remains a diagnostic for individual kernels ([[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/03 The Fleet in Production/09 Fleet Efficiency/V2-09.07 Program Goodput#^sec-performance-engineering-roofline|section 9.6.3]]) rather than the efficiency measure of a whole program.

**Definition 9.2: Model FLOPs utilization (MFU)**

***Model FLOPs utilization (MFU)*** is the fraction of the hardware’s theoretical peak throughput (${R_{\text{peak}}}$) consumed by FLOPs that directly advance model training or inference, excluding overhead from recomputation, padding, and synchronization.

1.  **Significance**: Measured over a job’s retained steps against the arithmetic ideal, MFU is that job’s program goodput. A one-percentage-point MFU increase across 10,000 identical GPUs adds useful throughput equivalent to 100 GPUs at peak, and whether that saves money depends on whether the recovered capacity displaces hardware or shortens paid runtime.
2.  **Distinction**: Unlike hardware utilization, which reports how often the accelerator is busy, MFU relates the model’s required FLOP rate to hardware peak. It excludes recomputation and padding from useful work even when they make a configuration feasible or faster.
3.  **Common pitfall**: A frequent misconception is that high GPU utilization implies high efficiency. A system can show 90 percent hardware utilization while achieving 30 percent MFU when busy intervals include communication or arithmetic far below peak. MFU exposes the gap but does not explain its cause, which traces must find.

↩︎ The compute axis, where the memory wall limits MFU, is established in **[[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/00 Introduction/01 Introduction/V2-01.06 The C³ Taxonomy and Foundations of Scale#^sec-vol2-introduction-dim-compute|Node compute substrate]]**.

The equality between MFU and program goodput under an arithmetic ideal follows directly from that definition. A retained step across ${N}$ accelerators performs ${O_{\text{useful}}}$ of required model work, which would take ${O_{\text{useful}}}/({N}{R_{\text{peak}}})$ at peak throughput, whereas the step actually runs in wall-clock time ${T_{\text{step}}}$. [[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/03 The Fleet in Production/09 Fleet Efficiency/V2-09.03 ML Goodput#^eq-perf-program-goodput-mfu|Equation 3]] expresses program goodput as the ratio of ideal step time to actual step time:

$$
{\eta_{\text{program}}} = \frac{{O_{\text{useful}}}/({N}{R_{\text{peak}}})}{{T_{\text{step}}}} = {\text{MFU}} \tag{3}
$$

^eq-perf-program-goodput-mfu

That identity holds only when MFU is measured over the retained steps that survived the runtime boundary, with input stalls already excluded. Published MFU numbers are often averaged over an entire wall-clock run that includes restarts and synchronous checkpoint pauses; multiplying such a run-averaged MFU by a separate runtime goodput factor would penalize those interruptions twice. Hardware FLOPs utilization (HFU), which counts all executed floating-point operations including activation recomputation, remains a useful diagnostic alongside MFU because the gap between HFU and MFU isolates the cost of recomputation. In goodput accounting, that recomputation gap is a program goodput loss even when trading extra compute for memory capacity is the right engineering choice. Similarly, when capacity planners size a training run in accelerator-hours by scaling peak throughput by retained-step MFU (**[[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/01 The Fleet/02 Compute Infrastructure/V2-02.05 Sustained Compute and Precision#^sec-compute-infrastructure-peak-vs-sustained-throughput-625a|Peak vs. sustained throughput]]**), they must still multiply by runtime and scheduling goodput to account for the hours lost outside steady-state steps.

The MFU that published training runs report stays below half of peak across every generation of frontier hardware ([[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/03 The Fleet in Production/09 Fleet Efficiency/V2-09.03 ML Goodput#^fig-mfu-progression|figure 2]]). Even for the same model architecture, expanding Llama 3 405B onto twice as many GPUs lowers its reported MFU, which the report attributes to the smaller batch per data-parallel group needed to hold the global batch constant.

An arithmetic ideal does not fit workloads that the hardware cannot make compute bound. An autoregressive decode step generating one token for a small batch must stream every parameter of the model from HBM to perform only a handful of operations per weight. Memory bandwidth therefore sets the step’s floor (**[[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/01 The Fleet/02 Compute Infrastructure/V2-02.04 The Memory Wall#^sec-compute-infrastructure-memory-bandwidth-token-latency-1b38|Memory bandwidth and token latency]]**), and at that bandwidth floor the accelerator’s matrix units run at well under 1 percent of peak arithmetic throughput. Charging that arithmetic shortfall to the program would blame kernel implementation for a gap that only a larger batch can close—and maximum batch size is constrained by the serving latency target rather than by the kernel. For bandwidth-bound decode, the natural reference for a single replica is the weight-only bandwidth floor: the time required to stream the model’s weight bytes at peak memory bandwidth. Program goodput against that ideal is **model bandwidth utilization** (MBU), defined as the achieved rate of weight-byte transfers divided by peak memory bandwidth. The common MBU definition also counts key-value cache bytes in the floor. This chapter counts weights alone, so reading the key-value cache lengthens the measured step without entering the floor and counts against the replica as a program loss, which keeps cache growth visible. At long context, where cache reads can rival the weight bytes, a low MBU can therefore reflect cache size rather than kernel quality. A decode step with high MBU and low MFU has saturated the limiting hardware resource rather than wasted it.

Choosing a named ideal also determines what can be aggregated across a multi-workload cluster. A fleet that evaluates training against peak arithmetic and decode against peak memory bandwidth cannot average the two into a single program goodput factor, because the two denominators measure different physical resources. Fleet-wide reporting must therefore either keep program goodput segmented by workload class or evaluate every workload against the arithmetic ideal and recognize that small-batch decode carries a structural batch-size penalty. Google’s fleet-efficiency framework takes the second path for its fleet-wide aggregate, measuring all workloads against a compute-based ideal ([[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/03 The Fleet in Production/09 Fleet Efficiency/V2-09.14 Source References#^ref-wongpanich2025fleet|Wongpanich et al. 2025]]), while MBU provides a direct diagnostic for individual bandwidth-bound decode replicas. Throughout this chapter, MFU serves as the default program goodput metric because it composes across the fleet, and MBU is reported alongside it whenever memory bandwidth sets the hardware ceiling.

For the chapter’s running Llama 2 70B training job, program goodput is the fleet MFU of its retained steps across all 128 GPUs. In the constructed example, that fleet MFU is about 48 percent, down from the 65 percent of a single-node baseline, because distributing each step across 16 nodes introduces inter-node collective communication that a single node never pays. Both MFUs rest on illustrative step times for a microbatch-scale step of about 2,215 tokens per data-parallel replica, so one optimizer update accumulates many such steps. The program goodput of 0.48 is therefore a per-microbatch value. The data-axis gradient reduction runs once per optimizer step and is amortized across the accumulated microbatches ([[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/03 The Fleet in Production/09 Fleet Efficiency/V2-09.08 Recovering Program Goodput#^sec-performance-engineering-overlap|section 9.7.2]]), which lowers program goodput over a full optimizer step to about 0.456. This chapter carries the per-microbatch value forward and states the full-step value wherever the job’s factors are composed. [[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/03 The Fleet in Production/09 Fleet Efficiency/V2-09.07 Program Goodput#^sec-performance-engineering-exposed-communication|Section 9.6.2]] isolates and quantifies the scaling loss.

Program goodput also connects directly to the efficiency expressions derived earlier in this book. The compute-time fraction of **[[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/00 Introduction/01 Introduction/V2-01.06 The C³ Taxonomy and Foundations of Scale#^eq-fleet-efficiency|equation 1.4]]** divides each device’s share of compute time by the distributed step time, and what it measures depends on how that compute time is counted. Counted as the ideal time at peak hardware throughput, the fraction equals the program goodput of a single step. Counted as the measured execution time on a single device, the compute time already contains that device’s own losses to memory stalls and kernel dispatch, so the fraction measures only what spreading the work across devices costs, which is the scaling efficiency. Multiplying it by the single device’s MFU then recovers program goodput. The effective fleet throughput in **[[05 Reference/Volume II - Scaling/V2 The C³ Taxonomy#^sec-appdx-c3-taxonomy-effective-flops|The effective FLOP/s concept]]** makes the same split explicit by multiplying peak cluster capacity by single-device MFU, multi-node scaling efficiency, and runtime goodput. The product of those first two terms equals the job’s fleet MFU, which is its program goodput, while scheduling goodput is absent only because that expression starts from accelerators the job already holds.

These boundary definitions refine two of the orchestrator’s utilization tiers from **[[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/02 Distributed ML/08 Fleet Orchestration/V2-08.13 Debugging Cluster Utilization#^sec-fleet-orchestration-resource-accounting|Allocated, active, and productive utilization]]** so that the three factors multiply without overlap. First, scheduling goodput counts only complete allocations. Accelerators held by a gang still waiting for its remaining ranks appear as allocated on a basic cluster dashboard, yet they are charged here as a scheduling loss because no synchronous step can execute on them. Second, the orchestrator’s productive tier measures retained work against a declared reference rate. When that reference is the job’s own fault-free step rate (with input ready and no interruptions), dividing productive capacity by complete-allocation capacity yields runtime goodput directly. When the reference is hardware peak throughput instead, the same ratio collapses runtime and program goodput into a single number and obscures which boundary lost the capacity. In the orchestration example of 500 GPUs allocated, 350 active, and 280 productive, the productive-to-allocated ratio of 56 percent represents runtime goodput only when the productive tier is normalized to the job’s own steady-state step rate.

By contrast, the orchestrator’s active tier does not correspond to a goodput boundary. An accelerator whose execution engines are active may be performing required model arithmetic, spinning in a collective while waiting on a straggler, recomputing discarded activations, or re-executing steps lost after a node failure. Those four activities represent useful work, two program goodput losses, and a runtime goodput loss; device activity counters therefore serve as diagnostic evidence for separating runtime from program losses rather than as a goodput factor in their own right.

The boundary between runtime goodput and program goodput also governs how delays inside a step are classified. The duration of a retained step includes any time spent waiting on a slow peer, a pipeline bubble, or a collective operation, but excludes stalls waiting for the storage pipeline to deliver the next batch. Input stalls are carved out and charged to runtime goodput because the data pipeline prevented the step from starting, and the data stall ratio of **[[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/01 The Fleet/04 Data Storage/V2-04.05 The Data Pipeline Equation#^eq-data-stall|equation 4.4]]** in **[[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/01 The Fleet/04 Data Storage/V2-04.05 The Data Pipeline Equation#^sec-storage-pipeline-equation|The Data Pipeline Equation]]** quantifies their share. Every other slowdown within a retained step is charged to program goodput, even when its root cause originates outside the user program.

In short, the accounting boundary determines *where* a loss is counted, whereas the physical cause determines *which system layer* fixes it. Stragglers illustrate why the two must remain distinct. **[[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/02 Distributed ML/07 Fault Tolerance/V2-07.10 Failure Detection and Recovery#^sec-fault-tolerance-reliability-reliability-distinguishing-stragglers-failures-285d|Stragglers versus fail-stop failures]]** treats a degraded accelerator as a reliability problem and evaluates when replacing the slow node justifies paying for a restart. In goodput accounting, however, a straggler is charged to program goodput: the job holds a complete allocation and retains every step in its checkpoints, yet those steps execute more slowly than the hardware ideal. Recording the straggler in program goodput captures the slowdown where it manifests. Program goodput drops while the straggler is present and recovers once the node is replaced. The remediation decision stays with the reliability controller, which weighs the recovered program goodput against the runtime goodput lost to draining and restarting the job.

Preemption separates cause from charge in the same way. The cluster scheduler triggers the eviction, yet the discarded steps since the last checkpoint and the subsequent reload are charged to runtime goodput because the job held a complete allocation while those hours were spent. By the same rule, a synchronous checkpoint that halts step execution is a runtime loss, whereas an asynchronous checkpoint that competes with the running step for memory or network bandwidth slows the retained step and is charged to program goodput. Ahead-of-time compilation before the first step reduces runtime goodput, while the execution speed of the compiled kernels governs program goodput. Across all of these cases, the three boundaries keep MPG internally consistent, though MPG itself is only one of several metrics in this book that carry the name “goodput.”

### One Word, Several Measures

^sec-performance-engineering-goodput-family

Quoting “goodput” without qualification leaves a reader unable to tell whether a job preserved its progress across restarts, used its share of the cluster effectively, met its serving latency targets, or extracted statistical progress from each training example. Across this book, the term appears in four distinct forms, each answering a different engineering question:

- *Run-level goodput* (**[[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/00 Introduction/01 Introduction/V2-01.06 The C³ Taxonomy and Foundations of Scale#^sec-vol2-introduction-dim-coord|Orchestration coordination layer]]**) measures a single job’s rate of useful training progress after discounting time lost to failures and replayed steps.
- *ML productivity goodput (MPG)* measures the fraction of total fleet capacity-time that becomes useful work at the hardware’s ideal rate.
- *Service-level objective (SLO) goodput* (**[[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/03 The Fleet in Production/10 Serving at Scale/V2-10.04 SLO Goodput and the Serving Tax#^sec-inference-scale-serving-tax-overhead-distribution-5ef3|SLO Goodput and the Serving Tax]]**) measures the rate of completed inference requests that satisfy both time-to-first-token and per-output-token latency targets, which bound the wait before a request’s first generated token and the interval between later tokens.
- *Statistical goodput*, which the Pollux scheduler maximizes in **[[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/02 Distributed ML/08 Fleet Orchestration/V2-08.10 Custom ML Schedulers#^sec-fleet-orchestration-pollux|Adaptive resource allocation (Pollux)]]**, weights raw training throughput by the convergence progress each training example contributes at the current batch size.

These four measures complement rather than replace one another. SLO goodput applies on top of MPG: a serving fleet that operates at a high fraction of its ideal hardware rate on average can still violate latency targets in the tail if batches are formed too aggressively. Similarly, statistical goodput evaluates progress per example rather than operations per second, so enlarging the global batch beyond the critical batch size can raise program goodput while reducing statistical goodput. Any quantitative efficiency claim must therefore state which goodput measure it reports, which denominator defines its boundary, and which time window it spans. The measurement contract of [[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/03 The Fleet in Production/09 Fleet Efficiency/V2-09.04 Measuring Goodput#^sec-performance-engineering-measuring-goodput|section 9.3]] pins down the workload, boundary, and measurement window so that two goodput numbers can be compared fairly.

↪ MPG applied across a whole portfolio of jobs, in fleet dashboards and chargeback, is developed in **[[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/03 The Fleet in Production/12 ML Operations at Scale/V2-12.09 Platform Engineering#^sec-ml-operations-scale-advanced-fleet-metrics-ml-productivity-goodput-mpg-fabf|ML productivity goodput across the portfolio]]**.
