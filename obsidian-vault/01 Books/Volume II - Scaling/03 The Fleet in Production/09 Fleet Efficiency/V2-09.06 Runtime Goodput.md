# V2-09.06 Runtime Goodput

[[Home|Home]] · [[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/Volume 2 Overview|Volume Overview]] · [[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/03 The Fleet in Production/09 Fleet Efficiency/V2-09 Fleet Efficiency|V2-09 Fleet Efficiency]]

^sec-performance-engineering-runtime-goodput

A training job that loses held time to input stalls, synchronous checkpoint pauses, hardware failures, and forced preemptions encounters each loss in a different unit. **[[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/01 The Fleet/04 Data Storage/V2-04 Data Storage#^sec-data-storage|Data Storage]]** expresses input stalls as a fraction of step time, **[[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/02 Distributed ML/07 Fault Tolerance/V2-07 Fault Tolerance#^sec-fault-tolerance-reliability|Fault Tolerance]]** expresses checkpointing and recovery overhead against fault-free training time, and **[[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/02 Distributed ML/08 Fleet Orchestration/V2-08 Fleet Orchestration#^sec-fleet-orchestration|Fleet Orchestration]]** prices preemption in wasted minutes per eviction event. Runtime goodput—the fraction of a job’s all-allocated held time that becomes retained progress—places all of those losses on a single common denominator. Expressed as shares of held time, every interruption can be compared directly so that engineers attack the largest loss measured on the job timeline rather than whichever subsystem they inspected most recently, while the mechanisms that shorten each loss remain with their owning chapters.

### What Runtime Goodput Counts

^sec-performance-engineering-runtime-goodput-counts

Holding a complete gang for an hour does not guarantee an hour of retained training steps: the job can spend much of that hour launching processes, waiting on a starved data pipeline, pausing for synchronous checkpoint commits, or re-executing steps erased by a node failure. Runtime goodput measures the fraction of all-allocated time that survives those interruptions as a ratio of two durations over the same calendar window. The denominator is the job’s held time from the gang-complete timestamp to release, taken directly from scheduler logs ([[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/03 The Fleet in Production/09 Fleet Efficiency/V2-09.05 Scheduling Goodput#^sec-performance-engineering-scheduling-evidence|section 9.4.3]]). The numerator is productive time: the count of retained steps multiplied by the job’s fault-free step duration. A step counts as retained once a committed checkpoint records its state update (or when the job finishes and commits its final weights). The fault-free step duration is the job’s steady-state step time with input batches ready and no recovery in progress on that same allocation. It is the mean input-ready step time over every retained step outside post-restart warmup, so intermittent slowdown episodes stay in the baseline and are charged to program goodput.

Normalizing the numerator to the job’s own fault-free step duration keeps the runtime boundary cleanly separated from the program boundary. Because the fault-free step duration already includes exposed collectives, pipeline bubbles, memory bandwidth stalls, and persistent stragglers, those per-step inefficiencies pass through the runtime boundary unchanged and are evaluated in program goodput. Steps immediately following a restart are handled differently. While instruction caches warm up, just-in-time kernels compile, and data-loader queues refill, any excess step duration above the steady-state fault-free baseline belongs to the recovery event that triggered the restart and is charged to runtime goodput. The fault-free baseline duration of each completed step, by contrast, counts as retained progress. Consequently, a degraded node that slows every step across a multi-day run registers as a program goodput loss, whereas a temporary slowdown confined to the warmup minutes after a restart registers as a runtime goodput loss.

Every other interval of held time that fails to produce retained steps is a runtime loss, ordered chronologically across a job’s lifecycle:

- *Startup* spans the interval from gang completion to the first training step while processes launch, libraries load, collective communicators initialize, checkpoint weights load, and compilers warm up. Because scheduler logs timestamp gang completion and the job timeline timestamps the first step, startup is measured directly between those two records—which is why [[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/03 The Fleet in Production/09 Fleet Efficiency/V2-09.05 Scheduling Goodput#^sec-performance-engineering-scheduling-evidence|section 9.4.3]] requires the scheduler, rather than the first step, to mark allocation completion.
- *Input waits* capture the time a retained step spends blocked on the data pipeline for its next batch, carved out of step duration per [[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/03 The Fleet in Production/09 Fleet Efficiency/V2-09.03 ML Goodput#^sec-performance-engineering-goodput-boundaries|section 9.2.3]].
- *Synchronous checkpoint pauses* capture the intervals during which training halts while accelerators or hosts flush state to persistent storage.
- *Failure recovery and rework* cover both the downtime from a fault through detection, relaunch, state reload, and warmup, and the held time spent earlier on post-checkpoint steps that the failure erased and the job had to repeat.
- *Forced preemption and planned reconfiguration* charge the post-checkpoint work discarded by an eviction, the subsequent reload after readmission, and the warmup before steady-state step times return, along with planned maintenance drains or elastic resizes. All of these belong to runtime goodput because the job held a complete allocation while the discarded or non-step hours were spent.

Three adjacent delays fall outside runtime goodput. Time a preempted job spends waiting in the scheduler queue before readmission is not held time and affects only time to result ([[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/03 The Fleet in Production/09 Fleet Efficiency/V2-09.05 Scheduling Goodput#^sec-performance-engineering-scheduling-goodput-counts|section 9.4.1]]). Background bandwidth contention from asynchronous checkpoint writes and the steady-state execution speed of compiled kernels both lengthen retained steps and belong to program goodput, whereas ahead-of-time compilation before the first step belongs to runtime startup. Keeping cold-start compilation and warmup in runtime goodput matches the measurement contract of [[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/03 The Fleet in Production/09 Fleet Efficiency/V2-09.04 Measuring Goodput#^tbl-performance-engineering-measurement-contract|table 2]], which excludes warmup trials from the steady-state step rate used for program goodput.

Finally, the measurement window for runtime goodput must span many interruption cycles ([[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/03 The Fleet in Production/09 Fleet Efficiency/V2-09.04 Measuring Goodput#^sec-performance-engineering-measurement-contract|section 9.3.1]]) and requires a clean closing rule: steps executed after the last checkpoint committed inside the window are neither durably retained nor lost yet. Closing the measurement window at a checkpoint commit avoids that ambiguity; if a window closes between commits, those trailing steps count as retained only once the next checkpoint succeeds.

### Composing Runtime Loss from Its Owners

^sec-performance-engineering-runtime-composition

A team whose job loses a tenth of its held time cannot tell from the aggregate runtime goodput alone whether to add storage bandwidth for input stalls, shorten checkpoint writes, or negotiate a tighter preemption budget. Because every non-productive interval of held time falls into exactly one of the five runtime-loss categories, those categories partition the gap between held time and productive time without overlap. Dividing each category’s duration by total held time expresses it as a fractional loss share, and the five shares add directly. Earlier chapters already derived each individual share:

1.  The *startup share* is the launch and compilation duration ${T_{\text{startup}}}$ from gang completion to first step, divided by the held duration ${T_{\text{wall}}}$ of that allocation.
2.  The *input-stall share* is the data stall ratio ${f_{\text{stall}}}$ of **[[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/01 The Fleet/04 Data Storage/V2-04.05 The Data Pipeline Equation#^eq-data-stall|equation 4.4]]**, which **[[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/01 The Fleet/04 Data Storage/V2-04.05 The Data Pipeline Equation#^sec-storage-pipeline-equation|The Data Pipeline Equation]]** derives from the bottleneck stage of the storage pipeline.
3.  The *checkpoint share* is the Young-Daly checkpoint waste ${W}({T_{\text{ckpt}}})$ of **[[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/02 Distributed ML/07 Fault Tolerance/V2-07.09 Checkpointing and Progress Preservation#^eq-ft-checkpoint-waste|equation 7.8]]** evaluated at the job’s checkpoint interval ${T_{\text{ckpt}}}$, combining synchronous write pauses with expected post-checkpoint rework after failures.
4.  The *failure-recovery share* is the per-fault recovery duration ${T_{\text{recovery}}}$ from **[[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/02 Distributed ML/07 Fault Tolerance/V2-07.10 Failure Detection and Recovery#^sec-fault-tolerance-reliability-reliability-recovery-procedures-002d|Recovery procedures]]** (detection, relaunch, checkpoint load, and warmup) divided by the cluster’s mean time between failures ${\text{MTBF}_{\text{system}}}$.
5.  The *preemption share* is the forced-preemption rate ${\lambda_{\text{preempt}}}$ multiplied by the per-eviction overhead ${T_{\text{preempt}}}$ from **[[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/02 Distributed ML/08 Fleet Orchestration/V2-08.09 Cost Optimization#^sec-fleet-orchestration-preemption-cascades|Preemption cost and cascades]]**.

[[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/03 The Fleet in Production/09 Fleet Efficiency/V2-09.06 Runtime Goodput#^eq-perf-runtime-loss-composition|Equation 4]] sums the five shares to give total runtime goodput loss:

$$
1 - {\eta_{\text{runtime}}} \approx \frac{{T_{\text{startup}}}}{{T_{\text{wall}}}} + {f_{\text{stall}}} + {W}({T_{\text{ckpt}}}) + \frac{{T_{\text{recovery}}}}{{\text{MTBF}_{\text{system}}}} + {\lambda_{\text{preempt}}} \, {T_{\text{preempt}}} \tag{4}
$$

^eq-perf-runtime-loss-composition

Writing runtime loss as an explicit sum turns optimization into a quantitative triage problem for a specific workload: which of the five terms dominates *this* job at *this* scale under *this* cluster policy. Each term carries the modeling assumptions of its owning chapter, so whenever a workload violates one of those assumptions, engineers refine that specific term using the owning chapter’s full model while keeping the additive boundary accounting intact.

↩︎ The checkpoint waste equation, its optimal interval, and the four-term recovery budget are established in **[[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/02 Distributed ML/07 Fault Tolerance/V2-07 Fault Tolerance#^sec-fault-tolerance-reliability|Fault Tolerance]]**.

[[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/03 The Fleet in Production/09 Fleet Efficiency/V2-09.06 Runtime Goodput#^eq-perf-runtime-loss-composition|Equation 4]] is a first-order sum because the individual models normalize their losses against slightly different baselines—step time for data stalls, fault-free compute time for Young-Daly waste, and arrival rates times event durations for recovery and preemption. When every individual loss share is small, those baseline differences alter the sum only to second order (fractions of a percent). Whenever a single term grows large enough to consume a major fraction of held time, the first-order approximation has already served its diagnostic purpose, and engineers measure the exact shares directly from the job timeline.

Summing the five terms is valid only when no lost minute is counted in two terms at once. Four boundary junctions require care so that the owning chapters’ formulas do not overlap:

- *Rework is counted once per interruption cause.* Because the checkpoint waste term ${W}({T_{\text{ckpt}}})$ already includes the expected steps lost after a hardware failure, the failure-recovery term ${T_{\text{recovery}}} / {\text{MTBF}_{\text{system}}}$ includes only detection, relaunch, reload, and warmup. Conversely, steps erased by a scheduler eviction belong to ${T_{\text{preempt}}}$ and must not be folded into ${\text{MTBF}_{\text{system}}}$ unless the two terms are solved jointly.
- *Warmup is charged to the event that triggered it.* Both the failure-recovery budget ${T_{\text{recovery}}}$ and the preemption tax ${T_{\text{preempt}}}$ already include post-restart warmup, so the initial startup term ${T_{\text{startup}}} / {T_{\text{wall}}}$ counts only cold launches that were not triggered by an in-run failure or preemption.
- *The data stall ratio ${f_{\text{stall}}}$ is measured in steady state.* Pipeline refill immediately after a restart is part of post-restart warmup; averaging ${f_{\text{stall}}}$ across restart warmup windows would charge that refill delay twice.
- *Only synchronous checkpoint pauses enter ${W}({T_{\text{ckpt}}})$.* When checkpoints are written asynchronously in the background, any memory or fabric contention they inflict on running steps lengthens retained step time and is charged to program goodput instead.

At the 10,000-GPU scale analyzed in **[[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/02 Distributed ML/07 Fault Tolerance/V2-07.09 Checkpointing and Progress Preservation#^sec-fault-tolerance-young-daly|The Young-Daly checkpoint interval]]**, checkpoint waste (about 5.9 percent) and failure recovery (about 2.8 percent) together consume nearly a tenth of held time. Both shares are evaluated here at the full component failure rate that governs the Young-Daly interval, so this recovery share exceeds the GPU-only recovery figure of **[[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/02 Distributed ML/07 Fault Tolerance/V2-07.10 Failure Detection and Recovery#^sec-fault-tolerance-reliability-reliability-recovery-procedures-002d|Recovery procedures]]**. Both failure-driven terms grow with device count, whereas startup, input stalls, and preemption do not. Consequently, jobs of different sizes lose runtime goodput to entirely different mechanisms. Because our running example is much smaller than that frontier reference, its loss terms rank in a different order.

**Napkin Math 9.3: Runtime loss of the Llama 2 70B job**

**Problem**: The Llama 2 70B training job holds 128 GPUs on a shared fleet for a week, under a per-job budget of 1 forced preemption a day. With each owner’s model applied to the job’s own configuration, what runtime goodput does it keep, and which term costs it the most?

**Variables**:

- Mean time between failures ${\text{MTBF}_{\text{system}}}$ is about 302 hours, from the component failure rates of **[[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/02 Distributed ML/07 Fault Tolerance/V2-07.09 Checkpointing and Progress Preservation#^sec-fault-tolerance-young-daly|The Young-Daly checkpoint interval]]**.
- The resumable checkpoint takes about 9.8 seconds to write at the 100 GB/s of the recovery budget in **[[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/02 Distributed ML/07 Fault Tolerance/V2-07.10 Failure Detection and Recovery#^sec-fault-tolerance-reliability-reliability-recovery-procedures-002d|Recovery procedures]]**, the job’s tenth of a parallel file system shared by ten jobs (**[[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/01 The Fleet/04 Data Storage/V2-04.07 Checkpoint Storage#^sec-storage-checkpointing|Checkpoint Storage]]**), so the Young-Daly interval ${T_{\text{ckpt}}}$ is about 77 minutes.
- Recovery ${T_{\text{recovery}}}$ is about 6.2 minutes per failure, and startup ${T_{\text{startup}}}$ is about 5.2 minutes per launch, the same steps without detection.
- The data stall ratio ${f_{\text{stall}}}$ is 7.7 percent of held time, the snapshot’s 5 percent of in-service capacity divided by its scheduling goodput of 0.65 ([[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/03 The Fleet in Production/09 Fleet Efficiency/V2-09.05 Scheduling Goodput#^sec-performance-engineering-scheduling-evidence|section 9.4.3]]).
- The preemption overhead ${T_{\text{preempt}}}$ is about 20.2 minutes per forced event, the 15 minutes of lost work that **[[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/02 Distributed ML/08 Fleet Orchestration/V2-08.09 Cost Optimization#^sec-fleet-orchestration-preemption-cascades|Preemption cost and cascades]]** assumes plus one startup.

**Math** (one line per term of [[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/03 The Fleet in Production/09 Fleet Efficiency/V2-09.06 Runtime Goodput#^eq-perf-runtime-loss-composition|equation 4]]):

- Startup: 5.2 minutes $\div$ 7 days $\approx$ 0.05 percent.
- Input waits: 7.7 percent.
- Checkpoint: 9.8 seconds $\div$ 77 minutes for writes, plus 77 minutes $\div$ (2 $\times$ 302 hours) for rework, $\approx$ 0.2 percent + 0.2 percent $\approx$ 0.4 percent.
- Recovery: 6.2 minutes $\div$ 302 hours $\approx$ 0.03 percent.
- Preemption: 1 per day $\times$ 20.2 minutes $\approx$ 1.4 percent.

**Result**: The five terms add to a runtime loss of about 10 percent, a runtime goodput of about 0.90. Input waits and preemption account for 9.1 percent of it, and checkpoint waste and recovery together for under half a percent.

**Systems insight**: On a mid-sized job that shares its fleet, the terms that grow with device count are the smallest, so the lever that reaches the most held time belongs to the input pipeline and the preemption budget rather than to the reliability layer.

Because the overall runtime goodput is already close to what a well-run job achieves, the engineering lesson lies in how the loss is divided. A team consulting only **[[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/02 Distributed ML/07 Fault Tolerance/V2-07 Fault Tolerance#^sec-fault-tolerance-reliability|Fault Tolerance]]** might invest in faster checkpoint writes and warm restarts, yet on this 128-GPU job those levers can recover at most the 0.4 percent and 0.03 percent shares assigned to them by the composition. Meanwhile, the input pipeline sized in **[[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/01 The Fleet/04 Data Storage/V2-04.05 The Data Pipeline Equation#^sec-storage-pipeline-equation|The Data Pipeline Equation]]** governs roughly 17× as much held time. Without a preemption cap, the 12 forced preemptions per day that **[[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/02 Distributed ML/08 Fleet Orchestration/V2-08.09 Cost Optimization#^sec-fleet-orchestration-preemption-cascades|Preemption cost and cascades]]** models for a low-priority run would consume 16.8 percent of held time—exceeding every other term and breaking the small-loss regime of the first-order sum.

Both job size and job duration reorder these terms. As device count grows, checkpoint waste and failure recovery expand until their combined loss overtakes input waits near 13,000 GPUs ([[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/03 The Fleet in Production/09 Fleet Efficiency/V2-09.06 Runtime Goodput#^fig-performance-engineering-runtime-scaling|figure 5]]). Similarly, the same 5.2 minutes startup cost that is negligible over a week consumes about 9 percent of an evaluation job that holds its allocation for only an hour. The composition does not pick a universal winner among these levers; rather, it shows each subsystem owner how much held time their lever can actually reach—a comparison no single subsystem can make in isolation.

^fig-performance-engineering-runtime-scaling

Composing the terms also exposes a cross-layer coupling that no single owner sees alone. Because a forced preemption discards all work since the last committed checkpoint just as a hardware failure does, it acts as an additional interruption when choosing the checkpoint cadence. Our running example’s 77 minutes interval was derived from hardware failures alone; counting its budgeted preemptions as interruptions in **[[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/02 Distributed ML/07 Fault Tolerance/V2-07.09 Checkpointing and Progress Preservation#^eq-ft-young-daly-interval|equation 7.7]]** shortens the optimal interval to about 21 minutes. Although the checkpoint cadence belongs to **[[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/02 Distributed ML/07 Fault Tolerance/V2-07.09 Checkpointing and Progress Preservation#^sec-fault-tolerance-young-daly|The Young-Daly checkpoint interval]]** and the preemption budget belongs to **[[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/02 Distributed ML/08 Fleet Orchestration/V2-08.09 Cost Optimization#^sec-fleet-orchestration-preemption-cascades|Preemption cost and cascades]]**, composing runtime goodput reveals that the two parameters are coupled parts of a single decision.[[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/03 The Fleet in Production/09 Fleet Efficiency/V2-09.15 Footnotes#^fn5|⁵]]

While [[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/03 The Fleet in Production/09 Fleet Efficiency/V2-09.06 Runtime Goodput#^eq-perf-runtime-loss-composition|equation 4]] describes an individual job, a pool’s aggregate runtime goodput is also lowered by idle reserved blocks and by zombie allocations that continue to hold devices after execution has stalled. Because [[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/03 The Fleet in Production/09 Fleet Efficiency/V2-09.05 Scheduling Goodput#^sec-performance-engineering-scheduler-attribution|section 9.4.2]] charges both intervals to runtime goodput, they appear only when the pool’s total held time is compared against the sum of its active jobs’ timelines.

### Evidence, Size, and the Serving Replica

^sec-performance-engineering-runtime-evidence

A job that hangs inside a collective past its detection timeout, warms up much more slowly than its recovery budget assumed, or compiles longer after each software release loses held time that no analytical model predicts. Measuring runtime goodput directly from the job timeline—the second rung of [[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/03 The Fleet in Production/09 Fleet Efficiency/V2-09.04 Measuring Goodput#^sec-performance-engineering-localize-factor|section 9.3.2]]—captures both the modeled terms and any unmodeled residual because the timeline’s events map directly onto the loss categories. Startup runs from the scheduler’s gang-complete event to the first step; input stalls come from the per-step input-wait field of each retained step; and checkpoint pauses appear as the intervals at each commit. Every failure or preemption contributes its detection, relaunch, and warmup intervals together with the steps it forced the job to repeat, while excluding any queue wait after an eviction. Finally, the retained-checkpoint markers establish which steps count toward progress, and the steady-state step times fix the fault-free rate.

Under this workflow, [[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/03 The Fleet in Production/09 Fleet Efficiency/V2-09.06 Runtime Goodput#^eq-perf-runtime-loss-composition|equation 4]] serves as an analytical check on the job timeline rather than a substitute for it. When prediction and measurement agree, the largest modeled term identifies the subsystem owner to call. When the measured loss exceeds the prediction, the unexplained residual represents held time lost to unmodeled hangs, slow warmups, or creeping initialization overheads. A residual that widens across many jobs after a deployment is precisely the regression caught by the runtime row of [[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/03 The Fleet in Production/09 Fleet Efficiency/V2-09.04 Measuring Goodput#^tbl-performance-engineering-scaling-regression-tests|table 4]].

The Llama 3 405B training run provides the benchmark for a well-run frontier job. Meta reported more than 90 percent effective training time, corresponding to a runtime goodput of at least 0.90 ([[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/03 The Fleet in Production/09 Fleet Efficiency/V2-09.14 Source References#^ref-dubey2024llama|Dubey et al. 2024]])—a demanding standard given the hardware failure cadence analyzed in **[[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/02 Distributed ML/07 Fault Tolerance/V2-07.02 Failure Analysis at Scale#^sec-fault-tolerance-reliability-reliability-failure-analysis-scale-6b4b|Failure Analysis at Scale]]**.

Although our running example’s runtime goodput of 0.90 matches that frontier figure to two decimal places, the agreement masks a difference in mechanism. Running on a dedicated cluster across 128× as many devices, the Llama 3 job had to fight for its runtime goodput against failure-driven checkpoint and recovery overheads. Our 128-GPU job loses almost nothing to hardware failures and nearly all of its lost tenth to input stalls and preemption. Because the same aggregate factor can point to entirely different owners, a fleet report must record the constituent loss terms alongside their sum.

By contrast, a standard benchmark run is short, dedicated, and timed only after initialization completes,[[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/03 The Fleet in Production/09 Fleet Efficiency/V2-09.15 Footnotes#^fn6|⁶]] so of the five runtime loss terms it experiences at most the steady-state input stall (perspective [9.1](https://mlsysbook.ai/vol2/09_performance_engineering/09_performance_engineering.html#psp-performance-engineering-hero-run-tax)). Any capacity plan extrapolated from a benchmark rate must therefore restore the remaining terms of this composition.

For a serving replica, the same boundary separates held time from ready time, so its runtime goodput is the ratio of ready time to held time in the replica’s lifecycle log. Provisioning precedes the complete allocation and belongs to scheduling goodput, whereas the weight fetch, model load, and warmup that follow—requiring about 5.7 minutes in the cold-start budget of **[[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/03 The Fleet in Production/10 Serving at Scale/V2-10.14 Autoscaling and Capacity Control#^sec-inference-scale-autoscaling-capacity-control|Autoscaling and Capacity Control]]**—are runtime losses. How heavily those minutes weigh on runtime goodput depends on the replica’s lifetime: a replica recycled once a day spends about 0.4 percent of its held time becoming ready, whereas a replica spun up for a single burst hour loses about 9 percent. Maintaining a warm pool of loaded, idle replicas does not eliminate this cost; it shifts the idle capacity from runtime goodput into program goodput as serving headroom.

Whether measured as a ready replica or a retained training step, crossing this boundary means that held time has become retained work. How fast that retained work executes relative to its hardware ideal is the domain of the third factor: program goodput.

↪ Cold-start latency, its decomposition into provisioning, loading, and warmup, and the pre-warming that hides it are developed in **[[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/03 The Fleet in Production/10 Serving at Scale/V2-10.14 Autoscaling and Capacity Control#^sec-inference-scale-autoscaling-capacity-control|Autoscaling and Capacity Control]]**.

**Checkpoint 9.1: Charging losses to factors**

Every lost interval is charged once, at the first boundary it fails to cross, and each factor is read from the record that can see its boundary.

**Which factor**: For a gang that holds half its devices while it waits, a zombie job, an input wait inside a retained step, and a straggling rank, can you name the factor each lowers and the layer whose lever removes it?

**Which record**: A job retains fewer steps than planned. Can you explain why the scheduler records and the job timeline are read before any step trace, and what each can and cannot establish?

**Which window**: Why can a one-day measurement report a runtime goodput near one for a job that loses a tenth of its held time over a month?

**Composing runtime loss**: Given a data stall ratio, a checkpoint interval, a failure rate with its recovery time, and a preemption budget, can you add the runtime loss terms and name the interval that must not be counted twice?
