# V2-09.04 Measuring Goodput

[[Home|Home]] · [[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/Volume 2 Overview|Volume Overview]] · [[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/03 The Fleet in Production/09 Fleet Efficiency/V2-09 Fleet Efficiency|V2-09 Fleet Efficiency]]

^sec-performance-engineering-measuring-goodput

Two measurements of the running Llama 2 70B job taken a week apart can report noticeably different program goodput because they traced different ranks, spanned a framework upgrade, started timing before compilation finished, or averaged across a failure recovery. The job’s real efficiency on the cluster need not have changed at all. A goodput factor can guide engineering decisions only when repeated measurements are directly comparable and when a drop can be traced cleanly to the boundary that lost the capacity. Measuring goodput at fleet scale therefore requires three elements: a measurement contract that holds confounding variables fixed, a diagnostic hierarchy that identifies the losing factor before probing its internal mechanism, and a telemetry sampling strategy whose collection overhead the production fleet can afford.

### The Measurement Contract

^sec-performance-engineering-measurement-contract

An apparent speedup between two trials can easily stem from altered input distributions, a warmer machine state, host timers that miss asynchronous kernel execution, or a relaxed convergence target rather than from the optimization under test. Distributed fleets introduce further confounding variables that single-machine benchmarks never encounter: two runs may receive placements with different switch hops, share fabric links with different neighbors, execute under different preemption policies, or span a window too brief to capture routine hardware interruptions. Industry benchmark suites prevent these confounds by locking down the workload, quality target, and timing rules before any system comparison begins ([[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/03 The Fleet in Production/09 Fleet Efficiency/V2-09.14 Source References#^ref-mattson2020mlperf|Mattson et al. 2020]]; [[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/03 The Fleet in Production/09 Fleet Efficiency/V2-09.14 Source References#^ref-reddi2020|Reddi et al. 2019]]), and production fleet engineering requires the same discipline for every goodput factor it tracks.

**Definition 9.3: Measurement contract**

***Measurement contract*** is the set of conditions a performance measurement fixes and reports, the workload and its quality gate, the system state and software version, the timing boundary, and the goodput factor measured over a named window with its statistical spread, so that two measurements under the same contract differ only in the change being evaluated.

1.  **Significance**: A contract makes the difference between two results attributable to the change under test, so a gain can be priced in accelerator-hours and repeated on another job.
2.  **Distinction**: Unlike a benchmark score, which reports one configuration under one set of rules, a contract governs every measurement a team takes, including the before and after of each change. Unlike a monitoring threshold, it states what a measurement must hold fixed to support a claim.
3.  **Common pitfall**: A frequent mistake is to fix the workload and hardware while letting the window drift. A runtime goodput measured over a day with no failures describes a better job than the one that runs for weeks on a shared fleet.

A complete measurement contract specifies the five categories in [[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/03 The Fleet in Production/09 Fleet Efficiency/V2-09.04 Measuring Goodput#^tbl-performance-engineering-measurement-contract|table 2]], pairing each required record with the misattribution it prevents. The workload envelope locks down the model revision, input and sequence-length distributions, global batch size, and the convergence or output-quality gate that defines valid work. The system-state record captures accelerator count and placement topology, clock and power limits, software, driver, numerical precision, and compiler versions, and—when evaluating scheduling goodput—the cluster pool and scheduling policy in effect. The timing boundary defines which initialization steps fall inside the timed region and how the host synchronizes with asynchronous device streams. Finally, the statistical report identifies the goodput factor, its denominator, and its calendar window alongside the trial count, central tendency, dispersion, step-time percentiles, and quality verification. Percentiles belong in the report because a mean step time can hide the slow tail episodes of an intermittent straggler. The fifth category, load generation, applies to serving measurements and must keep offering requests at the specified rate while the replica stalls, because a load generator that waits for each response eases off during a stall and hides it (**[[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/03 The Fleet in Production/10 Serving at Scale/V2-10 Serving at Scale#^sec-inference-scale|Serving at Scale]]**).

The required measurement window varies sharply across the three factors because each boundary loses capacity on a different timescale. Program goodput captures inefficiencies that repeat on every training step or decode iteration, so a window of several hundred warmed steady-state steps estimates it reliably. Runtime goodput captures episodic events such as node failures and rollbacks to the last checkpoint. A window shorter than the mean time between cluster interruptions can miss those events entirely and report a misleadingly high factor, so its window must span days to weeks at fleet scale (**[[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/02 Distributed ML/07 Fault Tolerance/V2-07 Fault Tolerance#^sec-fault-tolerance-reliability|Fault Tolerance]]**). Scheduling goodput tracks cluster contention, which fills queues during peak working hours and drains them overnight or between project deadlines, requiring a window that spans full demand cycles. Multiplying the three factors yields a job’s true MPG only when all three cover the same calendar window, even when program goodput within that window is estimated by sampling representative retained steps.

The timing boundary likewise determines whether startup overhead is attributed to runtime goodput or program goodput. End-to-end trials that start the clock before graph compilation and cache warmup measure the delay before the first productive step, which belongs to runtime goodput, whereas warmed steady-state trials isolate the per-step execution rate, which belongs to program goodput. Mixing cold-start and warmed runs can credit a code change for a speedup when the second trial simply hit a warm compiler cache; the measurement contract therefore reports cold-start duration and steady-state step time separately. When a more aggressive compiler pass speeds steady-state steps at the cost of a longer initial compile, it trades runtime goodput against program goodput—a trade-off that [[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/03 The Fleet in Production/09 Fleet Efficiency/V2-09.10 Trading Between Factors#^sec-performance-engineering-frontier-objective|section 9.9]] prices explicitly.

Industry benchmark suites illustrate how a strict measurement contract operates in practice. MLPerf Training measures wall-clock time to train a reference model to a specified validation target, and MLPerf Inference measures throughput under explicit latency bounds across standardized serving scenarios, evaluating complete hardware-software systems rather than isolated chips ([[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/03 The Fleet in Production/09 Fleet Efficiency/V2-09.14 Source References#^ref-mattson2020mlperf|Mattson et al. 2020]]; [[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/03 The Fleet in Production/09 Fleet Efficiency/V2-09.14 Source References#^ref-reddi2020|Reddi et al. 2019]]). Closed-division submissions hold the model architecture and optimizer fixed so that systems can be compared directly, while open-division submissions relax those constraints to showcase algorithmic and systems co-design. Because a benchmark submission runs on a dedicated, pre-warmed cluster allocation without multi-tenant queueing or long-run preemption churn, it primarily measures program goodput and reveals very little about scheduling or runtime goodput (perspective [9.1](https://mlsysbook.ai/vol2/09_performance_engineering/09_performance_engineering.html#psp-performance-engineering-hero-run-tax)).

Recording exact software versions in the system-state contract is essential because software upgrades alone can shift delivered throughput by a large multiple on identical silicon. Between September 2023 and April 2025, NVIDIA submitted the same eight-GPU DGX H100 node to four consecutive MLPerf Inference rounds while upgrading its inference stack from TensorRT 9.0 to 10.8 ([[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/03 The Fleet in Production/09 Fleet Efficiency/V2-09.14 Source References#^ref-reddi2020|Reddi et al. 2019]]; [[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/03 The Fleet in Production/09 Fleet Efficiency/V2-09.14 Source References#^ref-mlcommons2025inference|MLCommons 2025]]). Across those four rounds, offline throughput rose 2.7× on GPT-J, 1.4× on Llama 2 70B, and 1.2× on Stable Diffusion XL ([[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/03 The Fleet in Production/09 Fleet Efficiency/V2-09.04 Measuring Goodput#^fig-mlperf-software-gains|figure 3]]), whereas ResNet-50 and BERT—whose kernels were already mature—remained flat. Any before-and-after experiment that straddles a framework or compiler upgrade therefore confounds the upgrade with the optimization under test.

### Localizing the Lost Factor

^sec-performance-engineering-localize-factor

Even when a contract-controlled measurement confirms a genuine slowdown, knowing that a training run fell behind its schedule does not reveal which goodput boundary lost the capacity. An engineering team that opens a kernel profiler first will always find some exposed gap inside a step. The team can easily spend a week shaving milliseconds off a collective while most of the missing capacity was lost waiting for a complete allocation or recovering from unlogged restarts. Effective localization instead inspects telemetry from the cheapest, broadest record down to the most expensive, narrowest trace, matching each record to one boundary of [[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/03 The Fleet in Production/09 Fleet Efficiency/V2-09.03 ML Goodput#^tbl-performance-engineering-goodput-boundaries|table 1]].

At the top of that hierarchy sit scheduler records. For every submitted job, the cluster scheduler logs submission time, individual device assignments, the timestamp at which the minimum gang became complete, final release, and the resource pool, quota, and priority class under which the job ran. Pool-level logs add unallocated capacity and the fragmentation index from **[[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/02 Distributed ML/08 Fleet Orchestration/V2-08.13 Debugging Cluster Utilization#^sec-fleet-orchestration-resource-accounting|Allocated, active, and productive utilization]]**. Because the control plane already writes these records for admission control and chargeback, they carry zero extra instrumentation overhead and cover every job in the cluster. Scheduler records establish scheduling goodput directly and supply the all-allocated held time that forms the denominator of runtime goodput. What they cannot see is anything that happens *inside* a complete allocation: a wedged job that holds a full gang without executing a single step looks identical in scheduler logs to a job running at peak efficiency.

The second rung is the job timeline, the structured lifecycle log emitted by the job itself between its gang-complete timestamp and its release. The timeline records when the first training step began, the completion timestamp of every step, which steps each committed checkpoint retained, every failure, restart, and preemption event, and the time each step spent waiting on the input pipeline. Because it stores only a few scalar fields per step or lifecycle event, the timeline is compact enough to retain for every production job. It settles runtime goodput. The interval before the first step measures startup and compilation, pauses at checkpoint commits measure synchronous write overhead, steps repeated after a rollback measure rework, and the input-wait counter yields the data stall ratio of **[[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/01 The Fleet/04 Data Storage/V2-04.05 The Data Pipeline Equation#^eq-data-stall|equation 4.4]]**. Because the timeline also logs the wall-clock duration of every retained step, it flags a program goodput loss whenever retained steps run longer than their baseline target. It cannot distinguish, though, whether that slowdown came from exposed communication, memory stalls, or a straggling rank.

The third rung consists of step traces, the only telemetry that looks inside an individual step. Over a selected time window and set of ranks, a step trace captures every kernel launch, collective operation, memory copy, and host dispatch interval alongside hardware performance counters such as achieved HBM bandwidth. That detail partitions a retained step into computation, exposed communication, peer or pipeline waits, memory stalls, and host dispatch overhead, pinpointing which term of the Fleet Law (local work, communication, or synchronization; [[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/03 The Fleet in Production/09 Fleet Efficiency/V2-09.07 Program Goodput#^sec-performance-engineering-iron-law|section 9.6.1]]) an optimization must shorten. Because full tracing generates gigabytes of data and perturbs execution timing, it is reserved for short windows on sampled ranks; a clean step trace taken during a healthy hour says nothing about whether the job lost half its week to restarts.

| **Record**            | **Coverage and cost**                                                  | **Establishes**                                                                                                    | **Cannot establish**                                                                                    | **Factor it settles**                                      |
|:----------------------|:-----------------------------------------------------------------------|:-------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------|:--------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------|:-----------------------------------------------------------|
| **Scheduler records** | Every job and pool, continuously; written for admission and chargeback | Capacity not held by complete allocations; queue wait; gang-complete times; held time per job                      | Anything inside a held allocation, including whether any step ran                                       | Scheduling goodput; the denominator of runtime goodput     |
| **Job timeline**      | Every job, for its whole life; a few fields per step or event          | Startup, input waits, checkpoint pauses, restarts, preemptions, rework; retained step time; step-time distribution | Why a retained step takes as long as it does                                                            | Runtime goodput; detects but does not explain program loss |
| **Step traces**       | Selected ranks and short windows; large volume and some perturbation   | Intervals inside a step (computation, exposed communication, peer and stage waits, memory stalls, host dispatch)   | Losses outside the traced window (scheduling waits, restarts, checkpoint pauses, intermittent episodes) | Program goodput, and the Fleet Law term to shorten         |

Table 3: **Records That Localize a Goodput Loss**: Each record matches one accounting boundary, and the cheaper records come first because they cover the whole fleet continuously. A loss is traced to its factor before its mechanism is studied, and the step trace is collected only for a program loss the job timeline has already detected.

^tbl-performance-engineering-localization-records

Reading these three records from top to bottom ([[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/03 The Fleet in Production/09 Fleet Efficiency/V2-09.04 Measuring Goodput#^tbl-performance-engineering-localization-records|table 3]]) is a methodological requirement, not merely a cost-saving habit. Each rung settles one goodput factor and hands the remaining unexplained gap to the rung below, so engineers collect step traces only after scheduler logs and the job timeline confirm that retained steps themselves are running slowly. Skipping rungs leads to two classic diagnostic blunders. Starting with a step trace can pronounce a job healthy while it loses a third of its held hours to unexamined restarts. Starting and stopping at queue logs can blame the cluster scheduler for a week-long slowdown that the job timeline would immediately trace to a compiler regression. In both failure modes the local data are accurate, yet the diagnosis fails because the chosen telemetry cannot observe the boundary where the loss occurred.

For these three records to enforce the accounting boundaries of [[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/03 The Fleet in Production/09 Fleet Efficiency/V2-09.03 ML Goodput#^sec-performance-engineering-goodput-boundaries|section 9.2.3]], their instrumentation must align with those boundaries. If a job timeline logs only total step duration without separating data-loader stalls, every input wait is folded into step time and a runtime goodput loss masquerades as a program goodput loss. Similarly, because a single straggling accelerator stretches synchronous step time across every peer in its collective group, identifying which rank caused the slowdown requires lightweight per-rank step timers inside the job timeline.

Our running Llama 2 70B training job illustrates how the three rungs work together. Suppose that after one week on the shared cluster, the job has retained substantially fewer steps than predicted by a plan that assumed full-week allocation at the single-node step rate. Consulting the scheduler records first reveals that the job held partial node allocations before its full 16-node gang assembled—a scheduling loss quantified in [[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/03 The Fleet in Production/09 Fleet Efficiency/V2-09.05 Scheduling Goodput#^sec-performance-engineering-scheduling-evidence|section 9.4.3]]. Reading the job timeline from that gang-complete timestamp next isolates the initial startup delay, synchronous checkpoint pauses, steps re-executed after failures, and a modest input-stall fraction, which [[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/03 The Fleet in Production/09 Fleet Efficiency/V2-09.06 Runtime Goodput#^sec-performance-engineering-runtime-composition|section 9.5.2]] composes into runtime goodput. The same timeline also shows that every retained step ran longer than the single-node baseline from day one, proving that part of the shortfall lies in program goodput. Only at that point does the team trigger step traces on one data-parallel group over a brief window. Because per-rank timeline counters show no single outlier rank, the team inspects intervals common to all ranks and finds cross-node synchronization and collective waits with no computation beside them on every step, the exposed communication analyzed in [[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/03 The Fleet in Production/09 Fleet Efficiency/V2-09.07 Program Goodput#^sec-performance-engineering-exposed-communication|section 9.6.2]]. Each rung answers the exact question that the others cannot ([[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/03 The Fleet in Production/09 Fleet Efficiency/V2-09.04 Measuring Goodput#^fig-performance-engineering-localization-ladder|figure 4]]).

Diagnosing the eight-GPU serving replica follows the exact same three-rung progression. Cluster scheduler logs measure how long the replica waited for a full unfragmented node, the replica lifecycle log tracks weight loading, warmup, ready service, and restarts to establish runtime goodput, and decode step traces—evaluated against peak HBM bandwidth via MBU—measure how closely ready execution approaches the hardware ideal.

### Fleet Profiling by Sampling

^sec-performance-engineering-fleet-sampling

The first two rungs of the localization ladder scale effortlessly with cluster size because a scheduler log entry or a step-timeline record occupies only a few bytes whether a job spans one node or a thousand. Step traces do not scale in that way. When a job expands from 8 GPUs to 1,024 GPUs, multi-node bottlenecks emerge that no single-node trace can capture—such as fabric-crossing collectives and ranks stalled behind a distant straggler—so profiling must span many ranks at once. Yet if a 5-second Nsight Systems trace on one GPU produces 500 MB, capturing that same 5-second window across 1,024 GPUs generates roughly 512 GB of telemetry. No production cluster can ingest or store traces at that rate continuously, and logging every kernel launch perturbs the very step timing under study (perspective [9.2](https://mlsysbook.ai/vol2/09_performance_engineering/09_performance_engineering.html#psp-performance-engineering-physics-profiling-heisenberg-effect)). Deciding *which* ranks and time windows to trace is therefore a core part of fleet measurement.

Production fleets resolve this tension through hierarchical profiling, splitting telemetry between lightweight, always-on counters that detect an efficiency drop and targeted traces that diagnose its cause. Within a strict overhead budget, the always-on layer continuously records step duration, retained-step MFU, per-rank step timers, and hardware health telemetry (HBM bandwidth, power draw, and die temperature) across every active job. Once those counters flag that a goodput factor has degraded and narrow down where, targeted profiling captures a short trace window from specific ranks chosen to test a hypothesis. Comparing a suspected straggler directly against a healthy peer in the same collective group isolates the stall far more cleanly than tracing an arbitrary rank. ByteDance’s MegaScale system[[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/03 The Fleet in Production/09 Fleet Efficiency/V2-09.15 Footnotes#^fn4|⁴]] exemplifies this workflow. Lightweight CUDA-event timers record critical operations on every rank throughout production training, summarize per-rank latencies as a cluster-wide heat map, and drill into a synchronized timeline only for the parallel group that exhibits an anomaly ([[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/03 The Fleet in Production/09 Fleet Efficiency/V2-09.14 Source References#^ref-jiang2024megascale|Jiang et al. 2024]]). Below the step-trace rung, diagnosis then descends through framework operators, kernel timelines, and hardware performance counters along the profiling hierarchy of [[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/03 The Fleet in Production/09 Fleet Efficiency/V2-09.07 Program Goodput#^fig-optimization-hierarchy|figure 6]] ([[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/03 The Fleet in Production/09 Fleet Efficiency/V2-09.07 Program Goodput#^sec-performance-engineering-roofline|section 9.6.3]]).

To inspect inside steps continuously without paying for full traces, fleets rely on statistical profiling. A sampling policy selects a small fraction of kernels, steps, or ranks for detailed timing, and aggregating those samples reconstructs where step time is spent across the fleet. Google-Wide Profiling pioneered this approach across warehouse-scale computers by continuously sampling a small fraction of machines and aggregating the results into fleet-wide execution profiles ([[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/03 The Fleet in Production/09 Fleet Efficiency/V2-09.14 Source References#^ref-ren2010google|Ren et al. 2010]]). Applied to an ML accelerator fleet, fleet-wide aggregation ranks kernels and collectives by their share of total retained step time across all jobs. A collective primitive that consumes a modest slice of every job’s step often represents a far larger fleet-wide optimization target than a specialized kernel that dominates a single workload.

Statistical sampling is only as trustworthy as its stratification. A fleet sampling policy must stratify across job sizes and model architectures, pipeline stages and parallel groups, warmup and steady-state phases, and hours of the day. Sampling only rank 0 (which often performs extra logging and checkpoint coordination) or only the opening minutes of a job characterizes a special rank or phase rather than the fleet. Furthermore, because rare anomalies that occur once every several thousand steps can easily slip between periodic random samples, continuous sampling is paired with threshold triggers that automatically dump a detailed trace whenever an always-on timer crosses an anomaly bound.

Intermittent stragglers illustrate why triggered capture is essential. An accelerator can slow down for brief episodes due to thermal throttling, recoverable memory errors, transient fabric congestion, or host OS pauses such as page reclamation. Because a synchronous step completes only when its slowest participant finishes, a single rank running slower during an episode delays its entire collective group by up to that same fraction. The job’s cumulative program goodput loss scales with both the slowdown severity and the episode frequency. A periodic trace captured between episodes sees completely healthy execution. Always-on per-rank step timers catch the episodes as latency spikes, and crossing a duration threshold immediately triggers synchronized traces on both the lagging rank and a healthy peer. Correlating those triggered traces with device telemetry, switch counters, and host kernel logs then separates the rank that originated the stall from the peers that merely waited at the collective barrier.

### Detecting Regressions

^sec-performance-engineering-detecting-scaling-regressions-7c11

A production fleet changes continuously even when a model team leaves its training script untouched. Framework and compiler releases alter the kernels a model dispatches, driver and firmware updates modify clock management and collective algorithms, storage migrations shift input and checkpoint bandwidth, and scheduler policy updates reshape how allocations form. Any of those changes can erode a goodput factor across every job it touches—often only at multi-node scale, where a slight increase in peak activation memory triggers allocator defragmentation on one rank or a longer host pause stalls an entire synchronous gang. Systematic regression detection applies the measurement contract to every infrastructure or software change and monitors each goodput factor in the telemetry record that settles it ([[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/03 The Fleet in Production/09 Fleet Efficiency/V2-09.04 Measuring Goodput#^tbl-performance-engineering-scaling-regression-tests|table 4]]).

| **Testing tier**                   | **Factor** | **What it measures**                                                                                                    | **Regression caught**                                                                                                                                  |
|:-----------------------------------|:-----------|:------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------|:-------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------|
| **Small-scale canaries**           | Program    | Step time and MFU on one node and on a small multi-node allocation, establishing a scaling efficiency curve             | Code changes that look harmless on one GPU but bend the curve before full-fleet launch                                                                 |
| **Fleet baseline comparison**      | Program    | Every production run’s MFU of retained steps against the reference baseline for that architecture and software version  | Architecture, compiler, or configuration changes that reduce useful fleet work                                                                         |
| **Gray failure detection**         | Program    | Distribution of step times and per-rank step timers across the fleet                                                    | Straggler nodes; one node slowed by some fraction can lower synchronous data-parallel MFU by about that fraction                                       |
| **Startup and recovery baselines** | Runtime    | Time from gang completion to first step; checkpoint pause per commit; restarts, rework, and data stall ratio per day    | Compiler or framework upgrades that lengthen compilation, storage changes that slow checkpoint writes or input, recovery automation that stops working |
| **Allocation baselines**           | Scheduling | Queue wait to gang completion; capacity held in incomplete gangs; fragmentation index per pool over whole demand cycles | Scheduler policy or job-template changes that strand capacity or delay complete allocations                                                            |

Table 4: **Regression Test Tiers by Goodput Factor**: Program regressions are caught by canaries and fleet baselines of MFU and step time; runtime and scheduling regressions are caught only by baselines kept over long windows in the job timelines and scheduler records, because their losses are rare per step and appear across many jobs.

^tbl-performance-engineering-scaling-regression-tests

The program-goodput tiers catch most regressions introduced by model or kernel changes within minutes, because any per-step slowdown repeats on every iteration and manifests on a small multi-node canary. The runtime and scheduling tiers catch regressions that short canary runs cannot observe: a compiler release that adds minutes to graph compilation leaves steady-state step time untouched, and a quota policy that strands nodes in a shared pool surfaces only across a full diurnal demand cycle. Maintaining per-software-version and per-pool baselines under the same measurement contract allows engineers to tie any shift in scheduling, runtime, or program goodput directly to the release or policy change that introduced it. Benchmark runs miss the same runtime and scheduling losses that short canaries miss, so a cluster sized from benchmark numbers inherits every loss the benchmark omitted.

↩︎ Gray failures, the silent degradations that surface as stragglers rather than crashes, are established in **[[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/02 Distributed ML/07 Fault Tolerance/V2-07.10 Failure Detection and Recovery#^sec-fault-tolerance-reliability-reliability-distinguishing-stragglers-failures-285d|Stragglers versus fail-stop failures]]**.

**Systems Perspective 9.1: What a benchmark run measures**

Benchmark results, including MLPerf submissions, describe one tuned run under a specified protocol on a dedicated allocation. In goodput terms they measure program goodput, and useful work through the quality target, with scheduling goodput equal to one by construction and few of the interruptions a long production run accumulates. They are a reference for the program factor, not a capacity guarantee.

Production adds what the reference leaves out. Startup, checkpoint pauses, failures and rework, preemptions, and input waits are runtime losses, monitoring and contention with neighbors can lower program goodput further, and waiting for complete allocations is a scheduling loss no benchmark sees. As a planning scenario, if useful throughput is 10–20 percent below the rate that implied 30 days of training, the same work takes about 33.3–37.5 days. [[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/03 The Fleet in Production/09 Fleet Efficiency/V2-09.06 Runtime Goodput#^sec-performance-engineering-runtime-goodput|Section 9.5]] sizes the runtime share of that gap for a well-run job.

Detecting a regression and verifying an optimization are the same measurement performed in opposite directions. Every candidate change must be evaluated at the accounting boundary it targets—in retained step time and MFU for a program change, in the job timeline for a runtime change, and in scheduler logs for an allocation policy change—rather than in an isolated microbenchmark. Doubling the speed of a single kernel saves at most half of that kernel’s share of the step, and saves zero wall-clock time if the kernel already ran hidden behind an exposed collective. Because an optimization at one layer can degrade another factor, all three goodput factors are re-measured under the contract before promotion. Even a change that appears purely local can exhaust a shared hardware resource when deployed across thousands of nodes simultaneously, which is why production fleets gate infrastructure changes behind staged rollouts (**[[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/03 The Fleet in Production/12 ML Operations at Scale/V2-12.06 CI CD for ML at Scale#^sec-ml-operations-scale-staged-rollout-strategies-2d1f|Staged rollout strategies]]**). One of the clearest postmortems of a local cost turning into a fleet-wide outage comes from edge networking rather than ML training.

**War Story 9.1: The regex that saturated the edge (2019)**

**Context**: Cloudflare’s web application firewall evaluates rules on HTTP/HTTPS traffic across its global edge ([[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/03 The Fleet in Production/09 Fleet Efficiency/V2-09.14 Source References#^ref-cloudflare2019outage|Cloudflare 2019]]).

**Mechanism**: A rule deployed on July 2, 2019 caused excessive regular-expression backtracking and drove the CPUs serving HTTP/HTTPS traffic to nearly 100 percent utilization across the network. A previously removed CPU-use protection, missing performance tests, and a global rollout allowed the local computational cost to become a fleet-wide failure.

**Impact**: The resulting 27-minute outage disrupted proxying, content delivery, and firewall service. Traffic through the network dropped by about 82 percent, and users received 502 errors.

**Fix**: Disabling the firewall globally restored traffic; the offending change was then removed and the firewall re-enabled after testing. The postmortem reported restoring CPU-use protection and plans for performance tests, staged rule deployment, and a regular-expression engine with runtime guarantees.

**Systems lesson**: Correct outputs do not establish acceptable execution cost. A change that leaves every result correct can still lower goodput across the whole fleet at once. Performance-sensitive changes to an ML fleet, such as a compiler upgrade, a kernel library, or a new input shape, are therefore measured under the contract, staged on a small share of the fleet, and promoted only when the factors they touch hold.

A measurement contract that locks down each factor’s window, a three-rung telemetry ladder that localizes losses from the cheapest record downward, and continuous baselines that catch regressions together make each goodput boundary observable in production. The first boundary a workload encounters is the formation of a complete allocation, where fleet capacity can be lost before a single step begins.
