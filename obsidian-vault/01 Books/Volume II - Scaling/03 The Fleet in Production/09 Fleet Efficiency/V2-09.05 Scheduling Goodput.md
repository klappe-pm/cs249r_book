# V2-09.05 Scheduling Goodput

[[Home|Home]] · [[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/Volume 2 Overview|Volume Overview]] · [[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/03 The Fleet in Production/09 Fleet Efficiency/V2-09 Fleet Efficiency|V2-09 Fleet Efficiency]]

^sec-performance-engineering-scheduling-goodput

Partially assembled gangs, scattered node fragments, and draining reservations waste accelerator hours *before* any workload holds a complete allocation, whereas zombie jobs, input starvation, exposed collectives, and fragmented key-value caches waste capacity inside an allocation a job already holds. A cluster orchestrator’s general health dashboards surface both classes of waste together, but scheduling goodput counts only the pre-allocation losses; fixing the post-allocation losses leaves scheduling goodput unchanged. Separating pre-allocation losses from post-allocation losses, and quantifying the pre-allocation share, is what scheduling goodput adds to the orchestrator’s own view. The policy levers that recover those scheduling losses (atomic gang admission, backfill, bin packing, topology-aware placement, quota lending, and preemption policy) are developed in **[[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/02 Distributed ML/08 Fleet Orchestration/V2-08 Fleet Orchestration#^sec-fleet-orchestration|Fleet Orchestration]]**.

### What Scheduling Goodput Counts

^sec-performance-engineering-scheduling-goodput-counts

A rigid distributed training job holding half of its requested accelerators while waiting for the rest completes zero steps, not half as many, yet a naive utilization counter credits every assigned device as busy. Scheduling goodput eliminates that distortion by taking the ratio of two capacity-time integrals over the same calendar window. The denominator is the in-service capacity-time offered by the pool: its allocatable accelerators multiplied by the window duration. The numerator is the all-allocated capacity-time held by complete allocations, summed across jobs as each job’s device count multiplied by the interval from the moment its minimum gang became complete until release. A complete allocation corresponds to the minimum viable execution group of **[[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/02 Distributed ML/08 Fleet Orchestration/V2-08.05 Core Allocation Constraints#^sec-fleet-orchestration-gang-scheduling|Gang scheduling]]**—every accelerator, interconnect domain, and co-scheduled host resource required to execute one synchronous step. For a rigid job, that group is the full requested gang; for an elastic job, it is the active gang size between two reconfiguration boundaries, and any accelerators the job relinquishes re-enter the numerator only when another complete allocation claims them. Because nothing inside the allocation enters this ratio, a wedged job that holds a complete gang without running a step achieves the same scheduling goodput as a job executing at peak FLOP/s.

Every device-hour of in-service capacity outside a complete allocation is a scheduling loss, regardless of its operational cause. Capacity sits unassigned when offered demand falls below pool size, when waiting jobs have exhausted their team quotas, or when free accelerators are scattered across racks in fragments too small for any queued gang. Capacity is assigned yet incomplete when a gang holds an initial subset of nodes while waiting for the remainder, when two partial gangs deadlock over the last free nodes, when freed accelerators sit idle in a reservation draining for the head-of-line gang, or during brief container teardown and setup transitions between tenants. Because an incomplete gang cannot advance synchronous training, [[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/03 The Fleet in Production/09 Fleet Efficiency/V2-09.03 ML Goodput#^sec-performance-engineering-goodput-boundaries|section 9.2.3]] restricted the orchestrator’s allocated tier to complete allocations alone.

Accelerators removed from service for hardware repair or scheduled maintenance are excluded from the scheduling goodput denominator because no scheduling policy can place work on broken nodes. Those out-of-service hours are tracked alongside MPG as cluster availability, keeping hardware downtime visible without blaming the scheduler. If a report instead divides by total installed silicon, hardware unreliability is folded into scheduling goodput. The measurement contract ([[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/03 The Fleet in Production/09 Fleet Efficiency/V2-09.04 Measuring Goodput#^sec-performance-engineering-measurement-contract|section 9.3.1]]) therefore requires stating whether the denominator is in-service or installed capacity so that two fleet reports are never compared on mismatched baselines.

When scheduling goodput is low, scheduler logs distinguish between two fundamentally different causes that call for opposite remedies. If accelerators sat unallocated while the job queue was empty, the pool exceeds current demand—a capacity-planning and quota-lending problem. If accelerators sat unallocated or trapped in partial gangs while runnable jobs waited in the queue, placement constraints, rigid quotas, or fragmentation stranded the hardware—a scheduling-policy problem. Recording queue depth alongside every interval of unheld capacity separates the two immediately.

↪ Sizing a fleet against forecast demand, the remedy for capacity that no queued work wants, is developed in **[[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/03 The Fleet in Production/12 ML Operations at Scale/V2-12.10 Fleet Economics and Utilization#^sec-ml-operations-scale-planning|Capacity planning as a reliability control]]**.

Because unallocated fragments, partial gangs, and draining reservations belong to the shared pool rather than to any single workload, scheduling goodput is a property of the resource pool. Our running Llama 2 70B job inherits the scheduling goodput of the pool from which it draws its nodes. A job’s own time spent waiting in the queue is a separate metric. Queue wait reduces scheduling goodput only during intervals when pool accelerators actually sat outside complete allocations. While other complete jobs occupy the cluster, a queued job’s wait costs zero fleet goodput and instead lengthens that job’s time to result, a trade-off priced in [[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/03 The Fleet in Production/09 Fleet Efficiency/V2-09.10 Trading Between Factors#^sec-performance-engineering-frontier-objective|section 9.9]]. As the queueing analysis in **[[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/02 Distributed ML/08 Fleet Orchestration/V2-08.04 Scheduling Objectives and Their Conflicts#^sec-fleet-orchestration-objectives|Scheduling Objectives and Their Conflicts]]** demonstrated, driving pool utilization and scheduling goodput toward one causes queue waiting times to rise steeply.

### Attributing Scheduler Losses

^sec-performance-engineering-scheduler-attribution

Defining scheduling goodput strictly at the complete-allocation boundary means that not every failure mode managed by the orchestration team is a scheduling loss. If a zombie job were subtracted from scheduling goodput simply because orchestrator lease timeouts clean it up, those wasted hours would be deducted from capacity that the scheduling numerator already counted as a complete allocation, penalizing the same hours twice. Attributing a capacity loss to whichever team owns the remediation lever therefore breaks the multiplicative goodput identity. Whereas the operational debugging workflow in **[[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/02 Distributed ML/08 Fleet Orchestration/V2-08.13 Debugging Cluster Utilization#^sec-fleet-orchestration-debugging|Debugging Cluster Utilization]]** groups utilization anti-patterns by their observable symptoms, goodput accounting classifies each loss pattern by the *first boundary its capacity fails to cross*. Capacity outside a complete allocation reduces scheduling goodput; capacity inside a complete allocation that produces no checkpointed step reduces runtime goodput; and capacity in retained steps that execute below the hardware ideal reduces program goodput. Because each lost interval stops at the first boundary it fails, every device-hour is charged once. Applying that boundary test to the orchestration anti-patterns shows that the system layer responsible for fixing a loss often differs from the goodput factor that records it ([[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/03 The Fleet in Production/09 Fleet Efficiency/V2-09.05 Scheduling Goodput#^tbl-performance-engineering-scheduler-attribution|table 5]]).

↩︎ The utilization anti-patterns and the evidence that diagnoses each one are established in **[[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/02 Distributed ML/08 Fleet Orchestration/V2-08.13 Debugging Cluster Utilization#^sec-fleet-orchestration-anti-patterns|Common utilization anti-patterns]]**.

| **Loss pattern**                                        | **What the capacity is doing**                                                                             | **First boundary missed** | **Charged to**     | **Layer that removes it**                                                          |
|:--------------------------------------------------------|:-----------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------|:--------------------------|:-------------------|:-----------------------------------------------------------------------------------|
| **Partial-gang hold**, including hold-and-wait deadlock | Assigned to a rigid job still waiting for members, so no step can run                                      | Complete allocation       | Scheduling goodput | Orchestration (atomic group admission)                                             |
| **Reservation for a waiting gang**                      | Freed devices kept idle so the gang at the head of the queue can assemble                                  | Complete allocation       | Scheduling goodput | Orchestration (backfill under enforced runtime bounds)                             |
| **Fragmentation and capability mismatch**               | Free devices scattered so that no queued gang fits, or of a type no queued job requests                    | Complete allocation       | Scheduling goodput | Orchestration (bin packing, capability matching)                                   |
| **Topology mismatch, waiting**                          | Held free while a compact placement assembles                                                              | Complete allocation       | Scheduling goodput | Orchestration (placement policy)                                                   |
| **Topology mismatch, running**                          | Running across slower links than a compact placement would use                                             | Ideal rate                | Program goodput    | Collectives and parallel layout; orchestration (placement policy)                  |
| **Idle reservation**                                    | Reserved block held as an allocation while no job takes steps                                              | Retained step             | Runtime goodput    | Orchestration (quota reclamation and lending)                                      |
| **Zombie job**                                          | Complete allocation kept after execution has stopped                                                       | Retained step             | Runtime goodput    | Fault tolerance (failure propagation); orchestration (leases, progress monitoring) |
| **Preemption**                                          | Steps since the last checkpoint discarded; state reloaded and warmed up                                    | Retained step             | Runtime goodput    | Orchestration (preemption budgets); fault tolerance (checkpoint cadence)           |

| **Cache fragmentation limiting admission** (serving)    | A ready replica admits fewer concurrent requests than its node could serve                                 | Ideal rate                | Program goodput    | Serving (paged cache allocation)                                                   |

Table 5: **Attributing Orchestration Losses to Goodput Factors**: Each utilization loss pattern of fleet orchestration is charged to the first accounting boundary its capacity fails to cross. Scheduling goodput receives only the losses that occur before a complete allocation exists, and a topology mismatch lands in scheduling or program goodput depending on whether the job waited or ran. The factor a loss lowers and the layer that removes it are recorded separately.

^tbl-performance-engineering-scheduler-attribution

Idle accelerators appear in two different goodput factors, depending on their allocation state. When a partial gang, a stranded fragment, or a draining head-of-line reservation leaves accelerators idle *outside* any complete allocation, the loss belongs to scheduling goodput. By contrast, when a team holds a static capacity reservation (**[[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/02 Distributed ML/08 Fleet Orchestration/V2-08.12 Multi-Tenancy and Quotas#^sec-fleet-orchestration-quota-governance|Quota governance and organizational dynamics]]**) as a complete allocation while running no steps on it, that capacity crosses the scheduling boundary and fails at the runtime boundary, registering as a runtime goodput loss. Reclaiming an idle quota block and returning it to the shared pool therefore shifts the accounting before it improves fleet output. Whatever fraction of the reclaimed block a borrowing team puts to work is recovered, while any fraction that finds no borrower sits free in the pool and converts from a runtime loss into a scheduling loss. An orchestration policy can shift waste from one boundary to another without increasing useful work; only the product ${\eta_{\text{scheduling}}} \times {\eta_{\text{runtime}}} \times {\eta_{\text{program}}}$ reveals whether the fleet came out ahead.

In every row of [[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/03 The Fleet in Production/09 Fleet Efficiency/V2-09.05 Scheduling Goodput#^tbl-performance-engineering-scheduler-attribution|table 5]], the accounting charges the specific time interval that exhibits the loss pattern rather than tainting a job’s entire run. A zombie job wastes its complete allocation from the moment progress halts until the lease expires and releases the nodes (**[[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/02 Distributed ML/08 Fleet Orchestration/V2-08.05 Core Allocation Constraints#^sec-fleet-orchestration-deadlock|Deadlock prevention and detection]]**). An input-starved job’s data-loader stalls are charged to runtime goodput from the job timeline’s input-wait counter, while the active execution intervals between stalls pass into retained step time and are judged on their program goodput.

Finally, a communication or placement bottleneck on a running job lies entirely past the first two boundaries, so it is charged to program goodput, and a targeted step trace isolates the exposed collective in the Fleet Law’s communication term ([[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/03 The Fleet in Production/09 Fleet Efficiency/V2-09.07 Program Goodput#^sec-performance-engineering-exposed-communication|section 9.6.2]]). Once those post-allocation rows are routed to runtime and program goodput, the pre-allocation rows that remain can be measured directly from the scheduler’s logs.

### Evidence, Size, and the Serving Replica

^sec-performance-engineering-scheduling-evidence

Enforcing the complete-allocation boundary in production hinges on a single timestamp in the scheduler logs, the top rung of [[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/03 The Fleet in Production/09 Fleet Efficiency/V2-09.04 Measuring Goodput#^sec-performance-engineering-localize-factor|section 9.3.2]]. The cluster scheduler must explicitly record the instant a gang allocation becomes complete, as Kubernetes `PodGroup` admission does in **[[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/02 Distributed ML/08 Fleet Orchestration/V2-08.06 Orchestration Paradigms#^sec-fleet-orchestration-kubernetes|Kubernetes declarative container orchestration]]**, rather than inferring completion from the job’s first training step. Inferring gang completion from the first step would sweep process launch and compilation into the pre-allocation window, misclassifying a runtime startup loss as a scheduling loss. Likewise, if a scheduler logs only individual node bindings without a gang-complete event, analysts can reconstruct completeness for rigid jobs from their requested gang size, but cannot do so reliably for elastic jobs whose valid size changes over time.

When that gang-complete timestamp is present, scheduler logs provide everything needed to compute scheduling goodput: summing each job’s device count over the interval from gang completion to release yields the numerator, summing partially assigned gangs from first device assignment to gang completion quantifies partial-hold waste, and tracking unallocated devices alongside queue state accounts for the remainder. The utilization breakdown from **[[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/02 Distributed ML/08 Fleet Orchestration/V2-08.14 Fallacies and Pitfalls#^sec-fleet-orchestration-fallacies|Fallacies and Pitfalls]]** (the pitfall of treating all allocated GPU-hours as productive) illustrates how these boundaries partition a shared cluster pool of the kind that hosts our running Llama 2 70B job.

**Napkin Math 9.2: Scheduling goodput of a shared pool**

**Problem**: In a shared fleet’s snapshot, 80 percent of in-service capacity is assigned to jobs, 15 percent is held by groups still waiting for their remaining members, and another 5 percent sits in complete allocations waiting for input. What is the pool’s scheduling goodput, and how far can atomic group admission alone raise it?

**Variables**:

- Assigned share 0.80, which includes the incomplete-group share 0.15.
- Input-wait share 0.05, which lies inside complete allocations and is therefore a runtime loss, sized in [[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/03 The Fleet in Production/09 Fleet Efficiency/V2-09.06 Runtime Goodput#^sec-performance-engineering-runtime-goodput|section 9.5]].

**Math**:

- Scheduling goodput: ${\eta_{\text{scheduling}}}$ = 0.80 $-$ 0.15 = 0.65.
- Scheduling loss: 1 $-$ 0.65 = 0.35, the 0.20 no job holds plus the 0.15 held by incomplete groups.
- Ceiling for removing partial holds: group admission returns the 0.15 to the pool as free capacity, so ${\eta_{\text{scheduling}}}$ rises at most to 0.65 $+$ 0.15 = 0.80, and only if other jobs’ complete allocations take every freed device.

**Result**: The pool’s scheduling goodput is 0.65, so 35 percent of its capacity is lost before any step runs, and removing partial holds alone can raise it no higher than 0.80.

**Systems insight**: The scheduling loss of 0.35 is more than three times the at most 0.10 of held capacity that Llama 3 405B lost to all its interruptions on a dedicated cluster. Because the factors multiply, every later gain in a job’s runtime or program goodput reaches this pool at 0.65 of its value on a dedicated cluster.

This constructed pool snapshot—which also anchors the whole-fleet synthesis in [[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/03 The Fleet in Production/09 Fleet Efficiency/V2-09.09 The Whole Fleet#^sec-performance-engineering-whole-fleet|section 9.8]]—shows why atomic group admission is necessary but not sufficient. Releasing partial-gang holds raises scheduling goodput only insofar as backfill and bin packing refill the freed accelerators with complete gangs, and pushing past the 0.80 ceiling requires runnable demand for the remaining unallocated devices. Queue logs distinguish whether that unheld capacity is stranded by fragmentation and rigid quotas, which scheduling policy can recover, or lacks runnable demand altogether, which only quota lending or capacity planning can address.

A single fleet-wide average also conceals how scheduling policy distributes goodput across job sizes, which is why production fleets track scheduling goodput by size cohort. Across Google’s TPU fleet, scheduling goodput exceeds 95 percent for every size cohort, peaking at the smallest and largest jobs while dipping for medium-sized jobs ([[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/03 The Fleet in Production/09 Fleet Efficiency/V2-09.14 Source References#^ref-wongpanich2025fleet|Wongpanich et al. 2025]]). Every one of those measured cohorts sits far above the constructed snapshot of this section, which illustrates the scheduling losses rather than describing a measured fleet. Preemption policy explains that U-shaped curve. Evicting a multi-pod training run would discard a long initialization and significant post-checkpoint work, so the scheduler shields the largest gangs from preemption. At the other extreme, single-node jobs finish quickly and pack easily into small gaps when preempted, so the scheduler uses them to defragment the pool and concentrates evictions on medium-sized gangs instead. Raising the scheduling guarantee for one size class therefore lowers it for another—a policy trade-off that a single fleet-wide average hides.

The eight-GPU serving replica crosses the scheduling boundary as a single indivisible gang: one full node across which tensor parallelism partitions the model weights. Because each replica requires a whole node, serving pools are sensitive to the intra-node fragmentation that small training or evaluation jobs leave behind. A pool can have dozens of free GPUs scattered across partial nodes and still have zero whole nodes available. Both a replica waiting in the queue for an unfragmented node and dedicated serving nodes sitting without a placed replica count as scheduling losses. Once placed on a node, however, the replica holds a complete allocation even before it can answer requests: weight loading, warmup, and graph compilation are runtime losses, while spare capacity held on a ready replica for traffic bursts is a program loss. Placement records, replica lifecycle logs, and decode traces separate the three. For a replica as for a training job, scheduling goodput ends where a complete allocation begins, which leaves open how much of that held time becomes retained work.

1.  In a shared pool’s snapshot, 80% of in-service capacity is assigned to jobs, 15% is held by groups still waiting for their remaining members, and 5% sits in complete allocations waiting for input. What is the pool’s scheduling goodput, and how far can atomic group admission alone raise it?

    1.  0.80, rising to 0.95, because every assigned device counts as held and admission removes the input waits
    2.  0.60, rising to 0.80, because both the incomplete groups and the input waits are scheduling losses
    3.  0.65, rising at most to 0.80, and only if other jobs’ complete allocations take every freed device
    4.  0.65, rising to 1.0, because group admission eliminates every interval of unheld capacity

2.  A team’s reserved block is granted as an allocation and refused to everyone else while no job takes steps on it. Which goodput factor is charged, and why does reclaiming the block move part of the loss rather than recover all of it?

3.  True or False: A job’s own wait in the queue always lowers its pool’s scheduling goodput.

4.  Two pools report the same low scheduling goodput. In pool X, capacity sat free while the queue was empty; in pool Y, capacity sat free while gangs waited. Which reading of the scheduler records is correct?

    1.  Both pools need a better placement policy, because scheduling goodput measures the competence of the scheduler
    2.  Pool X is larger than its demand, a capacity planning question, while pool Y has capacity stranded by placement, quota, or fragmentation, a policy question
    3.  Pool X has a fragmentation problem and pool Y has more capacity than its demand
    4.  Neither value can be interpreted without step traces from the jobs in each pool
