# V2-09.11 Fallacies and Pitfalls

[[Home|Home]] · [[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/Volume 2 Overview|Volume Overview]] · [[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/03 The Fleet in Production/09 Fleet Efficiency/V2-09 Fleet Efficiency|V2-09 Fleet Efficiency]]

^sec-performance-engineering-fallacies

An organization can report a fleet MFU above the best published flagship runs while its shared cluster turns little more than a quarter of its offered capacity into training progress. Each misconception below treats a single factor, a single observation window, or a single kernel as the entire efficiency story; the corresponding goodput boundary exposes what that narrow view leaves out.

**Fallacy**: *A job with high MFU is using the fleet well.*

MFU measures program goodput and nothing else. Because it divides only by the time spent inside retained steps, it is blind to capacity that the job never held and to held time that produced no retained progress. In our running example, retained steps execute at a program goodput of 0.48—above the 0.41 reported for the Llama 3 405B pretraining run—yet the pool’s scheduling goodput of 0.65 and the job’s runtime goodput of 0.90 leave a pool-level MPG of only 0.28. Only the full product of [[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/03 The Fleet in Production/09 Fleet Efficiency/V2-09.03 ML Goodput#^eq-perf-ml-productivity-goodput|equation 1]], composed in [[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/03 The Fleet in Production/09 Fleet Efficiency/V2-09.09 The Whole Fleet#^sec-performance-engineering-whole-fleet-decomposition|section 9.8.1]], states how much of the fleet’s capacity becomes useful work.

**Fallacy**: *A fleet with high capacity, occupancy, and duty cycle is productive.*

The three traditional cluster metrics count whether accelerators are available, allocated, or electrically active, but none of them counts useful model progress. Availability counts free accelerators without regard to network topology, so a distributed job requiring a contiguous mesh can sit blocked in the queue beside a large but fragmented pool. Occupancy counts accelerators assigned to jobs, so a device stalled on input data, executing unoptimized kernels, or repeating steps lost to preemption still registers as fully occupied. Duty cycle counts whether a device’s execution engines are active, not what fraction of peak throughput they achieve or whether their operations advance the model. The fleet-goodput framework replaces all three metrics with the goodput factors precisely because each factor tracks capacity-time across the next boundary of useful progress rather than stopping as soon as the hardware appears busy ([[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/03 The Fleet in Production/09 Fleet Efficiency/V2-09.14 Source References#^ref-wongpanich2025fleet|Wongpanich et al. 2025]]).

**Pitfall**: *Charging each loss to the team that will fix it.*

Assigning losses by engineering team routes each problem to someone who can act on it, but doing so inside the goodput factors breaks the identity that allows those factors to multiply. Because a zombie job’s accelerators are already counted as held in the scheduling numerator, charging zombie time to scheduling goodput—on the grounds that the orchestrator’s lease mechanism will clean it up—subtracts the same lost hours twice. Similarly, charging a slow straggler node to reliability makes the slowdown vanish from program goodput, even though every synchronous step runs at the pace of that slowest rank. To preserve multiplicative accounting, the boundary where time is lost determines which factor records the loss, while the root cause determines which engineering team owns the fix ([[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/03 The Fleet in Production/09 Fleet Efficiency/V2-09.03 ML Goodput#^sec-performance-engineering-goodput-boundaries|section 9.2.3]]); a complete attribution table therefore carries both columns side by side ([[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/03 The Fleet in Production/09 Fleet Efficiency/V2-09.05 Scheduling Goodput#^tbl-performance-engineering-scheduler-attribution|table 5]]).

**Fallacy**: *A single profiling run is sufficient to characterize a job’s goodput.*

A single short run can characterize at most one factor, because the three goodput factors lose time on timescales that differ by orders of magnitude. Program goodput loses time inside every step, so a window of several hundred warmed steady-state steps estimates it reliably. Runtime goodput, by contrast, loses time in rare, expensive interruptions: on 128 GPUs, the running example encounters a hardware fault roughly once every 13 days, meaning a single-day profile typically captures no failure and no recovery. Scheduling goodput varies with cluster-wide demand, which fills queues during peak hours and drains overnight. For this reason, the measurement contract ([[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/03 The Fleet in Production/09 Fleet Efficiency/V2-09.04 Measuring Goodput#^sec-performance-engineering-measurement-contract|section 9.3.1]]) specifies both the factor and its required observation window, and fleet sampling ([[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/03 The Fleet in Production/09 Fleet Efficiency/V2-09.04 Measuring Goodput#^sec-performance-engineering-fleet-sampling|section 9.3.3]]) spreads short program traces across that longer window.

**Pitfall**: *Subtracting one overhead budget that mixes factors.*

A spreadsheet planning budget that reserves a few percent each for pipeline bubbles, checkpoints, failure recovery, scheduling delay, and preemption—and then subtracts their sum from one operational ratio—conflates all three goodput factors. Pipeline bubbles occur inside retained steps and belong to program goodput; scheduling delay precedes gang allocation and belongs to scheduling goodput; only checkpoints, failure recovery, and preemption belong to runtime goodput. Multiplying the remainder of such a mixed budget by a measured fleet MFU double-counts pipeline bubbles and folds scheduling losses into a factor that has no scheduling term. A sound capacity budget assigns each loss to its proper boundary first and sums only the terms that share the same factor ([[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/03 The Fleet in Production/09 Fleet Efficiency/V2-09.06 Runtime Goodput#^sec-performance-engineering-runtime-composition|section 9.5.2]]).

**Fallacy**: *More FLOP/s means a proportionally faster step.*

Raising peak arithmetic throughput shortens only the compute-bound portion of the iron law, and only where that computation is exposed on the critical path. The serving replica’s batch-one decode step uses roughly 0.3 percent of its FP16 arithmetic peak; doubling peak FLOP/s at the same HBM bandwidth leaves its 5.2 ms latency floor untouched and merely cuts its numerical MFU in half. Likewise, the running training example spends 65 ms of each 245 ms illustrative fleet step above its single-node baseline, in what the example assumes is cross-node synchronization and collective waits that faster arithmetic does not accelerate, and adopting a lower-precision number format encounters the exact same Amdahl limit ([[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/03 The Fleet in Production/09 Fleet Efficiency/V2-09.08 Recovering Program Goodput#^sec-performance-engineering-kernel-levers|section 9.7.3]]). Evaluating a step against the roofline model and the Fleet Law terms ([[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/03 The Fleet in Production/09 Fleet Efficiency/V2-09.07 Program Goodput#^sec-performance-engineering-roofline|section 9.6.3]]) predicts how much speedup a faster accelerator will actually deliver.

**Pitfall**: *Judging a job by its mean step time while ignoring the step-time distribution.*

A healthy mean step time can mask severe tail episodes. Because a synchronous distributed step completes only when its slowest rank finishes, a single device that experiences intermittent slowdowns—from thermal throttling, link bit-error retries, or host OS pauses—delays its entire collective group on every episode. Averaging across thousands of steps smears those stalls into what looks like a minor, uniform slowdown. Per-rank step-time percentiles expose those episodes as tail outliers and trigger targeted traces comparing the slow rank against a healthy peer ([[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/03 The Fleet in Production/09 Fleet Efficiency/V2-09.04 Measuring Goodput#^sec-performance-engineering-fleet-sampling|section 9.3.3]]), while the gray-failure regression check of [[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/03 The Fleet in Production/09 Fleet Efficiency/V2-09.04 Measuring Goodput#^tbl-performance-engineering-scaling-regression-tests|table 4]] monitors the same tail ratio across the fleet. Every such tail stall lowers program goodput regardless of which subsystem team eventually replaces the degraded node.

**Fallacy**: *The largest kernel is where program goodput is lost.*

The largest kernels in a transformer step are typically its dense matrix multiplications, and in a well-tuned implementation they already execute near the accelerator’s arithmetic ceiling. Normalization, activation, softmax, and optimizer kernels sit on the memory-bandwidth slope and perform a tiny fraction of the arithmetic, yet together they can consume a substantial share of on-device step time. At fleet scale, exposed inter-node collectives that do not appear in a single-GPU kernel ranking can outweigh them all. Indeed, the constructed drill-down for our running example ([[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/03 The Fleet in Production/09 Fleet Efficiency/V2-09.07 Program Goodput#^sec-performance-engineering-roofline|section 9.6.3]]) places its largest program loss in exposed cross-node synchronization and collective waits. Partitioning step time into matrix multiplications versus everything else, and matching the remainder against [[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/03 The Fleet in Production/09 Fleet Efficiency/V2-09.07 Program Goodput#^tbl-performance-engineering-bottleneck-patterns|table 7]], identifies which Fleet Law term holds the recoverable loss before any individual kernel is tuned.

**Pitfall**: *Treating a successful compilation as proof that the program is well optimized.*

A compiler applies only the transformations its passes implement under its internal cost model, and a completed build does not guarantee that those passes succeeded. Automatic sharding propagation inserts a resharding collective wherever two inferred tensor layouts conflict, allowing an unintended AllGather to appear inside every transformer layer ([[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/03 The Fleet in Production/09 Fleet Efficiency/V2-09.08 Recovering Program Goodput#^sec-performance-engineering-gspmd|section 9.7.1]]). Likewise, a dynamic shape distribution that triggers recompilation every few hundred steps surfaces as slow retained steps—lowering program goodput—rather than as a one-time startup cost ([[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/03 The Fleet in Production/09 Fleet Efficiency/V2-09.10 Trading Between Factors#^sec-performance-engineering-compilation-overhead|section 9.9.3]]). A compiled step should therefore be treated as a testable hypothesis, verified by checking the profiler trace’s collective count against the intended layout and by measuring program goodput before and after under the measurement contract.

**Fallacy**: *Fixing the largest loss first recovers the most useful work.*

The loss shares of [[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/03 The Fleet in Production/09 Fleet Efficiency/V2-09.03 ML Goodput#^eq-perf-goodput-loss-shares|equation 2]] describe where fleet capacity went in the past, not how much useful work an engineering lever will recover in the future. In the running example, scheduling loses 35 percent of fleet capacity and program execution loses 31 percent, yet a hypothetical perfect scheduler would convert only about 15 percent of fleet capacity into additional useful progress. Capacity recovered at the scheduling boundary must still pass through the runtime and program boundaries at their current rates. At the subsystem ceilings, the scheduling lever raises MPG from 0.28 to 0.35, whereas the program lever raises it to 0.38. Engineering priority is governed by the marginal value of one point of each factor—the product of the other two factors—and by each lever’s reachable ceiling ([[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/03 The Fleet in Production/09 Fleet Efficiency/V2-09.09 The Whole Fleet#^sec-performance-engineering-whole-fleet-decomposition|section 9.8.1]]), not by the raw size of the historical loss slice.

**Pitfall**: *Counting retained steps as useful work without checking quality.*

MPG counts retained steps at the ideal rate under the assumption that each step advances model quality as much as it did before an optimization. A change that accelerates steps by lowering numerical precision, inflating the global batch size, or relaxing synchronization can raise program goodput while requiring more steps to reach the target loss, causing the fleet to report higher goodput while delivering the trained model later. Both the quality gate of the measurement contract ([[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/03 The Fleet in Production/09 Fleet Efficiency/V2-09.04 Measuring Goodput#^sec-performance-engineering-measurement-contract|section 9.3.1]]) and the efficiency frontier’s time-to-quality-target metric ([[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/03 The Fleet in Production/09 Fleet Efficiency/V2-09.10 Trading Between Factors#^sec-performance-engineering-frontier|section 9.9.4]]) catch this degradation, and statistical goodput ([[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/03 The Fleet in Production/09 Fleet Efficiency/V2-09.03 ML Goodput#^sec-performance-engineering-goodput-family|section 9.2.4]]) quantifies it directly. A throughput speedup counts as useful work only after the quality gate passes.

Each of these pitfalls measures a single boundary in isolation and mistakes it for the whole system. Returning every diagnosis and every trade-off to the full multiplicative accounting prevents local optimizations from masking fleet-level losses.

1.  A team reports that its job’s retained steps run at 48% MFU, above the 41% of Llama 3 405B, and concludes that the job uses the fleet well. Why is the conclusion unsupported?

    1.  MFU above 45% is not achievable on these accelerators, so the measurement must be wrong
    2.  MFU overstates program goodput because it counts recomputation as useful work
    3.  MFU measures only program goodput; with the pool’s scheduling goodput of 0.65 and the job’s runtime goodput of 0.90, MPG is about 0.28
    4.  MFU must first be multiplied by the job’s scaling efficiency before it can be compared with Llama 3

2.  A serving replica’s batch-one decode step runs at about 0.3% of its FP16 peak against a bandwidth floor of about 5.2 ms per token. A new accelerator doubles peak FP16 arithmetic at the same memory bandwidth. What does the iron law predict for this step?

    1.  Decode time halves to about 2.6 ms per token
    2.  Decode time falls by about 0.3%, the share of peak the step uses
    3.  MBU halves, because more arithmetic capacity lowers bandwidth efficiency
    4.  The 5.2 ms floor is unchanged and MFU halves, because faster arithmetic shortens only the compute term, which is not exposed in this step

3.  True or False: Moving the running example’s training to a lower-precision format with twice the arithmetic throughput would roughly halve its 245 ms fleet step.

4.  Explain how a job’s mean step time can match its plan while intermittent stragglers lower its program goodput, and describe how the chapter’s measurement approach exposes them.

5.  Why does a successful compilation not establish that a program is well optimized?

    1.  A compiler applies the transformations it implements under its own cost model; derived layouts can insert resharding collectives in every layer, and a shape distribution that forces recompilation shows up as slow retained steps
    2.  Compilers optimize only host code, so accelerator kernels are left unchanged
    3.  A completed build disables fusion to guarantee numerical safety
    4.  Compilation removes all communication, so any remaining loss must come from faulty hardware

6.  In the running example, scheduling loses 35% of fleet capacity and the program 31%. Explain why a perfect scheduler would add only about 15% of fleet capacity as useful work, and what should decide which lever comes first.
