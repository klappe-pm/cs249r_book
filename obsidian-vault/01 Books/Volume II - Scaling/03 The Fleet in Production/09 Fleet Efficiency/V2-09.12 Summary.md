# V2-09.12 Summary

[[Home|Home]] · [[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/Volume 2 Overview|Volume Overview]] · [[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/03 The Fleet in Production/09 Fleet Efficiency/V2-09 Fleet Efficiency|V2-09 Fleet Efficiency]]

^sec-performance-engineering-summary

A machine learning fleet can be fully allocated and electrically busy while most of its capacity never becomes useful model progress. Capacity leaks away at three distinct boundaries across a workload’s lifecycle: before a job holds a complete gang allocation, while the job holds accelerators without retaining progress, and inside retained steps that execute below the ideal rate. Scheduling goodput, runtime goodput, and program goodput measure what survives each boundary, and their product defines ML productivity goodput ([[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/03 The Fleet in Production/09 Fleet Efficiency/V2-09.03 ML Goodput#^eq-perf-ml-productivity-goodput|equation 1]]). This multiplicative identity holds without double-counting only when every lost interval is charged once, at the first boundary it fails to cross.

Because the three factors lose time on different timescales—program losses inside every step, runtime losses during intermittent interruptions, and scheduling losses across diurnal demand cycles—a reproducible measurement contract must fix the observation window alongside the workload, the system state, and the timing boundary.

When composed from the owners’ modeled terms, the running example’s runtime loss came primarily from input stalls and preemptions rather than from hardware faults, demonstrating that identical headline factor values can point to entirely different subsystem owners. Across the shared pool, the three factors gave our running example an MPG of 0.28 and a cost per useful accelerator-hour equal to 3.5× the raw capacity price. Although scheduling lost the largest share of the pool’s total capacity and program execution lost the largest share of the job’s held time, the program boundary offered the highest-leverage engineering return. Its single largest loss, the exposed cross-node communication of each step, lay beyond the reach of any single-GPU kernel optimization.

**Key Takeaways: Fleet efficiency is a product of owned factors**

- **Useful work has three factors, each counted at one boundary**: Scheduling, runtime, and program goodput multiply into the share of fleet capacity that becomes useful work. Record each loss in the single factor whose boundary it fails to cross, and record the subsystem whose lever removes it in a separate attribution column, because boundary and owner rarely coincide.
- **MFU measures only program goodput**: High MFU proves that a job executes its retained steps efficiently, not that the fleet is used well. The running example’s retained steps run at 0.48 of the ideal rate while only 28 percent of the pool’s capacity becomes useful work, and bandwidth-bound decode workloads must be judged by MBU against a bandwidth floor instead.
- **Localize the factor before the mechanism**: Inspect scheduler logs first, job timelines second, and step traces third. Profiling a single step before localizing the lost factor can pronounce a job healthy while its capacity drains away in queue fragmentation or restart cascades that no step trace ever captures.
- **Levers act only through the term they shorten**: Kernel optimizations shorten local execution, sharding layouts set the communication volume, and overlap hides part of that transfer time; consequently, any single-node kernel speedup is diluted at fleet scale as exposed communication and synchronization claim a larger share of the step.
- **Speedups do not add, so re-measure**: Shortening one exposed interval frequently uncovers the next bottleneck, and accelerating the backward pass can expose collective transfers that computation previously hid. Re-measure the distributed step under the same contract after every change.
- **A loss share is not a recoverable gain**: Capacity recovered at an early boundary must still pass through later boundaries, so one percentage point of any factor is worth the product of the other two. Rank engineering levers by their marginal product value and their reachable subsystem ceilings rather than by raw loss shares.
- **Judge trades by the product**: A lever that raises one factor while lowering another improves fleet efficiency only when the product of the factors’ before-and-after ratios exceeds unity. Because adjacent factors share boundaries, a faster step raises program goodput while mechanically lowering runtime goodput, meaning a dip in one factor is not a regression if the product rises. A compilation build pays off only across the executions that reuse its cached artifact, and wall-clock time to result remains a distinct operational dimension on the efficiency frontier.

^callout-takeaways--9-12-884dc489

Ultimately, the cross-layer trade-offs in this chapter express the displacement of overhead (principle [[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/02 Distributed ML/V2 Distributed ML Principles#^pri-conservation-overhead|14]]) in a single quantitative currency. Inside a training step, a faster compute kernel shifts exposure from local execution onto inter-node communication. Across a job’s lifecycle, waiting for a compact placement shifts loss from program execution into the scheduler queue, an autotuning compilation shifts loss from step execution into startup, and maintaining a warm pool of serving replicas shifts cold-start latency into idle headroom. Overhead relocates across boundaries rather than vanishing, and the goodput product of the ML goodput decomposition (principle [[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/03 The Fleet in Production/V2 Fleet-In-Production Principles#^pri-ml-goodput|16]]) determines, for every relocation, whether the fleet came out ahead.

**What’s Next: From useful work to served requests**

Everything measured in this chapter averaged capacity over windows of hours to weeks and assumed that batches were waiting whenever an accelerator became ready. Serving at scale removes that assumption: user requests arrive stochastically and each must complete within a strict latency SLO, so queueing dynamics, continuous batching, and KV-cache admission determine how much of a replica’s measured capacity becomes compliant responses. The serving replica analyzed here, characterized by its HBM bandwidth floor and its cold-start readiness time, provides the foundation for that analysis, and its governing metric, SLO goodput, filters capacity after MPG by counting only the requests that meet their latency targets.
