# V2-10.11 Model Sharding for Inference

[[Home|Home]] · [[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/Volume 2 Overview|Volume Overview]] · [[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/03 The Fleet in Production/10 Serving at Scale/V2-10 Serving at Scale|V2-10 Serving at Scale]]

^sec-inference-scale-model-sharding-inference-82b3

When a deployment must retain a 70B model’s 140 GB of FP16 weights in accelerator memory, or when a 175B model’s 87.5 GB INT4 weight payload still exceeds a single 80 GB GPU, no single accelerator can hold the replica. Where quantization shrinks the representation, sharding keeps it intact and divides it across multiple GPUs that execute together as one logical replica. The resulting design adapts tensor and pipeline parallelism from training, but must evaluate their communication and scheduling costs against per-request inference deadlines.

### Memory and Latency Triggers for Model Sharding

^sec-inference-scale-sharding-becomes-necessary-81d4

A single-GPU replica fails when model weights overflow its HBM capacity or when its serial forward pass takes too long to meet the endpoint’s TTFT and TPOT targets. Model sharding addresses the memory and latency rows of [[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/03 The Fleet in Production/10 Serving at Scale/V2-10.03 Limits of Single-Machine Serving#^tbl-distribution-triggers|table 1]] through different mechanisms, and the timing analysis below determines whether parallel execution repays its communication cost.

The first driver is memory capacity. If the selected representation must remain in accelerator memory but does not fit on one device, the replica needs multiple devices. For a model with ${P}$ parameters at precision ${b}$ bits, [[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/03 The Fleet in Production/10 Serving at Scale/V2-10.11 Model Sharding for Inference#^eq-weight-memory|equation 11]] gives the weight payload before metadata and runtime state:

$$
{M_{\text{weights}}} = {P}\times \frac{{b}}{8} \text{ bytes} \tag{11}
$$

^eq-weight-memory

Applied to the 70B model in FP16 (16 bits), [[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/03 The Fleet in Production/10 Serving at Scale/V2-10.11 Model Sharding for Inference#^eq-weight-memory|equation 11]] recovers the payload quoted since [[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/03 The Fleet in Production/10 Serving at Scale/V2-10.03 Limits of Single-Machine Serving#^sec-inference-scale-singlemachine-serving-insufficient-3f13|section 10.2]]. Each parameter occupies two bytes, so the unquantized checkpoint alone requires:

$$
M_{\text{weights}} = 7 \times 10^{10} \times \frac{16}{8} = 140 \text{ GB}
$$

The result exceeds the 80 GB capacity of an H100 GPU, establishing a weight-only lower bound of 2 shards for an accelerator-resident replica. KV state, buffers, replication within the chosen partition, and uneven placement can require a larger degree.

The second driver is latency. Even when a model fits in memory, sharding can reduce latency by parallelizing computation. [[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/03 The Fleet in Production/10 Serving at Scale/V2-10.11 Model Sharding for Inference#^eq-parallel-time|Equation 12]] is the Fleet Law (**[[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/00 Introduction/01 Introduction/V2-01.06 The C³ Taxonomy and Foundations of Scale#^eq-fleet-law|equation 1.3]]**) applied to a single inference request, separating the compute term that sharding divides from the communication and synchronization terms it adds:

$$
{T_{\text{step}}}({N}) = \frac{{T_{\text{compute}}}}{{N}} + {T_{\text{comm}}}({N}) + {T_{\text{sync}}}({N}) - {T_{\text{overlap}}} \tag{12}
$$

^eq-parallel-time

where ${N}$ is the number of devices in the sharding group, ${T_{\text{comm}}}({N})$ is the communication overhead, ${T_{\text{sync}}}({N})$ is synchronization overhead, and ${T_{\text{overlap}}}$ is communication hidden behind useful compute. In the common no-overlap simplification with negligible extra synchronization, this reduces to ${T_{\text{compute}}}/{N}+ {T_{\text{comm}}}({N})$. Sharding provides latency benefit only when the nonoverlapped overhead is smaller than the time saved through parallelization.

### Tensor Parallelism

^sec-inference-scale-tensor-parallelism-b29f

Splitting every linear projection independently across GPUs would force an AllReduce synchronization after every matrix multiplication, stalling decode on interconnect latency. Tensor parallelism ([[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/03 The Fleet in Production/10 Serving at Scale/V2-10.20 Source References#^ref-shoeybi2019megatron|Shoeybi et al. 2019]]) avoids half of those collectives by splitting each layer with the column-row partitioning developed for training. This keeps the activation between paired linear layers local and costs two AllReduce operations per transformer block, one after attention and one after the feed-forward sublayer.

↩︎ Column-row tensor partitioning and its two AllReduce operations per transformer block are established in **[[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/02 Distributed ML/06 Distributed Training/V2-06.06 Model Parallelism#^sec-distributed-training-systems-systems-tensor-parallelism-d76e|Tensor parallelism]]**.

The attention computation partitions across heads, so with ${N_{\text{heads}}}$ heads and tensor-parallel degree ${N_{\text{TP}}}$ each device computes attention for ${N_{\text{heads}}}/{N_{\text{TP}}}$ of them when the head count divides evenly. The KV cache is sharded with the heads, so each device stores cache only for its own heads. A multi-head attention layout spreads many KV heads across the group, while grouped-query attention with eight KV heads leaves one per device in an eight-way group.

    8-way tensor parallelism, 64 KV heads (MHA):
    Device 0: KV cache for heads 0-7
    Device 1: KV cache for heads 8-15
    ...
    Device 7: KV cache for heads 56-63

Because each device holds cache only for its own heads, reusing that cache on other devices, as a handoff from a prefill pool to a decode pool does, requires compatible cache content and placement. Matching tensor-parallel configurations can align the head partitions, but the receiver must also use the same model state, prefix inputs, cache representation, and logical token positions. A different partition requires redistribution rather than direct shard reuse. Equal parallelism degree alone does not establish cache validity.

Each device applies its shard of the attention output projection to its local heads, and an AllReduce sums the projected partial outputs; its latency includes collective startup and coordination as well as payload transfer. [[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/03 The Fleet in Production/10 Serving at Scale/V2-10.11 Model Sharding for Inference#^fig-tensor-parallel-flow|Figure 16]] traces the resulting two synchronization points per layer across two GPU ranks.

![[90 Assets/Volume II/10 Inference/Tensor Parallel Flow 2d83f4dcb3.svg]]

Figure 16: **Tensor Parallelism for Inference**: Computation is distributed across devices by splitting tensor operations. Attention heads are partitioned across GPUs, requiring an AllReduce operation to synchronize results. Feed-forward networks use a column-row splitting strategy that requires only one AllReduce synchronization per block. This partition can reduce latency when saved compute exceeds communication cost, which is why the two collectives run over a fast local interconnect.

^fig-tensor-parallel-flow

The inference time with tensor parallelism follows [[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/03 The Fleet in Production/10 Serving at Scale/V2-10.11 Model Sharding for Inference#^eq-tensor-parallel-time|equation 13]]:

$$
{T_{\text{inference}}}= \frac{{T_{\text{compute}}}}{{N_{\text{TP}}}} + 2 \times {T_{\text{allreduce}}}({M}) \tag{13}
$$

^eq-tensor-parallel-time

[[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/03 The Fleet in Production/10 Serving at Scale/V2-10.11 Model Sharding for Inference#^eq-tensor-parallel-time|Equation 13]] is the per-layer instance of [[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/03 The Fleet in Production/10 Serving at Scale/V2-10.11 Model Sharding for Inference#^eq-parallel-time|equation 12]]. Here ${T_{\text{compute}}}$ is now the sequential compute time of one layer, and ${M}$ is the full activation payload each rank contributes to each AllReduce, priced by the ring cost model of **[[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/02 Distributed ML/05 Collective Communication/V2-05.07 AllReduce Algorithms and Topologies#^sec-communication-collective-operations-collective-operations-ring-allreduce-ffce|Ring AllReduce]]**. Compute divides ideally across ranks, but the reduced activation keeps its full shape, (tokens in the step) $\times$ (hidden width) $\times$ (bytes per element), and the factor of 2 counts the attention and feed-forward collectives (example [[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/03 The Fleet in Production/10 Serving at Scale/V2-10.11 Model Sharding for Inference#^exmp-inference-tensor-parallelism-llama-70b|10.6]]). When a model spans multiple nodes without high-bandwidth NVLink across every rank, those two per-layer collectives become prohibitively expensive, motivating inter-layer pipeline partitioning instead.

↩︎ Hiding tensor-parallel collectives behind compute on one node, and what that overlap can and cannot recover, is established in **[[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/03 The Fleet in Production/09 Fleet Efficiency/V2-09.08 Recovering Program Goodput#^sec-performance-engineering-overlap|Tensor-parallel collectives under contention]]**.

**Example 10.6: Tensor parallelism for Llama-70B**

**Scenario**: An engineering team deploys Llama-70B (70B parameters, FP16 footprint 140 GB) across an 8-GPU H100 node with NVLink ([[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/03 The Fleet in Production/10 Serving at Scale/V2-10.20 Source References#^ref-touvron2023llama2|Touvron et al. 2023]]).

**Diagnosis**: The calculation assumes 30 ms of serial layer compute as a reference, independent of the devices required to hold the weights: 12 ms for attention and 18 ms for the feed-forward block. That reference describes a long-prompt prefill, in which every prompt token passes through the layer in one step, so it is far larger than one layer’s share of a decode step on the reference replica, which passes only one new token per sequence through the layer. Ideal eight-way division gives 1.5 ms and 2.25 ms, and the two AllReduce operations of [[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/03 The Fleet in Production/10 Serving at Scale/V2-10.11 Model Sharding for Inference#^eq-tensor-parallel-time|equation 13]] add an assumed 0.3 ms each, for 4.35 ms per layer.

**Systems lesson**: The illustrative eight-way model gives a 6.9× reduction relative to its serial-compute reference (prefill time reduced from 2,400 ms to 348 ms). The entire shortfall from the ideal 8$\times$ is the two collectives, a fixed cost per layer that dividing compute cannot shrink. Their share of the layer therefore grows as per-device compute falls, which is why a decode step, with far less compute per layer than this prefill reference, feels the collective cost most.

### Pipeline Parallelism for Inference

^sec-inference-scale-pipeline-parallelism-inference-7ee3

Splitting an 80-layer model sequentially across four GPU stages leaves three accelerators idle during an isolated request and does nothing to shorten its forward pass. Pipeline parallelism distributes layers across devices so each stage runs a contiguous subset of layers and passes activations to the next stage without intra-layer synchronization.

For inference, pipeline parallelism creates bubbles differently than in training. Maintaining multiple in-flight requests fills the stages that an isolated request leaves idle and amortizes the initial bubble across steady-state token delivery ([[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/03 The Fleet in Production/10 Serving at Scale/V2-10.11 Model Sharding for Inference#^fig-pipeline-bubbles|figure 17]]).

↩︎ The pipeline bubble, the time stages sit idle while a pipeline fills and drains, and the microbatching that shrinks it in training are established in **[[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/02 Distributed ML/06 Distributed Training/V2-06.06 Model Parallelism#^sec-distributed-training-systems-systems-pipeline-parallelism-8748|Pipeline parallelism]]**.

![[90 Assets/Volume II/10 Inference/Pipeline Bubbles 52616ba194.svg]]

Figure 17: **Pipeline Parallelism Bubbles**: (a) An 80-layer model split into four stages passes one request’s activations from stage to stage, so splitting the layers alone does not shorten its sequential computation; it must still traverse all four stages. (b) With many requests in flight, later stages idle during the fill bubble, and from slot t4 onward every stage is busy and one request leaves the pipeline per slot. Pipeline parallelism therefore suits throughput-oriented serving better than latency-critical interactive serving.

^fig-pipeline-bubbles

For a single request, pipeline staging does not parallelize the dependent layers. If stage execution times are unchanged, the request still pays their sum plus transfers, so staging alone provides no execution-latency reduction. A comparison against a memory-constrained implementation that offloads weights can differ because keeping stages resident changes those execution costs.

Pipeline parallelism instead scales throughput by sustaining multiple concurrent requests across execution stages, as tracked in the steady-state schedule of [[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/03 The Fleet in Production/10 Serving at Scale/V2-10.11 Model Sharding for Inference#^fig-pipeline-bubbles|figure 17]](b). Once the pipeline reaches saturation, throughput is limited by the slowest stage. For ${N_{\text{PP}}}$ balanced stages with negligible transfer overhead, this is ${N_{\text{PP}}}$ times the throughput of executing the whole model sequentially on one device, not ${N_{\text{PP}}}$ times a single stage’s throughput. An individual request still traverses every stage, so its execution latency remains approximately the sum of the stage times.

Pipeline parallelism therefore suits inference when memory forces sharding but latency targets are relaxed, when aggregate throughput matters more than individual request latency, or when the interconnect handles point-to-point transfers better than fast AllReduce. The two sharding approaches differ in single-request latency, throughput, communication pattern, activation memory, and implementation complexity ([[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/03 The Fleet in Production/10 Serving at Scale/V2-10.11 Model Sharding for Inference#^tbl-pipeline-tensor-comparison|table 15]]).

| **Aspect**                 |               **Tensor parallelism**               |             **Pipeline parallelism**             |
|:---------------------------|:--------------------------------------------------:|:------------------------------------------------:|
| **Single-request latency** | Compute term reduced up to ${N_{\text{TP}}}\times$ | No reduction from overlapping this request alone |
| **Throughput**             |   Below ${N_{\text{TP}}}\times$ (AllReduce cost)   |    Up to ${N_{\text{PP}}}\times$ (pipelined)     |
| **Communication pattern**  |          AllReduce (bandwidth-intensive)           |        Point-to-point (latency-sensitive)        |
| **Memory efficiency**      |               Activations replicated               |             Activations passed along             |
| **Complexity**             |          Higher (requires custom kernels)          |         Lower (layer-level partitioning)         |

Table 15: **Pipeline vs. Tensor Parallelism**: Key trade-offs between layer-internal tensor splitting (can reduce single-request latency when compute savings exceed collective cost) and inter-layer pipeline staging (overlaps concurrent requests but introduces pipeline bubbles). Throughput is relative to sequential execution of the whole model on one device; per-layer AllReduces keep tensor parallelism below the ${N_{\text{TP}}}\times$ ideal, and balanced, full pipelines approach ${N_{\text{PP}}}\times$.

^tbl-pipeline-tensor-comparison

The decision starts with capacity feasibility and then compares latency and throughput on the available topology. Neither a fast interconnect nor a relaxed deadline alone establishes the best choice; the request mix and measured execution costs determine the useful partition.

### Expert Parallelism for MoE Models

^sec-inference-scale-expert-parallelism-moe-models-55f6

An MoE model activates only a small fraction of its parameters per token, yet replicating the entire expert pool on every GPU quickly exhausts device memory. Mixture-of-experts (MoE) models such as DeepSeek-V3 ([[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/03 The Fleet in Production/10 Serving at Scale/V2-10.20 Source References#^ref-deepseek2025v3|DeepSeek-AI 2025]]) and Mixtral ([[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/03 The Fleet in Production/10 Serving at Scale/V2-10.20 Source References#^ref-jiang2024mixtral|Jiang et al. 2024]]) replace each feed-forward network with parallel expert sub-networks and a lightweight router that selects a few experts per token, growing parameter capacity without proportionally growing expert arithmetic per token. Routing, shared layers, and communication still add work. Expert parallelism places different experts on different GPUs to distribute their storage and execution when the model and workload exceed a single device.

#### MoE Economics and Capacity Planning

^sec-inference-scale-moe-economics

MoE serving separates two capacity measures. The *total parameter count* sets the weight-storage requirement at the chosen precision, while the *active parameter count* estimates the weight-related arithmetic and traffic for each token. Neither count includes all runtime costs. An accelerator-resident deployment must accommodate inactive experts as well as active ones, even though each token uses only a subset.

The following comparison isolates weight-related costs at batch size 1. Streaming all weights of a dense 400B FP16 model moves 800 GB per step. Counting only DeepSeek-V3’s 37B active parameters gives approximately 74 GB in FP16, a 10.8× reduction in weight bytes per token. The proportional estimate of weight-related arithmetic is 10.8× lower. These are payload and arithmetic comparisons, not equivalent-quality model comparisons or measured speedups; cache traffic, expert placement, routing, and communication affect realized latency.

![[90 Assets/Volume II/10 Inference/Vol2 Inference Margin 002 4a66790c4b.svg]]

*Linear memory ladder comparing MoE resident weights at 1342 GB, dense weights at 800 GB (all resident and all read per step), and MoE active weights read per token at 74 GB. A bracket between the dense and MoE active bars marks the 10.8 times reduction.*

*MoE keeps every expert resident but activates few per token.*

The trade-off is resident capacity: keeping all experts on accelerators avoids fetching inactive experts when routing changes. Offloading can reduce that resident footprint but adds transfer and scheduling costs. DeepSeek-V3’s full model in FP16 requires approximately 1342 GB, requiring many devices if the entire FP16 weight set remains accelerator-resident.

**Napkin Math 10.8: MoE capacity planning**

This comparison uses a conservative decimal budget of 80 GB per GPU, matching the H100 nameplate label rather than the registered byte capacity used by the node estimator.

**Problem**: A team deploys DeepSeek-V3 (671B total parameters, 37B active per token) for a chatbot application. The model uses FP8 weights (1 byte per parameter). The cluster has 8-GPU nodes, each with 8$\times$ H100 GPUs (80 GB HBM per GPU, 640 GB per node). How many nodes are needed, and what is the per-token decode latency?

**Math**:

1.  **Memory**: Total weight memory = 671 GB. A single node (640 GB) is insufficient. 2 nodes provide 1,280 GB in aggregate. The remaining capacity must still accommodate KV state, metadata, buffers, and any replicated weights on each device.
2.  **Latency**: Count 37B active parameters (37 GB) per decode step. If their reads were evenly distributed across 16 GPUs, each would read about 2.3 GB. At the stated 3.35 TB/s HBM bandwidth, ${T_{\text{read}}}\approx$ 0.69 ms. The scenario adds 0.3 ms for routing. Expert placement and token routing do not guarantee this balance, especially at batch size 1.
3.  **Floor**: The simplified sum is $\approx$ 1.0 ms/token, corresponding to 1,000 tokens/s at batch size 1 under these assumptions. It is an optimistic estimate, not a measured rate or a rigorous latency bound; compute, KV traffic, synchronization, and imbalance remain unmodeled.

**Systems insight**: Sparse activation separates resident parameter capacity from the work selected for one token. Planning an accelerator-resident 671B model therefore requires both a per-device memory budget and a timing model for the actual expert placement. Dividing total active bytes by device count does not establish that every device can contribute bandwidth equally.

#### Expert Parallelism and Load Balancing

^sec-inference-scale-moe-parallelism

Expert parallelism carries over from training unchanged in mechanism. Each GPU holds a subset of experts, and each token travels to the devices holding the experts its gate selects. What changes in serving is who pays for imbalance, because an overloaded expert now delays live requests rather than a training step. In an MoE layer, a gating network selects ${k_{\text{experts}}}$ of the ${N_{\text{experts}}}$ experts for each token and sums their outputs weighted by its gate probabilities. An even expert placement assigns each device ${N_{\text{experts}}}/{N}$ experts, where ${N}$ is the number of expert-parallel devices, and each token then makes the round trip of **[[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/02 Distributed ML/06 Distributed Training/V2-06.06 Model Parallelism#^fig-moe-all-to-all-routing|Mixture-of-Experts (MoE) AllToAll Routing]]**. One AllToAll dispatches its representation to the ranks hosting its experts, and a second returns their outputs for the weighted sum.

The communication pattern differs from tensor parallelism. AllReduce delivers one combined result to every device, so per-link traffic is fixed by the message size. Expert parallelism uses AllToAll to deliver different routed tokens to different devices, so link and receiver load depend on the router’s choices, and the host of a popular expert becomes an incast point whose completion time the other hosts wait on (**[[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/02 Distributed ML/05 Collective Communication/V2-05.09 AllToAll Exchanges at Scale#^sec-collective-communication-moe-dynamic-routing|Dynamic routing and expert imbalance in MoE AllToAll]]**). Balancing the number of stored experts does not balance token traffic.

Serving inherits training’s balance controls without their guarantees. The auxiliary balancing loss shapes routing on training data, not on future traffic. The expert capacity factor caps accepted tokens per expert (1.25$\times$ is an illustrative setting), leaving the server to decide what happens to the excess. Dropping or rerouting it changes the model computation and needs quality validation, while expert buffering retains it at the cost of latency and queue capacity.

↩︎ The capacity factor, the auxiliary load-balancing loss, and the hot-expert skew they bound during training are established in **[[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/02 Distributed ML/06 Distributed Training/V2-06.06 Model Parallelism#^sec-distributed-training-systems-systems-expert-parallelism-mixture-experts-bc45|Expert parallelism (mixture of experts)]]**.

#### Routing Failure Modes

^sec-inference-scale-moe-routing-failures

Router behavior affects both system performance and model quality. Expert collapse during training concentrates traffic on a few experts, and at serving time a shifted input distribution can create hotspots without any change to the stored model. Token dropping under a capacity limit makes the computation differ from the uncapped model, so its quality impact must be evaluated together with any throughput gain. Because routing selects experts dynamically,[[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/03 The Fleet in Production/10 Serving at Scale/V2-10.21 Footnotes#^fn5|⁵]] the routing pattern becomes a systems workload rather than only a model-internal choice.

**Example 10.7: Expert parallelism for Mixtral-8x7B**

**Scenario**: Mixtral-8x7B (46.7B total parameters, ~12.9B active per token) is served across a 4-GPU group using top-2 routing.

**Diagnosis**: Replicating all 8 experts on every GPU requires the full 93.4 GB FP16 weight set per device, more than one 80 GB H100 holds. Placing 2 experts on each GPU (4-way expert parallelism) cuts each device’s share to about 25.8 GB, the shared attention layers plus its local experts. Every MoE layer, however, now needs an AllToAll dispatch and gather to move tokens to the devices that hold their selected experts.

**Systems lesson**: Expert parallelism trades AllToAll communication for per-GPU parameter memory. The trade pays only when the collective, repeated at every MoE layer, fits inside the decode budget that the smaller resident footprint makes possible.

^exmp-inference-expert-parallelism-mixtral-8x7b

Expert parallelism couples state placement to input-dependent access. Embedding-heavy recommendation models face a related problem: entity popularity determines which stored vectors receive traffic. Their sharding strategy must therefore balance storage and lookup load, just as expert placement must account for both resident weights and routed tokens.

### Embedding Sharding for Recommendation Systems

^sec-inference-scale-embedding-sharding-recommendation-systems-afde

Production recommendation models carry multi-terabyte embedding tables that cannot fit on any single server and would stall every ranking query if fetched over thousands of serial network hops. Meta’s DLRM reference architecture applies model parallelism to those tables while scaling dense layers separately ([[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/03 The Fleet in Production/10 Serving at Scale/V2-10.20 Source References#^ref-naumov2019deep|Naumov et al. 2019]]), and serving inherits the row-wise, column-wise, and hybrid layouts that training uses to shard them (**[[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/02 Distributed ML/06 Distributed Training/V2-06.06 Model Parallelism#^sec-distributed-training-systems-systems-parameter-servers-embedding-sharding-8821|Sparse embedding sharding]]**).

What changes at serving time is the constraint the layout answers to. Training shards tables for sparse update throughput, while serving must finish every lookup within a per-request deadline. Popularity skew concentrates lookups on the shards that own hot keys, and under a latency SLO the slowest of those shards sets the tail of every request that touches it. Serving layouts therefore replicate or column-partition hot entries even at extra storage cost, keep the cold tail row-sharded, and batch lookups by destination shard so that one request does not wait on thousands of sequential round trips.

The following scenario uses Archetype B (DLRM at Scale), a recommendation model built around large embedding tables, and combines row-wise, column-wise, and hybrid sharding. A single Archetype B model’s embedding tables occupy 10 TB or more. The platform-wide resident budget of 100 TB is deliberately set well above that, so that the sharding arithmetic is stressed at the upper end of the regime.

**Lighthouse 10.1: Archetype B (DLRM at Scale): Embedding sharding budget**

Consider an Archetype B recommendation workload with a large entity catalog and a smaller resident embedding tier:

**Scale**:

- Resident embedding budget (platform-wide): 100 TB
- Entity catalog: 10 trillion identifiers
- Embedding dimension: 128–256
- Shards: 1,000+ servers

The arithmetic of these bullets is the design constraint. A dense INT8 table with 10 trillion entities and 128 dimensions would require at least 1,280 TB, while 100 TB across the same entity count averages only 10 bytes per entity, a fraction of one 128-byte vector. Most entities therefore cannot hold a resident vector at all. The resident tier keeps the frequently accessed subset, and the long tail lives in another tier or a compressed representation, which is exactly the split the sharding strategy below is built around.

**Sharding strategy**:

The tier percentages below describe the admitted resident population, not the full entity catalog. Replica bytes count against the platform-wide resident budget, so the admitted population and replication scope must be chosen together.

- **Hot embeddings** (top 1 percent by access frequency): Replicated within selected shard groups
- **Warm embeddings** (next 10 percent): Column-sharded with 8-way parallelism
- **Cold embeddings** (remaining 89 percent): Row-sharded with consistent hashing

Each inference request requires approximately 5,000 embedding lookups. Instead of one RPC per lookup, the batched design accumulates lookups for 1 ms, deduplicates entities across requests, groups lookups by destination shard, dispatches to all shards in parallel, and assembles embeddings as responses arrive.

Illustrative before-and-after assumptions for sequential dispatch versus parallel shard batching appear in [[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/03 The Fleet in Production/10 Serving at Scale/V2-10.11 Model Sharding for Inference#^tbl-inference-meta-embedding-perf|table 16]].

| **Metric**                    | **Without optimization** |         **With optimization**          |
|:------------------------------|:------------------------:|:--------------------------------------:|
| **Sequential dispatch waves** |  5,000 individual RPCs   | One parallel wave; multiple shard RPCs |
| **Lookup latency**            |          50 ms           |                  2 ms                  |
| **Network bandwidth**         |         10 Gbps          |            40 Gbps (burst)             |

Table 16: **Illustrative Embedding Lookup Comparison**: Assumed latency and bandwidth compare sequential per-lookup RPCs with shard-batched parallel dispatch. One dispatch wave still contains multiple network requests; it does not mean one RPC to the entire fleet.

^tbl-inference-meta-embedding-perf

The lighthouse lesson is that sparse embedding placement and lookup coordination can dominate DLRM-scale serving even when dense arithmetic has spare capacity. The illustrated workload motivates measuring shard traffic and lookup latency before adding dense compute.

### Hybrid Sharding Strategies

^sec-inference-scale-hybrid-sharding-strategies-e605

Extending tensor parallelism across node boundaries stalls decode on slow inter-node AllReduces, while using pipeline parallelism alone leaves single-request latency untouched. Production systems therefore combine sharding strategies into hybrid sharding, using one axis to cut per-token latency inside a node, another to fit weights across nodes, and a third to route sparse components without forcing dense layers onto the wrong device. Joint placement across these axes is established in **[[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/02 Distributed ML/06 Distributed Training/V2-06.07 Hybrid Parallelism#^sec-distributed-training-systems-systems-hybrid-parallelism-12a0|Hybrid Parallelism]]**, so the serving question is which axis buys latency. Tensor and pipeline parallelism combine when a model needs both memory distribution and latency reduction:

    8 GPUs organized as 2 by 4 (pipeline stages by tensor parallel):

    Stage 0 (Layers 1-40):  TP across GPUs 0,1,2,3
    Stage 1 (Layers 41-80): TP across GPUs 4,5,6,7

The four-way tensor split can cut per-layer compute time by up to 4$\times$, while the two pipeline stages complete the eight-way memory distribution; pipelining adds no per-request latency benefit of its own. Expert-parallel MoE layers and recommendation models that keep embeddings on CPU servers beside a tensor-parallel dense model follow the same rule, and the recommendation split reappears in the case study of [[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/03 The Fleet in Production/10 Serving at Scale/V2-10.16 Case Studies#^sec-inference-scale-meta-recommendation-serving-bede|section 10.15.2]].

Hybrid sharding buys fit or latency by adding new communication patterns at every boundary between shards. The next question is whether that added communication time stays inside the latency and throughput budget.

### Interconnect Latency and Communication Overhead

^sec-inference-scale-communication-overhead-analysis-c1cf

Sharding a model across more GPUs can make inference slower rather than faster when collective synchronization takes longer than the compute time it saves. Each sharding strategy has a characteristic communication pattern, and each pattern carries a cost model developed earlier in this book. Pipeline parallelism passes activations point to point between stages and pays one startup latency plus a bandwidth term per transfer (**[[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/01 The Fleet/03 Network Fabrics/V2-03.05 Level 2 Transport and the Performance Model#^sec-network-fabrics-performance-model|The α-β performance model]]**). Tensor parallelism pays a ring AllReduce per collective, whose startup term grows linearly with the number of devices while its bandwidth term approaches twice the payload divided by link bandwidth (**[[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/02 Distributed ML/05 Collective Communication/V2-05.07 AllReduce Algorithms and Topologies#^sec-communication-collective-operations-collective-operations-ring-allreduce-ffce|Ring AllReduce]]**). Expert parallelism pays an AllToAll, whose completion time is set by the largest of per-message startup, endpoint injection or reception, and traffic across the fabric bisection (**[[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/02 Distributed ML/05 Collective Communication/V2-05.09 AllToAll Exchanges at Scale#^sec-communication-alltoall|AllToAll Exchanges at Scale]]**).

These models give an initial communication budget, not a complete prediction of request latency. Skewed expert traffic, network contention, or a different collective algorithm can change the realized timing, so the chosen topology and collective implementation must sustain that budget under the intended concurrent load.

Communication overhead depends heavily on the interconnect technology, because each candidate link fixes the physical bandwidth, latency, and scope available to the sharded model ([[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/03 The Fleet in Production/10 Serving at Scale/V2-10.11 Model Sharding for Inference#^tbl-inference-interconnect-specs|table 17]]).

| **Interconnect**   |    **Bandwidth**     | **Latency** |      **Use case**      |
|:-------------------|:--------------------:|:-----------:|:----------------------:|
| **NVLink (H100)**  |       900 GB/s       |   500 ns    |     Intra-node TP      |
| **PCIe Gen5**      |       64 GB/s        |    1 μs     | Intra-node (no NVLink) |
| **InfiniBand HDR** |  200 Gb/s (25 GB/s)  |    7 μs     |       Inter-node       |
| **Ethernet 100G**  | 100 Gb/s (12.5 GB/s) |    50 μs    | Inter-node (commodity) |

Table 17: **Data-Center Interconnect Specifications**: Bandwidth, latency, and intended scope for NVLink, PCIe Gen5, InfiniBand HDR, and 100G Ethernet, establishing the physical limits that bound achievable sharding speedups.

^tbl-inference-interconnect-specs

For tensor parallelism the latency column, not the bandwidth column, decides placement. Decode divides a small per-layer compute time across the group while still paying two collectives per layer for every generated token, so the fixed per-collective latency in [[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/03 The Fleet in Production/10 Serving at Scale/V2-10.11 Model Sharding for Inference#^tbl-inference-interconnect-specs|table 17]] sets the overhead share. The 0.3 ms per collective assumed in example [[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/03 The Fleet in Production/10 Serving at Scale/V2-10.11 Model Sharding for Inference#^exmp-inference-tensor-parallelism-llama-70b|10.6]] already lies an order of magnitude above the bandwidth-only transfer time on NVLink, so startup, synchronization, and launch costs dominate it. An inter-node link with higher per-message latency would multiply that cost on every token. That decode regime is why tensor-parallel groups are normally kept inside one NVLink domain.

### Selecting a Sharding Strategy

^sec-inference-scale-sharding-strategy-selection-f69c

Choosing the wrong sharding axis either burns interconnect bandwidth on per-layer collectives that the link cannot support or leaves single-request latency above the SLO. No approach wins on latency priority, throughput priority, interconnect limits, and implementation effort at once ([[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/03 The Fleet in Production/10 Serving at Scale/V2-10.11 Model Sharding for Inference#^tbl-sharding-selection|table 18]]).

| **Factor**                |        **Tensor parallel**         |   **Pipeline parallel**   |   **Expert parallel**   | **Embedding shard**  |
|:--------------------------|:----------------------------------:|:-------------------------:|:-----------------------:|:--------------------:|
| **Latency priority**      | Compute savings exceed collectives | No staging-only reduction |    Routing-dependent    |   Lookup-dependent   |
| **Throughput priority**   |         Collective-limited         |  Full, balanced pipeline  | Balanced expert traffic | Balanced lookup load |
| **Interconnect limited**  |              Poor fit              |         Good fit          |        Moderate         |       Good fit       |
| **Implementation effort** |                High                |            Low            |        Moderate         |         High         |

Table 18: **Sharding Strategy Selection Guide**: Evaluation of tensor, pipeline, expert, and embedding sharding across latency targets, throughput priorities, interconnect constraints, and implementation complexity.

^tbl-sharding-selection

Strategy selection starts from the deployment bottleneck. Tensor parallelism is a candidate when reduced layer execution can repay frequent collectives; pipeline parallelism distributes layers and can sustain throughput when enough concurrent work fills the stages. Expert and embedding sharding distribute sparse state while adding routed communication. These choices can be combined, but each must fit the per-device memory budget and the measured latency contract.

Once a replica’s memory footprint and execution capacity are established, demand determines how many replicas the service needs. The next decision is where to send each arriving request. Routing must account for work already in progress and reusable state, so that replica capacity becomes useful fleet capacity.
