# V2-10.05 Serving Cost Dominance in Production

[[Home|Home]] · [[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/Volume 2 Overview|Volume Overview]] · [[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/03 The Fleet in Production/10 Serving at Scale/V2-10 Serving at Scale|V2-10 Serving at Scale]]

^sec-inference-scale-serving-cost-dominates-training-cost-576a

![[90 Assets/Volume II/10 Inference/Inference Serving Cost Dominance e6e0b59e52.svg]]

*Two horizontal bars on a linear scale. A short gray bar shows a training cost of 2 million dollars; a long orange bar shows first-year serving cost of 18.25 million dollars. A bracket between their ends marks the 9.1 times ratio.*

*At this traffic, one year of serving costs about nine times the training run.*

A team that budgets millions of dollars to train a model can find within weeks of launch that serving traffic is spending more compute per month than the entire training run cost. While the serving tax in [[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/03 The Fleet in Production/10 Serving at Scale/V2-10.04 SLO Goodput and the Serving Tax#^eq-serving-tax|equation 1]] consumes a fraction of each request’s latency budget, those per-request costs compound across every query over the model’s operational lifetime. By the serving cost dominance principle ([[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/03 The Fleet in Production/V2 Fleet-In-Production Principles#^pri-serving-cost-dominance|17]]), recurring serving expense can dwarf upfront training expense by orders of magnitude because a model version incurs training cost once before deployment while serving cost recurs with traffic. Both activities can consume purchased or rented infrastructure; the distinction here is expense timing, not financial capitalization. In a high-volume deployment, that recurring traffic bill rapidly overtakes the one-time training investment (notebook [10.1](https://mlsysbook.ai/vol2/10_inference/10_inference.html#nbk-inference-serving-cost-multiplier)).

**Napkin Math 10.1: The serving cost multiplier**

**Problem**: A team has spent \$2,000,000 training a 70B parameter model and now serves it to 1,000,000 daily active users, each making 50 requests/day. Is training or serving the dominant cost over 1 year?

**Math**:

1.  **Training cost**: \$2,000,000 (one-time).
2.  **Metric**: Annual inference volume is $10^{6}\,\text{users} \times 50\,\text{reqs} \times 365\,\text{days} \approx$ 18.25B requests/year.
3.  **Cost per request**: Assume a 70B model on H100 costs $\approx$ \$0.001/request (input + output).
4.  **Annual serving cost**: 18.25B requests $\times$ \$0.001/request = \$18,250,000.

**Systems insight**: Serving costs 9.1× more than training in the first year alone. A 10 percent reduction in cost per request, at unchanged request volume and service quality, saves about \$1.8M in the first year, nearly paying for the original training run.

For one model version at a fixed query rate and average cost per served query, [[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/03 The Fleet in Production/10 Serving at Scale/V2-10.05 Serving Cost Dominance in Production#^eq-total-cost|equation 2]] combines training expense with recurring serving expense:

$$
{C_{\text{total}}}= {C_{\text{train}}} + {C_{\text{serving}}}\times {T_{\text{deployment}}}\times {\lambda_{\text{arr}}} \tag{2}
$$

^eq-total-cost

where ${C_{\text{train}}}$ is the one-time cost to train the model, ${C_{\text{serving}}}$ is the cost per query served, ${T_{\text{deployment}}}$ is the deployment duration in appropriate time units, and ${\lambda_{\text{arr}}}$ is the query rate. The rate must count the same served queries used to estimate unit cost. That cost must include the chosen serving boundary, including idle or reserved capacity when it is billed. Changing traffic mix, fleet size, or price requires evaluating separate intervals rather than treating one unit cost as constant throughout deployment.

The same leverage can hold across application categories with different cost structures. In the chapter’s illustrative recommendation scenario, a DLRM trained for \$8,000 (\$2,000 hardware for 1,000 GPU-hours at \$2/GPU-hour, plus 3× additional engineering cost for data and experimentation) and served at 10,000 QPS for 730 days accumulates $6.31 \times 10^{11}$ lifetime queries. At the assumed \$10/million queries, those queries cost \$6,307,200 to serve, producing a 788.4× ratio in this scenario. Other workload envelopes span orders of magnitude in both terms, and the sensitivity ranges in [[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/03 The Fleet in Production/10 Serving at Scale/V2-10.05 Serving Cost Dominance in Production#^tbl-cost-ratios|table 3]] are illustrative envelopes rather than market-price estimates.

| **Application**               | **Training cost** | **Annual serving cost** |
|:------------------------------|:------------------|:------------------------|
| **Recommendation (high QPS)** | \$10K–\$100K      | \$1M–\$10M              |
| **Search ranking**            | \$100K–\$1M       | \$10M–\$100M            |
| **LLM API**                   | \$1M–\$100M       | \$10M–\$1B              |
| **Internal analytics**        | \$1K–\$10K        | \$10K–\$100K            |

Table 3: **Illustrative Training and Serving Cost Ranges**: Independent sensitivity ranges for training a model version and serving it for a year. A cost ratio requires a paired training-and-serving scenario; it cannot be inferred from matching endpoints of independent ranges. A deployment must substitute measured traffic and a consistent accounting boundary.

^tbl-cost-ratios

A reduction in cost per completed request yields recurring savings only if workload volume and service quality stay fixed; lower latency alone does not reduce billed capacity. Unit cost also changes with daily request volume, as the constructed cost curves of [[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/03 The Fleet in Production/10 Serving at Scale/V2-10.05 Serving Cost Dominance in Production#^fig-serving-cost-curve|figure 2]] illustrate. Against its assumed price of \$0.05 per 1,000 tokens, the optimized curve crosses near $2 \times 10^{5}$ requests per day and the unbatched single-GPU curve near $4 \times 10^{7}$ requests. These crossings follow from the assumed curves, not measured profitability, so a deployment needs its own token mix, occupied capacity, and cost boundary to locate its break-even point.

Figure 2: **The Serving Cost Curve**: Constructed unit-cost curves cross an assumed price line at different request volumes. The optimized curve crosses earlier under these assumptions; the crossings do not establish measured profitability or guaranteed savings from quantization and batching.

^fig-serving-cost-curve

The cost ranges in [[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/03 The Fleet in Production/10 Serving at Scale/V2-10.05 Serving Cost Dominance in Production#^tbl-cost-ratios|table 3]] give a static comparison. [[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/03 The Fleet in Production/10 Serving at Scale/V2-10.05 Serving Cost Dominance in Production#^fig-serving-cost-crossover|Figure 3]] instead accumulates assumed monthly serving expense over a 36-month deployment and marks when the serving portion equals the initial training expense, within days in the plotted recommendation scenario and roughly six weeks in the 70B LLM scenario. These timings follow from the specified expenses, not from model size or user count, and a larger training expense, lower traffic, or shorter deployment can reverse which activity dominates. When recurring traffic makes inference the dominant expense, serving-specific designs can substantially reduce cost and power per query ([[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/03 The Fleet in Production/10 Serving at Scale/V2-10.20 Source References#^ref-patel2024|Patel et al. 2024]]), provided each optimization targets the workload’s actual bottleneck and the right level of the serving stack.

Figure 3: **The Serving Cost Crossover**: Cumulative total deployment cost (initial training plus ongoing serving) for three illustrative scenarios. Vertical markers (scenarios A and B) indicate when the serving portion equals the one-time training cost. In the two high-traffic scenarios, serving expense matches training expense within weeks or days. In the low-traffic scenario, training expense remains larger over the plotted period. **Self-Check: Question**

1.  A production recommendation model costs \$50,000 to train (a one-time expense) and serves 10,000 QPS continuously for 2 years (730 days) at an operational cost of \$10 per million queries. What is the ratio of lifetime serving cost to training cost?

    1.  Approximately $1.3\times$
    2.  Approximately $126.1\times$
    3.  Approximately $12.6\times$
    4.  Approximately $630.7\times$

2.  True or False: Serving cost always exceeds training cost once a model is large enough, because larger models cost more per query.

3.  A team estimates its cost per query from GPU time on fully loaded replicas, but its fleet also bills for reserved replicas that sit idle overnight to absorb the daytime peak. What does the lifetime cost model require?

    1.  Nothing more, because idle replicas do no work and therefore add no cost per query.
    2.  Doubling the query rate to account for the peak, because peaks set the cost.
    3.  Replacing cost per query with training cost, because idle capacity is a one-time expense.
    4.  Charging the billed idle and reserved capacity inside the unit cost, and counting the same served queries in the rate that was used to estimate that cost.

4.  A model costs \$2M to train, and its first year of serving costs about \$18M. Explain why a 10 percent reduction in cost per request can be worth nearly as much as the original training run, and state the conditions under which that saving is real.
