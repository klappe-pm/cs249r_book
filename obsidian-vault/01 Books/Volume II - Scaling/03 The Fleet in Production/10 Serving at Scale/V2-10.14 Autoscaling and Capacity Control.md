# V2-10.14 Autoscaling and Capacity Control

[[Home|Home]] · [[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/Volume 2 Overview|Volume Overview]] · [[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/03 The Fleet in Production/10 Serving at Scale/V2-10 Serving at Scale|V2-10 Serving at Scale]]

^sec-inference-scale-autoscaling-capacity-control

A serving autoscaler that reacts only to average accelerator utilization can scale in the wrong direction. Decode can saturate memory bandwidth while arithmetic units appear idle, long prompts can consume prefill capacity before request count rises, and queued work can violate an SLO while fleet-wide utilization remains moderate. The control signal must represent outstanding work relative to phase-specific service capacity. Every replica the controller adds is also a claim on accelerators that training jobs may hold, so fleet-level arbitration with training bounds how quickly and how far serving can grow.

↩︎ The arbitration between serving replicas and training jobs for shared accelerators, including the minutes-long cost of switching a device between them, is established in **[[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/02 Distributed ML/08 Fleet Orchestration/V2-08.11 Serving Capacity in the Shared Fleet#^sec-fleet-orchestration-boundary|The training-serving resource boundary]]**.

For a conventional fixed-cost model, queueing delay and request rate per ready replica can guide scaling. Autoregressive serving also needs prompt-token and output-token rates, KV occupancy, and measured phase service rates. A colocated replica must satisfy all phase requirements; disaggregated prefill and decode pools need separate counts and a feasible transfer budget. SLO goodput measures useful supply, but rejected work and backlog must remain visible as unmet demand. A latency threshold can detect deterioration after a model change, yet controller timing and headroom still need validation. Adding replicas also depends on their startup delay, and resizing a running replica’s accelerator allocation may require a restart or state migration, depending on the runtime and execution plan.

Scale-up and scale-down require asymmetric evidence. Scale-up acts on sustained queue growth, forecast demand, or declining deadline slack because cold-start delay makes late reactions expensive. Scale-down requires low demand over a longer window, enough spare capacity to survive one replica failure, and a drain plan for in-flight requests and any optional session cache. This hysteresis prevents oscillation in which replicas repeatedly load large weights, accept work, and terminate before amortizing startup cost.

The controller also needs observability that distinguishes demand from impaired supply: the per-request serving trace of [[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/03 The Fleet in Production/10 Serving at Scale/V2-10.12 Load Balancing and Request Routing#^sec-inference-scale-circuit-breakers-backpressure-e61c|section 10.11.10]], plus ready replicas, model-load failures, and per-phase service rate. Without readiness and service-rate measurements, an autoscaler can respond to a sick replica by adding cold replicas indefinitely while the original failure remains hidden.

### Scaling Response Time

^sec-inference-scale-scaling-response-time-analysis-0dbf

If a traffic spike hits a serving pool in 10 seconds but acquiring a GPU node and loading 140 GB of weights takes five minutes, the autoscaler cannot prevent the queue from overflowing during the gap. The scaling response budget is the sum of the control loop’s detection, decision, provisioning, and warmup delays, as [[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/03 The Fleet in Production/10 Serving at Scale/V2-10.14 Autoscaling and Capacity Control#^eq-scaling-response|equation 16]] makes explicit:

$$
{T_{\text{response}}}= {T_{\text{detect}}}+ {T_{\text{decide}}}+ {T_{\text{provision}}}+ {T_{\text{warmup}}} \tag{16}
$$

^eq-scaling-response

Each term has its own typical duration and its own primary lever, from a shorter metrics interval for detection to warm pools for provisioning and precompilation for warmup ([[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/03 The Fleet in Production/10 Serving at Scale/V2-10.14 Autoscaling and Capacity Control#^tbl-scaling-response-components|table 20]]).

| **Component**    | **Duration** |    **Optimization**     |
|:-----------------|:------------:|:-----------------------:|
| **Detection**    |    10–60s    | Reduce metrics interval |
| **Decision**     |     1–5s     |    Faster autoscaler    |
| **Provisioning** |   30s–5min   |       Warm pools        |
| **Warmup**       |    5–30s     |     Precompilation      |

Table 20: **Scaling Response Time Components**: The four phases of a scaling event with illustrative durations and their primary optimization lever. Provisioning dominates this example; a deployment must measure its own critical path, which may instead be dominated by artifact loading.

^tbl-scaling-response-components

Provisioning can dominate the illustrated budget, making already allocated capacity valuable. The large-model example that follows shows that artifact loading can instead dominate startup; the relevant term is the one measured on the deployment’s critical path. Finer-grained metrics can reduce detection delay but increase noise, while prepared scaling decisions and precompiled engines reduce other costs. A warm pool removes only the phases already completed at its stated readiness level.

### Cold Start and Predictive Scaling

^sec-inference-scale-cold-start-predictive

When a 70B replica takes nearly seven minutes to boot and stream its weights from object storage, waiting for a morning traffic ramp to appear in queue metrics guarantees several minutes of overloaded replicas. A cold start for a 70B replica shows the scale of that delay, and a demand forecast shows how a controller can begin the wait before the traffic arrives.

**Example 10.11: Cold start timeline for Llama-70B**

**Scenario**: An autoscaling engine provisions a Llama-70B replica during a traffic surge, using an H100-based allocation large enough for its FP16 weights, cache, and runtime state.

**Diagnosis**: The scenario’s sequential startup estimate is 6.7 minutes (${T_{\text{cold-start}}}= {T_{\text{provision}}}+ {T_{\text{load}}}+ {T_{\text{warmup}}}$): 1 minute for provisioning, 4.7 minutes to fetch 140 GB of artifacts at the assumed 0.5 GB/s, and 1 minute for device loading and warmup. Cached artifacts or overlapping phases can shorten this path; scarce capacity and shared storage can lengthen it. If existing headroom cannot absorb the surge, newly requested replicas arrive too late to protect short deadlines.

**Systems lesson**: Cold-start latency depends on artifact size, effective storage bandwidth, provisioning delay, compilation, and warmup. The controller must measure this distribution and begin provisioning far enough ahead that its high percentile fits inside the forecast horizon.

^exmp-inference-cold-start-timeline-llama-70b

**Example 10.12: Predictive scaling for traffic**

**Scenario**: A commercial chatbot service faces predictable diurnal traffic patterns, ramping from 500 QPS at night to 3,000 QPS at peak.

**Diagnosis**: A controller that waits for the morning ramps at 06:00 and 09:00 UTC before requesting capacity must cover the assumed 7-minute startup interval with existing headroom or admission limits. Without enough ready capacity, queues grow while replicas start. Forecasting can shift that wait ahead of the expected demand.

**Systems lesson**: Predictive autoscaling converts demand forecasts into planned pre-warming windows ([[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/03 The Fleet in Production/10 Serving at Scale/V2-10.14 Autoscaling and Capacity Control#^tbl-inference-chatbot-predictive-schedule|table 21]]). Starting 30 minutes ahead of the expected ramps exceeds the modeled startup time. Readiness still depends on actual provisioning and warmup, and forecast errors can leave the fleet short or idle. Reactive correction and an overload policy remain necessary.

| **Time** |  **Action**  | **Replicas active** | **Replicas starting** |
|:---------|:------------:|:-------------------:|:---------------------:|
| 05:30    |   Scale up   |          5          |      +10 warming      |
| 06:00    | Traffic ramp |         15          |          \-           |
| 08:30    |   Scale up   |         15          |      +15 warming      |
| 09:00    | Peak traffic |         30          |          \-           |
| 17:00    |  Scale down  |         20          |    -10 terminating    |
| 20:00    |  Scale down  |         10          |    -10 terminating    |
| 00:00    |  Scale down  |          5          |    -5 terminating     |

Table 21: **Predictive Scaling Schedule**: Per-time-bucket scaling actions, active replicas, and replicas in cold-start warming for the chatbot workload.

^tbl-inference-chatbot-predictive-schedule

**Systems insight**: Against an assumed reactive peak of 45 replicas, the forecast-based peak of 30 is a 33 percent reduction in peak replica count at equal unit cost. The reactive controller over-provisions because it reacts to a queue that has already formed and must add capacity for the backlog as well as the demand. Peak count is only part of the bill. Total spending counts replica-hours, including the pre-warming window, so the forecast pays only when the replica-hours spent warming ahead of demand are fewer than those the reactive overshoot would consume.

^exmp-inference-predictive-scaling-traffic

Example [[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/03 The Fleet in Production/10 Serving at Scale/V2-10.14 Autoscaling and Capacity Control#^exmp-inference-predictive-scaling-traffic|10.12]] shows how capacity can be prepared before demand arrives. [[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/03 The Fleet in Production/10 Serving at Scale/V2-10.14 Autoscaling and Capacity Control#^fig-predictive-reactive-scaling|Figure 21]] depicts a forecast that anticipates the surge, while the reactive curve starts later and temporarily lacks capacity. Whether that deficit produces queueing, rejection, or missed deadlines depends on admission and existing headroom. The predictive curve illustrates a successful forecast, not a guarantee that future demand or startup time will match it.

![[90 Assets/Volume II/10 Inference/Cell 49 Output 1 79bbb08ffe.png]]

Figure 21: **Predictive vs. Reactive Scaling**: Reactive scaling (gray line) responds to traffic spikes after they occur, leaving a capacity deficit (red region) during cold start in this scenario. Predictive scaling (green line) anticipates traffic and begins provisioning before the spike peaks, shrinking that deficit to a brief, small shortfall at onset in the illustrated forecast. Its early ramp does not eliminate every interval when demand exceeds capacity.

### Warm Pool Management

^sec-inference-scale-warm-pool-management-a360

Demand forecasts still miss unscheduled traffic spikes, and cold-starting a large model from scratch takes minutes that an interactive SLO cannot spare. Maintaining prewarmed replicas in a warm pool buys readiness before unexpected demand arrives. Sizing that pool begins with the total capacity needed during the provisioning delay, then subtracts capacity already ready to serve. If peak demand doubles and the chosen headroom factor is 1.5, the target is three times baseline capacity, assuming unchanged per-replica throughput. Partly prepared replicas count only if they can become ready before that capacity is needed. The expression $\text{Pool size} \times \text{GPU cost/hour} \times \text{Idle fraction}$ estimates the hourly cost attributed to idle GPU capacity; it excludes busy capacity and non-GPU resources. Readiness comes in tiers, each trading activation delay against the cost of waiting ([[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/03 The Fleet in Production/10 Serving at Scale/V2-10.14 Autoscaling and Capacity Control#^tbl-warm-pool-tiers|table 22]]).

| **Tier** | **State**                                   | **Activation requirement**           | **Cost while waiting**              |
|:---------|:--------------------------------------------|:-------------------------------------|:------------------------------------|
| **Hot**  | Model running on allocated GPU              | Route admitted work                  | Allocated GPU and service resources |
| **Warm** | Capacity allocated; runtime partly prepared | Finish initialization and validation | Allocated resources remain billable |
| **Cold** | GPU not allocated                           | Acquire capacity, load, and warm up  | Artifact storage; no allocated GPU  |

Table 22: **Tiered Warm Pool Readiness Levels**: Readiness trades activation delay against idle resource cost. Actual delay and billing depend on the deployment. Cold capacity is subject to quotas and physical availability.

^tbl-warm-pool-tiers

When a traffic spike hits, the controller routes work to ready replicas, activates prepared capacity, and requests additional instances if needed. Pool sizing must cover the measured provisioning delay and include an overload policy for capacity that cannot be acquired in time.

### Spot and Preemptible Instances

^sec-inference-scale-spot-preemptible-instances-c969

Revoking a spot GPU replica mid-generation destroys every in-flight sequence’s KV cache unless the worker can drain or migrate its active requests inside the cloud provider’s two-minute warning window. Discounted interruptible capacity enters serving through the same purchasing tiers that fleet capacity planning uses (**[[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/02 Distributed ML/08 Fleet Orchestration/V2-08.09 Cost Optimization#^tbl-cloud-capacity-reservation-tiers|Cloud Capacity Reservation Tiers]]**): reserved capacity for the steady baseline, on-demand capacity for bursts, and spot capacity for work that tolerates revocation. **[[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/02 Distributed ML/08 Fleet Orchestration/V2-08.09 Cost Optimization#^eq-fleet-orchestration-spot-cost|equation 8.3]]** derives the effective cost of preemptible capacity from its productive fraction. Serving adds a loss that training checkpoints do not cover, because the productive fraction must also pay for draining, rerouting, and rebuilding in-flight KV state without violating the affected requests’ SLO. [[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/03 The Fleet in Production/10 Serving at Scale/V2-10.14 Autoscaling and Capacity Control#^lst-spot-termination|Listing 2]] orders the shutdown path so new work stops first, in-flight work drains while time remains, recoverable state is persisted, and the load balancer stops sending traffic before the instance disappears.

↩︎ Capacity purchasing tiers and the effective cost of interruptible capacity are established in **[[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/02 Distributed ML/08 Fleet Orchestration/V2-08.09 Cost Optimization#^sec-fleet-orchestration-cost-optimization|Cost Optimization]]**.

``` python
async def handle_spot_termination():
    # Received 2-minute warning
    # 1. Stop admission and remove the replica from routing
    stop_accepting_requests()
    deregister_from_loadbalancer()

    # 2. Complete in-flight requests (if possible)
    await complete_inflight(timeout=90)

    # 3. Save state for resumption elsewhere
    save_kv_cache_to_storage()

    # 4. Terminate gracefully
    shutdown()
```

Listing 2: **Spot Termination Handling**: Graceful shutdown sequence that stops admission, deregisters from the load balancer, drains in-flight requests, and persists recoverable state.

^lst-spot-termination

Replica-level draining protects in-flight requests only if the upstream router avoids placing strict-SLO traffic on interruptible capacity in the first place. The gateway in [[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/03 The Fleet in Production/10 Serving at Scale/V2-10.14 Autoscaling and Capacity Control#^fig-spot-aware-routing|figure 22]] separates traffic by its tolerance for capacity revocation, and an eviction notice triggers draining when time permits; requests that cannot finish need a permitted retry, reconstruction, or rejection path.

![[90 Assets/Volume II/10 Inference/Spot Aware Routing e71a75c21a.svg]]

Figure 22: **Spot-Aware Traffic Distribution**: The gateway classifies requests by SLO and routes 70 percent of traffic, the SLO-critical share, to on-demand replicas that face no spot reclamation, and 30 percent, the best-effort share, to discounted spot replicas that can be preempted. When a spot worker receives an eviction notice, the gateway stops routing to it while it drains, and best-effort requests tolerate occasional retries. The traffic split is illustrative; cost savings depend on provisioned capacity, discounts, retries, and idle headroom.

^fig-spot-aware-routing

Savings under this split depend on provisioned GPU-hours, discounts, retries, and reserve capacity rather than on the traffic share, and spot capacity changes none of the fault-tolerance requirements of the SLO-critical path. Even a well-scaled single-region fleet, however, remains bounded by wide-area network latency to distant users and by the blast radius of a regional outage.
