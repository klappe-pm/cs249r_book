# V2-10.18 Summary

[[Home|Home]] · [[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/Volume 2 Overview|Volume Overview]] · [[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/03 The Fleet in Production/10 Serving at Scale/V2-10 Serving at Scale|V2-10 Serving at Scale]]

^sec-inference-scale-summary-bb6a

Serving at scale turns trained models and distributed hardware into a service under continuously arriving work. Training optimizes aggregate progress over a run; serving must also meet user-visible latency targets while paying recurring operating costs. The useful capacity of the fleet is the work it completes within its service contract, not the work its accelerators execute in isolation. Test-time compute sharpens the point. When quality is bought by spending more compute per query, the scaling curve that drove the fleet’s growth moves into the request path, and every increment of quality is paid again on every request.

Serving performance depends on the balance between compute and memory bandwidth. In the terms of the single-machine iron law recalled in **[[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/00 Introduction/01 Introduction/V2-01.06 The C³ Taxonomy and Foundations of Scale#^sec-vol2-introduction-fleet-law|The Fleet Law]]**, long-prompt prefill can be compute-bound (${O}/{R_{\text{peak}}}$), while small-batch decode is often bandwidth-bound (${D_{\text{vol}}}/{\text{BW}}$). Each decode iteration reads active weights and the sequences’ KV state; batching shares weight reads across more tokens, whereas longer contexts increase KV traffic. Single-replica efficiency (${\eta_{\text{hw}}}$) therefore depends on phase, batch size, and context length. Variable output lengths make fixed batch membership waste capacity after shorter requests finish.

To maximize throughput under memory-bandwidth constraints, modern serving architectures co-design the memory hierarchy and the iteration scheduler. Continuous batching recovers idle slots by inserting new requests at token boundaries. PagedAttention reduces physical memory fragmentation through fixed-block allocation while retaining partial-page waste. Prefill/decode disaggregation assigns prompt processing and token generation to separate hardware pools sized around their measured compute, memory, and communication costs.

When model state exceeds device memory, sharding spreads a replica across accelerators; when demand exceeds a replica’s service capacity, additional replicas supply aggregate throughput. In Fleet Law terms, sharding divides the compute term ${T_{\text{compute}}}/{N}$ of [[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/03 The Fleet in Production/10 Serving at Scale/V2-10.11 Model Sharding for Inference#^eq-parallel-time|equation 12]] but adds communication and synchronization $({T_{\text{comm}}}({N}), {T_{\text{sync}}}({N}))$ governed by interconnect latency floors (${\alpha}$) and transfer bandwidth (${\beta}$). Replication, routing, isolation, autoscaling, and multi-region placement add coordination costs and wide-area round trips to the same budget, and their tail control requires headroom. Across the serving hierarchy, the engineering objective is to increase useful completed work while preserving the latency and reliability contract under load.

**Key Takeaways: Serving capacity is constrained by its contract**

- **Serving cost recurs throughout deployment**: Training is paid per model version, while inference expense accrues with traffic (principle [[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/03 The Fleet in Production/V2 Fleet-In-Production Principles#^pri-serving-cost-dominance|17]]). In the chapter’s high-volume recommendation scenario, lifetime serving cost reaches 788.4× the training cost, making efficiency a recurring lever, measured as cost per completed request at a fixed request mix, quality, and SLO.
- **SLO goodput is the capacity measure**: A serving fleet’s capacity is the rate of completed requests that meet their TTFT and per-token targets for a fixed request mix, so batching, sharding, caching, and autoscaling help only when they raise that rate. Aggregate throughput overstates capacity whenever queueing, first-token delay, or slow token delivery makes completed work miss its contract.
- **Batching trades waiting for reuse**: Throughput saturates past a knee set by the ratio of fixed to per-request batch cost, and a batching window caps formation delay but not queueing for a busy replica. Little’s law then sizes the concurrency and replica count that a target rate and mean latency imply.
- **Decode turns memory into capacity**: Long-prompt prefill often binds on compute, while small-batch decode rereads active weights and expands private KV state token by token (principle [[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/03 The Fleet in Production/V2 Fleet-In-Production Principles#^pri-autoregressive-bottleneck|18]]). Continuous batching, PagedAttention, prefix reuse, and KV compression matter because they convert scarce HBM bandwidth and memory into admitted requests.
- **Prefill and decode compete for a shared worker**: A monolithic long-prompt prefill lands in every active stream’s token gap. Chunked prefill bounds that gap by moving the cost into the long prompt’s own time to first token, while separate prefill and decode pools remove the interference only when their KV-cache transfer is repaid in SLO goodput.
- **Model class sets the primitive**: Vision models benefit from predictable static or dynamic batches, recommenders from feature-parallel embedding sharding, and LLMs from iteration-level scheduling. A fleet scheduler must match the workload shape instead of applying one universal batch-size rule.
- **Distribution always sends a bill**: Sharding, routing, isolation, and failover buy capacity or resilience by spending communication and coordination. Tensor parallelism pays collectives on every generated token, so it is normally kept inside one fast interconnect domain. Power-of-two choices (principle [[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/03 The Fleet in Production/V2 Fleet-In-Production Principles#^pri-power-two-choices|19]]) needs only two probes, which for variable-length generation should compare remaining work, with atomic admit-or-reject at the destination because probe results go stale.

^callout-takeaways--10-18-c2b302ed

The serving cost dominance principle ([[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/03 The Fleet in Production/V2 Fleet-In-Production Principles#^pri-serving-cost-dominance|17]]) makes local efficiency valuable only when the service can turn it into useful capacity. Memory released by quantization may admit more sequences, but those sequences still compete for execution time and can push the endpoint beyond its latency budget. The economic comparison therefore holds the request mix, quality requirement, and service targets fixed before asking whether a change lowers the cost of completed work.

**What’s Next: From data center to edge**

The serving designs developed here coordinate accelerator replicas under data-center memory, network, and availability assumptions; even the global stack’s edge points of presence only answer from cache and leave model execution in the clusters. Many applications also run models on smartphones and other personal devices, where sustained power is a few watts, memory is shared with foreground work, and connectivity is intermittent. When those devices must also adapt to local data, the fleet has to decide where learning happens, which device updates it accepts, and how a population improves a shared model without collecting raw examples.
