# V2-10.10 Weight Quantization for Serving

[[Home|Home]] · [[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/Volume 2 Overview|Volume Overview]] · [[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/03 The Fleet in Production/10 Serving at Scale/V2-10 Serving at Scale|V2-10 Serving at Scale]]

![[90 Assets/Volume II/10 Inference/Inference Quantization Capacity Ladder 123393353f.svg]]

*Linear memory ladder comparing a 140 GB FP16 70B model with a 35 GB INT4 70B model. A dashed red vertical line marks one GPU's 80 GB capacity: the FP16 bar crosses it and the INT4 bar ends well inside it. A bracket marks the 4 times reduction.*

*INT4 turns a two-GPU model into a one-GPU candidate.*

Keeping a 70B model’s 140 GB of FP16 weights intact forces every replica across at least two 80 GB GPUs and makes every decode step wait on streaming 140 GB from HBM. Weight quantization for serving is therefore a bottleneck-selection decision: reducing precision turns that 140 GB FP16 weight payload into a 35 GB INT4 payload, allowing a model that previously required multiple GPUs to fit on one (perspective [10.3](https://mlsysbook.ai/vol2/10_inference/10_inference.html#psp-inference-quantization-serving-constraint)). The ideal payload reduction is 4$\times$, although metadata, runtime buffers, and KV state still enter the deployment’s memory budget. Because neither quality loss nor speedup is fixed by nominal bit width, both must be measured for the deployed representation and workload.

**Systems Perspective 10.3: Quantization as a serving constraint**

In serving systems, quantization changes the binding resource as well as the model’s size. Post-training quantization (PTQ) can shrink a deployed model without retraining, while quantization-aware training (QAT) spends training effort to preserve accuracy at lower precision. QAT simulates low-precision rounding in the forward pass and passes gradients through the rounding step as if it were absent, the straight-through estimator ([[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/03 The Fleet in Production/10 Serving at Scale/V2-10.20 Source References#^ref-bengio2013estimating|Bengio et al. 2013]]), so the weights adapt to the error they will carry. It earns that cost only after a PTQ recipe misses the quality budget at the target precision, and a recipe that passes at INT8 says nothing about INT4. The practical order is to deploy with PTQ when it meets quality and reach for QAT, or adapter-based fine-tuning of a quantized base, when it does not, rechecking quality after the final deployment conversion. The systems question is which constraint the lower-precision representation relaxes: GPU memory capacity, HBM bandwidth during decode, accelerator instruction support, or cost per served token.

Choosing a quantized representation starts with deciding which quantity should shrink: resident weights for fit, bytes moved per decode step for bandwidth, activations for prefill throughput, or the precision path supported by target hardware. Serving at scale also introduces distinct operational constraints: a deployment team may need post-training quantization without the original training corpus, quality must be preserved across diverse inputs, and hardware deployment targets vary from data center GPUs to edge accelerators. Lack of the original corpus does not imply data-free calibration; the methods that follow may still require representative deployment inputs. Production inference therefore starts from a decision model rather than from the methods. [[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/03 The Fleet in Production/10 Serving at Scale/V2-10.10 Weight Quantization for Serving#^tbl-serving-quantization-decision-model|Table 11]] ties each method family to the serving constraint it relaxes; what changes for each when it is deployed is its calibration cost, kernel path, and the evaluation a serving team must run. Method names such as W4A16 and W8A8 give the bit widths of the weights (W) and of the activations (A).

| **Binding serving constraint** | **Representation change**                       | **Method family**       | **Risk to test before rollout**             |
|:-------------------------------|:------------------------------------------------|:------------------------|:--------------------------------------------|
| Model does not fit in memory   | Quantize weights only                           | GPTQ, AWQ               | Calibration mismatch and quality regression |
| Decode is bandwidth-bound      | Reduce bytes read per token                     | W4A16 weight-only paths | Kernel support and KV-cache headroom        |
| Prefill is compute-bound       | Quantize weights and activations                | SmoothQuant W8A8        | Activation outliers and INT8 hardware path  |
| Outliers block low precision   | Change the coordinate basis before quantization | Rotation-based methods  | Transform overhead and limited runtime path |

Table 11: **Serving Quantization Decision Model**: Quantization methods are useful only when they relax the resource that binds the serving system. The method family follows from the bottleneck: fit, bandwidth, prefill compute, or outlier robustness.

### Outliers and Block-Wise Scaling

^sec-inference-scale-block-wise-scaling

When a single weight or activation channel is ten times larger than its neighbors, uniform per-tensor quantization must either clip that extreme value or stretch its step size across the entire range, collapsing the surrounding small values to zero. Large language models produce such outlier features routinely: in the models studied by Dettmers et al. ([[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/03 The Fleet in Production/10 Serving at Scale/V2-10.20 Source References#^ref-dettmers2022llmint8|2022]]), about 0.1 percent of hidden dimensions carried activation magnitudes roughly 3–20$\times$ larger than the rest. Weight tensors face the same damage when a few large weights set the scale for the whole matrix. Block-wise quantization contains that weight-side damage inside the stored format.

**Definition 10.6: Block-wise quantization**

***Block-wise quantization*** is an ML quantization scheme that partitions a weight tensor into nonoverlapping groups of ${b_{\text{block}}}$ elements and assigns each group its own scale. An affine scheme uses ${s}_i = ({x_{\max,i}}- {x_{\min,i}}) / (2^{b}- 1)$ together with an offset or zero point; a symmetric scheme centers the range at zero and needs only scale metadata. The metadata examples below assume symmetric scale-only quantization.

1.  **Significance**: With block size ${b_{\text{block}}} = 64$ and ${b}= 4$ bits, weights compress from 16 bits to 4 bits (a 4$\times$ memory reduction) while each FP16 scale adds $16/64 = 0.25$ bits per weight. This yields an effective bit-width of $4.25$ bits per weight: 6.25 percent overhead relative to the INT4 payload, or about 1.6 percent of the original FP16 weight size.
2.  **Distinction**: Unlike per-tensor quantization, which applies a single scale across the entire weight matrix, block-wise quantization contains outlier damage within individual blocks. Per-tensor scaling forces that scale to accommodate outlier values, which wastes precision on the majority of near-zero weights. A single extreme value therefore cannot degrade quantization fidelity for the whole tensor.
3.  **Common pitfall**: A frequent misconception is that block size is a free hyperparameter. Smaller blocks (${b_{\text{block}}} = 32$) can improve local range resolution but double the scale metadata overhead vs. ${b_{\text{block}}} = 64$. At ${b_{\text{block}}} = 16$, the scale adds 1 bit per weight: 25 percent overhead relative to the INT4 payload, or 6.25 percent of the original FP16 weight size. The effect on model quality and kernel speed must be evaluated rather than inferred from block size alone.

The metadata price stays small across formats. An INT8 scheme with one FP32 scale per 128 weights adds $32/128 = 0.25$ bits per weight, about 3 percent of its payload. Block-wise scaling therefore isolates outliers without the bandwidth penalty of a higher-precision format, which keeps the decode traffic saving of a low-bit format intact. [[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/03 The Fleet in Production/10 Serving at Scale/V2-10.10 Weight Quantization for Serving#^fig-block-quantization|Figure 15]] isolates this dynamic-range protection across an eight-weight sample containing a single outlier ($58.7$). Under per-tensor scaling (left), the outlier dictates a coarse scale factor (${s}\approx 0.462$) that forces six of the seven small weights to round to zero. Partitioning the tensor into four-element blocks (right) confines that coarse scale to the second block, allowing the uncorrupted first block to adopt a fine scale (${s}_1 \approx 0.00165$) that preserves full INT8 resolution.

![[90 Assets/Volume II/10 Inference/Block Quantization 8e4ffd64cb.svg]]

Figure 15: **Block-Wise versus Per-Tensor Quantization**: Under per-tensor scaling (left), one outlier weight sets the scale for the whole tensor, so six of the seven small weights round to zero and the seventh rounds to one. Under block-wise quantization (right), each group of weights gets its own scale: the block without the outlier keeps full INT8 resolution, while the small weights that share the outlier’s block are still crushed. Smaller blocks confine the damage further at the cost of more scale metadata.

### LLM-Specific Quantization Challenges

^sec-inference-scale-llmspecific-quantization-challenges-b7a2

Block-wise scaling handles weight outliers inside the stored format. The outlier activation problem is harder, because activations arrive at run time and their extreme channels cannot be rescaled offline block by block. A coarse scale loses resolution for smaller values, while a narrow range clips large ones. This trade-off is not unique to language models, but its channel structure motivates the methods below.

For a serving deployment the consequence is that no single INT8 scale suits both the outlier channels and the typical values. The method families therefore differ in which part of the serving contract they protect, at which stage they pay for it, and what they cost to deploy. LLM.int8() ([[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/03 The Fleet in Production/10 Serving at Scale/V2-10.20 Source References#^ref-dettmers2022llmint8|Dettmers et al. 2022]]) pays at run time. It detects outlier dimensions against a magnitude threshold, multiplies them in FP16 and the rest in INT8, and sums the two products. This preserves quality at the cost of outlier detection and a second execution path on every step.

GPTQ ([[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/03 The Fleet in Production/10 Serving at Scale/V2-10.20 Source References#^ref-frantar2023gptq|Frantar et al. 2023]]) and AWQ ([[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/03 The Fleet in Production/10 Serving at Scale/V2-10.20 Source References#^ref-lin2023awq|Lin et al. 2024]]) pay offline and both produce 4-bit weight-only artifacts. GPTQ quantizes a layer’s weights in sequence and adjusts the still-unquantized weights to compensate for the error already introduced, guided by a second-order estimate from calibration activations of how sensitive the layer’s output is to each weight. AWQ instead protects the salient weights, those that multiply large activations, because a small rounding error on a weight channel that multiplies a large activation produces a large output error. Multiplying those salient weight channels by a scale factor greater than one before group quantization forces them to span a larger portion of the shared 4-bit integer grid, reducing their relative rounding error while an inverse scale is applied to the corresponding input activations. Both methods need representative calibration inputs and supported packing kernels, a reconstruction objective that is only a proxy for task quality, and a comparison at the same model, bit budget, and held-out evaluation. AWQ also needs its inverse scales fused into the preceding layer of the deployed graph, or it spends part of the latency saving on an extra runtime operation.

SmoothQuant ([[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/03 The Fleet in Production/10 Serving at Scale/V2-10.20 Source References#^ref-xiao2023smoothquant|Xiao et al. 2023]]) targets low-precision matrix multiplication rather than weight-only storage and pays in a preprocessing transform. It divides each activation channel by a smoothing factor and multiplies the matching weights by the same factor. This leaves the product unchanged before rounding and migrates part of the extreme activation range into the weights, so that both tensors can fit inside INT8 for W8A8 execution. That matters for compute-bound prefill, although shrinking the activation range stretches the weight range and makes the weights harder to quantize. The smoothing factor therefore balances the two quantization errors, and production activation extremes can still differ from the calibration ranges. Rotation-based methods such as QuaRot ([[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/03 The Fleet in Production/10 Serving at Scale/V2-10.20 Source References#^ref-ashkboos2024quarot|Ashkboos et al. 2024]]) apply orthogonal Hadamard transforms that spread large coordinates across channels, which makes W4A4 a serving candidate at the price of some online rotation work.

None of these properties fixes quality. Each artifact must be compared with the FP16 baseline on the same model, evaluation data, and runtime, reporting its calibration set, group size, kernel path, and any online-transform overhead. Its serving benefit must also be measured as SLO-compliant throughput on the target workload rather than inferred from nominal bit width.

### W8A8 Versus Weight-Only Ceilings

^sec-inference-scale-smoothquant-migrating-quantization-difficulty-f37f

Quantizing only the weights (W8A16 or W4A16) cuts weight traffic during decode but leaves compute-bound prefill executing on FP16 Tensor Cores at unchanged arithmetic speed, whereas quantizing both weights and activations (W8A8) risks accuracy loss from activation outliers without further shrinking weight bytes. The prefill-decode split therefore decides which path can pay: both schemes shrink the weights, but only W8A8 changes the arithmetic ceiling. [[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/03 The Fleet in Production/10 Serving at Scale/V2-10.10 Weight Quantization for Serving#^tbl-smoothquant-w8a8|Table 12]] bounds what each 8-bit path can buy relative to the FP16 baseline.

| **Configuration**        | **Weight bytes** | **Prefill compute ceiling**  | **Decode weight-traffic ceiling** |   **Quality**    |
|:-------------------------|:----------------:|:----------------------------:|:---------------------------------:|:----------------:|
| **FP16 (baseline)**      |    1$\times$     |          1$\times$           |             1$\times$             |     Baseline     |
| **W8A16 (weights only)** |    Reduced 2×    |    1$\times$ (FP16 math)     |             Up to 2×              | Measure vs. FP16 |
| **W8A8 (SmoothQuant)**   |    Reduced 2×    | Up to 2× (INT8 Tensor Cores) |             Up to 2×              | Measure vs. FP16 |

Table 12: **W8A8 SmoothQuant vs. Weight-Only Quantization**: Ceilings derived from bytes per weight and the H100’s INT8-to-FP16 peak ratio, not measured speedups. Both schemes halve weight bytes, so both can at most halve weight traffic in bandwidth-bound decode. Only W8A8 moves prefill onto INT8 Tensor Cores. Realized gains and quality loss depend on kernels, calibration, and the request mix.

^tbl-smoothquant-w8a8

The distinction is which ceiling binds. Among these configurations, W8A8 enables INT8 matrix multiplication, with the illustrated 2× peak ratio providing a compute ceiling rather than a measured prefill speedup. Both quantized schemes halve the weight payload. If weight reads dominate decode, activation quantization does not further reduce that traffic, although it can change other memory and compute costs. Quantization and dequantization also add work. The end-to-end benefit therefore depends on the prompt/output mix, batch sizes, and measured execution path.

Hardware and software support decide which of these savings reach the serving workload, because the arithmetic format and the resident storage format need not be identical. A common INT4 weight-only kernel reads packed weights and dequantizes them to FP16 on chip during the GEMM, so it cuts weight capacity and read traffic without any INT4 arithmetic. For batch-one decode dominated by weight reads, throughput then scales roughly as memory bandwidth divided by the model’s weight bytes, omitting KV-cache traffic and execution overhead, and leaving the arithmetic in FP16 costs little because arithmetic is not the binding term. At large batch or in prefill, where execution approaches the compute ceiling, the gain comes instead from a weight-activation format, W8A8 or FP8 on hardware with FP8 Tensor Cores, which raises the ceiling that weight-only formats cannot move. In the ideal memory-bound case, 4-bit weights cut weight traffic by 4$\times$, but realized decode throughput depends on kernel support, dequantization overhead, KV-cache precision, batch shape, and whether HBM bandwidth is truly the bottleneck. A serving system can therefore run different formats at different operating points, with each configuration evaluated against the same quality criterion and its own latency targets, and the value of 4-bit serving is strongest when it increases model residency or batch capacity.

### Runtime Contract for Quantized Serving

^sec-inference-scale-framework-integration-945c

Loading a 4-bit checkpoint into a serving runtime that expands the weights back into resident FP16 tensors at startup or falls back to unoptimized kernels forfeits both the memory capacity and the HBM bandwidth savings before a single request arrives. Production serving frameworks determine whether a quantized artifact retains its intended advantages by converting packed weights inside the execution kernel rather than in HBM. The serving stack must therefore report the resident representation and kernel path, budget KV state around actual allocations, and expose quality and latency regressions ([[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/03 The Fleet in Production/10 Serving at Scale/V2-10.10 Weight Quantization for Serving#^tbl-quantized-serving-runtime-contract|table 13]]).

| **Runtime responsibility**              | **Why it matters**                                                                       |
|:----------------------------------------|:-----------------------------------------------------------------------------------------|
| Preserve the quantized artifact         | Full-model expansion into resident FP16 forfeits the reduced weight storage and traffic. |
| Select compatible low-precision kernels | Unsupported operators fall back to slower or higher-precision execution.                 |
| Plan memory around weights and KV cache | The capacity gain matters only if the freed memory becomes useful batch headroom.        |
| Validate quality and latency together   | A lower-bit path that meets latency but fails task quality is not a serving win.         |

Table 13: **Quantized Serving Runtime Contract**: Framework-specific APIs differ, but every production path has to preserve representation, choose supported kernels, plan memory, and validate quality under the deployed traffic mix.

^tbl-quantized-serving-runtime-contract

Weight quantization reduces the weight allocation, while PagedAttention reduces waste in allocating KV state. Both feed the same admission budget as the capacity estimator of notebook [10.5](https://mlsysbook.ai/vol2/10_inference/10_inference.html#nbk-inference-kv-cache-capacity-estimator), with quantized weights in place of FP16 ones. That estimate is optimistic, because a usable integer limit must round down after reserving runtime buffers, quantization metadata, and safety headroom, and sharded deployments must satisfy the budget on every device.

This comparison uses a conservative decimal HBM budget matching the A100 nameplate label, rather than the registered byte capacity used by the node estimator. A 70B model with 4-bit weights requires approximately 35 GB, leaving 45 GB on an 80 GB A100 for KV cache. With FP16 KV cache at about 2.6 GB per 1K-token sequence for the MHA baseline, this supports about 17 concurrent 1K-token sequences before other runtime overheads. With FP16 weights, the same 70B model would not fit on one A100 at all, so weight-only quantization changes both feasibility and batch capacity.

### Selecting a Quantization Format

^sec-inference-scale-quantization-selection-guidelines-24fb

Choosing a quantization format from datasheet TFLOPS or nominal bit width alone fails when a 4-bit checkpoint degrades task accuracy on production prompts or when a smaller replica completes fewer SLO-compliant tokens per GPU. The final selection must satisfy memory, quality, and latency requirements together. Native support makes a precision path a candidate without establishing the model’s quality at that precision, and no universal quality ordering separates 4-bit weight-only from 8-bit weight-and-activation quantization, so both are evaluated against the same task baseline. For compute-bound prefill, W8A8 offers the illustrated 2× INT8 compute ceiling; for decode dominated by weight reads, W4A16 can cut the weight payload by up to 4$\times$, provided conversion and other costs leave a useful saving.

These precision choices affect cost per million useful tokens. The A100 residency calculation explains why fewer GPUs become possible at all, because INT4 weights shrink the resident payload enough for a 70B replica to fit on fewer devices. [[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/03 The Fleet in Production/10 Serving at Scale/V2-10.10 Weight Quantization for Serving#^tbl-quantization-serving-cost|Table 14]] isolates the effect of GPU count by assuming equal per-GPU prices and the same completed token rate across three configurations. Under that contract, reducing the number of billed GPUs reduces cost proportionally. The calculation does not establish that the smaller replicas sustain the same rate or meet the same quality and latency targets.

| **Configuration**               | **Cost per 1M tokens (estimated)** |
|:--------------------------------|:----------------------------------:|
| **FP16 on 8$\times$ A100**      |               \$2.40               |
| **AWQ 4-bit on 4$\times$ A100** |               \$1.20               |
| **AWQ 4-bit on 2$\times$ A100** |               \$0.60               |

Table 14: **Quantization Impact on Serving Cost**: Estimated cost per million tokens at three configurations. At equal unit prices and unchanged useful token throughput, halving the GPU count halves the cost; halving it again brings the per-million-token cost to one quarter of the FP16 baseline. The cost savings come almost entirely from fewer GPUs rather than from per-GPU throughput gains.

^tbl-quantization-serving-cost

The illustrated 2–4$\times$ cost reduction follows from the assumed device-count reduction at unchanged useful throughput. If the smaller replica completes fewer qualifying tokens per second, cost per token falls by less and can increase. A deployment comparison must therefore combine billed replica cost with measured SLO-compliant output under the same quality contract.

Precision moves the capacity wall without removing it. A 175-billion-parameter model still needs about 87.5 GB for its 4-bit weight payload alone, more than one 80 GB H100 holds before KV state and runtime allocations. Keeping that representation accelerator-resident requires distributing it across devices. Offloading to host memory is another placement choice, but introduces transfers that must fit the latency budget. Sharding addresses the capacity problem while adding communication and coordination to each replica.
