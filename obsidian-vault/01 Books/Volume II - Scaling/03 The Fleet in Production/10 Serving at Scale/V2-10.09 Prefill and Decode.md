# V2-10.09 Prefill and Decode

[[Home|Home]] · [[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/Volume 2 Overview|Volume Overview]] · [[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/03 The Fleet in Production/10 Serving at Scale/V2-10 Serving at Scale|V2-10 Serving at Scale]]

^sec-inference-scale-prefill-decode

Admitting a 4,096-token prompt onto a worker that is already streaming replies to thirty users saturates the GPU’s Tensor Cores for hundreds of milliseconds and freezes every active token stream until the prompt finishes. A language-model request is two workloads in sequence. Prefill reads the whole prompt in one forward pass and writes its keys and values into the cache sized in [[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/03 The Fleet in Production/10 Serving at Scale/V2-10.08 Memory and Decode-Time Management#^sec-inference-scale-kv-cache-wall|section 10.7.1]], while decode extends that cache one token per step until the response ends. Because the two phases run the same weights on opposite sides of the roofline ridge, their asymmetry decides which optimization helps which phase, how a replica schedules them when they share a worker, and whether they should share one at all.

### The Phase Asymmetry

^sec-inference-scale-phase-asymmetry

That roofline split comes directly from how many tokens each phase feeds through a linear projection at once. During the prefill phase, the engine computes key-value representations across all prompt tokens (${S_{\text{prompt}}}$) concurrently. Because all prompt tokens are processed in parallel, the linear projection GEMM operations operate on large sequence dimensions (${M}= {S_{\text{prompt}}}$). For a projection layer with hidden dimension ${d}$, arithmetic intensity scales with prompt length:

$$
{I_{\text{prefill}}}\approx \frac{2 \cdot {S_{\text{prompt}}}\cdot {d}^2}{2 \cdot {d}^2} = {S_{\text{prompt}}}\quad \left[\frac{\text{FLOPs}}{\text{byte of weights loaded}}\right]
$$

 A 1,024-token prompt therefore performs about 1,024 FLOPs per byte of FP16 weights, well past an H100’s FP16 ridge. Under this weight-traffic-only approximation, increasing ${S_{\text{prompt}}}$ beyond the accelerator’s ridge point moves the projection’s roofline ceiling to the compute limit. Reaching that ceiling still depends on kernel efficiency; short prompts, attention, other memory traffic, and communication can limit the complete prefill phase, so prefill as a whole still requires profiling.

↩︎ The ridge point that separates compute-bound from bandwidth-bound execution is established in **[[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/01 The Fleet/02 Compute Infrastructure/V2-02.04 The Memory Wall#^sec-compute-roofline|Roofline model]]**.

In contrast, the subsequent decode phase generates a single token per sequence sequentially, so the GEMM’s row dimension is the number of sequences decoding together, not the prompt length. At batch one the intensity is about 1 FLOP per byte of FP16 weights. At the decode batch sizes this chapter considers, from tens of sequences up to the knee near 80 in notebook [10.2](https://mlsysbook.ai/vol2/10_inference/10_inference.html#nbk-inference-batching-efficiency-curve), the arithmetic intensity ${I_{\text{decode}}}\approx {B_{\text{dec}}}$ stays below the ridge point. This places the weight projections in a memory-bandwidth-bound regime under this approximation. Each decode iteration streams the active weights from HBM and reuses them across the batch, so the cost of each generated token falls as the batch grows until KV-cache capacity stops further admission, which is why batch size is the decode phase’s control knob. The approximation omits KV-cache reads, which grow with every sequence’s context, so larger batches and longer contexts add bytes to every step and hold decode on the bandwidth slope short of the ridge.

![[90 Assets/Volume II/10 Inference/Inference Decode Roofline 298ea123f0.svg]]

*A roofline silhouette: a blue memory-bandwidth-bound slope rising to a dashed ridge, then an orange compute-bound ceiling. A blue dot labeled decode sits low on the slope, well left of the ridge; an orange dot labeled prefill sits on the compute ceiling past the ridge.*

*Long-prompt prefill reaches the compute ceiling; decode stays on the bandwidth slope.*

The asymmetry also sorts the optimizations by phase. Tiled, IO-aware attention reduces attention’s memory traffic mainly during prefill, because only prefill forms the full prompt-by-prompt score matrix that a tiled kernel avoids writing to HBM (**[[05 Reference/Volume II - Scaling/V2 Single-Machine Foundations#^sec-appdx-dam-taxonomy-kernel-levers-tiling|Tiling and IO-aware attention]]**). A decode step has a single query row and no quadratic intermediate; it benefits instead from a fused kernel that reads each cached key and value once and computes scores, softmax, and the weighted sum in one pass. Speculative decoding ([[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/03 The Fleet in Production/10 Serving at Scale/V2-10.08 Memory and Decode-Time Management#^sec-inference-scale-speculative-decoding-c438|section 10.7.6]]) targets decode alone, and KV-cache quantization reduces decode’s storage and read traffic while its encoding cost begins when prefill writes the cache. Precision helps both phases through different mechanisms. A weight-activation format raises the compute ceiling that long-prompt prefill reaches, while low-bit weights shrink the bytes that weight-dominated decode streams ([[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/03 The Fleet in Production/10 Serving at Scale/V2-10.10 Weight Quantization for Serving#^sec-inference-scale-weight-quantization-serving-097b|section 10.9]]). Each gain depends on format and kernel support, conversion overhead, and the costs the change leaves untouched, and each must preserve output quality.

These are properties of each phase in isolation. Once continuous batching admits new prompts onto a worker that is decoding other streams, the asymmetry becomes a scheduling conflict between them, and a replica has two ways to resolve it: interleave the phases on one worker, or give each phase its own pool.

### Chunked Prefill and Prompt-Decode Interleaving

^sec-inference-chunked-prefill

Continuous batching admits work at iteration boundaries, yet production requests arrive with widely disparate prompt lengths. Admitting a long prompt in one piece holds the worker for its entire compute-bound prefill while the bandwidth-bound decode streams beside it wait.

**Definition 10.5: Chunked prefill**

***Chunked prefill*** is an LLM serving mechanism that divides long prompt evaluations into discrete token chunks of size at most ${C_{\text{chunk}}}$, interleaving prefill computation with ongoing decode iterations within a shared token budget.

1.  **Significance**: It limits the prompt-decode interference problem where monolithic prefills stall active decode streams for hundreds of milliseconds, giving the scheduler control over how much prefill work can lengthen the TBT of active streams. Meeting interactive streaming service-level objectives (SLOs) still requires measuring the resulting tail latency.
2.  **Distinction**: Unlike continuous batching with monolithic prefill (which prefills each prompt in one uninterrupted pass before the worker returns to decoding), chunked prefill treats prefill as a multi-step iterative process co-scheduled alongside memory-bound decode tokens.
3.  **Common pitfall**: Sizing the chunk budget ${C_{\text{chunk}}}$ too small incurs kernel launch and attention mask overheads, degrading overall prefill throughput. Sizing it too large allows prefill compute to monopolize execution time, causing TBT tail spikes.

With an unchunked prefill policy, the scheduler runs an incoming prompt through the whole model before returning to decode work. The interference comes from scheduling that whole operation without interleaving other requests’ decode steps, and the delay lands in the TBT of requests already decoding on the same worker. A long response can dilute an isolated stall in its average TPOT even though the user sees a conspicuous gap, so prioritizing the new prompt’s time to first token can consume the streaming-latency budget of requests already decoding. The measured prefill duration and the endpoint’s TBT target determine whether interleaving is necessary.

#### The Chunked Prefill Scheduling Mechanism

^sec-inference-chunked-prefill-scheduling

To limit this interference, chunked prefill (definition [10.5](https://mlsysbook.ai/vol2/10_inference/10_inference.html#dfn-inference-chunked-prefill), used by Sarathi-Serve ([[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/03 The Fleet in Production/10 Serving at Scale/V2-10.20 Source References#^ref-agrawal2024sarathi|Agrawal et al. 2024]])) partitions prompts into chunks of at most ${C_{\text{chunk}}}$ tokens. A candidate range such as $256\text{--}512$ tokens must be tested against the model, hardware, and streaming deadline rather than treated as a universal setting. A prompt of length ${S_{\text{prompt}}}$ requires at least $\lceil {S_{\text{prompt}}}/ {C_{\text{chunk}}}\rceil$ scheduling iterations; a smaller residual token budget can require more.

The two schedules in [[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/03 The Fleet in Production/10 Serving at Scale/V2-10.09 Prefill and Decode#^fig-chunked-prefill|figure 13]] hand the same long prompt to the same worker. Run whole, its prefill sits between two decode steps of every active stream, so each stream’s token gap stretches to the full prefill time. Split into chunks, the prompt advances one chunk per hybrid iteration beside the streams’ decode tokens, so each stream waits at most one hybrid iteration, while the prompt’s own first token arrives later than it would have under the monolithic schedule.

![[90 Assets/Volume II/10 Inference/Chunked Prefill b3bb2aa8ed.svg]]

Figure 13: **Chunked Prefill Trades One Prompt’s TTFT for Every Stream’s TBT**: Three streams decode while a long prompt P arrives; the timing is illustrative. (a) A monolithic prefill runs P’s whole prompt in one pass, and every active stream stalls for its full duration. (b) Chunked prefill gives P one chunk per hybrid iteration beside the streams’ decode tokens, so each stream’s gap shrinks to one hybrid iteration, and P’s first token arrives later. Chunking changes when the prefill work runs, not how much of it there is.

^fig-chunked-prefill

In each iteration, the scheduler constructs a hybrid batch combining active decode requests with prefill chunks under a global iteration token budget ${N_{\text{budget}}}$, a count of tokens rather than a time, as formulated in [[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/03 The Fleet in Production/10 Serving at Scale/V2-10.09 Prefill and Decode#^eq-chunked-prefill-budget|equation 8]]:

$$
{B_{\text{dec}}}+ \sum_{{i} \in {\mathcal{P}_{\text{active}}}} {C}_{{i}} \le {N_{\text{budget}}} \tag{8}
$$

^eq-chunked-prefill-budget

where ${B_{\text{dec}}}$ is the number of active decode tokens and ${C}_{{i}}$ is the number of prompt tokens allocated to waiting prefill request ${i}$, so every term counts tokens. The simplified loop assumes admission control reserves sufficient KV-cache capacity and a future decode slot for each admitted request, keeping the decode count within the iteration budget even after prefills are promoted. Each waiting request contributes at most one chunk per iteration, with its committed prompt offset unchanged until execution completes. [Algorithm 3](https://mlsysbook.ai/vol2/10_inference/10_inference.html#algo-chunked-prefill-scheduler) formalizes this iteration-level hybrid scheduling loop.

#### Weight-Loading Amortization via Decode Piggybacking

^sec-inference-chunked-prefill-piggybacking

Executing prefill and decode tokens together in shared projection operations amortizes weight loading across both phases, an effect termed **decode piggybacking**. Alternating separate prefill and decode operations limits scheduling interference but does not by itself establish this weight reuse.

In a small-batch isolated decode step, streaming the model parameters (${M_{\text{weights}}}$ bytes) from HBM to registers achieves low arithmetic intensity because only ${B_{\text{dec}}}$ tokens share the weight reads. Memory bandwidth then limits how much work the compute units can sustain, leaving arithmetic capacity available for a larger shared projection.

In a hybrid iteration, the model weights loaded from HBM into on-chip cache and registers are simultaneously reused for both the compute-dense prefill chunks ($\sum_{{i}} {C}_{{i}}$ tokens in total) and the memory-bound decode tokens (${B_{\text{dec}}}$ tokens). Ignoring attention-state traffic, communication, and scheduling overhead gives the projection-only lower-bound estimate in [[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/03 The Fleet in Production/10 Serving at Scale/V2-10.09 Prefill and Decode#^eq-chunked-prefill-step-time|equation 9]]:

$$
{T_{\text{step}}}\gtrsim \max\left(\frac{{M_{\text{weights}}}}{{\text{BW}_{\text{mem}}}}, \frac{2 \cdot ({B_{\text{dec}}}+ \sum_{{i}} {C}_{{i}}) \cdot {P_{\text{model}}}}{{R_{\text{peak}}}}\right) \tag{9}
$$

^eq-chunked-prefill-step-time

where ${P_{\text{model}}}$ is the active parameter count, ${\text{BW}_{\text{mem}}}$ is HBM memory bandwidth, and ${R_{\text{peak}}}$ is peak accelerator compute throughput. These quantities must describe the same device or shard. Balancing the total prefill token allocation against weight streaming can amortize weight-loading cost across both phases, but this lower bound cannot guarantee ${T_{\text{step}}}\le \text{SLO}_{\text{TBT}}$. Chunk sizing must leave room for the omitted costs and be validated against measured tail latency under the intended workload.

**Example 10.5: Sarathi: Chunked prefill implementation**

**Scenario**: An LLM inference server receives a mix of long input prompts ($10{,}000+$ tokens) and interactive short decode requests ([[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/03 The Fleet in Production/10 Serving at Scale/V2-10.20 Source References#^ref-agrawal2024sarathi|Agrawal et al. 2024]]).

**Diagnosis**: When the worker schedules a full long-prompt prefill before returning to decode, that prefill can occupy execution for hundreds of milliseconds and create a TBT spike for active streams.

**Systems lesson**: Chunking moves the cost of a long prompt rather than removing it. At $512$ tokens per chunk, a $10{,}000$-token prompt needs at least $\lceil 10{,}000/512 \rceil = 20$ iterations to finish its prefill. With this prompt as the only prefill request, active streams share each decode iteration with at most one of its chunks, while the prompt’s own time to first token stretches across those 20 iterations. Multiple waiting prompts can contribute additional chunks within the total iteration budget. The chunk budget sets that exchange between other streams’ TBT and the long prompt’s TTFT, so it must be tuned against both measured tails.

### Prefill-Decode Disaggregation

^sec-inference-scale-prefill-decode-disaggregation

Even with chunked prefill, every hybrid step still forces active decode streams to wait while prompt chunks execute on the same GPU, and a single worker pool must compromise between prefill’s compute-heavy parallelism and decode’s bandwidth- and capacity-heavy layout. Disaggregation removes that compromise by giving each phase its own hardware pool, while the KV-cache sizing of [[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/03 The Fleet in Production/10 Serving at Scale/V2-10.08 Memory and Decode-Time Management#^sec-inference-scale-kv-cache-wall|section 10.7.1]] sets the price of separating them. The cache that prefill builds is the exact payload that the network must hand off to the decode pool.

Disaggregation partitions the serving pipeline into specialized physical tiers ([[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/03 The Fleet in Production/10 Serving at Scale/V2-10.09 Prefill and Decode#^fig-disaggregated-serving|figure 14]]). In the illustrated long-prompt, small-decode-batch regime, the compute-bound prefill pool and the bandwidth-bound decode pool are joined by a remote direct memory access (RDMA) KV-cache transfer ([[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/03 The Fleet in Production/10 Serving at Scale/V2-10.20 Source References#^ref-zhong2024distserve|Zhong et al. 2024]]). This keeps the phases from competing on one accelerator and permits independent parallelism and capacity plans. The phase asymmetry tells each pool what to buy. The prefill pool wants arithmetic throughput for its compute-bound GEMMs, while the decode pool wants memory bandwidth and capacity for weight streaming and KV cache, which also makes it the natural home for aggressive weight quantization. The transfer and queueing at each pool still add to request latency, and other batch or context sizes can change which resources bind.

![[90 Assets/Volume II/10 Inference/Disaggregated Serving d942374161.svg]]

Figure 14: **Disaggregated Serving Architecture**: A long-prompt, small-decode-batch regime separates compute-bound prefill instances from memory-bandwidth-bound decode instances, mediated by RDMA KV-cache transfer. Because prefill sets time to first token and decode sets time per output token, each pool is sized for its own token rate. Other batch and context sizes can change the binding resources.

^fig-disaggregated-serving

The disaggregation decision therefore depends on a quantitative contract, not on the phase labels alone. If prefill produces ${M_{\text{KV}}}$ bytes of attention state and the usable inter-pool bandwidth is ${\beta}$, then the handoff consumes at least

$$
{T_{\text{handoff}}}= {\alpha}+ \frac{{M_{\text{KV}}}}{{\beta}}, \tag{10}
$$

^eq-prefill-decode-handoff

where ${\alpha}$ includes transfer setup and queueing for the operating point being evaluated; it is not a fixed network latency once load changes. The payload term sets the scale. A 128K-token request on the GQA model of notebook [10.5](https://mlsysbook.ai/vol2/10_inference/10_inference.html#nbk-inference-kv-cache-capacity-estimator) carries tens of gigabytes of KV state, so moving it over a single 450 GB/s NVLink direction takes on the order of a hundred milliseconds, and an inter-node link takes longer still. Spreading the transfer across a tensor-parallel group divides that time only when each device has its own path to its counterpart in the decode pool. Because ${M_{\text{KV}}}$ grows linearly with prompt length, the long prompts that make disaggregation attractive are also the prompts whose handoff costs most, and that cost lands on TTFT.

[[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/03 The Fleet in Production/10 Serving at Scale/V2-10.09 Prefill and Decode#^eq-prefill-decode-handoff|Equation 10]] therefore makes placement part of the service contract. The prefill pool must sustain the admitted prompt-token rate, the decode pool must sustain the admitted output-token rate, and both pools need headroom for the request-length distribution. Disaggregation justifies its KV-cache transfer cost only when eliminating phase interference and balancing the two pools raises SLO goodput. Otherwise, colocated chunked prefill avoids the additional handoff and pool coordination.

Whether the phases share a worker or split into pools, the base weights remain the largest fixed claim on each device’s memory and, for decode, the largest share of the bytes streamed per step. Weight quantization addresses that part of the budget, with numerical quality and execution cost determining how much of the storage saving becomes useful serving capacity.
