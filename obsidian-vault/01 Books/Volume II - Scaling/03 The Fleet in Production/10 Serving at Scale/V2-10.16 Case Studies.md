# V2-10.16 Case Studies

[[Home|Home]] · [[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/Volume 2 Overview|Volume Overview]] · [[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/03 The Fleet in Production/10 Serving at Scale/V2-10 Serving at Scale|V2-10 Serving at Scale]]

^sec-inference-scale-case-studies-9689

Individual serving optimizations such as quantization, continuous batching, sharding, or caching can look decisive in isolation yet still leave a production endpoint missing its latency, memory, or cost contract when combined under real traffic. The five design cases below are illustrative composites rather than descriptions of named companies’ internal deployments, and their request rates, latency budgets, hardware allocations, and update cadences follow the chapter’s scenario convention. Each case binds on a different constraint: one replica’s decode step, embedding scale, variable-length generation, cascade cost, or multimodal freshness. Orca and vLLM supply the previously cited scheduling and memory-management mechanisms; the scenarios combine those ideas with precision, routing, sharding, and admission control.

### One Replica Against Its Token-Latency Target

^sec-inference-scale-replica-decode-budget

Even before a serving cluster worries about routing or multi-region failover, a single replica can fail its per-token latency target on a batch of one, which leaves no headroom to batch additional requests. The first case stays inside one replica, the reference 70B model on eight NVLink-connected H100s with FP16 weights split eight ways, serving an interactive endpoint whose illustrative TPOT target is 50 ms. A batching policy can only spend the headroom that a batch-one decode step leaves, because every admitted sequence lengthens the step ([[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/03 The Fleet in Production/10 Serving at Scale/V2-10.07 Batching Strategies at Scale#^sec-inference-scale-physics-batching-efficiency-curve-021c|section 10.6.2]]), so the first question is whether one batch-one step fits inside the target at all. This constructed example illustrates budget accounting, not benchmark results. Every interval is an exposed, nonoverlapping contribution to the same request’s critical path, and time to first token, output quality, and throughput under load each need their own measurements.

The deployment’s FP16 weights occupy 140 GB, about 17.5 GB per GPU. The assumed baseline decode step is 85 ms, about 16× the 5.2 ms that streaming each shard at full HBM bandwidth would take, so the replica runs at an MBU of about 6.1 percent (**[[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/03 The Fleet in Production/09 Fleet Efficiency/V2-09.03 ML Goodput#^sec-performance-engineering-fleet-efficiency-metric-dff4|Program goodput and its measure]]**). That gap, not the weight-streaming floor, is what eager dispatch, unfused kernels, and exposed synchronization must explain, and [[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/03 The Fleet in Production/10 Serving at Scale/V2-10.16 Case Studies#^tbl-inference-replica-decode-budget|table 26]] apportions the whole step among those causes on one absolute-time boundary. In an actual trace, overlapping work must not be counted twice when constructing such a budget.

| **Exposed interval (ms)**   | **Baseline** | **After precision** | **After fusion and replay** |
|:----------------------------|-------------:|--------------------:|----------------------------:|
| GEMM                        |           34 |                18.7 |                        18.7 |
| Attention                   |         20.4 |                20.4 |                        15.3 |
| Element-wise operations     |         10.2 |                10.2 |                         3.4 |
| AllReduce                   |         10.2 |                10.2 |                        10.2 |
| Launch and runtime overhead |         10.2 |                10.2 |                           2 |
| **Total**                   |       **85** |            **69.7** |                    **49.6** |

Table 26: **Constructed Decode Budget for the Reference Replica**: Assumed nonoverlapping critical-path intervals for one batch-one decode step. Each column sums to its step duration; none is an empirical performance claim.

^tbl-inference-replica-decode-budget

The first experiment targets weight-read traffic with INT4 weight-only quantization, the bandwidth-bound decode row of [[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/03 The Fleet in Production/10 Serving at Scale/V2-10.10 Weight Quantization for Serving#^tbl-serving-quantization-decision-model|table 11]]. Assume that quality validation succeeds and that exposed GEMM time falls by 45 percent, with every other interval unchanged. The step takes 69.7 ms, still above the 50 ms target, because a large cut to one interval removes only that interval’s share of the step. INT8 KV-cache compression is a separate candidate whose benefit is capacity, as notebook [10.7](https://mlsysbook.ai/vol2/10_inference/10_inference.html#nbk-inference-precision-dividend) computes; it needs its own quality check and a new profile, so this column grants it no latency benefit.

The second experiment targets exposed attention, element-wise work, and launch gaps, using the single-replica levers of **[[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/03 The Fleet in Production/09 Fleet Efficiency/V2-09.08 Recovering Program Goodput#^sec-performance-engineering-kernel-levers|Kernel-level levers]]**. Assume a validated fused decode-attention kernel, the single-pass kernel over the KV cache of [[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/03 The Fleet in Production/10 Serving at Scale/V2-10.09 Prefill and Decode#^sec-inference-scale-phase-asymmetry|section 10.8.1]], reduces attention by 25 percent, fusion reduces element-wise work to 3.4 ms, and graph replay reduces launch and runtime overhead to 2 ms. These are scenario assumptions, and each intervention would be tested separately before the combination is retained. The step becomes 49.6 ms, inside the target with almost no slack and still about 9× above the bandwidth floor.

Communication remains 10.2 ms, unchanged in absolute time even as its share of the shorter step grows, and it costs far more than launch and transfer alone predict. Spread across the 160 AllReduces of an 80-layer step, that interval implies about 64 μs per collective, against roughly 5 μs of launch and transfer, or about 0.8 ms per step. The difference is the protocol and synchronization cost of small collectives that **[[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/03 The Fleet in Production/09 Fleet Efficiency/V2-09.08 Recovering Program Goodput#^sec-performance-engineering-overlap|Tensor-parallel collectives under contention]]** flags for measurement, and a trace that attributed it would make communication the next experiment.

The third experiment changes how much output each step yields. Assume a draft model proposes ${K}=$ 5 tokens in 4 ms, verification costs one round-two step, and each candidate is accepted independently with probability ${\alpha_{\text{acc}}}=$ 0.78. [[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/03 The Fleet in Production/10 Serving at Scale/V2-10.08 Memory and Decode-Time Management#^eq-speculative-expected-tokens|Equation 6]] gives 3.52 tokens per round, so a 53.6 ms round yields 15.2 ms per emitted token, an amortized 3.26-fold improvement in TPOT. The tokens, however, arrive in blocks. The gap between blocks is a whole round, 53.6 ms, which exceeds the same 50 ms if the endpoint also states it as a per-gap TBT target. Speculation thus meets an average-cadence contract with room to spare while missing a per-gap one on every round, which is why the TPOT and TBT targets of [[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/03 The Fleet in Production/10 Serving at Scale/V2-10.03 Limits of Single-Machine Serving#^sec-inference-scale-singlemachine-serving-insufficient-3f13|section 10.2]] are stated separately. The gain also belongs to batch one; as the speculative-decoding policy in [[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/03 The Fleet in Production/10 Serving at Scale/V2-10.08 Memory and Decode-Time Management#^sec-inference-scale-speculative-decoding-c438|section 10.7.6]] warns, larger batches consume the spare compute that made verification cheap.

The budget exposes bottleneck displacement without crediting a system improvement to an isolated kernel speedup. Precision changes one interval, fusion and replay change others, and speculation changes the output per round; their benefits compose only on a shared accounting boundary, with quality preserved. What the sequence produces is a set of candidate replica configurations, not a serving result. Accepting one for production means replacing the illustrative intervals with measurements, preserving the request-length distribution and offered load, and measuring SLO goodput ([[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/03 The Fleet in Production/10 Serving at Scale/V2-10.04 SLO Goodput and the Serving Tax#^sec-inference-scale-serving-tax-overhead-distribution-5ef3|section 10.3]]) at the batch the scheduler will actually admit. Memory freed by INT4 weights can permit that larger batch, but neither the freed capacity nor a faster batch-one step shows that the service meets every target at once. Where this first case stays inside one dense LLM replica fighting per-token latency, the next case shifts to a fleet where sparse embedding lookups across terabytes of state dominate the budget before dense GPU execution even begins.

### Embedding-Heavy Recommendation Serving

^sec-inference-scale-meta-recommendation-serving-bede

An embedding-heavy recommendation service binds on sparse lookup rather than dense forward-pass compute. Modeled on feeds, advertising, and content ranking, it processes billions of requests per day under a p99 budget below $10\text{ ms}$ for the ranking service, nested inside the roughly 100 ms end-to-end tail target of Archetype B (DLRM at Scale). The service spans hundreds of model variants and trillions of unique sparse features. The serving stack separates sparse lookup from dense ranking:

    User request → Feature collection → Embedding lookup → Model inference → Response

                        |                     |                  |
                        v                     v                  v
                 Feature Store        Embedding Servers      GPU Inference
                 (CPU, DRAM)         (CPU + SSD, 1000s)     (GPU, 100s)

The hybrid sharding strategy in lighthouse [10.1](https://mlsysbook.ai/vol2/10_inference/10_inference.html#lhs-inference-embedding-sharding-at-meta) supplies the sparse path: replication protects hot entries, column sharding spreads warm-entry access, and row sharding places the cold tail. Feature-level batching then combines lookups across requests. The remaining design decision is where each stage runs. In the GPU-CPU hybrid architecture, memory-rich CPU servers serve sparse embeddings while GPUs execute dense ranking. [[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/03 The Fleet in Production/10 Serving at Scale/V2-10.16 Case Studies#^tbl-meta-dlrm-hybrid-hardware|Table 27]] connects that placement to the end-to-end latency budget.

| **Component**          | **Hardware** | **Latency** | **Throughput** |
|:-----------------------|:------------:|:-----------:|:--------------:|
| **Embedding lookup**   |  CPU + SSD   |    2 ms     | 50M lookups/s  |
| **Feature processing** |     CPU      |    1 ms     |   10M ops/s    |
| **Dense ranking**      |     GPU      |   1.5 ms    |  100K infs/s   |

Table 27: **Illustrative Hybrid CPU/GPU Architecture**: Illustrative hardware-workload mapping for the recommendation scenario. CPU + SSD handles the bandwidth-bound sparse path; CPU handles low-arithmetic-intensity feature processing; GPU handles the compute-dense ranking forward pass. The component budgets exclude queueing and response delivery; their sum alone does not establish the p99 target.

^tbl-meta-dlrm-hybrid-hardware

The serving lesson is that recommendation latency is often dominated by embedding lookup rather than dense model inference. Feature-parallel batching and a hybrid CPU-GPU architecture match the hardware to the sparse and dense halves of the workload, which sets up a contrast with GPT-style API serving where request variance and autoregressive state dominate.

### A Multi-Model Generation API

^sec-inference-scale-openai-api-infrastructure-a65e

A multi-model generation API binds on request variance, because short prompts, long-context requests, and different model sizes share capacity while users expect predictable TTFT. The platform serves millions of requests per hour under a time-to-first-token target below $2\text{ s}$, with models from billions to hundreds of billions of parameters and contexts up to 128K tokens. The serving stack separates routing, scheduling, cache management, and shard groups:

    API Gateway → Rate Limiting → Request Router → Model Cluster → Response Streaming

                                         |
                                         v
                                  +-------------+
                                  | Model Pool  |
                                  | +---------+ |
                                  | | Large   | |
                                  | | 8×H100  | |
                                  | +---------+ |
                                  | +---------+ |
                                  | | Smaller | |
                                  | | 4×A100  | |
                                  | +---------+ |
                                  +-------------+

The main design decision is to prevent long prompts from blocking decode service for everyone else. An Orca-style design uses continuous batching to refill completed slots. Chunked prefill limits interference from long prompts by interleaving prompt chunks with ongoing generation. The TTFT target must be validated across prompt lengths; admitting a 128K-token prompt does not imply that it meets the short-request latency target. Slot reuse and long-prompt interference are separate concerns. Continuous batching addresses the first and chunking bounds the second, so neither mechanism alone establishes the utilization or tail latency the API will see.

The tensor-parallel degree must satisfy weight and KV-cache capacity while leaving enough latency budget for communication. This scenario allocates eight H100 GPUs per large-model shard group connected via high-speed NVLink for intra-node communication, with consistent hashing at the gateway to maintain session affinity and enable compatible cross-turn KV cache reuse. The eight-device allocation is a design choice to validate against the model, precision, and admitted context lengths, not a requirement of GPT-class models in general.

To prevent noisy neighbors from exhausting capacity, the illustrative API applies hierarchical rate limiting across multiple granularities: per-API-key request arrival limits, per-API-key token-per-minute consumption limits, organization-level capacity quotas, and global model capacity ceilings. Within those ceilings, capacity can also shift between models on queue depth, subject to model-loading delay and each service’s minimum allocation, using the autoscaling signals of [[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/03 The Fleet in Production/10 Serving at Scale/V2-10.14 Autoscaling and Capacity Control#^sec-inference-scale-autoscaling-capacity-control|section 10.13]].

LLM APIs are governed by variance control. Continuous batching reuses execution slots, prefix caching avoids compatible repeated work, and multi-tier rate limiting bounds admitted demand. Their combined effect on latency still depends on prompt scheduling and available capacity. Search ranking faces a different shape of the same problem. Many cheaper models must cooperate under one strict deadline.

### Search Ranking Under a Deadline

^sec-inference-scale-google-search-ranking-aab2

The search-ranking scenario binds on cascade cost, because each query coordinates many smaller models for query understanding, document relevance, and result ranking under one end-to-end budget rather than serving one large model. The system processes billions of searches per day under a target below $200\text{ ms}$ while evaluating thousands of candidate documents per query, so screening candidates with cheaper models reserves the expensive ranking budget for a smaller set.

To meet this latency budget while evaluating thousands of documents, the ranking cascade in [[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/03 The Fleet in Production/10 Serving at Scale/V2-10.16 Case Studies#^fig-ranking-cascade|figure 26]] screens candidate pools across four progressively expressive tiers. Early lexical and approximate-nearest-neighbor (ANN) embedding retrievers discard 99 percent of candidates within a 10 ms budget, reserving compute-intensive cross-attention transformers for the final top 100 items. This saving creates a recall constraint. A relevant document discarded upstream cannot be recovered by a more accurate downstream ranker, so each stage’s candidate budget must be evaluated for retained relevant items as well as latency.

↩︎ The vector indexes behind the L0 retrieval stage, and the storage cost of keeping them fast enough for a serving deadline, are established in **[[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/01 The Fleet/04 Data Storage/V2-04.10 Storage for Serving#^sec-data-storage-retrieval-infrastructure|Retrieval indexes]]**.

![[90 Assets/Volume II/10 Inference/Ranking Cascade 72bdb0fb23.svg]]

Figure 26: **Ranking Cascade Architecture**: To optimize latency and cost, search and recommendation systems use a cascade of increasingly complex models. Early stages (Retrieval) filter millions of candidates down to thousands using fast, cheap heuristics. Later stages use expensive, high-precision models only on the most promising candidates.

^fig-ranking-cascade

The central design decision is progressive refinement through a ranking cascade rather than one expensive model applied to every candidate. Each stage of the cascade spends a larger latency budget on a smaller candidate set ([[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/03 The Fleet in Production/10 Serving at Scale/V2-10.16 Case Studies#^tbl-google-ranking-cascade|table 28]]), so the expensive L3 transformer is affordable only because L0 through L2 have already cut the candidates by four orders of magnitude.

| **Stage**            |    **Model complexity**     |   **Candidates**   | **Latency budget** |
|:---------------------|:---------------------------:|:------------------:|:------------------:|
| **L0 (Retrieval)**   | Lexical and ANN embeddings  | 1,000,000 → 10,000 |       10 ms        |
| **L1 (First pass)**  |      Two-tower or GBDT      |   10,000 → 1,000   |       20 ms        |
| **L2 (Second pass)** |       Deep MLP (DLRM)       |    1,000 → 100     |       50 ms        |
| **L3 (Final rank)**  | Cross-attention transformer |      100 → 10      |       100 ms       |

Table 28: **Illustrative Ranking Cascade**: Each stage evaluates fewer candidates with a more expensive model. The final stage has the largest latency allocation in this scenario; candidate reduction makes that expense affordable.

^tbl-google-ranking-cascade

The cascade reduces final L3 evaluations by 10,000× compared to running L3 on all candidates. If the stage budgets are treated as additive model work and L3 cost scales linearly with candidate count, the L3 allocation implies 1 ms per candidate. Applying L3 to the full million-candidate set would then consume about 1000 s of model work, while the cascade budget sums to 180 ms. The resulting reduction is roughly 5,600× under these assumptions. This accounting estimate is not a measured latency speedup; batching and parallel execution can change elapsed time without changing the candidate count.

A deadline-constrained ranking ensemble can use speculative execution when it has a validated fallback-quality policy. Here, speculation means launching candidate ranking work in parallel under a deadline, not draft-token verification for LLM decoding. The final model starts on predicted inputs, such as cached or default scores, in place of the earlier models’ outputs, and its speculative result is used only if the actual outputs miss the deadline:

    # Instead of sequential:
    #   q1 = model1(query)
    #   q2 = model2(query)
    #   q3 = model3(query, q1, q2)

    # Speculative parallel:
    async_q1 = async model1(query)
    async_q2 = async model2(query)
    async_q3 = async model3(query, predicted_q1, predicted_q2)

    # Use actual results if they arrive in time, otherwise use speculative

Each sub-request carries an explicit remaining-time budget, and workers order their local queues by deadline proximity rather than arrival order. When a queued sub-request cannot complete before its budget expires, the worker returns a cached or default score immediately instead of timing out the parent query:

    Worker queue: [Doc1: 50 ms left] [Doc2: 30 ms left] [Doc3: 80 ms left]
                                          ↑ Process first

    If deadline will be missed:
      Return cached/default result rather than timing out

The serving lesson is that ranking cascades reduce cost by spending expensive models only on a shrinking candidate set, at the risk of losing relevant items upstream. Deadline propagation and priority scheduling expose the remaining time budget; meeting it also requires sufficient capacity and a validated fallback when work cannot finish. The next case adds a freshness constraint that deadline scheduling alone cannot solve.

### Multimodal Recommendation and Freshness

^sec-inference-scale-tiktok-multimodal-recommendation-91e9

The multimodal recommendation scenario binds on freshness under a tight ranking SLO: video understanding is expensive, new content arrives continuously, and user modeling remains online. The system ranks millions of videos per second under a p99 target below $50\text{ ms}$ while ingesting millions of new videos daily across video, audio, text, and interaction signals, which is why online and offline work must separate. The serving stack separates content and user paths:

    User request → User embedding → Candidate videos → Video understanding → Ranking

                        |                  |                    |
                        v                  v                    v
                 User Tower          Video Cache          Vision Models
               (Transformer)        (Pre-computed)        (On-demand)

The scenario separates user understanding (online) from content understanding (offline) through a two-tower architecture with caching.[[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/03 The Fleet in Production/10 Serving at Scale/V2-10.21 Footnotes#^fn9|⁹]] The user tower runs online on every request, while the video tower refreshes in hourly offline batches ([[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/03 The Fleet in Production/10 Serving at Scale/V2-10.16 Case Studies#^tbl-tiktok-two-tower-updates|table 29]]).

| **Tower**       | **Update frequency** | **Latency** | **Compute**  |
|:----------------|:--------------------:|:-----------:|:------------:|
| **User tower**  |      Real-time       |    5 ms     | GPU (online) |
| **Video tower** |        Hourly        |     N/A     | GPU (batch)  |

Table 29: **Illustrative Two-Tower Update Cadence**: User and video towers run on different cadences: user features change second-to-second and must run inference online; video features change much more slowly and are precomputed in hourly batches. This asymmetric scheduling is the design that takes vision inference out of the critical path for almost every request.

^tbl-tiktok-two-tower-updates

Video embeddings are precomputed and cached, eliminating vision inference from the critical path for most requests. A newly uploaded video without a cached embedding needs an explicit policy: process it before admitting it to recommendations, serve a validated fallback, or run vision inference on demand if the request budget permits. The hourly refresh cadence alone does not meet the shorter ingestion targets below; those targets require a separate path for new content.

The online path below separates candidate generation from a later dense reranker. Its user-tower budget is distinct from the reranker budget:

    User features → CPU preprocessing (1 ms)
                 → Embedding lookup (2 ms, CPU+DRAM)
                 → User tower (5 ms, GPU)
                 → Dense reranking (10 ms, GPU)
                 → Response formatting (1 ms)

New uploads enter an ingestion queue with priority tiers, so time-sensitive content is embedded within minutes and bulk imports absorb hours of slack, although priority only changes who waits when ingestion capacity falls short. The modality embeddings (video, audio, text) are combined late by a small fusion model, so updating one modality’s encoder requires a compatibility check and, when its embedding contract changes, regeneration of the cached embeddings.

The serving lesson is that freshness requires separating online and offline work. Two-tower architectures make aggressive caching possible because item embeddings can be refreshed asynchronously, while priority-based processing spends the freshest compute where it has the largest user impact.

### Cross-Cutting Observations

^sec-inference-scale-crosscutting-observations-742e

Trying to serve LLM token generation, sparse recommendation ranking, and multimodal retrieval through one generic model server forces every workload to pay for scheduling and memory abstractions it does not need. Across these cases, the common pattern is specialization rather than a universal serving stack. Embedding and retrieval stay separate from ranking and generation, hardware is matched to each stage, caches accept bounded staleness, candidate sets shrink before expensive work, and deadlines propagate so quality-latency trade-offs stay explicit under pressure. [[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/03 The Fleet in Production/10 Serving at Scale/V2-10.16 Case Studies#^tbl-serving-architecture-dimensions|Table 30]] distills the same choices along five design axes for the three workload families the cases cover. Its entries are candidate combinations for the illustrated constraint regimes, not prescriptions, because a model class alone does not fix a scheduler or topology, which also depend on model size, request mix, available hardware, and service targets.

| **Dimension**  |      **Vision/embedding**       |       **LLM (autoregressive)**        | **Recommendation**                    |
|:---------------|:-------------------------------:|:-------------------------------------:|:--------------------------------------|
| **Batching**   | Static/dynamic (uniform inputs) |     Continuous (variable outputs)     | Feature-parallel (sharded embeddings) |
| **Memory**     |   Preallocated (predictable)    |       Paged (variable KV cache)       | Distributed (embedding tables)        |
| **Scheduling** |       FCFS (uniform cost)       |      Preemptive (high variance)       | Priority-aware (SLO tiers)            |
| **Topology**   |       Single-GPU replicas       |       Tensor/pipeline parallel        | Hybrid CPU-GPU sharding               |
| **State**      |            Stateless            | Request state; optional session cache | Stateless or session features         |

Table 30: **Serving Architecture Dimensions by Workload Type**: The rows identify candidate design choices along five architectural dimensions. The workload’s state, scheduling, and hardware constraints guide selection; the service contract determines whether a combination supplies useful capacity.

^tbl-serving-architecture-dimensions

The case studies show why serving architectures must be adapted to their workloads. Applying conventional web-service assumptions to GPU-bound systems creates the fallacies and pitfalls examined next.

**Self-Check: Question**

1.  The reference 70B replica’s constructed batch-one decode step is 85 ms: 34 ms GEMM, 20.4 ms attention, 10.2 ms element-wise work, 10.2 ms AllReduce, and 10.2 ms launch and runtime overhead. INT4 weights cut only the GEMM interval, by 45 percent, leaving 69.7 ms. Why does this still miss a 50 ms TPOT target?

    1.  INT4 weights halve memory bandwidth, so the other intervals lengthen to absorb the saving
    2.  The bandwidth-only floor of about 5.2 ms already exceeds what INT4 can recover, so no precision change can help
    3.  The unchanged non-GEMM intervals still total 51 ms, so reducing GEMM alone by the assumed amount cannot reach the target
    4.  The TPOT target applies to prefill, and INT4 weights do not change prefill time

2.  After fusion and graph replay, the reference replica’s decode step is 49.6 ms. Adding speculative decoding with a 4 ms draft and about 3.5 emitted tokens per round brings the average time per output token to about 15 ms. Why can the endpoint still miss a 50 ms time-between-tokens (TBT) target?

3.  In an illustrative search-ranking cascade (L0: 1M to 10k in 10 ms; L1: 10k to 1k in 20 ms; L2: 1k to 100 in 50 ms; L3: 100 to 10 in 100 ms), what follows under a simplified additive candidate-work model?

    1.  It runs L3 on all 1,000,000 candidates using asynchronous DMA transfers without CPU involvement.
    2.  It replaces neural networks entirely with BM25 keyword matching across all stages.
    3.  It dynamically recompiles transformer weights at each stage using TensorRT-LLM.
    4.  It cuts final expensive L3 evaluations by $10{,}000\times$ (from 1,000,000 to 100), reducing total candidate-scoring compute by $\approx 5{,}600\times$ within a 180 ms budget.

4.  Compare the binding bottlenecks and serving topologies of the illustrative embedding-heavy recommendation scenario and autoregressive LLM API scenario.

5.  True or False: In the illustrative two-tower video recommendation scenario, both the user tower and the content tower must execute synchronously for every swipe.

6.  Which combination of architectural design choices addresses variable-length autoregressive generation and reusable session context in the chapter’s LLM serving scenario?

    1.  Continuous batching, paged memory management, preemptive scheduling, and stateful routing
    2.  Static batching, preallocated memory management, first-come-first-served (FCFS) scheduling, and stateless routing
    3.  Feature-parallel batching, distributed memory tables, priority-aware scheduling, and stateless routing
    4.  Streaming frame batching, pinned buffer allocation, non-preemptive scheduling, and edge-only topology
