# V2-11.17 Footnotes

[[Home|Home]] · [[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/Volume 2 Overview|Volume Overview]] · [[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/03 The Fleet in Production/11 Edge Intelligence/V2-11 Edge Intelligence|V2-11 Edge Intelligence]]

^footnotes

<sup>1</sup> **Differential privacy**: A mathematical framework that bounds information leakage by adding calibrated noise to computations. In edge learning, that noise becomes a systems cost because noisier updates usually require more rounds or more data to reach the same utility.

^fn1

<sup>2</sup> **Gboard (2017)**: One of the first widely reported commercial federated learning deployments at mobile scale. Published reports showed that keyboard models could improve from aggregated on-device updates while raw typed text remained local. The systems consequence is durable. Compressed updates and privacy mechanisms make cross-fleet learning possible over constrained mobile networks without centralizing raw keystrokes.

^fn2

<sup>3</sup> **Wake-word detection**: An always-on detector incurs inference cost throughout the listening period, even when no keyword occurs. Published keyword-spotting results on dedicated neural processors, plotted in [[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/03 The Fleet in Production/11 Edge Intelligence/V2-11.04 Design Constraints#^fig-edge-inference-landscape|figure 6]], spend on the order of 30 to 160 ${\mu}$J per evaluation, so the energy budget is set by how often the detector evaluates over the listening period as much as by the cost of any one evaluation. Local personalization adds a separate update cost and earns its place only if evaluation shows an acceptable trade-off between missed keywords, false activations, and energy use.

^fn3

<sup>4</sup> **Non-IID (not independent and identically distributed)**: Samples may be dependent or drawn from different distributions; in a device fleet, user-specific label and feature distributions can make local updates conflict. Such distributions can degrade federated averaging relative to independent and identically distributed (i.i.d.) baselines ([[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/03 The Fleet in Production/11 Edge Intelligence/V2-11.16 Source References#^ref-zhao2018federated|Zhao et al. 2018]]). The observed failure determines whether to change local work, aggregation, or personalization rather than prescribing one remedy for every non-IID population.

^fn4

<sup>5</sup> **Arduino Nano 33 BLE Sense**: With 256 KB SRAM, roughly 65,536× smaller than a flagship smartphone’s 16 GB, a single $224{\times}224{\times}3$ RGB image occupies ~151 KB and consumes roughly 60 percent of available memory; activation and gradient storage add several more live tensors, and optimizer state can push the training footprint far beyond the inference footprint. A convolutional neural network (CNN) layer can therefore exceed SRAM during backpropagation even when its weights fit. Quantization can reduce this footprint, but feasibility also depends on input size, saved activations, and which parameters are updated.

^fn5

<sup>6</sup> **STM32F4 microcontroller**: With 192 KB SRAM, a 168 MHz clock speed, and a single-precision floating-point unit, the main constraint is not the absence of floating point but the tiny memory and energy envelope. A dense layer with 1,000 input features and 10 hidden units requires approximately 40 KB for weights and biases in FP32, consuming about 20 percent of total SRAM before accounting for activations or gradients. Local updates must therefore fit around the device’s primary workload and power budget, and INT8 or fixed-point kernels remain preferable when accuracy allows.

^fn6

<sup>7</sup> **Quantization-aware training (QAT)**: Simulates quantization during training to adapt the model to low-precision inference ([[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/03 The Fleet in Production/11 Edge Intelligence/V2-11.16 Source References#^ref-jacob2018|Jacob et al. 2018]]; [[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/03 The Fleet in Production/11 Edge Intelligence/V2-11.16 Source References#^ref-krishnamoorthi2018quantizing|Krishnamoorthi 2018]]). Fake-quantization operations commonly retain floating-point training tensors, so QAT does not itself imply integer backward kernels or a smaller training-state budget. On-device adaptation must separately establish supported update arithmetic, state storage, and measured energy costs.

^fn7

<sup>8</sup> **SGD (stochastic gradient descent)**: Plain SGD keeps parameters and gradients, two parameter-sized arrays where Adam keeps four, so its lower state cost must still be weighed against the updates needed to reach the quality target.

^fn8

<sup>9</sup> **Apple Neural Engine**: Apple’s dedicated neural accelerator, first shipped in the A11 Bionic in 2017; its rating growth through the A17 Pro is tracked in [[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/03 The Fleet in Production/11 Edge Intelligence/V2-11.04 Design Constraints#^fig-edge-npu-tops|figure 5]]. Which of an update’s operators actually reach it, rather than falling back to the CPU or GPU, is the runtime question worked through in [[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/03 The Fleet in Production/11 Edge Intelligence/V2-11.04 Design Constraints#^sec-edge-intelligence-mobile-ai-accelerator-optimization-67fe|section 11.3.4.3]].

^fn9

<sup>10</sup> **Near-memory computing**: Places processing elements adjacent to or within memory arrays to cut the data movement that often dominates arithmetic energy ([[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/03 The Fleet in Production/11 Edge Intelligence/V2-11.16 Source References#^ref-horowitz2014|Horowitz 2014]]; [[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/03 The Fleet in Production/11 Edge Intelligence/V2-11.16 Source References#^ref-kwon2019|Kwon and Rhu 2018]]), which could make write-heavy on-device backpropagation more practical.

^fn10

<sup>11</sup> **LoRA (low-rank adaptation)**: Hu et al. ([[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/03 The Fleet in Production/11 Edge Intelligence/V2-11.16 Source References#^ref-hu2021lora|2021]]) learn rank-${r}$ decomposition matrices ${\mathbf{A}}$ and ${\mathbf{B}}$ instead of updating full weight matrices, reporting a 10,000$\times$ reduction in trainable parameters for their GPT-3 comparison. That reduction does not apply to the full model footprint: a model with 7B parameters has about a 14 GB FP16 weight footprint before gradients, optimizer state, and activations. Even at rank 16, adapter storage depends on the dimensions and number of adapted matrices, and deployment must still accommodate the frozen base model.

^fn11

<sup>12</sup> **MFCCs (mel-frequency cepstral coefficients)**: For a 320-sample audio window (20 ms at 16 kHz), 16-bit samples occupy 640 bytes, while 12–13 coefficients stored at 32 bits each occupy approximately 50 bytes. The roughly 13$\times$ reduction compares one waveform window with one feature vector; overlapping windows, additional features, and metadata change the storage ratio for a complete recording. Replay capacity depends on that complete representation, and keyword detection quality must be evaluated separately from byte savings.

^fn12

<sup>13</sup> **Wireless upload asymmetry**: Federated updates use the uplink, whose available throughput can differ substantially from the downlink and vary with coverage and contention. Transfer time depends on encoded update size and effective uplink goodput; energy also depends on radio state and transfer duration. Low-power links may add duty-cycle constraints. Compression is useful when these costs bind the update budget, but its necessity depends on the payload, link, and permitted synchronization interval.

^fn13

<sup>14</sup> **Gradient sparsification**: Selecting the largest 1–10 percent of gradient elements by magnitude reduces transmitted value count by 10–100$\times$ before coordinate indices and other metadata are included. Sparsified SGD and deep gradient compression retain untransmitted residuals for later updates rather than assuming that small components can be discarded permanently ([[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/03 The Fleet in Production/11 Edge Intelligence/V2-11.16 Source References#^ref-stich2018sparsified|Stich et al. 2018]]; [[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/03 The Fleet in Production/11 Edge Intelligence/V2-11.16 Source References#^ref-lin2018deep|Lin et al. 2018]]). Wire-byte savings depend on the encoding, while convergence depends on the residual handling, optimizer, and compression schedule; a value-count reduction alone establishes neither.

^fn14

<sup>15</sup> **MAML (model-agnostic meta-learning)**: Finn et al. ([[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/03 The Fleet in Production/11 Edge Intelligence/V2-11.16 Source References#^ref-finn2017model|2017]]) optimize for a model initialization from which one or a few gradient steps can yield good performance on a new task, with experiments including one-shot and few-shot settings. Meta-training is more expensive than ordinary finetuning because it differentiates through the adaptation process, but deployment adaptation can be limited to a small number of local updates. This makes MAML relevant for edge scenarios where inference compute is cheap but training data is scarce.

^fn15

<sup>16</sup> **Model inversion attack**: Reconstructs training data from model outputs or gradients by optimizing inputs that maximize confidence scores ([[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/03 The Fleet in Production/11 Edge Intelligence/V2-11.16 Source References#^ref-fredrikson2015|Fredrikson et al. 2015]]; [[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/03 The Fleet in Production/11 Edge Intelligence/V2-11.16 Source References#^ref-zhu2019deep|Zhu et al. 2019]]). In federated learning, gradient updates can leak more information than model outputs alone because they expose training-step information directly. Defenses such as gradient perturbation, secure aggregation, and differential privacy add protocol, utility, communication, or compute trade-offs whose magnitude depends on the threat model and deployment; **[[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/04 The Responsible Fleet/13 Security & Privacy/V2-13.09 Comprehensive Defense Architectures#^sec-security-privacy-gradient-inversion-risks-d2c1|Gradient leakage and privacy-preserving computation]]** develops the threat model for these attacks.

^fn16

<sup>17</sup> **Secure aggregation**: Cryptographic protocol where client pairs exchange random masks that cancel in the server-side sum, revealing only the aggregate gradient without exposing individual contributions ([[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/03 The Fleet in Production/11 Edge Intelligence/V2-11.16 Source References#^ref-bonawitz2017|Bonawitz et al. 2017]]). Production-scale federated systems such as Gboard combine secure aggregation with eligibility checks, drop-out handling, and round scheduling ([[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/03 The Fleet in Production/11 Edge Intelligence/V2-11.16 Source References#^ref-bonawitz2019towards|Bonawitz et al. 2019]]). The communication expansion and minimum-participation requirements depend on protocol parameters and the security model, so aggregation systems must wait for enough eligible devices before releasing a round.

^fn17

<sup>18</sup> **Straggler problem**: In data center training, stragglers slow synchronous progress; in federated learning, the problem is amplified by heterogeneous hardware, intermittent connectivity, and eligibility windows ([[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/03 The Fleet in Production/11 Edge Intelligence/V2-11.16 Source References#^ref-bonawitz2019towards|Bonawitz et al. 2019]]; [[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/03 The Fleet in Production/11 Edge Intelligence/V2-11.16 Source References#^ref-kairouz2021|Kairouz and McMahan 2021]]). Solutions like asynchronous aggregation and bounded staleness (ignoring updates computed against a model more than a fixed number of rounds old) improve throughput but introduce a fairness trade-off: aggressive straggler mitigation biases models toward users with high-end devices, potentially degrading performance for the most constrained users who need personalization most.

^fn18

<sup>19</sup> **EWC (elastic weight consolidation)**: Estimates each weight’s importance with a diagonal approximation to the Fisher information, a measure of how sensitive the earlier tasks’ loss is to that weight, and uses those estimates to weight a penalty on changes from previously learned parameters ([[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/03 The Fleet in Production/11 Edge Intelligence/V2-11.16 Source References#^ref-kirkpatrick2017|Kirkpatrick et al. 2017]]). In addition to current parameters, a basic implementation stores importance scores and reference parameter values. Retaining separate penalties for earlier tasks can increase that state further. Compared with replay, EWC trades stored examples for parameter-sized statistics; which uses less memory depends on model size, retained history, precision, and the penalty implementation.

^fn19

<sup>20</sup> **Microcontroller power budget**: In an illustrative operating point, 10 mW of active training for 10 seconds consumes 0.1 J. The number of updates that fit that session requires a measured time or energy per update. Battery lifetime additionally depends on usable capacity under the actual load, sleep power, radio activity, conversion losses, and duty cycle; nameplate capacity alone does not establish continuous-training lifetime.

^fn20

<sup>21</sup> **Byzantine fault tolerance in federated learning (FL)**: Named after the Byzantine Generals Problem ([[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/03 The Fleet in Production/11 Edge Intelligence/V2-11.16 Source References#^ref-lamport1982byzantine|Lamport et al. 1982]]), this property enables useful aggregation despite malicious or faulty participants; how many faulty participants a rule such as Krum or trimmed mean tolerates depends on that rule’s own conditions, which **[[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/02 Distributed ML/07 Fault Tolerance/V2-07.04 Failure Taxonomy#^sec-fault-tolerance-reliability-reliability-byzantine-failures-8a24|Byzantine failures]]** states. These defenses can increase communication and compute overhead because they require extra validation, robust statistics, or redundant participation.

^fn21
