# V2-11.05 Model Adaptation

[[Home|Home]] · [[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/Volume 2 Overview|Volume Overview]] · [[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/03 The Fleet in Production/11 Edge Intelligence/V2-11 Edge Intelligence|V2-11 Edge Intelligence]]

^sec-edge-intelligence-model-adaptation-6a82

The 10M-parameter model of [[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/03 The Fleet in Production/11 Edge Intelligence/V2-11.04 Design Constraints#^fig-training-memory-amplifier|figure 3]] is an inference candidate for a smartwatch with 500 MB of total system memory, because its FP32 weights occupy 40 MB. Training every parameter would need the full 360 MB stack, most of the watch’s memory before its operating system and foreground application take their share. The weight footprint alone does not establish inference fit; activation buffers, runtime workspace, and foreground reserve must also fit. Full-model training leaves much less room for that remaining state. The system therefore trains only a compact, strategically placed parameter set and, where it can, also shrinks the activations that the backward pass must keep. Resource-efficient model adaptation then determines both the local work a device can complete and the shared update format the fleet can aggregate.

The engineering choice balances adaptation expressivity against resource consumption. Full fine-tuning exposes all parameters to learning and can fit sufficiently small models; retaining a fixed model can be adequate when local context already supplies the needed personalization. Between these choices, selective updates offer smaller training state. The device resource envelope determines which candidates fit: a wearable-class device with 1 MB RAM has a different budget from a smartphone with 8 GB. The local variation then determines how much trainable capacity is useful. Bias updates may capture calibration changes, while shifts requiring new features need a richer update space. Runtime support, inference cost, shared-update compatibility, and rollback determine whether the candidate can enter the deployed system.

The selection of adaptation techniques follows this matching logic, from weight freezing ([[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/03 The Fleet in Production/11 Edge Intelligence/V2-11.05 Model Adaptation#^sec-edge-intelligence-weight-freezing-3407|section 11.4.2]]) to structured and sparse updates ([[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/03 The Fleet in Production/11 Edge Intelligence/V2-11.05 Model Adaptation#^sec-edge-intelligence-sparse-updates-879b|section 11.4.4]]). Their order of presentation is not a universal ranking of cost or expressivity; placement, update size, and runtime support determine those properties. Building on the resource accounting in [[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/03 The Fleet in Production/11 Edge Intelligence/V2-11.04 Design Constraints#^sec-edge-intelligence-design-constraints-c776|section 11.3]], the target is the smallest update space that captures the local variation: *preserve what works globally, adapt what matters locally*. Weight freezing restricts training to selected existing parameters, such as bias terms or final layers, whereas structured updates add low-rank or residual components to a frozen base and sparse updates select existing parameters according to their adaptation value and measured cost. Each choice must preserve enough useful capacity while fitting the device’s update budget.

### Transfer as the Premise

^sec-edge-intelligence-knowledge-transfer-adaptation-foundation

All three adaptation families work only because the device does not learn its representation from scratch. Training a feature extractor from random initialization on a few dozen noisy phone interactions would overfit immediately and exhaust the energy allowance before learning anything generalizable. Instead, every local update that fits an edge budget relies on knowledge transfer, in which a cloud-pretrained model supplies representations useful for a new task or domain. Reusing those representations reduces what the device must learn from local examples, and the benefit depends on what the tasks share. A model pretrained in the cloud can adapt with limited local updates when its representations retain the distinctions the new task requires. If a new input modality or objective requires features the base model does not provide, a small local update may be insufficient.

Transfer is therefore the premise of the adaptation methods that follow. When pretrained representations carry the distinctions a personal typing vocabulary, an individual gait, or a drifting sensor needs, bias-only updates, adapters, and head-only training (which retrains only the final classification layer) can learn a small correction while freezing most of the network. When that premise fails, head-only adaptation cannot recover distinctions absent from its fixed features. Earlier-layer adapters or broader updates may learn them, but their added state, computation, and data requirements must fit the device budget.

### Weight Freezing

^sec-edge-intelligence-weight-freezing-3407

When pretrained features already capture the structure of the task, the most direct way to shrink ${M_{\text{train}}}$ in [[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/03 The Fleet in Production/11 Edge Intelligence/V2-11.04 Design Constraints#^eq-edge-adaptation-peak-memory|equation 1]] is to freeze the majority of a model’s parameters and adapt only a minimal subset. **Bias-only adaptation** holds all weights fixed and updates only the bias terms (scalar offsets applied after linear or convolutional layers). The constraint reduces trainable parameters by 100–1000$\times$, removes the weight gradients and their optimizer state from ${M_{\text{train}}}$, and helps mitigate overfitting when training data is sparse or noisy.

Consider a standard neural network layer:

$$
{\mathbf{y}}= {\mathbf{W}}{\mathbf{x}}+ {\mathbf{b}}
$$

 where ${\mathbf{W}}\in \mathbb{R}^{{m}\times {n}}$ is the weight matrix, ${\mathbf{b}}\in \mathbb{R}^{m}$ is the bias vector, and ${\mathbf{x}}\in \mathbb{R}^{n}$ is the input. In full training, gradients are computed and applied to both ${\mathbf{W}}$ and ${\mathbf{b}}$. In bias-only adaptation, the system freezes the weight tensor (${\Delta}{\mathbf{W}}= {\mathbf{0}}$) and computes gradients exclusively for the bias:

$$
{\mathbf{W}}\leftarrow {\mathbf{W}}, \quad {\mathbf{b}}\leftarrow {\mathbf{b}}- {\eta}\frac{\partial {\mathcal{L}}}{\partial {\mathbf{b}}}
$$

This reduces the number of stored gradients and optimizer states, enabling training to proceed under memory-constrained conditions. The smaller trainable state helps only if the device also supports the arithmetic and activation schedule required by the update. In the bias-only setup of [[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/03 The Fleet in Production/11 Edge Intelligence/V2-11.05 Model Adaptation#^lst-bias-adaptation|listing 1]], all convolutional and fully connected weights remain frozen, and only the bias terms adapt to local data.

``` python
# Freeze all parameters
for name, param in model.named_parameters():
    param.requires_grad = False

# Enable gradients for bias parameters only
for name, param in model.named_parameters():
    if "bias" in name:
        param.requires_grad = True
```

Listing 1: **Bias-Only Adaptation**: Freezes model parameters except for biases to reduce memory usage and allow on-device learning.

^lst-bias-adaptation

This pattern restricts optimizer updates to bias terms. What it saves, and what it does not, follows from the two derivatives of the layer above. The weight gradient $\partial {\mathcal{L}}/\partial {\mathbf{W}}= (\partial {\mathcal{L}}/\partial {\mathbf{y}})\,{\mathbf{x}}^T$ needs the layer’s saved input ${\mathbf{x}}$, whereas the bias gradient $\partial {\mathcal{L}}/\partial {\mathbf{b}}= \partial {\mathcal{L}}/\partial {\mathbf{y}}$ needs only the gradient arriving from above. A frozen linear layer therefore no longer has to keep its input for its own sake. Reaching a bias in an earlier layer, however, still sends the gradient back through every later layer. Each nonlinearity or normalization on that path needs its own saved state (a ReLU needs at least the mask of which inputs were positive). Freezing parameters thus shrinks ${M_{\text{train}}}$ without eliminating backward computation or all saved activation state. This is valuable when adapting pretrained models to user-specific or device-local data where the core representations remain relevant but require calibration.

Tiny Transfer Learning (TinyTL) ([[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/03 The Fleet in Production/11 Edge Intelligence/V2-11.16 Source References#^ref-cai2020tinytl|Cai et al. 2020]]) targets the activation term of [[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/03 The Fleet in Production/11 Edge Intelligence/V2-11.04 Design Constraints#^eq-edge-adaptation-peak-memory|equation 1]] as well as trainable state. It builds on the derivative argument above. Because frozen linear and convolutional layers drop their saved inputs, the remaining activation footprint comes from nonlinearities, normalization layers, and any trainable side-paths added to recover expressivity. Adding a standard residual adapter beside a frozen block would require saving that block’s full-resolution input feature map to compute the adapter’s weight gradients, undoing the memory saving. TinyTL avoids that trap with lite residual modules that downsample the input feature map and project it to a narrow channel width before applying trainable convolutions, so the backward pass caches only a small low-resolution tensor at each block alongside compact group-normalization and activation state.

Full fine-tuning (Panel A of [[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/03 The Fleet in Production/11 Edge Intelligence/V2-11.05 Model Adaptation#^fig-tinytl-architecture|figure 7]]) computes gradients for every intermediate layer weight and caches every block’s full input map, whereas TinyTL (Panel B) freezes the convolutional backbone (gray blocks) and confines parameter updates to the downsampled lite residual side-paths and bias vectors ${\mathbf{b}}$ (blue blocks). The dashed backward-pass arrow spans the full network depth in both architectures. TinyTL’s memory reduction therefore stems not from truncating the backward pass, but from replacing full-resolution saved feature maps with the much smaller inputs of the lite residual modules.

![[90 Assets/Volume II/11 Edge Intelligence/Tinytl Architecture e78d9207cf.svg]]

Figure 7: **TinyTL Adaptation Paths**: (A) Full fine-tuning updates every weight and retains the state needed for their gradients. (B) TinyTL freezes the backbone weights (gray) and trains only each block’s bias vector (${\mathbf{b}}$), small lite residual modules beside the blocks, and the classifier (blue). The backward pass still traverses every frozen block to reach those biases, so the saving comes from what must stay live for it, not from a shorter backward path; the activation and normalization choices determine that state.

^fig-tinytl-architecture

The memory reduction is useful only when the frozen backbone remains expressive enough for the downstream task. The bias terms allow for minor but meaningful shifts in model behavior, particularly for personalization tasks. When domain shift is more significant, TinyTL can optionally incorporate small residual adapters to improve expressivity, all while preserving the system’s tight memory and energy profile.

TinyTL demonstrates that reducing saved activation state can matter more than reducing the number of trainable parameters alone ([[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/03 The Fleet in Production/11 Edge Intelligence/V2-11.16 Source References#^ref-cai2020tinytl|Cai et al. 2020]]). On its image-classification transfer benchmarks, TinyTL reports training-memory savings of up to 6.5× relative to fine-tuning the full network with little accuracy loss, so the adaptation working set can shrink to roughly 15 percent of the full fine-tuning footprint. The lite residual modules that recover adaptation capacity add only about 3.8 percent memory overhead in that study. Those savings depend on the selected feature extractor, input resolution, batch size, normalization, and residual-module configuration. A saving measured on those benchmarks says nothing about whether an arbitrary frozen backbone fits into microcontroller SRAM, and when a deployed model must learn richer feature combinations without custom downsampled side-paths, it needs general structured updates beside the frozen weights.

### Structured Parameter Updates

^sec-edge-intelligence-structured-parameter-updates-f1a1

Adapting a smartphone camera pipeline to a new low-light sensor profile or a mobile language model to a user’s technical vocabulary requires recombining features across channels, a change that scalar bias offsets ${\mathbf{b}}$ cannot express while the weight matrices ${\mathbf{W}}$ remain fixed. Unfreezing ${\mathbf{W}}$ directly would restore the full gradient and optimizer arrays in ${M_{\text{train}}}$, so the system instead adds compact, structured trainable matrices beside the frozen backbone. Residual adaptation modules ([[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/03 The Fleet in Production/11 Edge Intelligence/V2-11.16 Source References#^ref-houlsby2019parameter|Houlsby et al. 2019]]) and low-rank parameterizations ([[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/03 The Fleet in Production/11 Edge Intelligence/V2-11.16 Source References#^ref-hu2021lora|Hu et al. 2021]]) implement this choice in different ways. Both restrict parameter updates to the added components, but their training cost still includes the backward path through the surrounding network. The comparison with bias-only or full fine-tuning therefore requires both a quality target and a peak-memory measurement.

#### Adapter-Based Adaptation

^sec-edge-intelligence-adapterbased-adaptation-6a9a

A common implementation involves inserting residual adapters, which are small residual bottleneck layers, between existing layers in a pretrained model. Consider a hidden representation ${\mathbf{h}}$ passed between layers. A residual adapter introduces a transformation:

$$
{\mathbf{h}'}= {\mathbf{h}}+ {A}({\mathbf{h}})
$$

 where ${A}(\cdot)$ is a trainable function, typically composed of two linear layers with a nonlinearity:

$$
{A}({\mathbf{h}}) = {\mathbf{W}}_2 \, {\sigma}({\mathbf{W}}_1 {\mathbf{h}})
$$

 with ${\mathbf{W}}_1 \in \mathbb{R}^{{r}\times {d}}$ and ${\mathbf{W}}_2 \in \mathbb{R}^{{d}\times {r}}$, where ${r}\ll {d}$. This bottleneck design ensures that only a small number of parameters are introduced per layer.

The adapters act as learnable perturbations on top of a frozen backbone. Because they are small and sparsely applied, their weights add little storage, although their saved activations and optimizer state still count against the training budget. They let the model shift its predictions in response to new inputs.

The residual bottleneck in [[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/03 The Fleet in Production/11 Edge Intelligence/V2-11.05 Model Adaptation#^lst-lowrank-adapter|listing 2]] maps onto the adapter equation term by term. Its down-projection plays the role of ${\mathbf{W}}_1$, mapping width ${d}$ to the bottleneck width ${r}$. A ReLU supplies ${\sigma}$, and the up-projection plays the role of ${\mathbf{W}}_2$, restoring width ${d}$ before the input is added back. Such modules can be implemented efficiently on edge devices, particularly when the two projection matrices are small and fixed-point representations are supported.

``` python
import torch.nn as nn

class Adapter(nn.Module):
    def __init__(self, dim, bottleneck_dim):
        super().__init__()
        # Project from full dimension to bottleneck (e.g., 768 -> 16)
        self.down = nn.Linear(
            dim, bottleneck_dim
        )  # W1: learns compression
        # Project back to original dimension (e.g., 16 -> 768)
        self.up = nn.Linear(
            bottleneck_dim, dim
        )  # W2: learns expansion
        self.activation = nn.ReLU()

    def forward(self, x):
        # Residual connection: original + nonlinear bottleneck
        # Only adapter params trained; base model frozen
        return x + self.up(self.activation(self.down(x)))
```

Listing 2: **Residual Bottleneck Adapter**: The code implements a compact adapter module with down-projection and up-projection layers, reducing trainable parameter count while enabling efficient model adaptation on edge devices.

^lst-lowrank-adapter

#### Low-Rank Techniques

^sec-edge-intelligence-lowrank-techniques-4570

Another efficient strategy is to constrain the weight update itself to low rank. Low-rank adaptation (LoRA)[[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/03 The Fleet in Production/11 Edge Intelligence/V2-11.17 Footnotes#^fn11|¹¹]] leaves a base matrix ${\mathbf{W}}_0 \in \mathbb{R}^{{m}\times {n}}$ frozen and learns its change as the product of a down-projection ${\mathbf{A}}\in \mathbb{R}^{{r}\times {n}}$ and an up-projection ${\mathbf{B}}\in \mathbb{R}^{{m}\times {r}}$, with ${r}\ll \min({m},{n})$. The adapted layer adds ${\mathbf{B}}{\mathbf{A}}{\mathbf{x}}$ to the frozen output ${\mathbf{W}}_0 {\mathbf{x}}$. Training cost follows from that structure. The update ${\Delta}{\mathbf{W}}= {\mathbf{B}}{\mathbf{A}}$ has ${r}({m}+ {n})$ trainable parameters instead of ${m} {n}$, and only those parameters carry gradients and optimizer state.

↩︎ The low-rank adapter forward pass, in which a frozen base projection is summed with a trainable down- and up-projection, is established in **[[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/03 The Fleet in Production/10 Serving at Scale/V2-10.08 Memory and Decode-Time Management#^sec-inference-multi-lora-serving|Multi-LoRA serving and batched adapter execution]]**.

The decomposition rests on a linear-algebra fact. Any matrix can be expressed as a sum of rank-one matrices through singular value decomposition. Constraining updates to low rank (typically ${r}= 4$ to $16$) captures a restricted set of variation modes while reducing parameters. For a typical transformer layer with dimensions $768{\times}768$, full fine-tuning requires updating 589,824 parameters. With rank-4 decomposition, only $768{\times}4{\times}2 =$ 6,144 parameters are updated, a 99 percent reduction. Whether this retains most of the adaptation quality depends on the task, rank, and base model; the LoRA authors report that low-rank adapters can match or approach full fine-tuning on several transformer adaptation benchmarks ([[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/03 The Fleet in Production/11 Edge Intelligence/V2-11.16 Source References#^ref-hu2021lora|Hu et al. 2021]]).

LoRA was developed for transformer models but applies to any layer built on a weight matrix. From a systems engineering perspective, LoRA makes the trainable adapter the artifact that can be stored, transmitted, scheduled, and rolled back separately from the frozen base model.

Drawn to proportion for a square layer of width ${d}$, the adapted layer shows where the saving comes from ([[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/03 The Fleet in Production/11 Edge Intelligence/V2-11.05 Model Adaptation#^fig-adapter-lora-arch|figure 8]]). Input ${\mathbf{x}}\in \mathbb{R}^{d}$ passes through the frozen ${d}{\times}{d}$ base matrix ${\mathbf{W}}_0$ and, in parallel, through a thin ${r}{\times}{d}$ down-projection ${\mathbf{A}}$ and a ${d}{\times}{r}$ up-projection ${\mathbf{B}}$. The two paths sum to ${\mathbf{h}}= {\mathbf{W}}_0 {\mathbf{x}}+ {\mathbf{B}}{\mathbf{A}}{\mathbf{x}}$. The base weights need no parameter gradients or optimizer state, although backward computation may still traverse the base path. Folding the product into the base weights (${\mathbf{W}}_0 + {\mathbf{B}}{\mathbf{A}}$) removes separate adapter arithmetic for that merged model. Numerical compatibility, any requantization, and the cost of retaining merged copies determine whether this is the appropriate serving representation.

![[90 Assets/Volume II/11 Edge Intelligence/Adapter Lora Arch 8808f36fcc.svg]]

Figure 8: **LoRA Architecture**: The input ${\mathbf{x}}$ passes through the frozen base matrix ${\mathbf{W}}_0$ and, in parallel, through a trainable low-rank path: down-projection ${\mathbf{A}}$ (${r} \times {d}$) and up-projection ${\mathbf{B}}$ (${d} \times {r}$), whose product is the low-rank update ${\Delta}{\mathbf{W}}$. Only ${\mathbf{A}}$ and ${\mathbf{B}}$ carry gradients and optimizer state. For serving, the product ${\mathbf{B}}{\mathbf{A}}$ can be folded into ${\mathbf{W}}_0$, removing the separate adapter operations when the merged representation is supported. End-to-end latency still needs measurement.

^fig-adapter-lora-arch

Consider a mobile deployment where a language model with 7B parameters has a 14 GB FP16 weight footprint, before accounting for the additional gradients, optimizer states, and activations required by full fine-tuning. That footprint exceeds smartphones with about 8 GB total memory unless the base model is quantized, partitioned, or offloaded. Choosing LoRA rank 16 restricts the update space, and this chapter’s fleet examples budget a reference adapter update of 50 MB. That figure is a communication payload, not a training-memory estimate. The device must also fit the base model, gradients, optimizer state, and live activations under its execution plan.

The smaller payload also matters under intermittent connectivity. Transferring the full FP16 model in this example requires 14 GB, while the reference adapter update is 50 MB. Transfer time depends on encoded bytes, effective link throughput, and interruptions. An adapter-only transfer provides the saving only when the receiving device already holds a compatible base model; base-version changes must be included in the communication budget. In both the residual bottleneck and LoRA, the width or rank ${r}$ sets the trainable cost, so a deployment can size it to the memory each device class leaves for learning. The device-tier framework of [[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/03 The Fleet in Production/11 Edge Intelligence/V2-11.05 Model Adaptation#^sec-edge-intelligence-performance-vs-resource-tradeoffs-2be2|section 11.4.3.4]] decides which mechanism each class receives.

#### Edge Personalization

^sec-edge-intelligence-edge-personalization-a511

Adapters support multiple personalized contexts with one frozen backbone. For an input width ${d}$, a residual bottleneck of width ${r}$ uses $2d{r}$ matrix parameters, plus ${r}+{d}$ biases when both projections include them. A deployment with several adapters must sum this count over their insertion sites; rank or bottleneck width alone does not determine the total. Stored bytes then equal parameter count times bytes per stored value. With ${d}= 768$ and ${r}= 16$, one adapter holds 25,360 parameters, about 50.7 KB in FP16; one adapter after each of 12 blocks totals 304,320 parameters, roughly 0.6 MB. During training, gradients, optimizer state, and saved activations add to that storage footprint. A device can swap context-specific modules while sharing the much larger base model, provided it can hold the base and active adaptation state together.

This **adapter switching** pattern lets a smartphone select context-specific behavior without replacing the entire backbone. When the user enters a low-light environment, the system can load a compatible adapter into the vision pipeline; selecting an existing adapter does not itself require learning. The modularity extends naturally to federated learning: transmitting only the adapted parameters can reduce upload traffic relative to a full-model update, with savings determined by the total adapter count and encoding. Residual-adapter studies show that small trainable modules can adapt a shared visual backbone across multiple visual domains ([[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/03 The Fleet in Production/11 Edge Intelligence/V2-11.16 Source References#^ref-rebuffi2017learning|Rebuffi et al. 2017]]), making them a useful design point between personalization quality and system resources. Freezing the backbone preserves its parameters, while adapters provide a bounded place for user- or context-specific updates. The resulting model can still suffer catastrophic forgetting, the loss of previously learned behavior after new updates (defined in [[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/03 The Fleet in Production/11 Edge Intelligence/V2-11.06 Data Efficiency#^sec-edge-intelligence-experience-replay-737e|section 11.5.2]]).

**Napkin Math 11.2: The hidden cost of personalization**

**Problem**: A team is deploying a 10M parameter vision model to a smartphone with support for 10 different “User Contexts” (Home, Office, Car, etc.). If a full fine-tuned model requires 40 MB, how much storage does using residual adapters save instead?

**Math**:

1.  **Full fine-tuning**: 10 contexts $\times$ 40 MB = 400 MB.
2.  **Adapter approach**: (1 $\times$ 40 MB backbone) + (10 $\times$ 200 KB adapters) $\approx$ 42 MB.
3.  **Storage savings**: 400 MB/42 MB $\approx$ 9.5× total device storage reduction.
4.  **Per-context efficiency**: 40 MB/0.2 MB = 200×.

**Systems insight**: Multiple personalized models create a storage cost even when only one runs at a time. Storing 10 copies of a 40 MB model consumes 400 MB. In this example, sharing the frozen backbone and storing separate adapters reduces the marginal storage cost of a new context by 200×. This comparison establishes storage savings; the device must still accommodate the active model, adaptation state, and context-switching cost.

![[90 Assets/Volume II/11 Edge Intelligence/Vol2 Edge Intelligence Margin 002 67a4cd3c83.svg]]

*Blue memory ladder on a log scale comparing a 40 MB full model copy with a 0.2 MB adapter, with a 200× span marked between them.*

*Each new context costs an adapter, not a full model copy.*

In smartphone camera pipelines, environmental lighting, user preferences, and lens distortion vary between users. Residual modules can adapt a frozen shared model to these differences while leaving its base weights unchanged; the combined model’s behavior still requires validation. In voice-based systems, adapters similarly provide a way to test personalized speech recognition without retraining the full acoustic model. Keeping the base and adapter versions compatible also supports rollback or switching between user-specific versions when adaptation degrades performance.

#### Performance Vs. Resource Trade-Offs

^sec-edge-intelligence-performance-vs-resource-tradeoffs-2be2

Selecting an adaptation strategy requires accounting for both resource cost and expressivity. Bias-only updates retain a small trainable state but may lack the capacity to handle structural domain shifts. Full fine-tuning exposes every parameter to adaptation and adds corresponding gradient and optimizer state. LoRA and residual adapters offer intermediate choices whose cost depends on rank or width, insertion sites, and numerical representation. The same budget, [[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/03 The Fleet in Production/11 Edge Intelligence/V2-11.04 Design Constraints#^eq-edge-adaptation-peak-memory|equation 1]], governs each of these strategies. Because peak working memory ${M_{\text{peak}}}$ must jointly contain resident weights, trainable state ${M_{\text{train}}}$, and the peak of live activations under the device ceiling ${M_{\text{device}}}$, each strategy shrinks a different term. Bias-only and adapter methods shrink ${M_{\text{train}}}$, and TinyTL-style choices also shrink the activation term. Training only the final classifier, whose backward pass stops at the head, removes most of that term. Parameter storage alone therefore cannot determine whether background adaptation fits.

LoRA and residual adapters impose different deployment costs. For a fixed adapter and compatible numerical representation, LoRA’s linear update can be merged into the backbone before inference $({\mathbf{W}}_0 + {\mathbf{B}}{\mathbf{A}})$, avoiding separate adapter arithmetic in the forward path. A quantized base may require requantization and renewed quality validation after merging. Keeping a separate merged backbone for every context also sacrifices the storage benefit of sharing one base. Residual adapters remain distinct modules (${\mathbf{y}}= {f}({\mathbf{x}}) + \text{Adapter}({\mathbf{x}})$); their latency cost depends on shape, placement, and runtime, while switching contexts requires only the corresponding adapter state.

The runtime must support the selected adapter’s forward operators and its training path. These can use a dynamic graph or a statically compiled update schedule; dynamic graph construction is not itself a requirement. For an inference engine such as Open Neural Network Exchange (ONNX) Runtime Mobile, a runtime that executes an adapter’s forward path may still lack its backward operators, optimizer, or state management. The deployment must verify both paths, including any transfers between the processor that runs inference and the processor that performs updates.

For this multi-context personalization workload, where one device switches among several adapters, system architects can use a device-tier framework to organize candidate mechanisms. On flagship phones (Tier 1) with dedicated Neural Engines and 8 GB of RAM or more, residual adapters provide a strong balance of modularity and speed, because switching contexts swaps only adapter state. On mid-range devices (Tier 2) with 1 GB up to 8 GB, LoRA may fit when a fixed adapter can be merged into a compatible base representation before inference, trading switching flexibility for zero added inference arithmetic. For ultra-constrained IoT endpoints (Tier 3) with less than 1 GB RAM, bias-only updates may be the only practical option.

Budget phones show that the tier follows the memory available to learning rather than installed RAM. Their 4 GB places them in Tier 2, yet only 1 GB–2 GB remains for ML work after the operating system. A background adaptation task may be granted less, so a deployment can reasonably hold them to the Tier 3 mechanism. The assignments follow from the workload as much as from the hardware. A workload that keeps one adaptation per user, rather than many switchable contexts, loses the reason to keep adapters separate and can favor merged LoRA even on flagship devices. The runtime must still verify the base-model footprint and admit training within the measured device envelope.

### Sparse Updates

^sec-edge-intelligence-sparse-updates-879b

Bias-only updates and fixed adapters choose where the model may change before seeing local data, yet a local shift can alter specific convolutional filters or attention channels rather than every block uniformly. Sparse updates instead adapt selected existing parameters rather than adding modules. Their expressivity relative to adapters depends on the selected locations and parameter budget. A task-adaptive policy selects those locations using evidence about the current task or user. The resulting update space can target changes that a fixed subset misses, but selection itself consumes resources and does not guarantee that the complete training path fits the device.

The useful savings depend on what the runtime can omit. Unselected parameters need no gradient or optimizer state, but their operations may still lie on the backward path to selected parameters. A policy must therefore compare the measured update cost with its adaptation benefit; the fraction of trainable parameters alone does not predict the reduction in compute. Because layers do not contribute equally to local performance gains, identifying the minimal subset with the highest adaptation value lets the system spend scarce training capacity where it changes the model’s behavior most.

#### Sparse Update Design

^sec-edge-intelligence-sparse-update-design-ee7c

Let a neural network be defined by parameters ${\theta}= \{{\theta}_1, {\theta}_2, \ldots, {\theta}_{{N_L}}\}$ across ${N_L}$ layers. In standard fine-tuning, gradients are computed and updates are performed on all parameters:

$$
{\theta}_i \leftarrow {\theta}_i - {\eta}\frac{\partial {\mathcal{L}}}{\partial {\theta}_i}, \quad \text{for } {i}= 1, \ldots, {N_L}
$$

In task-adaptive sparse updates, the system selects a small subset ${\mathcal{S}}\subset \{1, \ldots, {N_L}\}$ such that only parameters in ${\mathcal{S}}$ are updated:

$$
{\theta}_i \leftarrow \begin{cases} {\theta}_i - {\eta}\frac{\partial {\mathcal{L}}}{\partial {\theta}_i}, & \text{if } {i}\in {\mathcal{S}}\\ {\theta}_i, & \text{otherwise} \end{cases}
$$

The challenge lies in selecting the optimal subset ${\mathcal{S}}$ given memory and compute constraints. A principled strategy is contribution analysis, an empirical method that estimates how much each layer contributes to downstream performance improvement. For example, one can measure the marginal gain from updating each layer independently:

1.  Freeze the entire model.
2.  Unfreeze one candidate layer.
3.  Finetune briefly and evaluate improvement in validation accuracy.
4.  Rank layers by performance gain per unit cost (e.g., per KB of trainable memory).

This layer-wise profiling yields a candidate ranking from which ${\mathcal{S}}$ can be constructed subject to a memory budget. Gains measured one layer at a time need not add when layers train together, and shared backward dependencies change the combined cost. The selected set therefore needs a joint quality and resource check, with the profiling cost included in the adaptation budget.

In a complete selective fine-tuning pipeline, that ranking is the middle of three stages ([[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/03 The Fleet in Production/11 Edge Intelligence/V2-11.05 Model Adaptation#^fig-ondevice-pretraining|figure 9]]). Stage 1 meta-trains the network in the cloud, which means training its initial weights so that a few later updates on new data adapt it well (the idea behind model-agnostic meta-learning, taken up in [[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/03 The Fleet in Production/11 Edge Intelligence/V2-11.08 Federated Learning Systems at Scale#^sec-edge-intelligence-federated-personalization-3c73|section 11.7.3]]). Stage 2 ranks layers or channel groups by adaptation benefit per unit of device cost and lets the device budget set the cut, and Stage 3 updates only the units above the cut on local data. The backward pass still runs through any frozen layer that lies between the loss and a selected unit, so the selected set must be costed under the complete execution schedule.

![[90 Assets/Volume II/11 Edge Intelligence/Ondevice Pretraining 5e222ade03.svg]]

Figure 9: **Selective On-Device Fine-Tuning**: (1) The full network is meta-trained in the cloud on generic data. (2) Each layer or channel group is ranked by its expected adaptation benefit per unit of device cost, and the device’s data, compute, and memory budget sets the cut. (3) On the device, only units above the cut are updated on local data while the rest stay frozen. Scores and cut position are schematic.

^fig-ondevice-pretraining

A concrete example is TinyTrain, a method designed to allow rapid adaptation on-device ([[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/03 The Fleet in Production/11 Edge Intelligence/V2-11.16 Source References#^ref-kwon2024tinytrain|Kwon et al. 2024]]). TinyTrain combines offline preparation with a task-adaptive sparse-update method that dynamically selects layers or channels to update using user data, memory limits, and compute limits. At runtime, the system updates only the selected subset rather than treating the whole network as trainable.

In implementation, selective layer updating turns the ranking into `requires_grad` decisions or equivalent graph masks. In the profiling-driven version, contribution scores and the memory limit decide which layers are unfrozen ([[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/03 The Fleet in Production/11 Edge Intelligence/V2-11.05 Model Adaptation#^lst-selective-update|listing 3]]).

``` python
# Selective layer update based on contribution analysis
# Layers selected via profiling: conv2 and fc have highest
# accuracy-per-KB impact for this task

for name, param in model.named_parameters():
    if "conv2" in name or "fc" in name:
        param.requires_grad = True  # Train high-impact layers
    else:
        param.requires_grad = False  # Freeze low-impact layers

# Parameter fraction follows layer shapes; adaptation quality requires evaluation
# Memory savings: gradient storage only for selected layers
```

Listing 3: **Selective Layer Updating**: This technique allows fine-tuning specific layers of a pretrained model while keeping others frozen, optimizing computational resources for targeted improvements.

^lst-selective-update

Consider a user wearing an augmented reality headset that performs real-time object recognition. As lighting and environments shift, the system must adapt to maintain accuracy, but training must occur during brief idle periods or while charging. TinyTrain addresses this constraint with task-adaptive sparse updates, in which the deployment process selects a small update set that fits the device’s memory and compute budget. The approach keeps adaptation fast, energy-efficient, and memory-aware when the target task and device profile match the method’s assumptions.

Task-adaptive sparse updates still introduce system-level trade-offs. Contribution analysis has a nontrivial computational cost even when it occurs during pretraining or initial profiling, so the deployment pipeline must budget for it. Stability also requires attention: if too few parameters are selected for updating, the model may underfit the target distribution, so teams need validation thresholds for the selected subset before deployment. The selection policy must also account for hardware-specific execution costs because some parameters show high contribution scores but prove expensive to update on certain architectures. Despite these trade-offs, task-adaptive sparse updates provide a practical mechanism to scale adaptation across deployment contexts from microcontrollers to mobile devices ([[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/03 The Fleet in Production/11 Edge Intelligence/V2-11.16 Source References#^ref-kwon2024tinytrain|Kwon et al. 2024]]).

#### Adaptation Strategy Comparison

^sec-edge-intelligence-adaptation-strategy-comparison-1faf

The design target is the smallest update space that captures the local change. Full fine-tuning pays every term of [[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/03 The Fleet in Production/11 Edge Intelligence/V2-11.04 Design Constraints#^eq-edge-adaptation-peak-memory|equation 1]]. Bias-only updates and head-only training fit minor calibration or new output categories. LoRA and residual adapters add bounded capacity beside a frozen backbone. Task-adaptive sparse updates spend profiling effort to select valuable existing parameters. A larger update space is justified only if the measured quality gain fits the device’s training budget. Each strategy shrinks a different budget term, and the choice must still be checked against expressivity, runtime support, and selection cost. [[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/03 The Fleet in Production/11 Edge Intelligence/V2-11.05 Model Adaptation#^tbl-adaptation-strategies|Table 4]] sets these adaptation strategy trade-offs side by side.

| **Technique**            |      **Trainable parameters**       |               **Memory overhead**                |   **Expressivity**   | **Use case suitability**                   | **System requirements**                         |
|:-------------------------|:-----------------------------------:|:------------------------------------------------:|:--------------------:|:-------------------------------------------|:------------------------------------------------|
| **Full Fine-Tuning**     |           All parameters            |      Highest; every term of the peak budget      |       Highest        | Large domain shift on capable devices      | Full backward support and memory headroom       |
| **Bias-Only Updates**    |           Bias terms only           | Minimal ${M_{\text{train}}}$; activations remain |         Low          | Simple personalization; low variance       | Extreme memory/compute limits                   |
| **Head-Only Training**   |          Final classifier           |     Minimal; backward pass stops at the head     |         Low          | New output classes; custom wake words      | Frozen encoder; reusable cached features        |
| **LoRA**                 | Low-rank factors per adapted matrix |   Low ${M_{\text{train}}}$; activations remain   |   Moderate to High   | One mergeable adaptation per user          | Merge step and requantization check for serving |
| **Residual Adapters**    |           Adapter modules           |   Low ${M_{\text{train}}}$; activations remain   |   Moderate to High   | Switching among several contexts on mobile | Mobile-class SoCs with runtime support          |
| **Sparse Layer Updates** |     Selective parameter subsets     |     Variable; depends on the selected layers     | High (task-adaptive) | Real-time adaptation; domain shift         | Requires profiling or meta-training             |

Table 4: **Adaptation Strategy Trade-Offs**: Six approaches to model adaptation compared by trainable parameters, the term of the peak-memory budget ([[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/03 The Fleet in Production/11 Edge Intelligence/V2-11.04 Design Constraints#^eq-edge-adaptation-peak-memory|equation 1]]) they shrink, expressivity, suitability, and system requirements. Methods that shrink only the trainable state ${M_{\text{train}}}$ still keep the activations their backward pass needs, while head-only training also removes most of the activation term by stopping the backward pass at the classifier.

^tbl-adaptation-strategies

Once an adaptation mechanism fits the device’s memory and execution budget, it must still learn from the available examples. Reducing trainable state does not create labels or ensure that a user’s recent observations represent future use. The next constraint is therefore statistical: extracting reliable learning signal from sparse local data while retaining useful prior behavior.

1.  Why does Tiny Transfer Learning (TinyTL) freeze convolutional backbone weights and adapt biases with optional lite residual modules?

    1.  Freezing removes backbone weight-gradient and optimizer state; suitable activation and normalization choices also reduce saved backward state.
    2.  Microcontroller hardware units physically short-circuit if matrix multiplications are performed in reverse during backpropagation.

    4.  Training bias parameters modifies the underlying kernel weights directly through implicit singular value decomposition.
