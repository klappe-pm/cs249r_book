# V2-11.08 Federated Learning Systems at Scale

[[Home|Home]] · [[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/Volume 2 Overview|Volume Overview]] · [[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/03 The Fleet in Production/11 Edge Intelligence/V2-11 Edge Intelligence|V2-11 Edge Intelligence]]

^sec-edge-intelligence-federated-systems

A round selects a cohort from the deployment population, but some selected devices overheat, lose Wi-Fi, enter power-saving mode, or power off before returning an update. A fleet of millions of smartphones need not participate in one round for these failures to matter. Turning the FedAvg protocol into a production fleet system requires deciding which clients are eligible and how long to wait, compressing updates to fit constrained uplinks, separating personal state from shared weights, enforcing cryptographic and statistical privacy bounds, and reconciling asynchronous updates across heterogeneous hardware.

### Client Scheduling

^sec-edge-intelligence-client-scheduling-f675

The first decision in any round is which devices to invite. Sampling devices uniformly at random without checking their power and network state would interrupt users mid-task, whereas accepting updates only from plugged-in flagship phones on fast Wi-Fi trains a global model skewed toward affluent, overnight-charging cohorts. Because real-world client availability shifts continuously with battery state, charging habits, and network coverage, client scheduling governs both the completion speed and the population coverage of distributed learning.

At a baseline level, federated ML systems define eligibility criteria for participation. A mobile deployment may require devices to be plugged in, connected to Wi-Fi, and idle to limit interference with foreground work and battery use. The chosen criteria determine which subset of the total population is considered “available” for a training round; they are deployment policy rather than requirements of federated learning itself.

Beyond these operational filters, devices also differ in their hardware capabilities, data availability, and network conditions. Some smartphones contain many recent examples relevant to the current task, while others have outdated or irrelevant data. Network bandwidth and upload speed may vary widely depending on geography and carrier infrastructure. As a result, uniform random selection from the eligible pool does not guarantee coverage of the full deployment population; eligibility has already excluded some devices and their data.

Availability-driven selection introduces participation bias, the fleet-level consequence of the admission test in [[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/03 The Fleet in Production/11 Edge Intelligence/V2-11.04 Design Constraints#^eq-edge-background-admission|equation 2]] (principle [[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/03 The Fleet in Production/V2 Fleet-In-Production Principles#^pri-participation-selects-population|20]]). Clients with favorable conditions, such as frequent charging, high-end hardware, and consistent connectivity, pass admission and participate repeatedly, while others are systematically underrepresented. This can skew the resulting model toward behaviors and preferences of a privileged subset of the population, raising both fairness and generalization concerns.

Participation bias becomes severe when a small eligible cohort supplies most completed updates while other cohorts rarely participate. The skew is measured per cohort as a coverage ratio, the cohort’s share of completed updates divided by its share of the deployed population. A ratio near one means the cohort is represented in proportion to its size, and a ratio well below one means the shared model learns that cohort’s behavior at less than its population weight. Completion logs supply the numerator and the deployment inventory the denominator, so the ratio can be computed without inspecting any update. The admission test and the round deadline both remove constrained devices faster than capable ones. Uniform selection among admitted devices passes the resulting skew through unchanged, so a budget cohort can fall from a fifth of the fleet to a sliver of the completed updates ([[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/03 The Fleet in Production/11 Edge Intelligence/V2-11.08 Federated Learning Systems at Scale#^fig-participation-coverage-funnel|figure 13]]).

Low coverage also feeds itself, because models become more strongly optimized for users with high-end devices and stable connectivity, potentially degrading performance for resource-constrained users who need adaptation the most. A keyboard prediction model might become biased toward the typing patterns of users with flagship phones who charge overnight, missing important linguistic variations from users with budget devices or irregular charging patterns.

Figure 13: **From Deployed Fleet to Aggregate**: Each stage between the deployed fleet and the aggregate filters devices, and the admission test and round deadline fall hardest on constrained cohorts. The admission test removes most budget phones, uniform selection among admitted devices preserves the skew it inherits, and the round deadline drops the slowest survivors. Cohort shares and stage sizes are illustrative, not measured.

^fig-participation-coverage-funnel

The order of these decisions limits what any remedy can do. Fleet orchestration admits only feasible allocations before a fairness policy divides them (principle [[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/02 Distributed ML/V2 Distributed ML Principles#^pri-feasibility-before-fairness|15]]), and a federated scheduler inherits the same order with a sharper consequence. Its eligibility filter fixes the candidate pool, so a fairness policy can redistribute rounds only among devices the admission test already accepted; a device that never qualifies is invisible to it.

Scheduling must therefore balance completion speed with population coverage. Stratified or quota-based sampling can prioritize underrepresented groups among eligible devices, while cumulative participation records can guide later invitations. Weighting methods require estimates of the population and selection process; they cannot recover observations from a cohort that never participates. Buffered asynchronous aggregation ([[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/03 The Fleet in Production/11 Edge Intelligence/V2-11.16 Source References#^ref-fedbuff|Nguyen et al. 2021]]), whose mechanism [[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/03 The Fleet in Production/11 Edge Intelligence/V2-11.08 Federated Learning Systems at Scale#^sec-edge-intelligence-asynchronous-device-synchronization-348e|section 11.7.5.1]] describes, staleness-aware methods ([[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/03 The Fleet in Production/11 Edge Intelligence/V2-11.16 Source References#^ref-rodio2024|Rodio and Neglia 2024]]), and fairness-oriented weighting ([[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/03 The Fleet in Production/11 Edge Intelligence/V2-11.16 Source References#^ref-fedstaleweight|Ma et al. 2024]]) offer additional controls. Their effectiveness must be checked through completed participation and cohort quality, rather than inferred from the scheduling rule alone. Example weighting in the aggregate ([[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/03 The Fleet in Production/11 Edge Intelligence/V2-11.07 Federated Learning Algorithms#^eq-fedavg-weighted-aggregation|equation 3]]) also gives large local datasets more influence whether or not they represent the target population any better.

Scheduling must also bound how long a round waits on its slowest participants. Network latency, battery depletion, or background process contention can make some devices (stragglers) take 10$\times$ longer than the median, and the round cannot aggregate until enough updates arrive.

![[90 Assets/Volume II/11 Edge Intelligence/Edge Intelligence Straggler Cutoff Strip 2324d07c4b.svg]]

*Sequence strip showing the first K federated-client updates accepted before the round closes, while a late straggler is dropped.*

*Enough valid reports let the round close before its slowest clients finish.*

To reduce waiting for stragglers, production federated learning (FL) systems employ **over-selection**. The server selects a candidate pool size ${K_{\text{candidates}}}$ larger than the target number of updates ${K_{\text{target}}}$ (the production system of Bonawitz et al. ([[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/03 The Fleet in Production/11 Edge Intelligence/V2-11.16 Source References#^ref-bonawitz2019towards|2019]]) selects about 130 percent of the target, ${K_{\text{candidates}}} \approx 1.3 \times {K_{\text{target}}}$). When enough valid reports arrive, the server can form an aggregate from ${K_{\text{target}}}$ completed updates rather than wait for every selected device. Completion then depends on how quickly the target number of valid reports arrives, but over-selection alone does not guarantee that the target is reached. A round deadline and an explicit acceptance or abandonment rule handle insufficient completion. Secure aggregation adds a participation floor and dropout recovery ([[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/03 The Fleet in Production/11 Edge Intelligence/V2-11.08 Federated Learning Systems at Scale#^sec-edge-intelligence-federated-privacy-a1ed|section 11.7.4]]); selecting the fastest reports must remain compatible with that protocol. Accepting the first reports to arrive discards those of the slowest selected devices, which are disproportionately the constrained cohorts that admission already thinned, so over-selection with a round deadline can concentrate the participation bias just described. Shorter rounds must therefore be evaluated against cohort coverage and wall-clock time to the declared quality target. Because these scheduling decisions set convergence rate, model generalization, energy consumption, and user experience together, poor scheduling produces excessive stragglers, overfitting to narrow client segments, or wasted computation.

### Bandwidth-Aware Update Compression

^sec-edge-intelligence-bandwidthaware-update-compression-7730

Many of the stragglers that miss a round deadline fail on the network transfer rather than on local computation: transmitting full-precision weights or gradients over an asymmetric mobile uplink can exhaust a client’s connectivity window or battery allowance before its update ever reaches the coordinator.[[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/03 The Fleet in Production/11 Edge Intelligence/V2-11.17 Footnotes#^fn13|¹³]] Update compression therefore begins with a bottleneck diagnosis: when transfer is limiting, the design decision is which information to quantize, sparsify, or keep local. The smaller payload must then be evaluated against encoding cost, update error, and the rounds required to reach the quality target.

The compression decision separates into three levers, each changing a different part of the communication bottleneck: model compression reduces bits per update, selective sharing reduces which parameters travel, and architectural partitioning keeps some state local. Model compression methods reduce the wire footprint of transmitted updates through quantization, sparsification, or subsampling. Instead of sending full-precision gradients, a client transmits 8-bit quantized updates or communicates only the top-${K_{\text{top}}}$ gradient elements[[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/03 The Fleet in Production/11 Edge Intelligence/V2-11.17 Footnotes#^fn14|¹⁴]] with highest magnitude, the same encodings that cluster training applies to its collective payloads. At the edge they bind in a different place. They act on a slow, asymmetric uplink rather than a fabric, under secure aggregation that must still combine the encoded updates, and inside a per-round communication total ([[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/03 The Fleet in Production/11 Edge Intelligence/V2-11.08 Federated Learning Systems at Scale#^eq-edge-federated-communication-volume|equation 4]]) that also counts downloads and retries. These transformations are communication mechanisms, not privacy mechanisms; a smaller embedding, sparse update, or low-precision gradient may still expose membership, attributes, or reconstructable features.

↩︎ Gradient quantization, top-${k}$ sparsification, and error feedback that carries unsent updates forward are established in **[[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/02 Distributed ML/05 Collective Communication/V2-05.10 Gradient Compression Under Bandwidth Scarcity#^sec-communication-collective-operations-collective-operations-gradient-compression-7a5c|Gradient Compression Under Bandwidth Scarcity]]**. Convergence depends on the optimizer and compression assumptions.

Selective update sharing further reduces communication by transmitting only subsets of model parameters or updates. In layer-wise selective sharing, clients update only certain layers, typically the final classifier or adapter modules, while keeping the majority of the backbone frozen. This reduces the update payload and limits which shared representations can change, but the trainable subset can still overfit nonrepresentative client data.

Architectural partitioning requires specifying what crosses the boundary. A personal/shared parameter partition keeps local modules private while aggregating selected shared parameters. Split learning instead divides execution between client and server, exchanging intermediate activations and backward gradients at the cut. These designs have different traffic and exposure patterns; neither guarantees a communication reduction or privacy merely by dividing the model.

These approaches can use the FedAvg aggregation protocol ([[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/03 The Fleet in Production/11 Edge Intelligence/V2-11.07 Federated Learning Algorithms#^sec-edge-intelligence-federated-aggregation-protocols-5d37|section 11.6.2.2]]) only when their encoding and shared parameter schema support weighted aggregation. Under secure aggregation, which sums masked updates without inspecting them, weighted aggregation also requires every client to share one quantization scale and one coordinate template for sparse values ([[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/03 The Fleet in Production/11 Edge Intelligence/V2-11.08 Federated Learning Systems at Scale#^sec-edge-intelligence-federated-privacy-a1ed|section 11.7.4]]). Local-only parameters must stay outside the shared aggregate altogether.

Whatever the encoding, the total communication volume for round ${r}$ is

$$
{M_r}= {M_{\text{download},r}}+ {M_{\text{upload},r}}+ {M_{\text{protocol},r}}+ {M_{\text{retry},r}}, \tag{4}
$$

^eq-edge-federated-communication-volume

[[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/03 The Fleet in Production/11 Edge Intelligence/V2-11.08 Federated Learning Systems at Scale#^eq-edge-federated-communication-volume|Equation 4]] makes download, upload, protocol, and retry traffic explicit. The download distributes model state, the upload carries client updates, protocol traffic covers authentication and cryptographic setup, and retries account for failed or repeated transfers. Optimizing only ${M_{\text{upload},r}}$ can therefore move rather than remove the bottleneck. Additional local computation amortizes network transfers, and each communication-efficient alternative to it carries its own cost. Compression may degrade gradient fidelity, selective updates can limit model capacity, and split architectures complicate coordination. Summed over a training run, the upload term alone can exceed the raw data that federation was meant to keep local, even when each update is far smaller than that data.

↪ The break-even condition under which encoding and decoding time costs less than the transfer time compression saves is developed, with a worked example, in **[[05 Reference/Volume II - Scaling/V2 Communication Foundations#^sec-appdx-comm-foundations-compression|Compression Economics]]**.

![[90 Assets/Volume II/11 Edge Intelligence/Vol2 Edge Intelligence Margin 004 e9465a5e51.svg]]

*Violet network ladder on a log scale comparing a 200 MB raw-image upload with a 2.5 MB federated update, with an 80× span marked between them.*

*One compressed update is 80× smaller than a week’s raw-image upload.*

**Napkin Math 11.3: Model updates vs. raw data**

**Problem**: A team is designing a federated camera-personalization system that learns from 200 MB of compressed user images per week. Compare one upload of those images with one compressed model-update uplink round.

**Math**: Bandwidth efficiency is the ratio of raw data volume to model update size.

1.  **Raw data upload**: 200 MB/week.
2.  **One federated uplink round**: A compressed model update (5M params) is 2.5 MB.
3.  **One-round uplink reduction**: 200 MB/2.5 MB = 80×.

**Systems insight**: For this scenario, one 2.5 MB uplink is 80× smaller than one 200 MB raw-data upload. That ratio is not the end-to-end communication saving of a training run: after roughly 80× uplink rounds, cumulative update uploads alone equal the raw-data volume, before counting global-model downloads, retries, or protocol overhead. The verdict therefore turns on the round schedule. A deployment that runs one round per day uploads seven updates per week of data, far below the break-even count, and saves most of the uplink traffic. One that runs a round every hour uploads 168 updates per week, more than the break-even count, and moves more bytes than shipping the images would. Per-round traffic must be multiplied by the expected round count before federation can claim a bandwidth saving.

The local-work schedule and update encoding jointly determine communication cost. [[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/03 The Fleet in Production/11 Edge Intelligence/V2-11.08 Federated Learning Systems at Scale#^fig-gradient-compression|Figure 14]] compares uncompressed 32-bit values (Panel A) with uniform 8-bit quantization (Panel B), which gives a $4\times$ reduction in value bytes before scales and other metadata are counted. Top-${k}$ sparsification with retained error feedback (Panel C) must encode coordinate identities as well as selected values. Error feedback retains one residual value per update coordinate between transmissions. Its storage depends on the update size and residual precision and must fit the device allowance alongside training and replay state. The receiving path must reconstruct or aggregate these encodings consistently, with separate compatibility requirements when secure aggregation hides individual updates.

![[90 Assets/Volume II/11 Edge Intelligence/Gradient Compression 447033410a.svg]]

Figure 14: **Gradient Compression Techniques**: Standard updates transmit full-precision values; quantization maps values to lower-precision buckets, such as FP32 to INT8; and sparsification transmits only the most significant top-${k}$ gradients. The latter two mechanisms reduce bytes transferred but introduce representation error or residual state that the training algorithm must manage.

^fig-gradient-compression

The quantization panel (Panel B) is labeled “zero client state” because it keeps no persistent error-feedback residual; encoding buffers, scales, and temporary workspace still enter the device budget. Compression is useful when its reduction in total communication outweighs encoding work and any extra rounds needed to reach the quality target. A smaller upload helps a round only if transfer was what made clients miss the deadline or excluded a cohort, and the round’s completion logs show which. Smaller payloads can let clients on slow or congested uplinks finish before their resource window closes, so compression is judged by completed participation and cohort outcomes, not by bytes saved alone. Even when transfer becomes feasible, the shared model may still fail to capture local variation.

### Federated Personalization

^sec-edge-intelligence-federated-personalization-3c73

Compressing updates lets more devices finish a round, but clients that complete compatible updates can still pull the shared model in opposite directions when a dialect phrase, a regional keyboard shortcut, or an individual’s wrist-worn sensor placement conflicts with the population mean. A single global model may serve the average user adequately while concealing persistent accuracy losses for specific cohorts. Personalized federated learning retains shared representation learning while allowing selected parameters or models to specialize locally, trading added local state, training cost, and overfitting risk on sparse user data against per-user quality.

Let ${\theta}_k$ denote the model parameters on client ${k}$, and ${\theta_{\text{global}}}$ the aggregated global model. For ${N_{\text{clients}}}$ clients, counted as in [[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/03 The Fleet in Production/11 Edge Intelligence/V2-11.07 Federated Learning Algorithms#^eq-fedavg-weighted-aggregation|equation 3]], traditional FL seeks to minimize a global objective:

$$
\min_{\theta}\sum_{k=1}^{{N_{\text{clients}}}} {w}_k {\mathcal{L}}_k({\theta})
$$

 where ${\mathcal{L}}_k({\theta})$ is the local loss on client ${k}$, and ${w}_k$ is a weighting factor (e.g., proportional to local dataset size). This formulation optimizes the weighted average loss of a shared model ${\theta}$; it does not guarantee acceptable performance for every client. Local loss landscapes ${\mathcal{L}}_k$ can differ across clients, reflecting non-IID data distributions and varying task requirements.

Personalization modifies this objective to allow each client to maintain its own adapted parameters ${\theta}_k$, optimized with respect to both the global model and local data:

$$
\min_{{\theta}_1, \ldots, {\theta}_{{N_{\text{clients}}}}} \sum_{k=1}^{{N_{\text{clients}}}} \left( {\mathcal{L}}_k({\theta}_k) + {\lambda_{\text{reg}}}\cdot {\mathcal{R}}({\theta}_k, {\theta_{\text{global}}}) \right)
$$

Here, ${\mathcal{R}}$ penalizes deviation from the global model, for example with the squared distance that FedProx adds to each local loss, and ${\lambda_{\text{reg}}}$ controls the strength of that penalty. The per-client parameters ${\theta}_k$ are thus state that the plain federated objective does not have, held near the shared model by the penalty rather than trained on local data alone. The displayed objective describes local specialization around a given global reference; a complete protocol must additionally specify how that reference is trained and refreshed. The penalty trades local fit against proximity to the shared model, without guaranteeing improvement for every client.

In a wearable health monitor that classifies physical activities from physiological signals, this objective separates shared motion features from individual gait signatures, resting baselines, and sensor placements. Finetuning the final classification layer or low-rank adapters around the global reference ${\theta_{\text{global}}}$ addresses those user-specific differences, provided evaluation establishes that the local examples actually improve accuracy on rare activity classes with little supervision.

Several personalization strategies address the trade-offs between compute overhead, information exposure, and adaptation speed. One widely used approach is local finetuning, in which each client downloads the latest global model and performs a small number of gradient steps using its local data. This method avoids transmitting those examples during adaptation, but the resulting local state and any later update remain privacy-sensitive. It may also yield suboptimal results when the global model is poorly aligned with the client’s data distribution or when the local dataset is extremely limited.

Another effective technique involves personalization layers, where the model is partitioned into a shared backbone and a lightweight, client-specific head, typically the final classification layer ([[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/03 The Fleet in Production/11 Edge Intelligence/V2-11.16 Source References#^ref-arivazhagan2019federated|Arivazhagan et al. 2019]]). One such method, FedPer, has clients train both the shared base and their personal head, but only the shared base participates in server aggregation; the head remains local. A separate head-only adaptation policy can freeze a downloaded backbone to reduce local training cost when the relevant variation lies mainly in output categories or decision boundaries.

Clustered federated learning offers an alternative by grouping clients according to similarities in their data or performance characteristics, and training separate models for each cluster. This strategy can enhance accuracy within homogeneous subpopulations, though it introduces additional system complexity and may require exchanging metadata to determine group membership.

Finally, meta-learning approaches, such as model-agnostic meta-learning (MAML),[[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/03 The Fleet in Production/11 Edge Intelligence/V2-11.17 Footnotes#^fn15|¹⁵]] aim to produce a global model initialization that can be quickly adapted to new tasks with a few local updates ([[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/03 The Fleet in Production/11 Edge Intelligence/V2-11.16 Source References#^ref-finn2017model|Finn et al. 2017]]). A meta-learned initialization is especially useful when clients have limited data or operate in environments with frequent distributional shifts.

Each strategy occupies a different point in the personalization trade-off space. Local finetuning, personalization layers, clustered federated learning, and meta-learning differ in compute overhead, information exposure, and adaptation latency ([[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/03 The Fleet in Production/11 Edge Intelligence/V2-11.08 Federated Learning Systems at Scale#^tbl-personalization-strategies|table 7]]). The mechanism determines what state is shared; a privacy guarantee requires separately specified controls and assumptions.

|:---------------------------|:--------------------------------------------------|:---------------------:|:--------------------------------------------------------:|:-----------------------:|

Selecting an on-device personalization strategy requires balancing adaptation capacity against memory residency. Placement sets the dependency path, and the execution schedule sets how much of that state stays resident. Keeping the personal head or adapter on the device also narrows what crosses the device boundary, but the shared backbone updates that still leave the client remain derived from private local examples and require explicit privacy protection.

### Federated Privacy

^sec-edge-intelligence-federated-privacy-a1ed

Even when personal parameters and raw training examples never leave the phone, an adversary or curious server that inspects an uploaded gradient or weight delta ${\Delta}{\theta}$ can reconstruct typed text, recover facial features, or determine whether a specific phrase appeared in a user’s local dataset. Model inversion attacks[[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/03 The Fleet in Production/11 Edge Intelligence/V2-11.17 Footnotes#^fn16|¹⁶]] and membership inference attacks exploit the fact that transmitted updates encode the very examples that produced them.

Two protective measures address that leakage. **Secure aggregation**[[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/03 The Fleet in Production/11 Edge Intelligence/V2-11.17 Footnotes#^fn17|¹⁷]] protocols reveal a combined result while hiding individual updates beyond what that result itself reveals, subject to the protocol’s assumptions about collusion and participating clients. Differential privacy, a formal bound on how much any one protected user’s or record’s inclusion can change a released result, is obtained by clipping each contribution and adding noise calibrated to that bound. The deployment must specify which unit receives that protection.

These controls solve different problems. Secure aggregation limits what the coordinator learns about each submitted update, whereas differential privacy bounds how much a declared privacy unit can affect a released result. A deployment therefore needs a privacy accounting log that records the unit of protection, clipping rule, noise mechanism, sampling assumptions, $({\epsilon},{\delta})$ expenditure for every release, and cumulative composition across training and federated analytics ([[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/03 The Fleet in Production/11 Edge Intelligence/V2-11.16 Source References#^ref-abadi2016deep|Abadi et al. 2016]]; [[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/03 The Fleet in Production/11 Edge Intelligence/V2-11.16 Source References#^ref-kairouz2021|Kairouz and McMahan 2021]]). Clipping bounds each contribution before noise is calibrated. The parameters ${\epsilon}$ and ${\delta}$ describe the permitted change in output probabilities when the protected user or record is included or removed; composition accounts for repeated releases involving that unit. Resetting a round counter does not reset privacy loss for a user who participates again.

↪ The composition rules that turn a privacy accounting log’s per-release entries into one cumulative privacy budget are developed in **[[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/04 The Responsible Fleet/13 Security & Privacy/V2-13.10 Differential Privacy#^sec-security-privacy-privacy-budget-composition-edbe|Privacy budget composition]]**.

[[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/03 The Fleet in Production/11 Edge Intelligence/V2-11.08 Federated Learning Systems at Scale#^fig-secure-agg|Figure 16]] models this cryptographic boundary through pairwise zero-sum masking. Clients A and B negotiate a shared random mask ${\mathbf{s}}$ via Diffie-Hellman key agreement, which lets the two clients derive the same secret from public values that the server relays but cannot use to compute it. They perturb their local updates into pseudo-random vectors (${\mathbf{y}_A} = {\Delta}{\theta_A}+ {\mathbf{s}}$ and ${\mathbf{y}_B} = {\Delta}{\theta_B}- {\mathbf{s}}$) before crossing into the server domain. When the aggregation server sums the received vectors, the symmetric masks cancel (${\mathbf{s}} - {\mathbf{s}} = 0$), yielding the exact unmasked aggregate ${\Delta}{\theta_A}+ {\Delta}{\theta_B}$. This two-client illustration assumes both clients complete the exchange and neither reveals its mask to the server. It demonstrates cancellation, not a complete protocol for collusion resistance or dropout recovery.

A real round has many clients, and some drop out after agreeing on masks but before uploading. Each dropped client leaves its pairwise masks uncancelled in the sum. The full protocol therefore has every client split the secret seeds of its masks into shares held by the other clients, and any threshold number of those shares can rebuild a seed ([[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/03 The Fleet in Production/11 Edge Intelligence/V2-11.16 Source References#^ref-bonawitz2017|Bonawitz et al. 2017]]). After the upload deadline, surviving clients send the shares of the dropped clients’ seeds, and the server regenerates and subtracts the dangling masks. Recovery therefore costs an extra message round and computation that grows with the number of dropouts, and it succeeds only if at least the threshold number of clients survive; otherwise the round is abandoned. Over-selection adds to this cost. A client that completed key agreement but whose report the coordinator discards once the target count arrives is a dropout to the protocol, so every discarded report adds recovery work, and the clients kept must still clear the threshold.

That threshold is one reason for a participation floor. The other is the sum itself, because an aggregate over a few clients reveals too much about each of them. The coordinator must therefore invite enough clients that the expected survivors clear the floor, and a cohort that falls short is held for a later round rather than aggregated.

Masking also constrains how clients encode their updates ([[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/03 The Fleet in Production/11 Edge Intelligence/V2-11.08 Federated Learning Systems at Scale#^sec-edge-intelligence-bandwidthaware-update-compression-7730|section 11.7.2]]). With cleartext uploads, the server can dequantize each client’s tensor with that client’s scale factor and scatter sparse index-value pairs into a dense buffer before summing. Under secure aggregation the masks are added in modular integer arithmetic, so each upload is a fixed-point integer vector, and the server sums these masked vectors element by element without inspecting individual uploads. Clients that quantize updates must therefore quantize to a shared fixed-point scale so their masked integers represent the same unit at every coordinate, and clients that sparsify updates must project onto a shared coordinate template or sketch rather than uploading client-specific index lists that would fail to align and would reveal which parameters changed.

Secure aggregation also creates a robustness conflict. Byzantine-resilient rules such as Krum, coordinate-wise filtering, or per-client anomaly inspection need access to individual updates, while secure aggregation deliberately hides those updates ([[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/03 The Fleet in Production/11 Edge Intelligence/V2-11.16 Source References#^ref-blanchard2017machine|Blanchard et al. 2017]]; [[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/03 The Fleet in Production/11 Edge Intelligence/V2-11.16 Source References#^ref-bonawitz2017|Bonawitz et al. 2017]]). A system cannot claim both arbitrary per-client inspection and conventional secure aggregation over the same messages. It must instead validate clients before masking, use attested execution and bounded local updates, compute only aggregation-compatible checks, or adopt a protocol designed to evaluate approved robust statistics without revealing raw client contributions.

↩︎ Byzantine failures, in which a worker stays alive but returns wrong results, and aggregation rules such as Krum and trimmed mean that bound their influence are established in **[[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/02 Distributed ML/07 Fault Tolerance/V2-07.04 Failure Taxonomy#^sec-fault-tolerance-reliability-reliability-byzantine-failures-8a24|Byzantine failures]]**.

![[90 Assets/Volume II/11 Edge Intelligence/Secure Agg 3e35bf86c6.svg]]

Figure 16: **Secure Aggregation Protocol**: A simplified two-client view of pairwise masking. Clients A and B agree on a shared random mask ${\mathbf{s}}$; A uploads ${\mathbf{y}_A} = {\Delta}{\theta_A} + {\mathbf{s}}$ and B uploads ${\mathbf{y}_B} = {\Delta}{\theta_B} - {\mathbf{s}}$, so each upload alone looks random to the server. Summing them cancels the mask and yields exactly ${\Delta}{\theta_A} + {\Delta}{\theta_B}$. The server learns only the aggregate, which can still leak information unless differential privacy bounds it.

^fig-secure-agg

Secure aggregation and differential privacy can protect different boundaries, but their composition must follow the threat model. If a trusted server adds noise after receiving an exact aggregate, the privacy guarantee applies to the released noisy result; it does not hide that exact aggregate from the server. A protocol designed for distributed noise can instead let the server receive only a noisy aggregate. Clipping, noise calibration, dropout assumptions, and repeated-release accounting must match the chosen protocol. Neither mechanism establishes that local data or updates are trustworthy.

3.  Communication: Choose between gradient quantization (e.g., 8-bit) and structured sparsity under constrained bandwidth, and justify the bandwidth/accuracy trade-off.

### Large-Scale Device Orchestration

^sec-edge-intelligence-largescale-device-orchestration-1360

A round that selects a million phones overnight will see some fraction overheat, lose connectivity, or be unplugged before they report, and a few may submit corrupted or deliberately malicious updates. Coordinating that population requires protocols that handle Byzantine clients, network partitions that temporarily cut participants off, and constrained communication. These challenges differ from controlled data center training, where high-bandwidth networks and managed infrastructure narrow the trust and availability envelope.

#### Asynchronous Device Synchronization

^sec-edge-intelligence-asynchronous-device-synchronization-348e

Federated learning inherits failures from distributed systems and machine learning, but its coordination objective is not consensus, the problem of making replicated servers agree on one ordered log of operations. Federated clients agree on no such log. A coordinator instead decides which stochastic client updates are eligible, how stale updates are weighted, and which aggregation rule can tolerate faulty participants without letting them steer the model. Devices crash, lose power, or disconnect during rounds, while compromised clients can submit poisoned updates crafted to push the shared model toward an attacker’s goal. Robust aggregation can reduce the influence of some faulty participants, but its assumptions, compute cost, and compatibility with secure aggregation must be explicit.

The trust chain begins before aggregation. A production coordinator authenticates the device and application instance, verifies the signed model and training-policy version, checks eligibility and freshness, binds the returned update to that version and round nonce, and records whether the update passed protocol-level bounds. Hardware-backed attestation can strengthen claims about code identity on supported devices, but it cannot prove that local examples are benign or representative. The resulting update-integrity record supports rejection, rollback, and incident analysis without treating device identity as evidence that the gradient is correct.

Network partitions are the harder case. Unlike a data center network, a federated fleet must absorb clients that stay offline for hours or days while traveling, out of coverage, or powered down. Asynchronous coordination protocols keep training moving despite missing participants, but they must balance staleness (accepting outdated contributions) against freshness (prioritizing recent but possibly sparse updates). Buffered asynchronous aggregation ([[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/03 The Fleet in Production/11 Edge Intelligence/V2-11.16 Source References#^ref-fedbuff|Nguyen et al. 2021]]) shows the mechanism. It removes the round barrier, letting each client train whenever its admission test passes and upload when done, while the server accumulates arriving updates in a buffer and applies their aggregate once a fixed number have arrived. Each update is down-weighted by its staleness, the number of server versions published since the client downloaded its starting model, so a slow client still contributes without dragging the shared model back toward an old state.

Conventional pairwise secure aggregation ([[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/03 The Fleet in Production/11 Edge Intelligence/V2-11.08 Federated Learning Systems at Scale#^sec-edge-intelligence-federated-privacy-a1ed|section 11.7.4]]) does not fit this buffer. Its masks cancel only among the clients fixed at key agreement, whereas the buffer fills with whichever clients finish. Staleness weighting breaks the masks as well. Weighting a masked update on the server also scales its masks, so they no longer cancel, and the client cannot apply the weight before masking because its staleness is known only when the update arrives. A buffered design therefore needs a secure-aggregation protocol built for it.

↩︎ Gradient staleness, the delay between the model version a worker computed against and the version its update modifies, and its effect on convergence under bounded and fully asynchronous synchronization are established in **[[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/02 Distributed ML/06 Distributed Training/V2-06.05 Scaling Efficiency and Convergence#^sec-distributed-training-systems-systems-staleness-impact-bsp-vs-ssp-vs-asp-e153|Staleness impact across synchronization models]]**.

Recovery must distinguish coordinator state from device-local state. The coordinator checkpoints the global model and the optimizer and protocol state needed to resume accepted progress; this does not require a simultaneous checkpoint on every device. An incomplete round needs an explicit acceptance or abandonment rule, followed by evaluation of the population represented by its completed updates. State reconciliation identifies a returning device’s base version and determines which shared state it must download and which personal state remains compatible. Server-side load balancing can redistribute coordination work, but replacing an unavailable client with a faster one also changes the local data contributing to training. Whereas convergence guarantees for synchronous training assume that every participant completes each round, a federated coordinator must treat stragglers[[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/03 The Fleet in Production/11 Edge Intelligence/V2-11.17 Footnotes#^fn18|¹⁸]] and dropouts as the normal case and state what its convergence argument assumes about them.

#### Heterogeneity Management at Fleet Scale

^sec-edge-intelligence-managing-milliondevice-heterogeneity-86c1

Million-device orchestration is a tiering decision, in which the system decides which clients can train, which can only report lightweight updates, and how much bias that selection introduces. The difficulty is that hardware capabilities, network conditions, data distributions, and availability patterns vary simultaneously, challenging traditional distributed machine learning assumptions about homogeneous participants operating under similar conditions.

Illustrative peak compute ratings differ by about 1,200× between flagship smartphones rated at 35 TOPS and, below Archetype C’s phone-class clients, IoT microcontrollers rated at 0.03 TOPS. These ratings do not translate into a training-speed ratio, because supported precision, backward operators, memory traffic, and sustained execution determine update time. Memory spans the same 65,536× range from microcontroller SRAM to flagship phone RAM met in [[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/03 The Fleet in Production/11 Edge Intelligence/V2-11.04 Design Constraints#^sec-edge-intelligence-model-constraints-9232|section 11.3.2]], and at fleet scale that range decides which update state each tier can retain at all. Energy budgets further restrict when that work can run. Background adaptation in this example targets roughly 500–1000 mW, while phones may sustain 2–3 W for foreground ML workloads and burst higher briefly. Network throughput, latency, and interruption patterns must likewise be measured for the participating clients before selecting update frequency and compression.

Measured scores show a spread of the same order among phones alone, without counting microcontrollers. The AI Benchmark from ETH Zurich runs a fixed suite of quantized and floating-point inference workloads on each phone processor through its Android acceleration path ([[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/03 The Fleet in Production/11 Edge Intelligence/V2-11.16 Source References#^ref-ignatov2019aibenchmark|Ignatov et al. 2019]]; [[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/03 The Fleet in Production/11 Edge Intelligence/V2-11.16 Source References#^ref-ethz2026aibenchmark|ETH Zurich Computer Vision Lab 2026]]). Among processors released from 2020 on, which a client fleet can still contain, the scores span about 400×, from the Snapdragon 460 (2020) to the Dimensity 9600 Pro (2026), and [[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/03 The Fleet in Production/11 Edge Intelligence/V2-11.08 Federated Learning Systems at Scale#^fig-mobile-ai-spread|figure 17]] plots every processor in the ranking. Even flagships of the same generation differ by 14×, with the Google Tensor G4 scoring that far below the Dimensity 9600 M, largely because each score reflects the processor’s software path as well as its silicon. The Tensor G4 result runs through Android’s generic neural-network interface, while the leaders use their vendors’ own runtimes. These are inference scores, not training speeds, but they bound the problem from the side that matters for scheduling. No single local-work quota fits devices that differ by two orders of magnitude in measured throughput.

Tiered participation can assign different amounts of local work while preserving a compatible shared-update contract. A capable phone may complete more steps, while a constrained client contributes a smaller update or a separately defined aggregate statistic. These outputs are not interchangeable simply because they come from the same fleet. More work on strong devices also cannot replace the observations held by weak devices, so the policy must evaluate both computational contribution and population coverage.

The scale challenges extend beyond device heterogeneity to coordination overhead. Federated learning does not run a consensus protocol among millions of clients, nor does hierarchical aggregation perform local consensus. Hierarchical aggregation reduces fan-in through regional aggregators, which validate an eligible cohort and combine its updates before forwarding a partial aggregate; agreement on an ordered replicated log, if needed for the coordinator’s own metadata, remains a separate control-plane service. Edge infrastructure can provide aggregation points, but each added tier creates another trust boundary and another place where weighting, clipping, privacy accounting, and failure recovery must remain consistent.

Tiering interacts with the participation bias analyzed under client scheduling ([[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/03 The Fleet in Production/11 Edge Intelligence/V2-11.08 Federated Learning Systems at Scale#^sec-edge-intelligence-client-scheduling-f675|section 11.7.1]]). Capability-based selection shortens rounds but concentrates completed updates on strong devices, so stratified sampling across tiers must be paired with per-cohort evaluation of completed participation and model quality. Availability also has a daily rhythm; devices that charge overnight in one time zone form a different cohort from those available during another zone’s working hours.

Orchestration brings client scheduling, update compression, personalization, privacy, and tiering into a coherent policy for a single federated round. Deployed systems, however, do not stop after one round, and their devices keep applying local and federated updates week after week.
