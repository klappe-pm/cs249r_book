# V2-03.10 Monitoring and Debugging

[[Home|Home]] · [[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/Volume 2 Overview|Volume Overview]] · [[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/01 The Fleet/03 Network Fabrics/V2-03 Network Fabrics|V2-03 Network Fabrics]]

^sec-network-fabrics-monitoring

Network performance problems in ML clusters are insidious because they show up as **silent waste** rather than explicit failures. A degraded transceiver causing a 10 percent reduction in effective bandwidth might slow each training iteration by only 2–3 percent. The slowdown is small because the lost bandwidth stretches only the exposed communication share of the step (the 30 percent assumed in the oversubscription estimate, less whatever overlap hides), and stretches it by roughly the inverse of the bandwidth that remains. A drift that small is easily masked by the natural variance of checkpointing or data loading. Over a 30-day training run on 1,024 GPUs, this invisible drag accumulates to roughly 15,000–22,000 wasted GPU-hours, burning about \$30,000–\$44,000 at \$2/GPU-hour without triggering a single alarm.

Traditional IT monitoring tools like SNMP or ICMP ping measure connectivity, not the sustained throughput required by RDMA. Effective observability requires a three-layer approach: **application monitoring** (NCCL algorithmic bandwidth), **transport monitoring** (PFC pause frames, retransmission rates), and **physical monitoring** (FEC errors, signal attenuation) ([[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/01 The Fleet/03 Network Fabrics/V2-03.15 Source References#^ref-jeaugey2017nccl|Jeaugey 2017]]; [[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/01 The Fleet/03 Network Fabrics/V2-03.15 Source References#^ref-gangidi2024rdma|Gangidi et al. 2024]]). The three layers retrace the five-level model from the top down. Application monitoring observes the collective that Level 5 integrates, transport monitoring observes the Level 2 recovery and Level 4 congestion machinery, and physical monitoring observes the Level 1 wire. Only by correlating signals across these layers can operators detect that a “slow training run” is caused by a single degraded cable in one rack.

### Link-Level Telemetry

^sec-network-fabrics-link-telemetry

A marginal cable or overheating transceiver rarely drops the link outright; instead, it accumulates symbol errors, triggers transmit discards, or retrains to a slower link state while the job keeps running. Catching that physical drift requires polling switch and NIC port counters, which report transmitted and received data, transmit discards, symbol errors, and link-down events; on InfiniBand a utility such as `perfquery` reads them, and Ethernet/RoCE devices expose the equivalent telemetry through their own management interfaces ([[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/01 The Fleet/03 Network Fabrics/V2-03.15 Source References#^ref-linuxManpages2026perfquery|Linux man-pages project 2026]]). Data counters measure utilization, discards warrant investigation against the port’s traffic and recovery policy, and symbol errors and link-down events are evidence of a degrading signal. Correlating those counters with link rate, lane state, and transport behavior narrows the cause; a counter alone does not identify a defective cable or transceiver.

Polling link state alongside traffic counters can reveal **silent degradation**. A link operating at HDR (200 Gb/s) rather than the expected NDR rate (400 Gb/s) can remain connected while offering half the expected bandwidth. A link that trains with two of its four lanes active after a connector fault fails the same way: it stays up while its bandwidth is halved. Both cases are visible only when negotiated rate and lane state are compared with the deployment baseline.

For 1,024 GPUs and roughly 3,000 links, the assumed 0.1 percent point-in-time degradation probability gives 3 degraded links in expectation and, under independent events, a 95 percent probability of at least one degradation. This probability measures something different from the daily figure in notebook [3.4](https://mlsysbook.ai/vol2/03_network_fabrics/03_network_fabrics.html#nbk-network-fabrics-probability-transceiver-degradation). That notebook asks how often a new degradation begins; this estimate asks how many degraded links are present at any moment. Standing prevalence, the share of links degraded at any moment, is roughly the onset rate times how long a degraded link stays in service, and silent degradation, by definition, stays in service until someone looks, so prevalence can far exceed what a small daily onset rate suggests. Both rates are scenario assumptions rather than measured fleet data.

A job slows when an affected path limits its required traffic; fleet-wide exposure alone does not determine the job’s slowdown. When the degraded link lies on a ring AllReduce’s path, however, the cost reaches every participant, because each round waits for its slowest hop and the whole ring proceeds at the pace of that one link.

↪ Gray failure, the general case of a component that degrades without failing, and why a synchronous job feels it as a straggler, is developed in **[[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/02 Distributed ML/07 Fault Tolerance/V2-07.10 Failure Detection and Recovery#^sec-fault-tolerance-reliability-reliability-distinguishing-stragglers-failures-285d|Stragglers versus fail-stop failures]]**.

### Bandwidth and Latency Validation

^sec-network-fabrics-bw-testing

Clean port counters and an active 50 GB/s NDR link state still do not guarantee that a node pair can deliver that rate to a training job: a degraded PCIe path, a cross-socket NUMA bounce, or a congested spine cut can throttle RDMA transfers without incrementing a single physical error counter. Active validation must therefore measure the bandwidth and latency the job can actually achieve across the complete host-to-host path. Pairwise tests such as `ib_write_bw` can populate an **all-pairs bandwidth matrix**, or a sampled subset for a large fleet ([[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/01 The Fleet/03 Network Fabrics/V2-03.15 Source References#^ref-linuxRdma2026perftest|linux-rdma project 2026]]). A low-throughput pair identifies a path to investigate. Comparing the affected pairs with routing and physical telemetry helps distinguish a shared switch or cable problem from endpoint limitations.

For latency-sensitive synchronization, `ib_write_lat` runs a ping-pong test with small RDMA writes and reports half the measured round-trip time as one-way latency. A reading such as 2 ${\text{µ}}$s, 5 ${\text{µ}}$s, or 100 ${\text{µ}}$s must be compared with a baseline for the same endpoints, message size, path, and load, because no single value is a universal fault threshold or proof that traffic has fallen back to a slower transport. Application-level tests such as `nccl-tests` then exercise collective communication across the intended worker group. Point-to-point results establish a path baseline, while collective tests reveal costs from concurrent traffic and scheduling that isolated pairs cannot expose.

### Systematic Debugging Workflow

^sec-network-fabrics-debug-workflow

When a training job reports lower-than-expected throughput, the diagnostic order should preserve the layer model rather than starting with cables. The following sequence moves from application symptoms toward physical causes through the three monitoring layers, with steps 1 and 2 at the application layer, steps 3 and 4 at the transport layer, and step 5 at the physical layer:

1.  **Check GPU utilization**: Rule out compute bottlenecks with accelerator telemetry (`nvidia-smi`, for example). High SM utilization alone does not rule out a network bottleneck, because communication kernels can also keep SMs active. Correlate utilization with the compute and collective portions of the step timeline. Low utilization does not automatically implicate the fabric, however. A starved input pipeline (data loading or storage) produces the same symptom, so confirm the data path is keeping the accelerators fed before investigating the network.
2.  **Inspect collective-library logs**: Enable the library’s debug output (`NCCL_DEBUG=INFO` in NCCL, for example) to reveal which network transport was selected, the detected bandwidth between nodes, and any fallbacks to slower protocols.
3.  **Run point-to-point tests**: Run a pairwise bandwidth test (such as `ib_write_bw`) between specific nodes in the job. A single degraded link can bottleneck the entire ring in a ring AllReduce.
4.  **Check PFC/ECN counters**: Inspect switch counters along the path. Sustained PFC activity indicates persistent congestion that should be investigated at the scheduler or routing level.
5.  **Validate physical layer**: Check symbol errors and CRC counts to identify failing transceivers or cables.

In this order, application symptoms and collective logs show whether the network is at fault and whether the library fell back to a slower protocol, point-to-point tests identify the failing path, transport counters show whether congestion is involved, and physical telemetry confirms whether the wire itself is degrading. Each layer therefore narrows the search before any hardware is replaced.

**Checkpoint 3.5: Diagnosing a training slowdown**

Your 175B model training job has been running for 3 days on the 1,024-GPU reference cluster. You notice that the iteration time has gradually increased from 4.2 s to 4.8 s (a 14 percent slowdown). The GPU utilization reported by `nvidia-smi` has dropped from 92 percent to 85 percent.

**First diagnostic**: choose the first diagnostic step, name the tool that confirms whether the bottleneck is communication-bound or compute-bound, and cite the evidence that distinguishes the two.

**Physical layer**: identify physical-layer causes that could explain a 50 percent bandwidth drop when `ib_write_bw` finds one node pair at 24 GB/s instead of the expected 48 GB/s, and verify them using switch counters.

**Collective impact**: estimate the impact on overall AllReduce time when the degraded link lies in the ring AllReduce path, then evaluate whether a hierarchical reduction that routes less of the collective through that link would help.
