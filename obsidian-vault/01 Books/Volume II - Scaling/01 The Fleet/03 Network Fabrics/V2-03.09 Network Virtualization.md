# V2-03.09 Network Virtualization

[[Home|Home]] · [[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/Volume 2 Overview|Volume Overview]] · [[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/01 The Fleet/03 Network Fabrics/V2-03 Network Fabrics|V2-03 Network Fabrics]]

^sec-network-fabrics-virtualization

When a single training job leaves racks or spine links idle between runs, operators carve the cluster among multiple tenants and jobs, only to discover that a neighbor’s checkpoint burst or evaluation sweep can starve a synchronous AllReduce across shared NICs and switch buffers. Network virtualization must make sharing compatible with bandwidth allocations, latency targets, and access isolation. These requirements interact because tenants still share physical links and buffers. An operator might reserve 80 percent of the cluster’s ${\text{BW}_{\text{bisect}}}$ for training and allocate the remaining 20 percent to inference and preprocessing. Enforcing that policy requires admission control and compatible scheduling along the relevant paths; a virtual NIC or separate traffic class alone does not guarantee the end-to-end share.

### Hardware NIC Virtualization with SR-IOV

^sec-network-fabrics-sriov

Routing multi-tenant traffic through a hypervisor’s software virtual switch reintroduces the exact host-CPU overhead and microsecond jitter that RDMA was built to bypass. Cloud providers instead expose NIC resources to virtualized GPU instances through **single-root I/O virtualization (SR-IOV)**, a standard that allows a physical NIC to present multiple **virtual functions (VFs)**. Each VF exposes its own hardware queues, doorbell registers (which software writes to tell the NIC that work is waiting), and DMA mappings, and software can assign each VF to a workload. A directly assigned VF can remove host software mediation from the active DMA path, while drivers and virtualization software still configure access and manage the device. Performance depends on the NIC, I/O placement, and competing traffic. Because VFs share physical resources, placement logic must reserve NIC capacity and QoS policy alongside GPUs rather than treat the network as an unbounded pool.

However, hardware-level isolation still needs explicit bandwidth policy. SR-IOV exposes multiple VFs that share the NIC’s physical resources; administrators can add per-VF or per-group QoS policies to cap or guarantee bandwidth, such as assigning eight VFs 50 Gb/s each on a 400 Gb/s NIC. For the 175B-parameter model training on a multi-tenant cluster, that configured partitioning can prevent a neighboring tenant from stealing bandwidth, but it also enforces a hard ceiling on peak throughput. The training job must be architected to operate within this slice, as no amount of software optimization can burst beyond the configured VF limit.

### Traffic Isolation and Quality of Service

^sec-network-fabrics-qos

Per-VF rate caps bound what each tenant injects at the NIC, but traffic from different tenants still merges on the fabric’s shared switch buffers and spine links. When the 175B model is midway through a gradient AllReduce and a neighboring job initiates a 100 GB checkpoint save, that burst occupies a 400 Gb/s port for 2 s at full line rate. It can also claim a large share of the shared spine links for that interval, which stretches every AllReduce whose traffic crosses them. To prevent this noisy-neighbor effect inside the fabric, high-performance switches rely on **quality of service (QoS)** mechanisms that enforce fairness at the packet and queue level.

InfiniBand isolates traffic through virtual lanes (VLs), which maintain separate credit and buffer state within a physical link across up to 16 virtual lanes (one of them reserved for subnet management) ([[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/01 The Fleet/03 Network Fabrics/V2-03.15 Source References#^ref-infiniband2000spec|InfiniBand Trade Association 2000]]). If a storage burst exhausts the credits of its own lane, a gradient lane with available credits can continue transmitting, even though both lanes still share link bandwidth and arbitration. Ethernet and RoCE fabrics achieve a similar separation through device-dependent **traffic class** mappings and queue policies. Within those mappings, **enhanced transmission selection** allocates a share of link bandwidth to each traffic class, and switches may also partition buffer and forwarding resources into **network slices**. Across both stacks, these mechanisms reduce interference only when classification, resource allocation, and scheduling agree along the entire path, because shared priorities or buffer pools can still couple tenants.

Virtualization solves the sharing problem but makes performance diagnosis harder. When a training job slows down on a multi-tenant cluster, the cause could be a physical link degradation, a noisy neighbor exceeding its bandwidth allocation, or a misconfigured QoS policy. Distinguishing these cases requires systematic monitoring.
