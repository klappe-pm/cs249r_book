# V2-03.06 Level 3 Switch and Topology

[[Home|Home]] · [[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/Volume 2 Overview|Volume Overview]] · [[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/01 The Fleet/03 Network Fabrics/V2-03 Network Fabrics|V2-03 Network Fabrics]]

^sec-network-fabrics-topology

A cluster can have thousands of fast links at the edge and still starve its AllReduce operations if the cross-sectional capacity at the narrowest point in the switching hierarchy is insufficient. The physical arrangement of switches determines whether that cross-section throttles collective communication or sustains full line-rate exchange across all participating workers. Two properties govern this capability: bisection bandwidth ${\text{BW}_{\text{bisect}}}$ and whether the fabric is non-blocking. The bisection bandwidth theorem (principle [[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/01 The Fleet/V2 Fleet Principles#^pri-bisection-bandwidth|6]]) captures the constraint, stating that traffic crossing a cut is limited by the aggregate bandwidth of that cut. Bisection bandwidth quantifies the worst-case throughput ceiling that the topology imposes on global collectives.

**Definition 3.4: Bisection bandwidth**

***Bisection bandwidth*** is a network topology metric defined as the minimum aggregate link capacity crossing any partition that divides the cluster into two equal halves, representing the worst-case throughput ceiling for traffic that must cross that partition, including global collectives.

1.  **Significance**: Bisection bandwidth ${\text{BW}_{\text{bisect}}}$ directly sets the cluster-scale bandwidth ceiling for global synchronization. A 1,024-GPU fat-tree with 400 Gb/s links at 1:1 subscription provides $\text{BW}_{\text{bisect}} = 512 \times 50\,\text{GB/s} = 25.6\,\text{TB/s}$ per direction; a 4:1 oversubscribed spine reduces this to 6.4 TB/s, increasing the transfer-time lower bound for a fixed cut-crossing payload by 4×. Actual AllReduce slowdown depends on rank placement, the collective schedule, and which links are saturated.
2.  **Distinction**: Unlike aggregate bandwidth (the sum of all edge link speeds, which can be high even in a poorly connected topology), ${\text{BW}_{\text{bisect}}}$ measures global connectivity. A star topology with 1,000 edge links all meeting at one central switch has high aggregate bandwidth but ${\text{BW}_{\text{bisect}}}$ limited by that switch’s backplane capacity.
3.  **Common pitfall**: A frequent misconception is that adding more leaf switches always increases ${\text{BW}_{\text{bisect}}}$. In a three-tier fat-tree, oversubscribing the spine layer (giving each spine switch fewer uplinks to the core than downlinks to its leaves) reduces ${\text{BW}_{\text{bisect}}}$ below the edge-link total regardless of how many leaf switches are present.

ML training includes global collectives and AllToAll phases that can drive traffic across topology partitions, making full ${\text{BW}_{\text{bisect}}}$ a useful capacity target. A smaller cut becomes a bottleneck when the workload’s crossing traffic exceeds what it can deliver within the communication budget, and rank placement together with the collective schedule determines how much traffic crosses that cut.

Bisection bandwidth measures cut capacity; a non-blocking fabric provides paths that can sustain simultaneous input-output pairings at line rate with suitable routing. Full ${\text{BW}_{\text{bisect}}}$ and balanced uplink capacity support that goal, but routing must also spread traffic across the available paths. In the oversubscribed designs considered here, ${\text{BW}_{\text{bisect}}}$ shrinks by a factor equal to the oversubscription ratio. The transfer-time bound for a fixed payload that saturates the cut rises by that same factor, whereas a collective that keeps most traffic within each half need not experience the same slowdown.

↪ A slower cut lengthens the training step only when the extra transfer time cannot hide behind computation. The communication-to-compute diagnostic that tests this is developed in **[[05 Reference/Volume II - Scaling/V2 The C³ Taxonomy#^sec-appdx-c3-taxonomy-comp-comm|Compute cap communication]]**.

**Definition 3.5: Non-blocking fabric**

***Non-blocking fabric*** is a network with sufficient internal path capacity to support any permutation of input-output port pairs simultaneously at full line rate under a suitable routing assignment.

1.  **Significance**: In ML fleets, a non-blocking fabric can support full-rate permutation traffic with suitable routing, preserving the available ${\text{BW}_{\text{bisect}}}$ term for global collectives. A 2:1 oversubscribed spine, a common data-center compromise, halves the effective ${\text{BW}_{\text{bisect}}}$, doubling the communication time when that cut is the saturated bottleneck and dropping scaling efficiency ${\eta_{\text{scaling}}}$ accordingly. In a scenario workload assumed to spend 30 percent of each step communicating, doubling that share while the computation share stays fixed lengthens each step, and the longer step costs roughly 23.1 percent of total cluster throughput. The bisection definition above and notebook [3.2](https://mlsysbook.ai/vol2/03_network_fabrics/03_network_fabrics.html#nbk-network-fabrics-bisection-bandwidth-cost-oversubscription) use 4:1 instead, a cost-optimized spine; the notebook writes out the throughput formula behind both results.
2.  **Distinction**: The guarantee concerns a permutation, in which each input and output belongs to one pairing. It does not promise full line rate from every sender to every receiver at once, nor does it make an arbitrary routing assignment free of contention.
3.  **Common pitfall**: A frequent misconception is that non-blocking means zero congestion. Endpoint congestion (incast) can still occur if multiple senders simultaneously target the same receiver port, regardless of how much internal fabric capacity is available.

Fat-trees build on Clos-style non-blocking network principles to supply full ${\text{BW}_{\text{bisect}}}$ ([[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/01 The Fleet/03 Network Fabrics/V2-03.15 Source References#^ref-clos1953|Clos 1953]]), rail-optimized networks trade some global routing flexibility for lower cabling cost, and dragonfly topologies reduce optical link counts while introducing workload-placement constraints ([[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/01 The Fleet/03 Network Fabrics/V2-03.15 Source References#^ref-kim2008|Kim et al. 2008]]). This trade-off between guaranteed bisection bandwidth and cabling cost drives every topology decision in large-scale ML clusters, as the comparisons in [[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/01 The Fleet/03 Network Fabrics/V2-03.06 Level 3 Switch and Topology#^sec-network-fabrics-fat-tree|section 3.5.2]], [[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/01 The Fleet/03 Network Fabrics/V2-03.06 Level 3 Switch and Topology#^sec-network-fabrics-rail-optimized|section 3.5.3]], and [[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/01 The Fleet/03 Network Fabrics/V2-03.06 Level 3 Switch and Topology#^sec-network-fabrics-dragonfly|section 3.5.4]] show, and every switched design begins at the first-hop switch that aggregates a rack or rail of servers.

### Top-Of-Rack (ToR) and the Failure Domain

^sec-network-fabrics-tor

Consider a rack containing four nodes with eight GPUs each: if all 32 accelerators attach to a single top-of-rack (ToR) switch with no alternate path, losing that switch disconnects the entire rack from the job at once. Because the ToR aggregates every server link in the rack, it becomes a shared failure domain, one level of the physical hierarchy defined in **[[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/01 The Fleet/02 Compute Infrastructure/V2-02.06 The Node#^sec-compute-infrastructure-node-health-reliability-56a6|Node health and reliability]]**, and recovery then depends on available paths, surviving state, and the job’s recovery policy. This one-switch-per-rack wiring is the simpler baseline used here to teach radix and failure domains. The reference cluster itself wires each GPU to a rail switch, a layout developed in [[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/01 The Fleet/03 Network Fabrics/V2-03.06 Level 3 Switch and Topology#^sec-network-fabrics-rail-optimized|section 3.5.3]]. That layout reshapes the failure domain, because losing one rail switch cuts one GPU in each of many nodes rather than every GPU in one rack.

The same boundary shapes state placement. To survive a rack-wide outage, a job needs recoverable state outside that rack and a way to resume on available capacity. Topology-aware scheduling must therefore consider both fast communication paths and the failures that several nearby resources share.

↪ Nested failure domains, and why redundant state must span them to survive a correlated rack or ToR outage, are developed in **[[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/02 Distributed ML/07 Fault Tolerance/V2-07.04 Failure Taxonomy#^sec-fault-tolerance-reliability-reliability-correlated-failures-95e8|Correlated failures]]**.

The switch **radix**,[[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/01 The Fleet/03 Network Fabrics/V2-03.16 Footnotes#^fn5|⁵]] its number of ports, determines how many endpoints and uplinks it can connect. In the reference allocation, a 64-port switch assigns 32 ports to servers, one for each of 32 GPUs, and 32 ports to the spine. With equal port rates, the 1:1 ratio avoids oversubscription at that boundary; delivered throughput still depends on forwarding and the paths beyond it. The 1,024-GPU cluster therefore requires approximately 32 such switches, although the physical rack count also depends on node density and the cabling layout.

### Fat-Tree (Clos) Networks

^sec-network-fabrics-fat-tree

The ToR provides non-blocking bandwidth within a single rack, but connecting the reference cluster’s 32 first-hop switches into a cluster that preserves full ${\text{BW}_{\text{bisect}}}$ across every possible communication pair requires a topology that scales cross-sectional capacity with cluster size. The fat-tree (also called a Clos network) achieves this by adding parallel switches at each tier above the leaves, so the aggregate uplink capacity always matches the total edge bandwidth feeding into it. In this vocabulary, leaf switches attach servers or racks, spine switches connect leaves within a pod, and core switches connect multiple pods when a third tier is needed; other sources call the leaf and spine tiers edge and aggregation. A pod here is a switching domain, the group of leaves that one set of spines joins, which can be narrower than the multi-rack pod of **[[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/01 The Fleet/02 Compute Infrastructure/V2-02.08 The Pod#^sec-compute-pod|The Pod]]**. A multi-rack pod in that sense can contain several fat-tree pods.

**Definition 3.6: Fat-tree**

***Fat-tree*** is a hierarchical ML cluster network topology in which the number of parallel paths (and therefore aggregate cross-sectional capacity) increases at each switch tier toward the spine, providing full ${\text{BW}_{\text{bisect}}}$ and multiple equal-cost routes between any two nodes ([[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/01 The Fleet/03 Network Fabrics/V2-03.15 Source References#^ref-al-fares2008|Al-Fares et al. 2008]]).

1.  **Significance**: A ${k}$-ary fat-tree built from radix-${k}$ switches supports ${k}^2/2$ hosts in a standalone two-tier configuration and ${k}^3/4$ hosts in a three-tier configuration with full ${\text{BW}_{\text{bisect}}}$. With 64-port switches, a standalone two-tier fabric supports 2,048 GPUs and a three-tier fabric supports 65,536 hosts. This is a capacity bound for suitable routing and admissible traffic; it does not eliminate endpoint contention or guarantee that a particular AllReduce implementation reaches line rate.
2.  **Distinction**: Unlike a standard tree (where bandwidth at the root is a single bottleneck shared by all leaves), a fat-tree replaces each root with multiple spine switches whose combined uplink capacity matches the total edge bandwidth, eliminating the bottleneck.
3.  **Common pitfall**: A frequent misconception is that fat-trees guarantee zero network cost. They require $\mathcal{O}({N}\log {N})$ switches and dense cabling, so a non-blocking three-tier fat-tree at the 4,000-GPU scale needs hundreds of switches and more than ten thousand links before a single accelerator is purchased.

A three-tier radix-4 fabric (${k}=4$) shows the non-blocking structure at a scale small enough to draw ([[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/01 The Fleet/03 Network Fabrics/V2-03.06 Level 3 Switch and Topology#^fig-fat-tree-detail|figure 9]]), with four core switches interconnecting four dashed pod modules. Leaf and spine switches split their ports between the tiers below and above; core switches use all their ports to connect pods. Each tier boundary therefore carries 16 links. The highlighted route traverses five switches and is one of $({k}/2)^2 = 4$ equal-cost routes from a GPU in Pod 1 to Pod 4. Parallel paths supply the capacity, while routing must distribute traffic across them to use it.

![[90 Assets/Volume II/03 Network Fabrics/Fat Tree Detail 55d9e1f278.svg]]

Figure 9: **Non-blocking Fat-Tree Topology**: A three-tier fat-tree built from radix-4 switches (${k}=4$) connects ${k}^3/4 = 16$ GPUs through four pods. Leaf and spine switches split their ports equally between the tiers below and above; core switches use all their ports to connect pods. Each tier boundary carries 16 links, supporting full ${\text{BW}_{\text{bisect}}}$ with suitable routing. The highlighted leaf-spine-core-spine-leaf route between pod 1 and pod 4 is one of $({k}/2)^2 = 4$ equal-cost paths (the other three are drawn lighter), and exploiting those alternatives without excessive reordering requires compatible switch and endpoint mechanisms.

^fig-fat-tree-detail

The fat-tree[[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/01 The Fleet/03 Network Fabrics/V2-03.16 Footnotes#^fn6|⁶]] is a common default for ML clusters because a non-blocking design can provide full ${\text{BW}_{\text{bisect}}}$ across arbitrary partitions. This capacity benefits global AllReduce even though implementations such as rings and trees do not require every pair of ranks to communicate at once. The number of hosts that the guarantee can cover depends on switch radix and tier count.

A fat-tree built from switches with radix ${k}$ supports ${N_{\text{hosts}}}= {k}^{3}/4$ hosts at three tiers. A standalone two-tier non-blocking fabric can instead use ${k}$ leaves, each with ${k}/2$ host ports and ${k}/2$ uplinks, connected to ${k}/2$ spines. Each spine uses all ${k}$ ports to reach the leaves, providing ${k}^{2}/2 =$ 2,048 host ports for radix-64 switches. In the three-tier design, each spine switch reserves half of its ports for the core tier and uses the other ${k}/2$ ports to connect to leaves, so one pod has ${k}/2$ leaves and $({k}/2)^2 =$ 1,024 host ports. Because each radix-${k}$ core switch connects one port to each pod, the core tier can join up to ${k}$ pods for the full ${k} \times ({k}/2)^2 = {k}^3/4$ host capacity. These are different port allocations and switch counts rather than two ways of counting the same fabric, and while the 1,024-GPU reference cluster fits within a standalone two-tier design, the distinction matters as soon as a cluster outgrows that limit.

A 4,096-GPU deployment cannot join two fully populated standalone 2,048-host fabrics at their spines, because every spine port is already occupied by a leaf connection. Instead, it is built as four 1,024-host pods of the three-tier design, joined through a core tier. A concrete bisection estimate (notebook [3.2](https://mlsysbook.ai/vol2/03_network_fabrics/03_network_fabrics.html#nbk-network-fabrics-bisection-bandwidth-cost-oversubscription)) shows how quickly oversubscription at that spine or core boundary turns into synchronization delay.

**Napkin Math 3.2: Bisection bandwidth: The cost of oversubscription**

**Problem**: A cluster designer is choosing between a “non-blocking” (1:1) fat-tree and a “cost-optimized” (4:1) spine for a \$300M cluster of 1024 GPUs. This is a separate economics scenario rather than the running 175B job: each GPU contributes a 5 GB gradient payload, and the workload is assumed to spend ${f}=$ 30 percent of each step communicating. That share is a scenario input. It is larger than the 13 percent of the illustrative schedule in [[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/01 The Fleet/03 Network Fabrics/V2-03.02 The Synchronization Backbone#^fig-bandwidth-cliff-gantt|figure 2]], and neither is a measured share of the reference job. How much slower will the AllReduce be on the cheaper network, and what does that cost?

**Math**: Bisection bandwidth (${\text{BW}_{\text{bisect}}}$) is the minimum pipe diameter between halves of the cluster. Assume a unidirectional ring whose ranks alternate between the two halves of the cluster. Every ring edge then crosses the cut, with ${N_{\text{GPU}}}/2$ senders in each direction. Assume balanced routing, no competing traffic, and negligible startup latency. ReduceScatter plus AllGather contributes a traffic factor $2({N_{\text{GPU}}}-1)/{N_{\text{GPU}}}$ = 1.998 per GPU; multiplying by ${N_{\text{GPU}}}/2$ gives the bytes crossing the cut in each direction. With a 5 GB payload, a traffic factor of 1.998, and 512 senders per direction, 5,115 GB crosses the cut. A ring that groups ranks within each half would have fewer crossing edges and a different cut-load bound.

1.  **Non-blocking (1:1)**: ${\text{BW}_{\text{bisect}}} =$ 512 $\times$ 50 GB/s $=$ 25,600 GB/s per direction.
    - Time: 5,115 GB / 25,600 GB/s $\approx$ 0.200 s.
2.  **Oversubscribed (4:1)**: ${\text{BW}_{\text{bisect}}} =$ 25,600 GB/s divided by 4 = 6,400 GB/s per direction.
    - Time: 5,115 GB / 6,400 GB/s $\approx$ 0.799 s.
3.  **Throughput**: If the communication share stretches by the slowdown factor ${r}$ while computation stays fixed, then $\text{relative throughput} = \frac{1}{(1-{f}) + {f}\,{r}} = \frac{1}{0.70 + 0.30 \times 4} = \frac{1}{1.90} \approx 0.53$, a loss of about 47.4 percent of cluster throughput.
4.  **Cost**: That loss times the \$300M capital cost is about \$142.1M.

**Systems insight**: For this cut-saturating placement, saving money on spine switches creates a 4× bottleneck for global synchronization, and on a \$300M cluster that bottleneck idles about \$142.1M of compute. The same formula gives the 23.1 percent loss of the 2:1 spine in the non-blocking definition. This is an equivalent-capacity cost under the stated workload assumptions, not a direct equipment loss. Oversubscription must be evaluated against the collective traffic actually crossing the constrained cut.

⇄ **Contrast:** The GPT-3 synchronization example in **[[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/00 Introduction/01 Introduction/V2-01.06 The C³ Taxonomy and Foundations of Scale#^sec-introduction-energyscale-invariant-a0d6|The energy accounting boundary]]** assumes pure data parallelism and no overlap, so its exposed communication share far exceeds the share assumed here.

The subscription ratio at each tier therefore determines whether a fat-tree can sustain global collectives, and the tier count determines how far it can grow. Scaling beyond the 2,048-host two-tier limit requires a three-tier architecture with core switches, enabling the fabric to reach 65,536 hosts, but the added scale carries both cost and latency. Each of the ${k}$ pods contains ${k}/2$ leaf switches and ${k}/2$ spine switches (${k}^2$ switches across all pods), and their ${k}/2$ spines each send ${k}/2$ uplinks into $({k}/2)^2 = {k}^2/4$ core switches. A non-blocking ${k}=64$ three-tier tree therefore requires $5{k}^2/4 =$ 5,120 switches across the core, spine, and leaf layers. At \$10,000 to \$50,000 per switch, the switching layer alone represents \$51M to \$256M. Typical hop counts also rise, from two inter-switch links and three switch traversals for intra-pod traffic (leaf-spine-leaf) to four inter-switch links and five switch traversals for inter-pod traffic (leaf-spine-core-spine-leaf), adding serialization delay and switch processing time to the ${\alpha}$ latency term across thousands of synchronization steps. Rail-optimized topologies respond to both pressures by matching the physical cabling pattern to the collective communication pattern.

### Rail-Optimized Topology

^sec-network-fabrics-rail-optimized

When tensor-parallel groups stay inside each node’s high-bandwidth NVSwitch domain, data parallelism exchanges gradients only between GPUs that hold the same model shard in the same local slot position across nodes, yet a standard fat-tree wires all eight GPUs of a node into a shared ToR switch. A **rail-optimized topology** instead cables external network ports by local slot position ([[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/01 The Fleet/03 Network Fabrics/V2-03.06 Level 3 Switch and Topology#^fig-rail-optimized-cabling|figure 10]]): GPU 0 in every node connects to the Rail 0 switch, GPU 1 connects to Rail 1, and so on across the cluster.

![[90 Assets/Volume II/03 Network Fabrics/Rail Optimized c0dc57dafe.svg]]

Figure 10: **Rail-Optimized Physical Wiring**: In dense accelerator nodes such as the DGX H100, each GPU’s network port is cabled by local slot position: GPU 0 in every node connects to the rail 0 switch, GPU 1 to the rail 1 switch, and so on. Three nodes and four of each node’s eight GPUs are drawn, with the rail 0 cables emphasized; further nodes attach to the same rail switches. Tensor-parallel traffic stays on each node’s NVSwitch, same-rank data-parallel traffic stays on its own rail, and a spine layer (not drawn) bridges the rails for cross-rank traffic.

^fig-rail-optimized-cabling

This slot-aligned wiring exploits the stratified traffic that 3D parallelism produces. Because each node holds one full tensor-parallel group, with one shard on each GPU, each data-parallel replica assigns the corresponding model shard to the same local GPU slot (where the slot number is a local rank within a node, distinct from the worker’s global rank in the distributed job). Replicas are identical copies of one layout, so nodes that hold the same pipeline stage hold the same slice of the model in a given slot. During data-parallel AllReduce, GPU 0 on one node synchronizes its shard’s gradients with GPU 0 on other nodes, but rarely communicates with GPU 1. Isolating each local rank onto its own rail switch fabric removes same-rank traffic from shared rack switches and, when a rail fits on a single switch, shortens the path between corresponding GPUs. The isolation holds however a rail is built, whereas the shorter path does not.

**Napkin Math 3.3: The rail-optimized dividend**

**Problem**: A team is synchronizing per-rank data-parallel gradients across 128 nodes. In a standard fat-tree, each message between same-rank GPUs traverses its source leaf switch, a spine switch, and the destination leaf switch (3 switch traversals). In a rail-optimized network built from rail switches with at least one port per node, all corresponding GPUs sit on the same rail switch (one traversal). On the reference cluster that requires rail switches with more ports than the radix-64 switch used so far, so the estimate describes a higher-radix design. How much latency dividend does the rail design earn when a rail fits on one switch?

**Math**: This estimate counts only the switch-traversal contribution to communication latency (${\alpha}$), using the same assumed delay for each traversal.

1.  **Standard latency**: 3 traversals $\times$ 0.6 μs = 1.8 μs.
2.  **Rail-optimized**: 1 traversal $\times$ 0.6 μs = 0.6 μs.
3.  **Result**: 3× lower latency.

**Systems insight**: For synchronous data-parallel AllReduce, this 3× reduction applies to the modeled switch-latency contribution, not to total collective time. Its effect on scaling efficiency (${\eta_{\text{scaling}}}$) depends on payload size, delivered bandwidth, compute time, and overlap. The single-switch rail in this example avoids the spine traversal for same-rank traffic. The dividend is conditional. On the reference cluster’s radix-64 switches each rail outgrows one switch and regains the extra traversals, so what rails reliably buy there is isolation, developed below.

The hop saving is only part of the case for rails. Large language model fleets adopt the pattern because it matches how their parallelism plan divides traffic.

**Lighthouse 3.1: Archetype A (GPT-4/Llama-3): The rail-optimized fleet**

Archetype A’s use of 3D parallelism generates two distinct traffic patterns that pull in opposite directions: (1) large, bandwidth-hungry gradient averaging for data parallelism, and (2) high-frequency, latency-sensitive activation exchanges for tensor parallelism. Keeping tensor parallelism inside each node’s NVLink domain leaves data-parallel gradient traffic on the fabric, and rails give each same-rank data-parallel group its own switch network. A rail can provide a single switch hop between nodes when its endpoint count fits the available switch ports. Larger rails require additional switching stages, so rail alignment alone does not guarantee single-hop communication. Either way, the lesson for Archetype A is that how the fabric is wired to the parallelism plan matters as much as how many accelerators it connects.

For the cluster of 1,024 GPUs spanning 128 nodes, this creates eight parallel networks of 128 GPUs each, requiring at least one switch port per endpoint in each rail. A single-switch realization therefore needs a radix at least as large as the rail endpoint count; the earlier radix-64 switch cannot realize this rail alone, which is why notebook [3.3](https://mlsysbook.ai/vol2/03_network_fabrics/03_network_fabrics.html#nbk-network-fabrics-rail-optimized-dividend) assumed higher-radix rail switches. Built from radix-64 switches instead, each rail becomes a small two-tier fabric that preserves rank alignment but adds hops. For the bandwidth-hungry gradient exchanges of data parallelism, the larger benefit is isolation: each rail carries only its own rank’s traffic, so the eight rail networks operate concurrently without competing for shared spine capacity. When a rail fits on one switch, the shorter path also trims the startup cost a ring pays on every round.

That same isolation creates a sharp trade-off, because traffic that must reach GPUs of different ranks requires bridging across rails. Such traffic includes expert routing in a mixture of experts (MoE) model, which sends each token to the few expert subnetworks chosen for it, and pipeline-stage handoffs that do not align with the rail wiring. Large clusters therefore often employ a hybrid approach, using rail-optimized leaves for same-rank data-parallel traffic while bridging the rails with a full fat-tree spine to support cross-rank communication patterns.

↪ Expert parallelism, in which each rank hosts a different subset of experts and tokens travel to them by AllToAll, is developed in **[[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/02 Distributed ML/06 Distributed Training/V2-06.06 Model Parallelism#^sec-distributed-training-systems-systems-expert-parallelism-mixture-experts-bc45|Expert parallelism (mixture of experts)]]**.

### Dragonfly and Torus Alternatives

^sec-network-fabrics-dragonfly

At tens of thousands of endpoints, a non-blocking three-tier fat-tree forces a third layer of core switches and tens of thousands of long optical cables into the budget, driving network capital expenditure into the tens or hundreds of millions of dollars. Two alternative topologies attack that cost wall from opposite directions: tapering the global switch tier or removing external fabric switches altogether.

A **dragonfly**[[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/01 The Fleet/03 Network Fabrics/V2-03.16 Footnotes#^fn7|⁷]] topology organizes high-radix routers (the dragonfly literature’s name for switches) into fully connected groups joined by global optical links. Each group has ${a}$ routers, and each router has ${p}$ terminal ports and ${h}$ global channels. The ratio of ${h}$ to ${p}$ sets how much global bandwidth each group receives relative to the traffic its terminals can inject, so global bandwidth is a design parameter rather than a fixed percentage of aggregate injection bandwidth. The balanced configuration analyzed by Kim et al. ([[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/01 The Fleet/03 Network Fabrics/V2-03.15 Source References#^ref-kim2008|2008]]), ${a}=2{p}=2{h}$, is one setting of that knob. A tapered dragonfly gives each router fewer global channels than terminal ports, trading cut capacity for fewer long optical links. The routers in a group are fully connected, and the groups are joined by global links. A minimal route between two groups therefore takes at most one local hop to the router holding the right global link, one global hop, and one local hop in the destination group. No minimal path exceeds three router-to-router hops, regardless of system size. Short paths come cheaply, but performance for jobs spanning groups depends on the chosen ${p}/{h}$ ratio, routing, and traffic matrix, because every inter-group transfer shares the provisioned global links.

A **torus** topology connects each node directly to its neighbors in a multidimensional grid, most commonly a 3D torus where every node links to its six adjacent peers (up/down, left/right, front/back). The design offers full local bandwidth with minimal switching hardware, because every neighbor is a single hop away. However, global communication requires traversing the diameter of the mesh ($\mathcal{O}({N}^{1/3})$ hops), making latency scale poorly with cluster size. Google adopted this architecture for its Tensor Processing Unit (TPU) pods because the collectives that dominate its training workloads can be scheduled as rings along each torus dimension, a nearest-neighbor pattern that maps naturally onto the physical grid. The limitation becomes apparent with MoE, which relies on AllToAll communication patterns. On a torus, this traffic can congest the limited ${\text{BW}_{\text{bisect}}}$ of the mesh and degrade performance relative to a non-blocking switch fabric.

The trade-off becomes concrete for the 4,096-endpoint deployment that the three-tier fat-tree reached with four pods. Arranged as a $16 \times 16 \times 16$ torus with wraparound links, the same endpoints need no external switching stages. Splitting the cube in half across one axis cuts a $16 \times 16$ plane of interior links and an equal plane of wraparound links, so only $2 \times 16^2 =$ 512 links cross the bisection, against 2,048 in the non-blocking fat-tree. With 50 GB/s per link and direction, the torus offers 25.6 TB/s across the cut against the fat-tree’s 102.4 TB/s, a 4× gap. Because wraparound links cap the distance along each 16-node axis at 8 hops, the torus’s worst-case path across all three axes crosses $3 \times 8 =$ 24 neighbor hops against the fat-tree’s five switch traversals. Because a cubic torus’s cut grows only as ${N}^{2/3}$ while its endpoint count grows as ${N}$, that capacity gap widens at fixed link rate.

Whether the gap matters depends on the traffic. A ring AllReduce scheduled along each torus dimension moves data only between neighbors and combines contributions before forwarding them, so it leans little on the narrow cut. An MoE AllToAll or an embedding exchange among a recommendation model’s table shards instead sends a large share of its traffic between distant endpoints and drives it straight into the cut, with the exact pressure set by placement and message sizes. Consequently, the collective schedule and traffic matrix determine whether the torus’s lower cross-sectional capacity limits completion time.

↪ AllToAll schedules and their cost at scale, where every rank sends a distinct share to every other rank, are developed in **[[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/02 Distributed ML/05 Collective Communication/V2-05.09 AllToAll Exchanges at Scale#^sec-communication-alltoall|AllToAll Exchanges at Scale]]**.

At the scale of the 1,024-GPU reference cluster, the four families range in cut capacity from 25.6 TB/s in the non-blocking fat-tree down to 6.4 TB/s in a 3D torus, while an assumed interconnect cost per Gb/s of bisection bandwidth runs from \$1.5/Gb/s for the switchless torus to \$4/Gb/s for the tapered dragonfly ([[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/01 The Fleet/03 Network Fabrics/V2-03.06 Level 3 Switch and Topology#^fig-network-topologies|figure 11]]). The cheapest capacity is also the least capacity, which is the trade every topology decision prices. The dragonfly’s assumed cost per Gb/s is the highest here because its savings come from replacing long optical runs and switch tiers at large scale, which Kim et al. ([[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/01 The Fleet/03 Network Fabrics/V2-03.15 Source References#^ref-kim2008|2008]]) reports for systems of at least 16,000 terminals; at 1,024 GPUs it has few such runs to replace. The rail’s 12.8 TB/s applies to cross-rail traffic only, because same-rank traffic stays on its own rail. Radix, endpoint count, link speed, routing mechanisms, and equipment pricing all shift these trade-offs; the topology family alone dictates neither raw cross-sectional capacity nor total cost.

### Optical Circuit Switching and Reconfigurable Topology

^sec-network-fabrics-ocs

Every topology compared in [[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/01 The Fleet/03 Network Fabrics/V2-03.06 Level 3 Switch and Topology#^fig-network-topologies|figure 11]] is fixed when the facility is cabled, locking the cluster into a single physical graph regardless of which workload runs on it. A statically cabled Clos hierarchy ([[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/01 The Fleet/03 Network Fabrics/V2-03.06 Level 3 Switch and Topology#^sec-network-fabrics-fat-tree|section 3.5.2]]) fixes the oversubscription ratio and bisection pattern whether the active job is pipeline-dominated, data-parallel, or AllToAll expert-routed. A statically cabled torus ([[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/01 The Fleet/03 Network Fabrics/V2-03.06 Level 3 Switch and Topology#^sec-network-fabrics-dragonfly|section 3.5.4]]) is equally rigid: it fits dimension-ordered rings, but it congests under MoE AllToAll traffic and cannot rewire its links to match the job.

Switching those multi-tier fabrics electrically also adds a recurring power cost at every hop. Each electrical packet switch inspects headers and chooses an outgoing link only after an Optical-Electrical-Optical (OEO) conversion: incoming photons hit a photodiode, high-speed SerDes circuits deserialize the electrical signal, switch ASICs buffer and route packets across crossbars, and output SerDes drive lasers to re-transmit light over optical fiber. At 800 Gb/s and 1.6 Tb/s per-port data rates, SerDes and transceiver electronics consume significant energy ([[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/01 The Fleet/03 Network Fabrics/V2-03.04 Level 1 Wire and Link#^sec-network-fabrics-serdes|section 3.3.3]]), and every additional switch tier repeats that conversion cost for each bit that crosses it. Changing the physical connections between node groups in light, for the duration of a workload allocation rather than packet by packet, avoids both static wiring and per-hop OEO conversion.

**Definition 3.7: Optical circuit switching**

***Optical circuit switching (OCS)*** is a physical networking architecture that routes optical signals directly using Micro-Electro-Mechanical Systems (MEMS) mirror arrays without Optical-Electrical-Optical (OEO) conversion, enabling dynamic, software-defined topology reconfiguration and high energy efficiency at warehouse scale.

1.  **Significance**: An optical circuit bypasses packet-by-packet electronic processing in the switching path. The complete system still consumes power for actuators, control, and transceivers. Its compatibility across line rates depends on optical loss, wavelengths, and endpoint budgets, so protocol transparency does not imply unrestricted upgrades.
2.  **Distinction**: Unlike electrical packet switches (which inspect headers, buffer packets in SRAM, and make nanosecond routing decisions per packet), an OCS establishes semi-static physical optical circuits with millisecond-scale switching latency ($10$–$30$ ms), making it suitable for topology-level reconfiguration rather than dynamic packet routing.
3.  **Common pitfall**: Assuming OCS replaces packet switching entirely. OCS operates at circuit granularity: it configures cluster-scale topologies (such as 3D tori or job-specific sub-clusters) and routes around hardware faults, while packet switching or accelerator-native interconnects handle fine-grained, intra-step message routing.

Google’s TPU v4 supercomputer ([[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/01 The Fleet/03 Network Fabrics/V2-03.15 Source References#^ref-jouppi2023|Jouppi et al. 2023]]) deployed optical circuit switching in production. Instead of wiring 4,096 chips into an inflexible electrical fat-tree, the system groups accelerators into $4{\times}4{\times}4$ node cubes and routes the inter-cube links through an array of 3D MEMS optical circuit switches (codenamed Palomar).

The optical layer serves two purposes, adapting the topology to the workload and recovering from faults. For workload adaptation, the physical interconnect can be reconfigured to match the communication structure of the running model. A training run using 1D pipeline parallelism, whose stages form a line, can stretch its slice along one long axis to streamline stage-to-stage transfers. Conversely, a 2D or 3D tensor-parallel partition, which splits each tensor across a grid of chips, can configure a compact cubic 3D torus that minimizes average hop count, while an MoE workload can maximize bisection bandwidth across specific expert groups.

Optical switching also supports fault isolation by changing connections around unavailable hardware where spare connectivity permits. The capability matters because in a statically cabled torus, a single failed link or node can break a ring along an entire dimension and degrade every job that spans it. With optical circuit switching, the mirrors can be redirected to bypass the failed cube or fiber and splice healthy cubes into a complete, smaller torus slice without manual recabling. The fabric manager must first detect the fault, choose a feasible allocation, and establish the new circuits, which means circuit-switching time is only one part of recovery; a training job may also need to rebuild its process groups and restore state before computation resumes, so optical reconfiguration latency alone does not specify recovery time.

The trade-off is switching latency. Because MEMS mirrors physically rotate to steer collimated light beams, reconfiguration occurs on a mechanical timescale that is much longer than packet forwarding in an established electrical path. Circuit setup changes connectivity, while packet forwarding uses connectivity already in place. Consequently, OCS raises the power and protocol ceiling of the switching layer while leaving reconfiguration latency as the limiting constraint, so it is deployed for job-level topology allocation and fault isolation, not for per-message dynamic routing. Within that limit, OCS turns the choice of topology from a decision fixed when the fabric is cabled into one made for each job.

Each topology comparison so far has considered one traffic pattern at a time. In practice, clusters serve multiple workloads with different communication patterns, and topology selection must balance their competing demands.

**Checkpoint 3.4: Topology selection for your workload**

The choice of network topology dictates the upper bound of training efficiency. Warm up by matching each single-workload pattern to its ideal topology, then design a fabric that must serve several at once.

**Single-workload picks**

**AllReduce bandwidth**: select the topology that maximizes ${\text{BW}_{\text{bisect}}}$ for a standard data-parallel job dominated by AllReduce.

**MoE traffic**: select the topology that best sustains a mixture-of-experts model’s AllToAll-dominant, bursty communication, in which any rank may send to any other.

**Neighbor locality**: select the topology that best fits a ring AllReduce scheduled along each dimension of the machine, where every transfer goes to a physical neighbor.

**Designing for a mixed cluster**

You are designing the network for a new ML cluster that will run two primary workloads: (1) training a 175B-parameter language model using 3D parallelism (tensor, pipeline, and data parallelism), and (2) serving a mixture-of-experts model that relies heavily on AllToAll communication to route tokens to the correct experts.

**Training topology**: select the topology (fat-tree, rail-optimized, or hybrid) that best matches tensor parallelism within groups of 8 GPUs and data parallelism across all nodes, then justify the choice.

**MoE topology**: revise the topology choice for unpredictable AllToAll traffic where any GPU may need to communicate with any other GPU.

**Oversubscription risk**: identify which workload suffers more under 2:1 spine-layer oversubscription and propose a scheduling strategy to mitigate the impact.

Topology provides the structural capacity for the cluster of 1,024 GPUs to communicate, but structure alone does not guarantee performance. When the 1,024 GPUs synchronize contributions to the model’s 350 GB gradient state, that theoretical capacity collides with the reality of **fabric behavior**: congestion control and routing dynamics determine whether this traffic flows smoothly or gridlocks under the strict synchronization of the BSP model.
