# V2-03.04 Level 1 Wire and Link

[[Home|Home]] · [[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/Volume 2 Overview|Volume Overview]] · [[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/01 The Fleet/03 Network Fabrics/V2-03 Network Fabrics|V2-03 Network Fabrics]]

^sec-network-fabrics-wire-link

At 400 Gb/s and beyond, the physics of signal transmission imposes hard limits on cable length, power consumption, and error rates. Signal attenuation in copper and chromatic dispersion in fiber dictate how far an accelerator can sit from its switches before communication fails. Every higher-level protocol and topology is ultimately constrained by what the physical wire can carry; these limits shape the geometry of the entire cluster.

### Signal Integrity and PAM4

^sec-network-fabrics-pam4

Achieving 400 Gb/s and higher line rates requires balancing signaling speed against channel loss and detection errors. A port reaches such a rate by summing several lanes, the parallel serial channels that each carry one signal, so a 400 Gb/s NDR port carries four lanes at 100 Gb/s each. The **symbol rate** of a lane counts transmitted symbols per second, each occupying one time slot whether or not the voltage changes. Raising the symbol rate raises the frequencies the channel must carry, and copper loses more signal at higher frequencies. Carrying more bits in each symbol, by using more than two signal levels, raises the bit rate without that penalty, and PAM4 is the scheme high-speed links use to do it.

**Definition 3.2: PAM4 signaling**

***PAM4 signaling*** is a modulation scheme that represents two bits per symbol with four signal levels, doubling the raw bit rate relative to binary signaling at the same symbol rate.

1.  **Significance**: PAM4 increases the raw ${\text{BW}}$ available at a given symbol rate. The reduced separation between levels makes error-control design part of that gain. High-speed Ethernet uses forward error correction (FEC), including Reed-Solomon RS(544,514), with processing latency that depends on the physical-layer (PHY) design and implementation ([[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/01 The Fleet/03 Network Fabrics/V2-03.15 Source References#^ref-anslow2016rsFec|Anslow 2016]]; [[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/01 The Fleet/03 Network Fabrics/V2-03.15 Source References#^ref-yin2022fecRequirements|Yin et al. 2022]]). FEC contributes to the fixed path budget modeled by the ${\alpha}$ term in [[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/01 The Fleet/03 Network Fabrics/V2-03.05 Level 2 Transport and the Performance Model#^sec-network-fabrics-performance-model|section 3.4.4]]; it is one component alongside propagation, switching, and endpoint processing.
2.  **Distinction**: At equal symbol rate and voltage swing, NRZ (Non-Return-to-Zero) binary signaling carries one bit per symbol with greater separation between levels. PAM4 carries two bits per symbol with smaller level spacing; the link must compensate through its signal-quality and error-correction design.
3.  **Common pitfall**: Doubling bits per symbol does not guarantee twice the usable payload throughput or a fixed latency penalty. Coding overhead, channel quality, and the chosen PHY implementation determine delivered performance.

The price is noise margin. With four levels packed into the same voltage swing, the gap between adjacent levels in PAM4 shrinks to one third of the NRZ gap ([[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/01 The Fleet/03 Network Fabrics/V2-03.04 Level 1 Wire and Link#^fig-pam4-vs-nrz|figure 4]]), making the link highly susceptible to noise. Consequently, high-speed links operate near the physical limits of reliable detection and require **forward error correction (FEC)** at the physical layer, commonly using Reed-Solomon RS(544,514)-family codes in high-speed Ethernet designs ([[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/01 The Fleet/03 Network Fabrics/V2-03.15 Source References#^ref-anslow2016rsFec|Anslow 2016]]; [[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/01 The Fleet/03 Network Fabrics/V2-03.15 Source References#^ref-ethernetAlliance2025roadmap|Ethernet Alliance 2025]]).

FEC processing is an implementation-dependent latency term; IEEE 802.3df operator material treats roughly 100 ns of host-side FEC with the RS(544,514) code (called KP4 in the standard) and hundreds of nanoseconds of end-to-end module latency as meaningful at AI/HPC fabric timescales ([[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/01 The Fleet/03 Network Fabrics/V2-03.15 Source References#^ref-yin2022fecRequirements|Yin et al. 2022]]). Because each switch on a path decodes and re-encodes the signal, the FEC cost accumulates with the number of switches a packet traverses.

A path’s length is its **hop count**, counted as switch traversals or as inter-switch links; where the two counts differ, each example states which one it uses. A two-tier fabric attaches servers to leaf switches and joins the leaves through spine switches ([[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/01 The Fleet/03 Network Fabrics/V2-03.06 Level 3 Switch and Topology#^sec-network-fabrics-fat-tree|section 3.5.2]]), so a packet between leaves traverses three switches. At an assumed 100 to 200 ns per switch, that leaf-spine-leaf route incurs a scenario budget of 300 ns–600 ns of one-way FEC latency, or 600 ns–1200 ns for a round trip.

Against the 350 GB gradient of the 175B job, a few hundred nanoseconds are negligible, because bulk transfers are governed by bandwidth. The cost lands instead on small, latency-bound messages, such as the control handoffs that coordinate pipeline stages, and on collectives whose many sequential steps each pay it. This physical-layer latency sets a floor on the fabric’s fixed startup latency, formalized in [[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/01 The Fleet/03 Network Fabrics/V2-03.05 Level 2 Transport and the Performance Model#^sec-network-fabrics-performance-model|section 3.4.4]] as the ${\alpha}$ term, that no software optimization can remove.

Figure 4: **From NRZ to PAM4 Signaling**: Both panels send six symbols in the same time over the same total voltage swing. NRZ (left) uses two levels and carries one bit per symbol, 6 bits in all; PAM4 (right) uses four levels and carries two bits per symbol, 12 bits in all, doubling the data rate without raising the symbol rate. The cost is noise margin: adjacent PAM4 levels sit one third as far apart (arrows), so high-speed links need forward error correction (FEC), which adds to the fabric’s fixed ${\alpha}$ latency.

^fig-pam4-vs-nrz

Doubling line rate without doubling the symbol rate therefore trades signal-to-noise ratio (SNR) for bandwidth, and forward error correction is what lets the link operate that close to the channel limit. Because that tighter margin leaves less room for channel attenuation, it also shortens the distance a passive copper cable can span before the link must switch to optics.

### Reach and Medium for Copper and Optics

^sec-network-fabrics-copper-optics

Past one to three meters, passive copper attenuates high-frequency PAM4 signals beyond what even FEC can recover, forcing any link that exits a rack onto active optical cables or pluggable optics at a steep jump in cost and power. The choice of physical medium therefore determines how far a link can reach before latency, power, and cabling budgets reshape the cluster geometry, and the recurring cable and optics options differ along exactly those attributes ([[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/01 The Fleet/03 Network Fabrics/V2-03.04 Level 1 Wire and Link#^tbl-network-fabrics-link-media|table 3]]).

| **Medium**                     | **Reach**                                   | **Cost profile**                                                                                                              | **Latency impact**                                                                      | **Typical placement**            |
|:-------------------------------|:--------------------------------------------|:------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------|:----------------------------------------------------------------------------------------|:---------------------------------|
| **DAC (direct attach copper)** | 1 to 3 meters, shrinking as lane rate rises | Lowest cost (~\$50) and no optical module power                                                                               | Lowest media latency; no optical conversion, though high-speed lanes still pay FEC      | Within a rack                    |
| **AOC (active optical cable)** | 3 to 30 meters                              | Higher cost (~\$500) from permanently attached transceivers (the modules that convert between electrical and optical signals) | Electrical/optical conversion adds latency on top of the forward error correction (FEC) | Rack-to-rack or row-scale links  |
| **Pluggable optics**           | More than 30 meters                         | Highest module and power cost, but replaceable optics and fiber cords                                                         | Optical conversion adds latency on top of FEC; distance enables topology reach          | Network core and pod-scale links |

Table 3: **Cable and Optics Placement Trade-Offs**: Common interconnect media occupy different reach, latency, and cost points, so cluster designers use copper inside racks and optics as distance begins to dominate placement.

^tbl-network-fabrics-link-media

The high-speed copper and optical paths considered here both include FEC, so choosing optics does not by itself remove error-correction overhead. The ranges in [[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/01 The Fleet/03 Network Fabrics/V2-03.04 Level 1 Wire and Link#^tbl-network-fabrics-link-media|table 3]] describe representative placements rather than fixed boundaries between media. Optical interconnects can extend the layout beyond a passive copper channel’s budget, while pluggable optics also permit separate replacement of modules and fiber. Reach, delivered latency, power, and serviceability jointly determine the choice.

**Systems Perspective 3.2: The cost of distance**

In an ML fleet, distance is money. Assuming two spine-layer optical links per accelerator, a cluster of 10,000 GPUs requires ~20,000 optical links at the spine layer alone. At \$500 per complete link, that represents \$10 million in cabling. For power, count one selected spine-side pluggable module per link at 20 W per module, giving 400 kW for those endpoints alone. Each optical link also has a module at its opposite end; this subtotal excludes those modules, switch electronics, and cooling, so it is not the full fabric power budget. The cost dictates cluster geometry: architects pack accelerators as densely as possible (70–100 kW per rack) to maximize cheap copper and minimize expensive optics.

### SerDes, Link Budget, and Power

^sec-network-fabrics-serdes

Driving thousands of high-speed links across a cluster burns tens of kilowatts just to serialize bits onto the wire, a noticeable share of the accelerators’ own thermal budget. Every port depends on a **serializer/deserializer (SerDes)** circuit that converts parallel data from the switch application-specific integrated circuit (ASIC) into a serial stream, and its power cost accumulates across the provisioned ports. For the 1,024-GPU reference cluster, assume a full-${\text{BW}_{\text{bisect}}}$ fabric with roughly 3,000 high-speed links across its host and switch tiers. Count one selected switch-side port per link in this power subtotal. Each counted 400 Gb/s port is assigned 25 W, more than the 20 W pluggable-module figure in the cost-of-distance estimate because it also counts the switch-side SerDes that drives the module. The model totals 75 kW, equivalent to 10.5 percent of the accelerators’ combined thermal design power. This comparison isolates the assumed power of the selected switch-side ports, excluding the opposite endpoints and the rest of the switch chassis; it does not establish total fabric power or the fraction of measured cluster energy consumed by networking.

The **link budget** allocates the signal loss a path can tolerate while meeting its error target. Losses are counted in decibels (dB), a logarithmic power ratio in which every 10 dB is a further tenfold attenuation (3 dB is roughly a halving), so the losses of cables and connectors in series add directly. In this simplified accounting, a lane has a 30 dB allowance. A 3-meter DAC cable contributing 18 dB of loss and connectors contributing 2 dB leave 10 dB of margin. Higher signaling frequencies increase copper loss, so preserving reach can require more signal conditioning, a different cable, or optics. Aggregate port speed alone does not determine cable reach; lane rate, channel construction, and active components matter. The design must meet both the signal-quality target and the power budget before the transport layer can rely on the link.

    1.  PAM4 triples the baud rate on the physical wire but eliminates the need for receiver equalization
