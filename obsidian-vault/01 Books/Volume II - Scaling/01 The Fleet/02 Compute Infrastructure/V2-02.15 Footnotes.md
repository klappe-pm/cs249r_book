# V2-02.15 Footnotes

[[Home|Home]] · [[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/Volume 2 Overview|Volume Overview]] · [[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/01 The Fleet/02 Compute Infrastructure/V2-02 Compute Infrastructure|V2-02 Compute Infrastructure]]

^footnotes

<sup>1</sup> **Systolic array**: From Greek *systole* (contraction), the name describes data pulsing through a grid of processing elements in a heart-like rhythm. Kung and Leiserson ([[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/01 The Fleet/02 Compute Infrastructure/V2-02.14 Source References#^ref-kung1979systolic|1979]]) proposed the design for VLSI chips whose limiting resource was memory access rather than logic ([[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/01 The Fleet/02 Compute Infrastructure/V2-02.14 Source References#^ref-kung1982|Kung 1982]]): each operand fetched once is reused by every cell it passes. That ratio of computation to memory traffic is exactly what matrix multiplication at ML scale rewards.

^fn1

<sup>2</sup> **HBM origin**: Standardized by JEDEC in 2013 as a joint development between AMD and SK Hynix, originally for graphics cards. ML accelerators adopted HBM because neural networks share the same bandwidth-hungry, capacity-moderate access pattern as high-end rendering. Each HBM generation has roughly doubled bandwidth (128 GB/s in HBM1 to 1.2 TB/s per stack in HBM3e), yet the gap between memory bandwidth and arithmetic throughput continues to widen, which makes HBM a necessary but never sufficient response to the memory wall.

^fn2

<sup>3</sup> **SRAM energy (pJ/bit)**: Reading a bit from an 8–32 KB on-chip SRAM array costs roughly 0.15–0.3 pJ at the 45 nm node ([[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/01 The Fleet/02 Compute Infrastructure/V2-02.14 Source References#^ref-horowitz2014|Horowitz 2014]]), while fetching a bit from HBM2 costs about 4 pJ ([[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/01 The Fleet/02 Compute Infrastructure/V2-02.14 Source References#^ref-oconnor2017fine|O’Connor et al. 2017]]). This order-of-magnitude energy difference is the physical driver behind kernel fusion: every intermediate result kept in SRAM instead of written to HBM saves both time and significant power and thermal headroom.

^fn3

<sup>4</sup> **TSV (through-silicon via)**: A vertical copper pillar, 5–10 micrometers in diameter, etched through a silicon die to connect stacked layers. Originally developed for CMOS image sensors in smartphone cameras, TSVs enabled HBM by replacing centimeters of PCB trace with tens of micrometers of vertical silicon, roughly a 1,000$\times$ shorter signal path, which is what lets a stack expose a 1024-bit interface within an accelerator’s power budget.

^fn4

<sup>5</sup> **Crossbar switch**: A topology providing a dedicated path between every input-output pair simultaneously. An ${k}{\times}{k}$ crossbar needs ${k}^2$ crosspoints, so its cost grows quadratically with port count; that growth is why a full crossbar stays inside a node and larger systems switch to multi-stage fabrics. Within that bound, the crossbar buys the all-to-all bandwidth that tensor parallelism demands.

^fn5

<sup>6</sup> **Dennard scaling**: Named after Dennard et al. ([[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/01 The Fleet/02 Compute Infrastructure/V2-02.14 Source References#^ref-dennard1974|1974]]) at IBM, who showed how MOSFET dimensions, voltage, and current could scale so that power density stayed roughly constant. Later voltage-scaling limits and leakage effects ended that constant-power-density regime; Hennessy and Patterson ([[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/01 The Fleet/02 Compute Infrastructure/V2-02.14 Source References#^ref-hennessy2019|2019]]) describe the resulting post-Dennard power wall as one reason modern architecture turned toward parallelism and specialization. For ML infrastructure, the V100-to-B200 TDP trajectory (300 W to 1,000 W) is an illustration of the post-Dennard planning problem: cooling and power delivery are now co-equal design constraints with the silicon itself.

^fn6
