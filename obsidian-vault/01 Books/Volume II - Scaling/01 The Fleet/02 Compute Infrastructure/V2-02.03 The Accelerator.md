# V2-02.03 The Accelerator

[[Home|Home]] · [[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/Volume 2 Overview|Volume Overview]] · [[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/01 The Fleet/02 Compute Infrastructure/V2-02 Compute Infrastructure|V2-02 Compute Infrastructure]]

^sec-compute-accelerator-spectrum

The progression begins with the accelerator, because every wall above it is measured against the rates one device can compute, store, and draw. An accelerator purchase commits the fleet to a workload and software stack for longer than one experiment. A research group changing model operations frequently needs programmability; a service executing a stable computation at sustained volume can justify more specialization. The familiar trade-off between control flexibility and arithmetic efficiency matters here because an architectural mismatch is replicated across the fleet. Comparing programmable and specialized accelerators therefore starts with how much of the workload and compiler stack can remain stable over the deployment’s lifetime.

**Systems Perspective 2.1: The generality tax**

General-purpose CPUs allocate silicon to caches and control logic as well as arithmetic. Branch predictors, reorder buffers, and instruction decoders support code with irregular control flow and data access. Matrix multiplication has a more regular schedule, so some of that flexibility contributes little to its dominant computation. The generality tax (principle [[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/01 The Fleet/V2 Fleet Principles#^pri-generality-tax|1]]) is the area and energy devoted to capabilities outside the dominant workload.

Specialized accelerators reduce this tax by replacing general-purpose control with a dataflow matched to the workload. In the approximate shares of [[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/01 The Fleet/02 Compute Infrastructure/V2-02.03 The Accelerator#^fig-accelerator-spectrum|figure 2]], the arithmetic fraction of the die rises from roughly 5–10 percent on a CPU to 50–60 percent on a GPU, 70–80 percent on a Tensor Processing Unit (TPU), and over 90 percent on a custom application-specific integrated circuit (ASIC). The shares mark a direction rather than measured proportions for any product. The selection question is how much of the complete workload can use the specialized hardware.

![[90 Assets/Volume II/02 Compute Infrastructure/Accelerator Spectrum fcd787f1df.svg]]

Figure 2: **The Accelerator Spectrum**: Each die is drawn as a grid of 100 cells divided between arithmetic units (dark) and control, cache, and other logic (light), with the medium cells marking the spread of each approximate, illustrative share. Read left to right, the arithmetic share grows from 5 to 10 percent on a CPU to over 90 percent on a custom ASIC, because hardwired dataflow replaces instruction fetch, decode, and scheduling; each step buys throughput per watt by narrowing the range of model architectures the silicon runs well.

^fig-accelerator-spectrum

Along the spectrum of [[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/01 The Fleet/02 Compute Infrastructure/V2-02.03 The Accelerator#^fig-accelerator-spectrum|figure 2]], GPUs retain substantial programmability and provide 10–100$\times$ the matrix throughput of CPUs, while custom ASICs at the far end hardwire a specific dataflow for maximum efficiency at the cost of flexibility. The spectrum is not a ranking from “bad” (general-purpose CPU) to “good” (custom ASIC). It is a continuum of trade-offs in which each position is the best match for a different combination of workload characteristics and deployment constraints.

GPUs represent the first major step away from general-purpose computing toward large-scale parallelism while retaining programmability. An NVIDIA H100, for example, contains 16,896 CUDA cores organized into 132 streaming multiprocessors (SMs). The SMs keep thousands of threads in flight under the single instruction, multiple threads (SIMT) model, so a programmer-written kernel can express any mathematical operation, from convolutions to attention to custom loss functions. Neural network operations have uniform enough control flow that this programmability costs the GPU little on ML workloads.

TPUs specialize the matrix dataflow while retaining other units for operations that do not map onto it. Google’s Tensor Processing Units use a **systolic array**[[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/01 The Fleet/02 Compute Infrastructure/V2-02.15 Footnotes#^fn1|¹]] architecture, a fixed grid of multiply-accumulate (MAC) units that pass operands and partial results between neighboring cells on a regular schedule, so local reuse replaces per-operation instruction fetch and memory traffic. The complete accelerator still needs control, data movement, and execution of nonmatrix operations.

The cost of this efficiency is reduced programmability. Models must be compiled through Accelerated Linear Algebra (XLA), which maps high-level operations onto the fixed dataflow. Workloads with irregular control flow or dynamic shapes may not map well to this architecture, and the compilation process itself can be time-consuming (minutes to hours for complex models), which slows the iteration cycle during model development.

The programming model distinction between GPUs and TPUs has practical implications for organizational decisions. A research team that frequently adds custom operations, such as new attention patterns or activation functions, needs a path to implement them as kernels without waiting for compiler support, and must check how each platform expresses and optimizes those operations. A team running a stable computation at scale can place more weight on sustained utilization and compilation cost amortized over many runs. CUDA kernel support and XLA graph compilation offer different development paths, but neither establishes a utilization advantage without evaluating the workload and implementation. Software fit belongs in the hardware comparison because unsupported or poorly mapped work can leave specialized arithmetic idle.

**Example 2.1: The TPU origin story**

**Scenario**: In 2013, Google projected that if users spoke to Android phones for 3 minutes per day using voice search, handling the inference load on general-purpose CPUs would require doubling data center compute capacity at prohibitive cost ([[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/01 The Fleet/02 Compute Infrastructure/V2-02.14 Source References#^ref-jouppi2017|Jouppi et al. 2017]]).

**Diagnosis**: Standard CPU architectures paid a heavy generality tax, spending significant silicon area and power on out-of-order execution, branch prediction, and complex caches rather than matrix multiplication throughput.

**Systems lesson**: Hardware specialization is economically justified when a workload is large, stable, and dominated by a single mathematical kernel (MatMul). The TPU v1 hardwired a $256 \times 256$ systolic array to deliver 92 TOPS (INT8) within a 75 W footprint, establishing custom ASIC accelerators in production serving infrastructure.

^exmp-compute-infrastructure-tpu-origin-story

Custom ASICs occupy the specialized end of the spectrum, where the economics of silicon can justify giving up much of the general-purpose programmability. When an organization runs a stable workload at enormous scale, designing a chip specifically for that workload may become economically rational. A custom ASIC can improve energy per operation and sustained utilization on its target workload by removing features that computation does not require.

Tesla’s Dojo D1 chip, for example, is optimized for the spatial and temporal structure of video-based vision models. Its hundreds of tightly coupled processing nodes are designed around the dataflow needs of Tesla’s vision pipeline, with on-chip SRAM sized to keep spatial tiles close to the compute units. This reduces the repeated round-trips to off-chip memory that a general-purpose GPU would require for the same computation. AWS Trainium takes a different approach, targeting the broad category of Transformer training rather than a single model. Trainium systems combine compiler-managed memory scheduling with hardware-assisted collective communication, so common training patterns such as tensor-parallel synchronization and data-parallel reductions can be optimized in the accelerator fabric rather than handled entirely by host software.

The risk of custom silicon follows from the same specialization. A change in the dominant model architecture can reduce the value of a fixed dataflow if the new operators, tensor shapes, or memory demands map poorly onto it. Sharing matrix operations across model families can preserve some of that investment, so a change from convolutional neural networks (CNNs) to Transformers does not by itself make an accelerator unusable. The forecasting window is long. Even Google’s first TPU, a high-priority project, took 15 months to design, verify, build, and deploy ([[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/01 The Fleet/02 Compute Infrastructure/V2-02.14 Source References#^ref-jouppi2017|Jouppi et al. 2017]]), and a chip then serves for several years more inside a facility planned to outlast multiple accelerator generations ([[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/01 The Fleet/02 Compute Infrastructure/V2-02.10 Infrastructure Technologies That Move the Walls#^sec-compute-emerging|section 2.9]]). The design decision must therefore anticipate which operations will remain useful years after the workload was profiled, and how much flexibility is needed for the rest. In 2015, few would have predicted that attention-based Transformers would replace CNNs as the dominant architecture within five years, a span of only a few ASIC design cycles.

Custom silicon is therefore a bet on workload stability, and the organizations that make this bet are typically those with enough scale to justify a large up-front design investment and enough workload volume to amortize that NRE (nonrecurring engineering) cost across many chips. Google can justify the TPU’s development cost because high-volume services such as Search, YouTube recommendations, and Gmail spam filtering can amortize a shared accelerator platform. A research lab running a few hundred GPUs cannot justify the same investment.

The bet can pay off when workloads are stable. The first-generation TPU reported 30–80$\times$ the performance per watt of contemporary GPUs and CPUs on its inference workloads ([[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/01 The Fleet/02 Compute Infrastructure/V2-02.14 Source References#^ref-jouppi2017|Jouppi et al. 2017]]), and TPU v4 reported 1.3–1.9$\times$ lower power than the A100 for similar-sized training systems ([[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/01 The Fleet/02 Compute Infrastructure/V2-02.14 Source References#^ref-jouppi2023|Jouppi et al. 2023]]). A workload shift can strand that advantage because the chip’s fixed dataflow may not accommodate a substantially different computational pattern. A different specialization bets on physical scale rather than a fixed dataflow, expanding the compute fabric to the size of an entire wafer.

### Wafer-Scale Engines

^sec-compute-wafer-scale

Slicing a silicon wafer into discrete chips forces every exchange between accelerators to leave the die and cross slower, higher-energy package, board, or rack links. Wafer-scale engines (WSE) avoid those crossings by keeping most of a silicon wafer intact as one continuous compute fabric the size of a dinner plate, as in the Cerebras WSE-3, rather than dicing the wafer and reconnecting the pieces across packages and boards.

For its 2024 WSE-3 configuration, Cerebras reported 900,000 AI-optimized cores, 44 GB of distributed on-chip SRAM, and 21 PB/s of aggregate memory bandwidth in its registration filing ([[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/01 The Fleet/02 Compute Infrastructure/V2-02.14 Source References#^ref-cerebras2024s1|Cerebras Systems 2024]]). These specifications are not directly comparable to GPU-cluster peak FLOP/s or high-bandwidth memory (HBM) bandwidth because the architectures expose different dataflows, memory organizations, precisions, and workload mappings. The durable comparison is topological. On-wafer links avoid package-, board-, and rack-level transfers for work that maps onto the fabric.

A multi-GPU chassis and a wafer-scale engine draw their package and board boundaries in different places ([[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/01 The Fleet/02 Compute Infrastructure/V2-02.03 The Accelerator#^fig-wafer-scale-engine|figure 3]]). The eight GPUs of a chassis exchange state over NVLink, NVIDIA’s GPU-to-GPU link, and NVSwitch chips join those links into one fabric. Transfers to other nodes cross the external network. A wafer-scale engine instead routes between 900,000 on-wafer cores on the 300 mm wafer. Defect-aware routing lets the fabric use working regions despite local manufacturing flaws. In the documented CS-2 configuration, twelve power supplies in a 9+3 redundant arrangement could deliver up to 23 kW to the WSE-2 ([[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/01 The Fleet/02 Compute Infrastructure/V2-02.14 Source References#^ref-Cerebras2021|Systems 2021]]). That configuration-specific design illustrates why wafer-scale power delivery and cooling require system-level liquid-cooling hardware rather than a conventional accelerator heatsink.

![[90 Assets/Volume II/02 Compute Infrastructure/Wse Architecture ae76f49250.svg]]

Figure 3: **Wafer-Scale Engine (WSE) Architecture**: A DGX H100 node (left) joins eight separately packaged GPUs through an NVSwitch fabric and must leave the chassis over InfiniBand to reach other nodes, so every exchange crosses a package, board, or network boundary. A WSE (right) instead uses most of a 300 mm wafer as one routed mesh of cores, each with local SRAM; defect-aware routing steers traffic around unusable cores, and the on-wafer mesh replaces off-package transfers for mapped operations. The WSE-3 figures are the vendor’s reported totals and are not directly comparable to GPU HBM bandwidth.

^fig-wafer-scale-engine

Wafer-scale engines specialize the *physical* architecture while retaining flexibility in the *computational* model through programmable cores. That trade-off exchanges manufacturing complexity and defect-aware routing for an on-wafer fabric that replaces package- and board-level communication between its cores, though SRAM capacity and data injection still constrain which parts of a workload can benefit from that locality. Where the wafer-scale engine removes the package boundary by never dicing the wafer, chiplet designs keep the package and change what goes inside it.

### Chiplet-Based Accelerators

^sec-compute-chiplet-accelerators

Once a monolithic processor die grows to the optical exposure limit of a lithography scanner, adding more arithmetic units or HBM controllers on a single piece of silicon becomes impossible without stitching exposures at lower yield and sharply higher cost. Chiplet architectures, exemplified by NVIDIA’s Blackwell and AMD’s Instinct MI300 series, break through that ceiling by partitioning the processor into smaller dies connected by a high-bandwidth die-to-die interconnect on a common package substrate. This approach addresses two physical limits that constrain monolithic designs.

First, the maximum die size is capped by the photolithographic reticle limit, the largest area that a lithography scanner can pattern in a single exposure. The limit comes from the optics of photolithography, because the lens system of an extreme-ultraviolet (EUV) scanner projects a pattern onto a field of only about $26{\times}33$ mm (858 mm²). A larger die would need multiple exposures stitched together, which is technically possible but sharply raises cost and lowers yield. The limit caps the number of Tensor Cores (the GPU’s matrix-multiply units), SMs, and HBM stacks a monolithic GPU can integrate. Chiplet designs bypass this limit by placing multiple dies on a single package, with the B200’s two reticle-limited dies effectively creating a processor of about 1,700 mm².

Second, manufacturing yield depends on die area and defect density. Under the standard Poisson defect model, the fraction of defect-free dies falls exponentially with die area at a fixed defect density, because a single killer defect anywhere on the die can render it unusable. Smaller dies expose less area to each defect, and testing can select known-good chiplets before package assembly, so one defect discards a small die rather than a reticle-sized one. The resulting yield advantage must be weighed against the cost and yield of the package, its interconnects, and the additional testing. Chiplets change the manufacturing trade-off; they do not guarantee a lower cost per unit of compute.

The trade-off is the die-to-die interconnect. Communication between chiplets within a package is faster than communication between packages (NVLink) but slower than communication within a single monolithic die (the on-die mesh network). Workloads that generate frequent, fine-grained communication between processing elements (such as operations that share data between nonadjacent SMs) may experience a latency penalty when those SMs reside on different chiplets. GPU vendors mitigate this by making the die-to-die link transparent to the programmer, so the software sees a single logical GPU regardless of the underlying chiplet topology. Seeing why modern accelerators reached that multi-die boundary requires tracing how successive GPU and TPU generations spent their single-die transistor budgets first.

### Evolution of GPU Architectures

^sec-compute-infrastructure-evolution-gpu-architectures-566b

NVIDIA’s data center GPUs show the specialization bet placed one generation at a time. Each generation added hardware aimed at a bottleneck that production ML workloads had exposed, rather than more transistors of the same kind. The Volta architecture (2017) introduced the first-generation Tensor Core, dedicated matrix-multiply-accumulate hardware placed beside the existing CUDA cores, because neural network training spends most of its time in matrix multiplications. Volta accelerated that dominant pattern without giving up the general-purpose programmability that made GPUs attractive for research.

The Ampere architecture (2020) expanded Tensor Core support to TF32 (a format with FP32’s range and less precision) and BF16 and introduced Multi-Instance GPU (MIG), which partitions one GPU into up to seven isolated instances so that several inference workloads can share it. The change that mattered most for infrastructure was Ampere’s third-generation NVLink, which doubled per-GPU bandwidth to 600 GB/s from Volta’s 300 GB/s, the bandwidth that tensor parallelism needs because it splits each layer’s matrices across GPUs.

The Hopper architecture (2022) added the Transformer Engine, whose FP8 recipes and autocast integration, the software that selects each operation’s precision, run supported Transformer operations in FP8 with managed scaling while retaining higher precision where the configured recipe or implementation requires it. Hopper also introduced NVLink 4.0 at 900 GB/s per GPU and the NVLink Switch, enabling NVLink connectivity beyond the 8-GPU boundary.

The Blackwell architecture (2024) takes the chiplet route of [[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/01 The Fleet/02 Compute Infrastructure/V2-02.03 The Accelerator#^sec-compute-chiplet-accelerators|section 2.2.2]] past the 858 mm² reticle limit. The B200 pairs two GPU dies in one package over a 10 TB/s die-to-die link, fast enough that the two dies appear to software as a single logical GPU for many operations, and its fifth-generation NVLink doubles intra-node bandwidth again to 1800 GB/s per GPU. Its rating in [[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/01 The Fleet/02 Compute Infrastructure/V2-02.03 The Accelerator#^tbl-gpu-evolution|table 1]] is an FP8 figure, so a comparison at FP16 or BF16 should use the vendor’s lower rate for that precision.

Each generation thus targeted the bottleneck its predecessor exposed. Volta added dedicated matrix hardware, Ampere widened mixed-precision support, Hopper added FP8 for Transformer workloads, and Blackwell worked around the die-size limit with two dies in one package. Because architects choose those targets by profiling the workloads of their era, accelerator design follows model architecture, which is why Transformer workloads have shaped accelerator design since 2017.

Because accelerator generations turn over faster than the facility that houses them, a data center must anticipate refresh cycles before the first rack arrives. Those generations also changed interconnect capacity and power ([[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/01 The Fleet/02 Compute Infrastructure/V2-02.03 The Accelerator#^tbl-gpu-evolution|table 1]]). From V100 to B200, dense FP16 throughput rose 18$\times$, memory bandwidth 8.9$\times$, and thermal design power (TDP) 3.3$\times$ ([[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/01 The Fleet/02 Compute Infrastructure/V2-02.03 The Accelerator#^fig-gpu-scaling-trends|figure 4]]). These are growth multiples over the whole span, not annual rates, and their ratios carry the planning lesson. Each byte delivered from memory must now feed about twice as many operations, and each watt of the power allowance supplies more than five times as many peak operations. That first ratio raises the data reuse a workload needs to approach peak throughput. Even with the fivefold gain in peak operations per watt, reaching the 18$\times$ compute multiple still required raising per-device TDP by 3.3$\times$, and that higher per-device wattage multiplied by device count sets the rack load. A facility that replaces accelerators one for one to gain compute density therefore outruns both its memory delivery and its cooling envelope.

### Evolution of TPU Architectures

^sec-compute-tpu-evolution

Faster per-chip arithmetic leaves a cluster stalled if inter-chip collectives still have to traverse host CPUs and general-purpose PCIe switches. Google’s TPU trajectory attacked that bottleneck directly by treating the multi-rack pod, rather than the individual chip, as its unit of design from its second generation onward. While GPUs emphasize peak per-chip throughput and software flexibility (CUDA), every TPU chip since TPU v2 integrates dedicated inter-chip interconnect (ICI) links that route traffic directly between accelerators without touching the host CPU.

The generation sequence shows how that pod-first design moved from inference to training. After the inference-only TPU v1 of the origin story, TPU v2 (2017) and TPU v3 (2018) shifted the architecture toward training. They added bfloat16 (BF16), which avoids the dynamic range problems of standard FP16, and they scaled the pod concept until a TPU v3 pod joined 1,024 chips with about 126 PFLOP/s of aggregate BF16 peak. TPU v4 (2021) extended the same logic into the network itself. It added an optical circuit switch so the physical topology could be reconfigured for different workloads, introduced SparseCore units that accelerate embedding lookups, and raised per-chip BF16 throughput to 275 TFLOP/s.

The TPU v5p (2023) continued this pod-scale design for high-end training. It features 459 TFLOP/s of BF16 compute, 95 GB of HBM2e with 2,760 GB/s memory bandwidth, and 1,200 GB/s bidirectional ICI bandwidth per chip. The ICI figure matters for hardware selection because collectives such as AllReduce run over those links. AllReduce combines values such as gradient sums across a group of accelerators and returns the result to every participant. **[[05 Reference/Volume II - Scaling/V2 Communication Foundations#^sec-appdx-comm-foundations-allreduce|AllReduce]]** develops the complexity model and algorithm choices that determine when those exchanges limit training throughput. The pod-first design took shape over successive TPU generations ([[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/01 The Fleet/02 Compute Infrastructure/V2-02.03 The Accelerator#^tbl-tpu-evolution|table 3]]).

The GPU and TPU lines follow two design philosophies ([[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/01 The Fleet/02 Compute Infrastructure/V2-02.03 The Accelerator#^tbl-gpu-evolution|table 1]], [[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/01 The Fleet/02 Compute Infrastructure/V2-02.03 The Accelerator#^tbl-tpu-evolution|table 3]]). GPU evolution packs more arithmetic, in ever lower-precision Tensor Core formats, onto one package, and turns to chiplets once a single die reaches its size limit. TPU evolution treats the pod as the product. Per-chip arithmetic matters, but inter-chip bandwidth and reconfigurability decide how much of it a large training job can use. Both lines combine local computation with memory and interconnect improvements; they place those resources differently. The warehouse-scale perspective of **[[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/00 Introduction/01 Introduction/V2-01.03 From Single Node to Fleet#^sec-introduction-shift-warehousescale-computer-2ccd|The shift to the warehouse-scale computer]]** treats chip throughput, collective bandwidth, and reconfigurability as parts of one system ([[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/01 The Fleet/02 Compute Infrastructure/V2-02.14 Source References#^ref-barroso2019|Barroso et al. 2019]]), so the useful comparison is how each candidate sustains the intended workload across that complete system.

### Matrix Units

^sec-compute-matrix-units

Both lines concentrate their peak arithmetic in specialized matrix units, called Tensor Cores on NVIDIA GPUs and Matrix Multiply Units (MXUs) on Google TPUs. A Tensor Core executes matrix-multiply-accumulate instructions of the form ${\mathbf{D}} = {\mathbf{A}}\times {\mathbf{B}}+ {\mathbf{C}}$ on small tile matrices, concentrating the dominant neural-network operation in dedicated hardware that supplies most of the chip’s peak arithmetic.

With 528 Tensor Cores across the chip (4 per SM, 132 SMs), the H100 reaches its 989 TFLOP/s dense FP16/BF16 peak. The important systems point is the architectural specialization rather than the exact per-cycle instruction accounting. The die dedicates a large fraction of its arithmetic area to dense tiled matrix operations rather than general-purpose scalar execution.

Google’s MXUs organize multiply-accumulate units into the systolic array introduced with the TPU above. In the MXU’s weight-stationary dataflow, a weight tile stays resident in the array while activations stream through it. Every operand fetched from memory is therefore reused by an entire row or column of cells, which is the reuse that a Tensor Core kernel obtains by staging tiles in shared memory and registers. Each TPU v5p chip contains two TPU cores (which Google calls TensorCores, a name unrelated to NVIDIA’s Tensor Cores) with four $128{\times}128$ MXUs each, together providing 459 TFLOP/s of BF16 throughput. Approaching that peak requires compatible tile shapes and a sustained operand supply; pipeline fill, partial tiles, and dependencies can leave cells idle. Vector and scalar units handle other operations, so complete-workload efficiency depends on how computation maps across the accelerator.

For our 175B model, compiler and kernel support determine how much work reaches the specialized arithmetic units. CUDA kernels can combine matrix operations with thread-level work, while XLA maps the computation graph onto the TPU dataflow. In either stack, fusion keeps intermediate results in registers or shared memory instead of repeatedly writing them to HBM. Sustained throughput therefore depends on operation coverage and data reuse as well as the matrix units themselves.

**Definition 2.1: Tensor core**

***Tensor Core*** is a specialized ML accelerator hardware unit that performs a fused matrix-multiply-accumulate operation ${\mathbf{D}} = {\mathbf{A}}\times {\mathbf{B}}+ {\mathbf{C}}$ on small tiles (for example, $16{\times}8{\times}16$ in BF16) as a single hardware instruction, delivering dramatically higher throughput than general-purpose CUDA cores by trading programmability for fixed-function matrix arithmetic.

1.  **Significance**: Tensor Cores provide the bulk of the H100’s 989 TFLOP/s FP16/BF16 peak (and ~1,979 TFLOP/s at FP8), roughly 14.8× the ~67 TFLOP/s delivered by CUDA (vector) cores alone. This comparison changes both the execution unit and precision. Reductions and element-wise operations in layer normalization or softmax require a different performance model; their throughput is not a fixed fraction of the matrix peak.
2.  **Distinction**: A Tensor Core is a fixed-function matrix-multiply-accumulate unit, not a faster general-purpose core. CUDA cores execute arbitrary scalar and vector work; Tensor Cores execute only the tiled ${\mathbf{D}} = {\mathbf{A}}\times {\mathbf{B}}+ {\mathbf{C}}$ primitive, which is why peak throughput and delivered throughput diverge so sharply by workload.
3.  **Common pitfall**: A frequent misconception is that all GPU operations benefit from Tensor Cores. Operations must map to the ${\mathbf{D}} = {\mathbf{A}}\times {\mathbf{B}}+ {\mathbf{C}}$ tile computation to use those units. Element-wise activations, normalization, and embedding lookups have different execution and data-access demands. A fixed 10–30$\times$ penalty per FLOP is not a workload-independent model for their runtime.

^tbl-accelerator-comparison

Across the whole spectrum, from CPU to custom ASIC, the architectures differ in arithmetic core, execution model, and memory control ([[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/01 The Fleet/02 Compute Infrastructure/V2-02.03 The Accelerator#^tbl-accelerator-comparison|table 4]]). For the 175B running model, the choice among these columns is not purely about peak FLOP/s. A research lab experimenting with novel architectures weekly can justify the GPU’s generality tax by its flexibility. An organization deploying a fixed Transformer at scale for years may find that the TPU’s dataflow efficiency or a custom ASIC’s power advantage dominates total cost. The accelerator spectrum is ultimately an economic question of *how much flexibility can be surrendered, given the stability of the workload*. Even when the H100’s arithmetic engine reaches 1,979 TFLOP/s in its advertised low-precision mode, eligible operations approach that throughput only when data reaches the compute units fast enough. Accelerator selection therefore requires a second comparison, between the workload’s data demand and the memory system that feeds the arithmetic.
