# V2-02.11 Fallacies and Pitfalls

[[Home|Home]] · [[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/Volume 2 Overview|Volume Overview]] · [[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/01 The Fleet/02 Compute Infrastructure/V2-02 Compute Infrastructure|V2-02 Compute Infrastructure]]

^sec-compute-fallacies-pitfalls

Because the bottlenecks move rather than disappear, compute infrastructure teams often optimize the visible specification while missing the binding physical constraint. Each fallacy and pitfall below turns a node, rack, pod, or facility lesson into an operational warning.

**Fallacy**: *More GPUs always means faster training.*

**[[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/00 Introduction/01 Introduction/V2-01.09 Fallacies and Pitfalls#^sec-vol2-intro-fallacies-pitfalls-f804|Fallacies and Pitfalls]]** warned against reading a compute budget as a wall-clock prediction. The hardware form of the same error assumes that training time scales linearly with GPU count, so that doubling the GPUs halves the training time. In practice, communication overhead grows with cluster size, and this chapter’s modeled scaling efficiencies show how fast.

Amdahl’s law bounds speedup when a fixed workload has a fixed serial fraction. Distributed training also incurs communication, pipeline, and I/O costs that can change with partitioning and scale; these are not all an invariant serial fraction. For a 175B-parameter model with 350 GB of gradients, the AllReduce at each training step must combine contributions across the participating group. It can do so through ring or tree exchanges; direct transfers between every pair are not required.

Even for a carefully engineered fabric, the modeled scaling efficiency of the running model falls from 0.87 at 256 GPUs to 0.83 at 1,024 GPUs and 0.56 at 8,192. Growing from the reference cluster to the 8,192-GPU scenario of [[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/01 The Fleet/02 Compute Infrastructure/V2-02.08 The Pod#^sec-compute-pod|section 2.7]] therefore multiplies the hardware 8× but the speed only 5.4×, and 44 percent of the larger cluster’s locally achievable throughput is lost to communication and pipeline bubbles. That run still finishes in days, so the scale is worth buying for a deadline, but each added GPU returns less than the one before it; without an engineered fabric and overlapped communication, the curve flattens far sooner.

The correct approach is to profile useful compute time, exposed communication time, and idle time at the current scale before buying the next pod. If the added GPUs mostly increase exposed communication, procurement is buying idle time rather than training progress. **[[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/02 Distributed ML/06 Distributed Training/V2-06.05 Scaling Efficiency and Convergence#^sec-distributed-training-scaling-math|The mathematics of scaling efficiency]]** turns this local ratio into a scaling-efficiency estimate before committing to procurement.

**Pitfall**: *Planning training throughput from peak FLOP/s alone.*

Peak FLOP/s describes only one roofline ceiling. At the H100 FP16 ridge point of approximately 295.2 FLOP/byte, the bandwidth and arithmetic ceilings meet; lower-intensity operations cannot reach the arithmetic peak through more compute alone. In the chapter’s batch-1 weight-streaming estimate, decode near 1 FLOP/byte uses well under 1 percent of the arithmetic peak. Prefill and training supply more reuse, yet even well-tuned large training runs commonly sustain only 30–50 percent of peak once memory-bound attention and normalization kernels, activation recomputation, dependencies, and communication are counted. Procurement needs those measurements for the intended execution phase.

**Fallacy**: *Power and cooling constraints can be handled after accelerator purchase.*

Teams that plan GPU purchases from compute requirements without verifying that the target facility can deliver the required power and cooling run into hard physical limits with long lead times. A single rack of four DGX H100 systems requires 33.5 kW of power. Upgrading a data center’s electrical infrastructure (new transformers, switchgear, and UPS capacity) and its cooling plant (chillers, piping, pumps) are construction projects planned in months, not days. Even seemingly simple changes, such as upgrading the PDU in a single rack from 20 kW to 60 kW, can require new cabling and circuit breaker panels. Organizations that order hardware without confirming facility readiness risk having millions of dollars of GPUs sitting in shipping containers in the parking lot while the building is retrofitted. Accelerators can arrive faster than a facility can be upgraded to power and cool them, which makes this mismatch a practical planning risk.

**Pitfall**: *Buying a newer accelerator without checking the workload’s arithmetic intensity.*

Suppose a team deploys B200 GPUs (4,500 TFLOP/s FP8 peak) for an inference workload serving a 7B-parameter model at batch size 1, where an arithmetic intensity of approximately 1 FLOP/byte places the workload deep in the memory-bound region. The B200’s memory bandwidth (8 TB/s) is 2.4× higher than the H100’s (3.35 TB/s). Comparing B200 FP8 against H100 FP16/BF16-class throughput gives a peak arithmetic ratio of about 4.6×, but also changes the precision convention. At a fixed weight footprint, the bandwidth-only model instead predicts a gain of about 2.4×. The low intensity limits the fraction of arithmetic peak that this model can use, though it does not measure idle time; the comparison that matters is bandwidth per dollar at the measured serving rate, and the larger arithmetic peak alone does not establish better value.

**Fallacy**: *Air cooling is sufficient for AI workloads.*

Operators of legacy facilities may assume that adding fans and chiller capacity is sufficient for any denser AI rack. In practice, the cooling choice depends on the system’s heat-rejection design and the facility’s available airflow, pressure, inlet temperature, and containment. Engineered air remains viable for some dense configurations, while higher rack densities can require direct liquid cooling to avoid thermal throttling or exceeding facility limits.

**Fallacy**: *All GPU-hours are equivalent.*

A common error in cost comparisons is to treat one GPU-hour on an H100 as interchangeable with one GPU-hour on an A100 or V100. Under the same dense FP16 peak convention, H100 offers 3.2× the arithmetic rate of A100 and 7.9× that of V100. If a workload sustained the same fraction of those peaks with the same operation count and target quality, 10,000 V100 GPU-hours would correspond to approximately 1,264 H100 GPU-hours. That is a conditional compute-only comparison, not a predicted training time, because memory, communication, numerical recipes, and software can change sustained efficiency. Procurement must compare measured cost per useful computation or per completed run, rather than rental price or peak FLOP/s alone.

**Pitfall**: *Upgrading the network before identifying the training bottleneck.*

A network upgrade from 200 Gb/s to 400 Gb/s InfiniBand changes training time only through the communication it accelerates on the critical path, the mirror image of the faster-accelerator fallacy in **[[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/00 Introduction/01 Introduction/V2-01.09 Fallacies and Pitfalls#^sec-vol2-intro-fallacies-pitfalls-f804|Fallacies and Pitfalls]]**. In a no-overlap example with 20 seconds of computation and 2 seconds of communication, doubling transfer bandwidth reduces the estimated step from 22 to 21 seconds, an improvement of 4.5 percent. That result assumes the communication term scales with bandwidth and other terms remain fixed. Adding GPUs or optimizing kernels requires a separate estimate because either can change communication and overlap. The Fleet Law keeps those costs visible when comparing investments.

**Fallacy**: *Average power draw is sufficient for cooling design.*

Accelerator power draw varies across communication, memory, and compute phases. A workload average below the configured sustained envelope does not establish the cooling requirement because synchronized compute phases can raise many devices toward that envelope together. Thermal design must use the vendor’s sustained design target under the intended inlet and cooling conditions, while the electrical path must separately tolerate documented transients. Otherwise thermal throttling can reduce sustained throughput without producing a crash or explicit application error.

**Pitfall**: *Selecting accelerators by HBM capacity without checking bandwidth.*

Capacity and bandwidth answer separate feasibility questions, and a model that fits in HBM but cannot be served within its latency target is as unusable as one that does not fit. Consider two hypothetical options for a 70-billion-parameter model: (a) 192 GB of HBM2e at 2.04 TB/s, and (b) 80 GB of HBM3 at 3.35 TB/s. Option (a) holds the 141 GB FP16 weight payload, whose streaming floor is 141 GB/2.04 TB/s = 69.2 ms. Option (b) cannot hold 141 GB in its 80 GB capacity, but the 71 GB INT8 payload fits before other live state is counted. Its weight-transfer floor is 71 GB/3.35 TB/s = 21 ms, a 3.3× ratio between the two estimates. That ratio combines faster memory with fewer bytes; option (a) could also use a validated INT8 recipe. Selection must compare like-for-like quality, complete live-state capacity, and measured latency at the intended concurrency.

**Fallacy**: *Data loading can be fixed after deployment.*

Teams that optimize only GPU kernels and communication can still find their accelerators waiting for input. The data loading pipeline must sustain the cluster’s consumption rate across storage, preprocessing, and host-to-GPU transfer. The chapter’s pre-tokenized language-model example needs modest raw bandwidth, but tokenization, sequence packing, and shuffling can change the host-side demand. Teams must size CPU workers, buffering, and storage concurrency from the actual pipeline. Local NVMe staging and prefetching are useful when they relieve the measured bottleneck; neither substitutes for testing the complete path at fleet concurrency.

**Pitfall**: *Buying homogeneous clusters without modeling workload diversity.*

Uniform hardware simplifies support and placement, but that benefit must be weighed against the useful work existing devices can still perform. For our 175B model, training and low-batch decode stress different resources. Separate pools can exploit that difference without placing mismatched devices in the same tightly synchronized group.

The A100’s 2.04 TB/s and the H100’s 3.35 TB/s supply different bandwidth ceilings. Retaining older devices for an inference pool can reduce cost when their measured throughput, power, and support costs compare favorably at the required latency. The distinction is between *job-level* balance within a synchronized group and *cluster-level* hardware uniformity. A heterogeneous fleet can preserve balanced groups, but its matching benefit must cover the added scheduling and operational complexity.

**Pitfall**: *Treating commissioning and burn-in as schedule slack.*

Commissioning is part of the system build, not a buffer that can be consumed when hardware delivery slips. Burn-in tests, cable validation, firmware qualification, thermal soak, and collective-communication stress runs are the first time the node, rack, network, and facility layers operate as one machine. Compressing that phase turns installation defects into production failures, where each debugging cycle idles the full fleet rather than a small acceptance-test window.

**Self-Check: Question**

1.  The modeled optimizer step of the 175B reference job (TP8 $\times$ PP8 $\times$ DP16, a global batch of 1,536 sequences) takes $8.71\text{ s}$ on 1,024 GPUs. Doubling the cluster to 2,048 GPUs with the same layout and global batch gives 32 data-parallel replicas, and the modeled step falls to $4.64\text{ s}$. What scaling efficiency does the doubling achieve?

    1.  100% (ideal linear speedup, halving step time to $4.36\text{ s}$).
    2.  $\approx 94\%$ (speedup of $\approx 1.88\times$ from $8.71\text{ s}$ to $4.64\text{ s}$, because part of the step does not shrink as replicas are added).
    3.  50% (step time remains completely unchanged at $8.71\text{ s}$).
    4.  $\approx 125\%$ (superlinear speedup due to increased aggregate L2 cache capacity).

2.  Explain how neglecting facility readiness and commissioning can delay an ML training program by months even after GPUs have been delivered to the loading dock. Identify two concrete critical-path failure modes.

3.  A team serving a 70B-parameter LLM at batch size 1 on H100s observes poor token generation latency and proposes upgrading to a B200 ($4{,}500\text{ FP8 TFLOP/s}$ peak, $\approx 8\text{ TB/s}$ HBM3e) primarily to capture the $\approx 2.3\times$ boost in peak arithmetic throughput. Which hidden assumption in their reasoning is fundamentally flawed?

    1.  Assuming liquid-cooled racks are capable of hosting high-density B200 servers.
    2.  Assuming checkpoint storage bandwidth is necessary during inference serving.
    3.  Assuming InfiniBand switches are required for single-node inference deployments.
    4.  Assuming that peak arithmetic throughput governs performance when the modeled batch-1 decode workload is limited by weight bandwidth; the HBM bandwidth ratio is the relevant first estimate.

4.  A cost analyst evaluates cloud GPU pricing by comparing hourly rates: H100 at \$4.00/GPU-hour vs. V100 at \$0.80/GPU-hour, concluding that V100 is $5\times$ cheaper. Using throughput equivalence (an H100 delivers $\approx 7.9\times$ the FP16 Tensor throughput of a V100), explain why this hourly rate comparison is a procurement fallacy.
