# V2-02.06 The Node

[[Home|Home]] · [[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/Volume 2 Overview|Volume Overview]] · [[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/01 The Fleet/02 Compute Infrastructure/V2-02 Compute Infrastructure|V2-02 Compute Infrastructure]]

^sec-compute-node

The running model needs more memory than one accelerator provides. A node supplies several accelerators in one chassis, but each retains its own memory; dividing the work creates communication that the local fabric must sustain. Tensor parallelism divides an operation across accelerators, pipeline parallelism assigns successive model stages to different groups, and data parallelism assigns different training examples to model replicas whose updates must be combined. These strategies impose different traffic patterns, and the node’s internal fabric decides which of them it can carry at the speed the partition needs.

Aggregating accelerators solves the capacity problem only if the interconnect between them is fast enough that the group behaves as one machine rather than as several machines passing messages. The 175B model’s weights span at least five accelerators, and the full training state spans an entire 8-H100 node and more, so some model state must live on different chips. Depending on the partition, execution exchanges partial results, activations, gradients, or parameter shards. Whether those exchanges are cheap or ruinous depends on the local fabric, which is why the node is the first scale level where the accelerator’s specifications matter less than the wires between accelerators. The training system can combine tensor parallelism, optimizer sharding, and memory offload as needed to meet its capacity budget, and each strategy places a different demand on the interconnect.

**Definition 2.4: Node**

***Node*** is the ML training cluster’s physical server chassis that aggregates multiple accelerators (typically 8) through a high-speed intra-node interconnect (NVLink or ICI), providing a physical packaging and failure boundary. The fast communication domain can coincide with one chassis or extend across several, depending on the fabric.

1.  **Significance**: The NVLink bandwidth within a DGX H100 node reaches 900 GB/s bidirectional, or 450 GB/s per direction, while inter-node InfiniBand NDR provides 50 GB/s, a 9× per-direction gap that constrains where each parallelism strategy can run. For this DGX H100 topology, frequent tensor-parallel collectives favor the intra-node NVLink domain; pipeline parallelism can span nodes when the network sustains stage-boundary activation traffic. The node is also the primary failure domain. A single node failure in a bulk synchronous parallel training job, where workers advance in lockstep at step barriers, idles the entire cluster until recovery.
2.  **Distinction**: Unlike a single accelerator (which provides fast HBM bandwidth but limited capacity), a node aggregates 8$\times$ the HBM capacity and 8$\times$ the compute of a single chip. That is enough to place large FP16 weight shards (8 $\times$ 80 GB = 640 GB on DGX H100), but not enough to keep the full Adam training state in HBM.
3.  **Common pitfall**: A frequent misconception is that nodes in a distributed training job operate independently. In bulk synchronous parallel training, all nodes are synchronously coupled at every step barrier. A performance degradation on one node (from thermal throttling, a faulty NIC, or a slow storage read) stalls the entire cluster and drops overall MFU for the duration of that step.

Holding the overflow in host DRAM cannot replace wiring eight accelerators together. A server’s DDR5 channels supply ample capacity (512 GB to 2 TB per node) at a fraction of HBM’s cost, but their bandwidth is orders of magnitude too slow to feed Tensor Cores on the active forward and backward path. Pooling the HBM of eight accelerators inside one chassis keeps the active working set in high-bandwidth memory, with the caveat that each accelerator’s HBM remains private to its own package. Software must shard or replicate tensors explicitly and invoke collectives or peer-to-peer transfers whenever an operation crosses device boundaries, so an eight-GPU node delivers 8$\times$ aggregate capacity and 8$\times$ aggregate compute without ever behaving like a single shared-memory processor.

The capacity arithmetic also explains the appeal of multiple packages. Holding a 175B model’s 350 GB of FP16 weights on one accelerator, at the example 17–24 GB per HBM stack, would require 350 GB spread across roughly 15–21 stacks. That exceeds the 5-stack H100 and 8-stack B200-class configurations used here. Such a package would need its own area, yield, power, and cooling analysis. Distributing capacity across 8 accelerators instead uses available packages and pays for communication between their private memories.

### Bandwidth Hierarchy

^sec-compute-bandwidth-hierarchy

Placing a layer-by-layer tensor-parallel collective across a 50 GB/s inter-node link instead of a 450 GB/s intra-node link multiplies its transfer floor ninefold and stalls every accelerator in the group. Multi-accelerator systems are governed less by the *number of chips* than by the *speed at which they can exchange data* across physical boundaries. Data movement speed drops by orders of magnitude as it crosses package, board, and rack boundaries, each with a different physical medium, connector technology, and signaling constraint. This physical ordering of data transfer rates, from on-chip SRAM at the fast end to the wide-area network at the slow end, is the bandwidth hierarchy; this ordering dictates where in the hierarchy each type of parallelism can operate efficiently. The cliff between intra-node and inter-node links is where the communication wall first appears.

The hierarchy reflects the physical cost of distance. Shorter signal paths over denser wiring achieve higher throughput at lower energy per bit, so at each boundary the effective bandwidth $({\text{BW}})$ drops by roughly an order of magnitude while latency $({L_{\text{lat}}})$ rises. The exact size of each gap depends on link count, signaling, and the measurement boundary, but the gradient itself sets a scaling ceiling for distributed training. The common mistake is to treat all cluster communication as equal. Parallelism placement must be hierarchy-aware, because putting high-frequency synchronization such as tensor parallelism on a slow tier leaves the arithmetic units $({R_{\text{peak}}})$ waiting and collapses scaling efficiency $({\eta_{\text{scaling}}})$, the share of ideal linear speedup that the cluster delivers.

The bandwidth hierarchy can be read as concentric zones around each accelerator ([[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/01 The Fleet/02 Compute Infrastructure/V2-02.06 The Node#^fig-infra-bandwidth-hierarchy|figure 9]]), losing 7.4$\times$ to 9$\times$ of bandwidth at each physical boundary on the way out through NVLink, InfiniBand, and remote storage. The innermost zone is the HBM on the same package, with terabytes per second of bandwidth and sub-microsecond access latency. The next zone holds the other accelerators in the node’s NVLink domain, reachable at hundreds of gigabytes per second per GPU with microsecond-scale transfer latency. The outermost zone spans the other nodes in the cluster, reached through InfiniBand ports that each supply tens of gigabytes per second per direction, with point-to-point latency measured in single-digit microseconds. Latency figures are comparable only when they measure the same thing (a memory access, a link transfer, or a complete collective), and bandwidth ratios only when they compare matching endpoints and directions, as the representative paths of [[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/01 The Fleet/02 Compute Infrastructure/V2-02.06 The Node#^tbl-bandwidth-hierarchy-compute|table 9]] do.

| **Domain**           |  **Interconnect**  | **Bandwidth** |   **Latency**    | **Scaling limit** |
|:---------------------|:------------------:|:-------------:|:----------------:|:-----------------:|
| **Intra-package**    | Silicon interposer |  ~3.35 TB/s   | $<100\text{ ns}$ |    Single chip    |
| **Intra-node**       |     NVLink/ICI     |   ~450 GB/s   |     ~0.5 μs      | Node (8–16 chips) |
| **Intra-node (I/O)** |   PCIe Gen5 x16    |   ~64 GB/s    |      ~1 μs       | CPU-GPU, NIC-GPU  |
| **Inter-node**       |   InfiniBand NDR   |   ~50 GB/s    |      ~5 μs       |  Pod (thousands)  |

Table 9: **The Bandwidth Hierarchy**: These representative paths have different bandwidth budgets. Link bandwidths are per direction; link count, signaling, and system configuration determine the gap between tiers. These example bandwidth gaps favor placing frequent tensor-parallel collectives within the fast communication domain, which need not coincide with a server boundary.

**Napkin Math 2.3: The physics of the staircase**

**Problem**: A 10 GB buffer must move one hop at each tier of the hierarchy, with a local HBM read as the reference point. How much does the tier it crosses change the transfer time?

**Math**: Transfer time is ${T_{\text{transfer}}}= {D_{\text{vol}}} / {\text{BW}}$.

1.  **HBM (local read, the reference point)**: 10 GB / 3,350 GB/s $\approx$ 3.0 ms.
2.  **NVLink (intra-node)**: 10 GB / 450 GB/s $\approx$ 22.2 ms.
3.  **InfiniBand (inter-node)**: 10 GB / 50 GB/s $\approx$ 200 ms.

**Systems insight**: For the same payload, the example inter-node path has a transfer-time lower bound 9× larger than the example intra-node path. Size alone does not decide which traffic may cross that cliff; frequency and overlap do. Tensor parallelism exchanges activations inside every layer, on the critical path, so it favors the fast local NVLink domain in this configuration. Data parallelism moves the largest buffer, the full gradient, but only once per step and overlapped with the backward pass, so it can cross the scarce InfiniBand tier. A partition that fits in memory can still spend much of a step waiting if its exposed exchanges exceed the communication budget.

Each cliff in the bandwidth staircase has a physical cause at its distance scale. Millimeter-long traces on a silicon interposer can carry a wide, slow-clocked parallel interface without serializer/deserializer (SerDes) transceivers. NVLink crosses tens of centimeters of board copper, which requires SerDes transceivers with equalization to recover the signal at high per-lane rates. PCIe pays for universal compatibility with protocol overhead (packet headers, flow-control credits, and error checking). InfiniBand crosses meters of cable between racks, adding optical conversion on longer runs, forward error correction, and switch hops. **[[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/01 The Fleet/03 Network Fabrics/V2-03.04 Level 1 Wire and Link#^sec-network-fabrics-serdes|SerDes, link budget, and power]]** develops the signaling physics behind these cliffs. For node design, link count and sustained bandwidth must be evaluated together with the power and cooling load of the components that provide them.

The practical consequence for model partitioning is that tensor parallelism (TP), which splits individual matrix multiplications across accelerators and requires collective exchanges within layers, generates communication volume proportional to the model’s hidden dimension hundreds of times per second. Over NVLink, each of these exchanges takes under a millisecond for the running model ([[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/01 The Fleet/02 Compute Infrastructure/V2-02.06 The Node#^sec-compute-dense-nodes|section 2.5.2]] computes the idealized lower bound). At the reference InfiniBand port rate, the bandwidth-only lower bound for the same exchange is 9× as large; the step-time effect also depends on startup, routing, and overlap. For this topology TP therefore stays within a node, although a system whose fast scale-up fabric spans several chassis can place a tensor-parallel group across them; communication cost, rather than the server enclosure, determines the useful partition.

The hierarchy also explains why pipeline parallelism occupies an intermediate position in the bandwidth requirements. In pipeline parallelism, the model is divided into sequential stages, with each stage assigned to a different group of accelerators. The communication between stages consists of activations flowing forward during the forward pass and gradients flowing backward during the backward pass, with a volume proportional to the batch size times the hidden dimension.

For our 175B model with hidden dimension 12,288 and one sequence of 2,048 tokens per microbatch, the activation tensor at each stage boundary is approximately $1 \times 2,048 \times 12,288 \times 2 B$ bytes (FP16) $\approx$ 50 MB. This is far smaller than the 350 GB gradient AllReduce required by data parallelism, which is why pipeline parallelism places less demanding requirements on the inter-node network.

Activation transfers occur once per microbatch per stage boundary. That is far less often than tensor parallelism’s AllReduce inside every layer, yet more often than data parallelism’s gradient exchange, which happens once per step. Small messages at an intermediate frequency let pipeline parallelism tolerate the lower bandwidth of inter-node links, which makes it the natural way to split a model whose layers do not fit in one node.

Both pipeline and data parallelism therefore cross node boundaries, for different reasons ([[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/01 The Fleet/02 Compute Infrastructure/V2-02.06 The Node#^tbl-compute-parallelism-traffic|table 10]]). Pipeline traffic is small per message and sits between the other two in frequency. The data-parallel gradient exchange is the largest transfer, but it occurs once per step and can overlap the backward pass. That overlap makes it the most latency-tolerant of the three, although any unfinished exchange still delays the update. For the example topology, the resulting mapping places tensor parallelism within the node, pipeline stages across nodes, and data-parallel groups across the cluster. This *de facto* pattern is a starting point for the capacity and timing calculation; a different scale-up domain, message size, or overlap opportunity can justify a different placement.

| **Strategy**             | **Size per exchange**                            | **Frequency**                            | **Overlap with computation**                  | **Placement on this topology**               |
|:-------------------------|:-------------------------------------------------|:-----------------------------------------|:----------------------------------------------|:---------------------------------------------|
| **Tensor parallelism**   | Activation-sized partial results                 | Inside every layer, forward and backward | Little; later operations depend on the result | Within the NVLink domain                     |
| **Pipeline parallelism** | One microbatch’s activations at a stage boundary | Once per microbatch per boundary         | Partial, through microbatch scheduling        | Across nodes, adjacent stages close together |
| **Data parallelism**     | The full gradient                                | Once per step                            | Substantial, during the backward pass         | Across the cluster                           |

Table 10: **Traffic Shape by Parallelism Strategy**: The placement rule follows from size and frequency together. Frequent exchanges on the critical path need the fastest tier, while the largest exchange can cross the slowest tier because it happens once per step and overlaps the backward pass.

^tbl-compute-parallelism-traffic

The mapping constrains how the scheduler assigns devices and nodes. Members of a tensor-parallel group belong inside one fast domain (on this topology, one chassis joined by NVLink), because their dependent exchanges recur inside every layer. Adjacent pipeline stages belong in the same rack or neighboring racks, where their activation and gradient traffic crosses the fewest InfiniBand switch hops. Data-parallel groups can span the whole cluster. Physical adjacency helps only when it corresponds to better connectivity. The node’s PCIe hierarchy adds another placement constraint by connecting accelerators to host CPUs, network interface cards (NICs), and storage, including the path used to reach host DRAM.

#### The PCIe Hierarchy Within a Node

^sec-compute-infrastructure-pcie-hierarchy-within-node-c56f

While NVLink carries GPU-to-GPU traffic, PCIe Gen5 connects accelerators, host CPUs, network adapters, and storage through the node’s peripheral hierarchy. Host DRAM attaches to the CPU’s memory controllers; a GPU transfer to that memory also traverses its host-facing PCIe path. PCIe bandwidth, approximately 64 GB/s per direction (128 GB/s bidirectional) per x16 link, can therefore constrain data loading, checkpointing, or GPU-to-network transfers. Which traffic competes depends on the actual links and switches those paths share.

A standard DGX H100 node features two host CPUs, each managing a PCIe root complex, the CPU-side hub where its PCIe lanes originate, and PCIe switches fan those lanes out. Each of the 8 GPUs and each of the 8 InfiniBand host channel adapters (HCAs, the network interface cards of an InfiniBand fabric) has its own x16 link ([[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/01 The Fleet/02 Compute Infrastructure/V2-02.06 The Node#^fig-node-pcie-numa|figure 10]]). This configuration provides an aggregate theoretical bandwidth of about 2 TB/s within the chassis. However, this bandwidth is shared among competing traffic streams. During a single training step, the PCIe bus simultaneously carries host-to-GPU data batches, GPU-to-HCA gradient shards for inter-node AllReduce, and periodic CPU-to-NVMe checkpoint writes. The gradient shards travel through GPUDirect remote direct memory access (RDMA), which moves data between GPU memory and the HCA without a host-memory copy. Contention between these streams can cause unexpected pipeline stalls, particularly when gradient synchronization saturates the links typically used for data loading.

The dual-CPU architecture introduces significant non-uniform memory access (NUMA) effects. The 8 GPUs are physically partitioned, with 4 connected to CPU 0’s root complex and 4 to CPU 1’s. A GPU connected to CPU 0 can reach CPU 0’s DRAM through its local PCIe root complex, whereas access to CPU 1’s DRAM also traverses the inter-socket link. The latency and bandwidth penalty depends on the processor, socket interconnect, PCIe topology, transfer size, and concurrent traffic, so no fixed percentage applies across nodes. Data-loading pipelines should measure this topology and place worker processes, pinned buffers (host memory locked in place so that devices can read it directly), NICs, and target GPUs within the same NUMA domain when the local path improves sustained ingestion. Placement is therefore part of the parallelism strategy, because a scheduler that ignores PCIe roots, NIC locality, and NUMA domains can turn an otherwise valid tensor- or pipeline-parallel plan into a host-bound bottleneck.

### Dense Node Designs

^sec-compute-dense-nodes

Given the bandwidth hierarchy, the node must connect 8 accelerators with enough bandwidth for tensor-parallel operations across their private memories. A switched fabric can provide full bisection bandwidth, enough capacity across any equal division of the endpoints to carry their full injection rate. Comparing a ring, a mesh, and a switched design shows what connectivity costs and which communication patterns it supports.

Eight GPUs in one chassis can be wired as a ring, a full mesh, or a switched fabric ([[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/01 The Fleet/02 Compute Infrastructure/V2-02.06 The Node#^fig-node-topology-comparison|figure 11]]). A ring is inexpensive, needing only ${G_{\text{node}}}$ links for ${G_{\text{node}}}$ GPUs, and suits ring-AllReduce, in which each GPU sends to one neighbor while receiving from the other so that every link stays busy. Tensor parallelism, however, issues frequent collectives after sharded matrix operations. These include ReduceScatter, which leaves each participant with one shard of a combined result, and AllGather, which assembles shards at every participant. A ring’s fixed multi-hop paths (up to four hops between distant GPUs) constrain those patterns. A full mesh reaches every peer in one hop but needs ${G_{\text{node}}}({G_{\text{node}}}-1) / 2 = 28$ links for 8 GPUs, a count that grows quadratically and soon becomes impractical to route. The NVSwitch crossbar puts every pair one switch hop apart and provides full-bisection switched connectivity with a manageable number of switch chips.

![[90 Assets/Volume II/02 Compute Infrastructure/Node Topology Comparison 625937bf17.svg]]

Figure 11: **Intra-node GPU Topology Comparison**: Three ways to wire eight GPUs inside one node. Left: a ring gives each GPU only two links, so traffic between distant GPUs passes through up to four hops. Center: a full mesh reaches every peer in one hop but needs ${G_{\text{node}}}-1$ links per GPU and ${G_{\text{node}}}({G_{\text{node}}}-1)/2$ links in total, which becomes impractical to route beyond about eight GPUs. Right: an NVSwitch fabric gives each GPU one set of links to the switch and puts every pair one switch hop apart, with up to 450 GB/s per direction per H100 SXM GPU (900 GB/s bidirectional); that per-GPU injection bandwidth is not the aggregate bisection bandwidth of the switch fabric.

^fig-node-topology-comparison

Consider the NVIDIA DGX H100 architecture. Eight H100 GPUs sit on a single baseboard, each connected to four NVSwitch chips via 18 NVLink 4.0 lanes. The NVSwitch chips form a non-blocking crossbar[[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/01 The Fleet/02 Compute Infrastructure/V2-02.15 Footnotes#^fn5|⁵]] through which any GPU can reach any other GPU. Each GPU’s port budget is 900 GB/s bidirectional, or 450 GB/s in one direction, shared across that GPU’s simultaneous transfers; it is not a separate full-bandwidth link for every GPU pair.

The result is a node with 640 GB of aggregate HBM capacity and a combined peak compute throughput of 8 $\times$ 1,979 TFLOP/s, while each GPU retains private HBM. Training frameworks partition tensors explicitly across those devices, and NVSwitch reduces the communication cost between shards without making placement or collective synchronization transparent. The NVSwitch chips themselves consume approximately 200 W each (800 W total for the NVSwitch fabric), a nontrivial power overhead justified by the bisection bandwidth it provides. In physical terms, the NVSwitch fabric alone consumes more power than an entire previous-generation GPU (the V100 drew 300 W).

Tracing one microbatch of the running model through its tensor-parallel exchanges shows what that NVSwitch power budget buys. Assume the schedule of the shared reference step, with two activation-sized AllReduces per layer in each forward and backward pass, one after the attention block and one after the feed-forward network; the partition and collective schedule are scenario inputs, not a universal Transformer schedule. During the modeled tensor-parallel forward pass, each GPU holds one shard of each weight matrix and computes its portion of the matrix multiplication. For a Transformer layer with hidden dimension 12,288 (typical for a 175B model), one projection maps each token’s internal vector, already split across the 8 GPUs, back to the full hidden width. Each GPU holds a $12{,}288{\times}1{,}536$ slice of that projection’s weight matrix, which keeps the full output width but covers only its own eighth of the input. Every output value is a sum over all input values, so one GPU can compute only part of that sum. Each GPU therefore produces a full-width vector of partial sums for every token, and an AllReduce operation sums those partial results across all 8 GPUs over NVLink.

↪ How tensor parallelism splits weight matrices across devices is developed in **[[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/02 Distributed ML/06 Distributed Training/V2-06.06 Model Parallelism#^sec-distributed-training-systems-systems-tensor-parallelism-d76e|Tensor parallelism]]**.

The logical output tensor for one AllReduce contains $1 \times 2{,}048 \times 12{,}288 \times 2$ bytes (FP16). For a microbatch of one 2,048-token sequence, that payload is roughly 50 MB. An ideal ring AllReduce with 8 participants moves $2({N}-1)/{N}=$ 1.75 times the payload per GPU (**[[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/02 Distributed ML/05 Collective Communication/V2-05 Collective Communication#^sec-collective-communication|Collective Communication]]** derives this factor), or about 88 MB. At 450 GB/s of one-direction bandwidth, the transfer-time lower bound is approximately 0.196 ms.

With two AllReduces in each of the 96 layers in both the forward and the backward pass, the 384 transfers sum to about 75.2 ms per microbatch before overlap. NVSwitch provides the paths needed for the collective, but this value remains an ideal bandwidth-only lower bound that excludes startup latency, software overhead, and competing traffic.

↪ The ${\alpha}$-${\beta}$ model, which adds the fixed per-message startup latency this bound omits to the bytes-over-bandwidth transfer time, is developed in **[[05 Reference/Volume II - Scaling/V2 Communication Foundations#^sec-appdx-comm-foundations-alpha-beta|The α-β Communication Model]]**.

Judging that bound needs a compute time to compare it with. At about $6 \times {P}$ floating-point operations per training token, the group needs 604 ms of computation per microbatch when each GPU sustains 45 percent of its 989 TFLOP/s dense FP16 peak; the fractions below scale with that time, so a longer compute time makes the same communication matter less. Against it, the unoverlapped NVLink lower bound leaves computation at 88.9 percent of combined compute-plus-communication time. The reference job splits the layers across 8 pipeline stages, so each stage’s group carries the same fraction of both the transfers and the computation, and this share does not change. Summed over the 96 microbatches of one optimizer step, one stage’s transfers come to 0.90 s, the tensor-parallel communication time modeled for the shared reference step. Actual tensor-parallel efficiency can be higher when collectives overlap useful computation or lower when latency, contention, load imbalance, and nonparallel work add delay, so it must be measured rather than inferred from peak link bandwidth alone.

**Example 2.3: The NVLink bandwidth surprise**

**Scenario**: A team evaluates tensor parallelism over a hypothetical PCIe Gen5 path with 64 GB/s bandwidth, using the payload and the 604 ms per-microbatch compute time derived with the NVLink estimate above.

**Diagnosis**: The modeled tensor-parallel schedule executes 384 AllReduce events per microbatch across 96 layers. Its PCIe bandwidth-only transfer times sum to 528 ms per microbatch. Without overlap, communication accounts for 46.7 percent of the combined compute and transfer estimate; this is not a measured stall fraction.

**Systems lesson**: Under the same assumptions, NVLink lowers the transfer bound to 0.196 ms per AllReduce and raises computation to 88.9 percent of the combined time. The comparison explains why this partition favors a fast local fabric. Real collective performance must also include startup, topology, contention, and overlap.

^exmp-compute-infrastructure-nvlink-bandwidth-surprise

The DGX H100 pairs each GPU with its own InfiniBand ConnectX-7 HCA, which reads and writes GPU memory directly over PCIe through GPUDirect RDMA, so inter-node gradient exchange avoids staging copies in host DRAM while still consuming PCIe and network bandwidth. The host CPU prepares and loads batches, submits kernels, and coordinates the training framework. Sufficient CPU capacity and I/O bandwidth let that work overlap the accelerator fabric’s computation and communication; otherwise, slow preprocessing or submission can starve the GPUs even when their local memory and interconnect are fast enough.

↪ The RDMA and GPUDirect data path that bypasses host memory is developed in **[[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/01 The Fleet/03 Network Fabrics/V2-03.05 Level 2 Transport and the Performance Model#^sec-network-fabrics-rdma|RDMA and GPUDirect]]**.

Host CPUs also pay a “data center tax” of shared infrastructure work. A profile of Google’s fleet found that such common building blocks consumed nearly 30 percent of cycles across jobs ([[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/01 The Fleet/02 Compute Infrastructure/V2-02.14 Source References#^ref-kanev2015profiling|Kanev et al. 2015]]), and network protocol processing, storage virtualization, and security functions add to that load on cloud hosts. ML nodes can therefore incorporate data processing units (DPUs) or SmartNICs. Devices like the NVIDIA BlueField DPU offload these infrastructure tasks to dedicated ARM cores and hardware accelerators integrated into the network adapter itself. Moving the control plane for RDMA, firewall rules, and storage protocols to the DPU recovers cycles for the ML pipeline: data loading, tokenization, and kernel orchestration on the host CPU. The DPU also acts as an isolated security domain, allowing cloud providers to maintain control over the network and storage layer via the DPU while granting customers full access to the host CPU and GPUs. For our 175B model training, DPU offload can keep RDMA protocol processing outside the host CPU critical path for gradient synchronization, which flows from GPU HBM toward the network fabric without host CPU involvement.

#### Alternative Node Architectures

^sec-compute-infrastructure-alternative-node-architectures-2e3c

Spending board area, power, and cost on four NVSwitch chips makes sense when tensor-parallel collectives sit inside every layer, but workloads dominated by embedding capacity, data parallelism, or cost-sensitive serving hit different limits first. Alternative node designs reallocate that budget. An AMD MI300X server emphasizes large HBM capacity on each package, Google TPU pods wire chips directly into a mesh without a central baseboard switch, and Intel/Habana Gaudi nodes integrate Ethernet RDMA ports directly onto the accelerator die. Comparing them against a DGX H100 node reveals where each architecture spends its scarce budget.

In a switch-rich node like the DGX H100, universal any-to-any local exchange is the priority because each Transformer layer places intra-node collectives inside the model’s inner loop. The 900 GB/s H100 NVLink fabric therefore buys a software abstraction, in which the training framework can treat eight accelerators as one tightly coupled tensor-parallel group for the layers that must be split across chips.

Other systems move that boundary. TPU pods use the inter-chip interconnect and a mesh-oriented topology instead of making a central switch the defining component of the node; a TPU v5p chip’s vendor-specified ICI bandwidth is 1,200 GB/s. The mesh can reduce switch cost and expose a regular structure to the compiler, but it also makes placement more visible, because the runtime must respect neighbor relationships rather than assuming that every chip pair has the same communication path. That is a reasonable trade when the workload is regular enough for the compiler and collective libraries to map communication onto the topology.

The MI300X shifts the pressure point from local switching toward memory capacity. Its package exposes 192 GB of HBM at 5.3 TB/s, compared with 80 GB on an H100-class accelerator. An eight-accelerator MI300X node would therefore provide 1,536 GB of aggregate HBM, enough to hold the 350 GB FP16 weight tensor for our 175B running model. That does not eliminate the optimizer, gradient, activation, and fragmentation problem that [[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/01 The Fleet/02 Compute Infrastructure/V2-02.06 The Node#^sec-compute-node-memory|section 2.5.3]] quantifies, but it changes how often the system must spill state into slower memory tiers and how much microbatch headroom remains before offload becomes mandatory.

Gaudi takes a different route by integrating RDMA-capable Ethernet into the accelerator and using a more network-centric design for communication. A Gaudi 2 accelerator’s 24 integrated 100 GbE ports supply 300 GB/s of aggregate RDMA over Converged Ethernet (RoCE) bandwidth, against the 900 GB/s of local exchange per GPU in an H100-class NVLink node. The newer Gaudi 3 keeps that Ethernet-centric design while carrying 128 GB of HBM per accelerator, so capacity is not the trade being made. The question is whether simplifying the fabric and using Ethernet consistently across node and pod is worth giving up the high local exchange bandwidth of a switch-rich NVLink design. That trade can be attractive when data parallelism, inference serving, or cluster cost dominates, and less attractive when tensor-parallel collectives sit inside every layer.

These alternatives are not a ranking of vendors. They are different placements of scarce resources: switching bandwidth in the node, regular topology in the pod, HBM capacity in the package, or network attachment on the accelerator. For the 175B model used throughout this chapter, the tensor-parallel portion of training makes local accelerator communication unusually important, so the DGX-style answer is natural. In other regimes, especially memory-heavy recommenders, topology-regular training, or cost-sensitive serving fleets, a different boundary can become the one that matters most. Software ecosystem maturity, supply availability, and total cost of ownership then decide whether the theoretical architectural fit becomes a deployable system.

### Node Memory Partitioning

^sec-compute-node-memory

The node’s aggregate HBM must hold far more than the weights, and how that state divides across the node’s memory tiers decides which parallelism strategies are possible. Consider the memory budget for training our 175B model. The model weights in FP16 require 350 GB. The Adam optimizer maintains two additional values for every parameter (first and second moments) in FP32 precision, adding 175 $\times 10^9 \times 2 \times 4$ bytes = 1,400 GB. Gradients require another 350 GB. This subtotal is approximately 2.1 TB, far exceeding both a single accelerator’s 80 GB HBM and an 8-H100 node’s 640 GB aggregate HBM, and it still omits two terms. Activations are the intermediate results the backward pass needs. The FP32 master weights are a full-precision copy of the parameters that the optimizer updates, kept because an update far smaller than a weight would be lost to FP16 rounding; this is the mixed-precision copy introduced with the precision formats in [[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/01 The Fleet/02 Compute Infrastructure/V2-02.05 Sustained Compute and Precision#^sec-compute-tensor-cores|section 2.4]].

A common misconception is that a model’s memory footprint equals its weight storage. With those two terms included, the FP32 master weights, optimizer moments, gradients, and activations collectively consume about 7–8$\times$ the memory of the FP16 parameters alone. These components reside in different tiers of the node’s memory hierarchy. For our 175B model, training memory partitions across six distinct components ([[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/01 The Fleet/02 Compute Infrastructure/V2-02.06 The Node#^tbl-memory-breakdown|table 11]]).

| **Component**              | **Precision** | **Memory per parameter** | **Total for 175B model** |
|:---------------------------|:-------------:|:------------------------:|:------------------------:|
| **Model weights**          |     FP16      |         2 bytes          |          350 GB          |
| **Gradients**              |     FP16      |         2 bytes          |          350 GB          |
| **Master weights**         |     FP32      |         4 bytes          |          700 GB          |
| **Optimizer (Adam ${m}$)** |     FP32      |         4 bytes          |          700 GB          |
| **Optimizer (Adam ${v}$)** |     FP32      |         4 bytes          |          700 GB          |
| **Activations**            |     Mixed     |         Variable         |        100–400 GB        |
| **Total**                  |               |                          |      **2.9–3.2 TB**      |

Table 11: **Training Memory Breakdown for a 175B-Parameter Frontier Model**: The optimizer state alone (first and second moments in FP32) consumes 4$\times$ the memory of the model weights, making optimizer memory the dominant component of training memory. This is why memory optimization techniques focus heavily on optimizer state sharding and offloading.

^tbl-memory-breakdown

The 2.9–3.2 TB budget of [[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/01 The Fleet/02 Compute Infrastructure/V2-02.06 The Node#^tbl-memory-breakdown|table 11]] extends the earlier subtotal with the master weights and activations. Its five persistent rows sum to 16 bytes per parameter, or 2.8 TB, the training state given for Archetype A in **[[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/00 Introduction/01 Introduction/V2-01.07 The Fleet Stack and the Lighthouse Archetypes#^sec-introduction-archetype-gpt4llama3-b02f|Archetype A (GPT-4/Llama-3)]]**, and activations add the rest. The Adam optimizer state in FP32, not the FP16 weights, is the dominant term, and the activation peak, which scales with batch size and sequence length, is the most variable. The lesson for parallelism selection is that the FP16 weights are among the smaller pieces of what the node must hold, so a strategy that only shards weights leaves the largest consumers, the FP32 optimizer moments and master weights, untouched.

A DGX H100 node provides 640 GB of aggregate HBM across its 8 GPUs, plus 2 TB of host DDR5 DRAM. The HBM capacity alone is insufficient for the full training state, as the memory arithmetic for each parallelism strategy shows. With pure data parallelism, each GPU must hold replicated state, and even the 2,100 GB subtotal of weights, gradients, and optimizer moments, before master weights and activations, is physically impossible on an 80 GB device. Even ZeRO stage 3, the form of ZeRO sharding that divides all three components among the workers, yields 2,100 GB / 8 ≈ 262.5 GB per GPU when confined to the 8 GPUs of a single node, still more than triple the available HBM.

Fitting the state into HBM therefore requires expanding across multiple nodes so that a larger pool of accelerators shares the memory footprint. The reference job does so with two partitions that follow the bandwidth hierarchy. Tensor parallelism inside each node splits the tensors and operations within every layer across its 8 GPUs, and pipeline parallelism across nodes gives each of 8 nodes a contiguous block of 12 of the model’s 96 layers. One model replica therefore spans 64 GPUs on 8 nodes. Splitting every component over those 64 GPUs leaves each one 5.47 GB of FP16 weights, the same again of gradients, and 32.8 GB of FP32 master weights and optimizer moments.

Per GPU, the persistent training state comes to 2,800 GB / 64 ≈ 43.8 GB, which fits on an 80 GB device (85.9 GB in decimal bytes) with about 42 GB left for activations, communication buffers, and workspace. That fit sets the pipeline depth. Four stages would leave 87.5 GB per GPU, more than the device holds, so eight is the shallowest power-of-two pipeline that fits. The 16-way data parallelism across the rest of the cluster then repeats the whole 64-GPU partition, and no state needs to be sharded across replicas.

The 43.8 GB per-GPU subtotal is only the starting point, because the activations on top of it grow with microbatch size and sequence length. If activations and workspace do not fit in the remaining headroom, the next step is to shard the optimizer state across the data-parallel replicas, using the memory-efficient data parallelism developed in **[[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/02 Distributed ML/06 Distributed Training/V2-06.04 Data Parallelism#^sec-distributed-training-systems-systems-memoryefficient-data-parallelism-zero-fsdp-0e69|Memory-efficient data parallelism with ZeRO and FSDP]]**. Dividing the FP32 master weights and moments among the 16 replicas would cut the per-GPU state to about 13 GB, and each replica would then update only its own share of the parameters and pass the result to the others. The memory-budget exercise in [[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/01 The Fleet/02 Compute Infrastructure/V2-02.06 The Node#^sec-compute-infrastructure-putting-together-memory-budget-exercise-f357|section 2.5.3.1]] examines a single node without this cross-node partitioning and shows why optimizer offload alone is insufficient there. This arithmetic is why tensor parallelism, pipeline stages, optimizer sharding, activation checkpointing, and memory tiering are used together for large-model training. The node is therefore a high-bandwidth compute and communication unit, not a standalone container for the entire training state.

Partitioning alone can still leave too little room for activations, so it is combined with techniques that shrink the live state. Activation checkpointing stores only a subset of intermediate activations for the backward pass and recomputes the rest from the nearest checkpoint, trading extra forward computation for activation memory, a trade that **[[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/02 Distributed ML/06 Distributed Training/V2-06.04 Data Parallelism#^sec-distributed-training-systems-systems-compute-phase-forward-backward|Compute-phase forward and backward passes]]** develops.

CPU offloading stores state in host DDR5 DRAM and may also move optimizer computation to the CPU. The implementation determines which gradients, weights, or optimizer buffers traverse the PCIe Gen5 path at 64 GB/s. Its cost depends on those bytes, host computation, and the time available to overlap transfers; a short GPU update does not establish cheap offload. NVMe offloading extends storage to SSDs, with the illustrative 5–7 GB/s sequential read rate introducing a further bandwidth constraint that prefetching can hide only when consumption leaves enough time.

An offloaded configuration uses the node’s memory hierarchy as a tiered system sorted by how often each piece of state is touched. HBM holds the weight shards, activations, and accumulating gradients needed on every layer of the forward and backward passes. Host DDR5 absorbs the optimizer moments and master weights, which are read and written only once per step during the parameter update. NVMe extends capacity for the largest models by holding seldom-accessed shards that can be prefetched within the step budget. A configuration with sufficient distributed HBM can instead retain optimizer shards on accelerators. The placement follows the capacity and transfer-time calculation, rather than a fixed assignment of every state type to one tier.

The training framework’s memory manager moves state between these tiers much as a cache controller does, prefetching the next layer’s offloaded weights into HBM during the current layer’s computation and evicting state that will not be needed soon. Prefetch depth sets the balance, because too shallow a prefetch stalls computation on PCIe transfers and too deep a prefetch crowds out the current step’s activations and gradients. [[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/01 The Fleet/02 Compute Infrastructure/V2-02.06 The Node#^tbl-node-memory|Table 12]] quantifies the gaps the manager must bridge, more than two orders of magnitude in bandwidth between HBM and an NVMe drive. For our 175B model, a capacity plan must therefore choose among additional sharding, offload, and reductions in live state, and the next budget tests whether a proposed combination actually fits within one node.

| **Memory tier** |       **Capacity**        |       **Bandwidth**        |          **Latency**           |       **Role**        |
|:----------------|:-------------------------:|:--------------------------:|:------------------------------:|:---------------------:|
| **GPU HBM3**    | 80 GB $\times$ 8 = 640 GB |     3.35 TB/s per GPU      | $<1\text{ }{\text{µ}}\text{s}$ |  Active computation   |
| **Host DDR5**   |           2 TB            | hundreds of GB/s aggregate |        ~1 μs over PCIe         |    Optimizer state    |
| **NVMe SSD**    |          8–30 TB          |   up to 7 GB/s per drive   |            ~100 μs             | Overflow, checkpoints |

Table 12: **Node Memory Hierarchy**: Each tier trades capacity for bandwidth. Latencies are seen from the GPU, so host DRAM includes the PCIe crossing, and a GPU’s own share of host bandwidth is limited by its PCIe link rather than by the aggregate DDR5 figure. The training framework’s memory manager must orchestrate data flow across these tiers to fit models whose total state exceeds the aggregate HBM capacity.

#### Node-Level Memory Budget

^sec-compute-infrastructure-putting-together-memory-budget-exercise-f357

Combining all five memory components for our 175B model on a single DGX H100 node in example [[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/01 The Fleet/02 Compute Infrastructure/V2-02.06 The Node#^exmp-compute-infrastructure-memory-budget-175b-training-dgx-h-h100-86fd59c2|2.4]] shows why closing the budget requires coordinating sharding, checkpointing, and offload together rather than tuning any one component in isolation.

**Example 2.4: Memory budget for 175B training on DGX H100**

**Scenario**: An engineering team calculates the memory budget for training a 175B parameter model using 8-way tensor parallelism on an 8-GPU DGX H100 node (80 GB HBM per GPU).

**Diagnosis**: Without ZeRO sharding, each GPU’s TP-8 slice of the optimizer state alone requires 175 GB per GPU, which is more than the weights and gradients combined (88 GB). A separate FP32 master-weight shard adds 87.5 GB. Together with retained FP16 gradients, the assumed activations, and workspace, these components bring the per-GPU budget to 375 GB, well past the 80 GB HBM limit. That budget already assumes activation checkpointing; its activation peak is a scenario input that must be replaced by measurements for the chosen microbatch and sequence length.

**Systems lesson**: Sizing training memory requires managing weights, gradients, optimizer states, activations, and buffers holistically, because relieving one component does not necessarily clear the envelope. Offloading the optimizer moments and master weights to host DDR5 removes those FP32 components and still leaves 113 GB per GPU, because 8-way tensor parallelism keeps a full weight and gradient shard resident on every GPU. Further reductions require offloading or shrinking another live component. Alternatively, adding devices creates an additional data-parallel group across which ZeRO can shard state. The same eight GPUs cannot supply that group, because together they are already the tensor-parallel group of one replica. With no pipeline stages, the tensor-parallel degree times the data-parallel degree equals the device count, so eight-way tensor parallelism on eight GPUs leaves a data-parallel degree of one.

### Data Loading and I/O Pipeline

^sec-compute-data-loading

Even after the node’s memory budget closes, its accelerators still need a continuous stream of training data from outside the chassis. If the data loading pipeline cannot keep pace with the accelerators’ consumption rate, the most expensive component in the node sits idle; this is the I/O bottleneck. For pre-tokenized language-model training, the raw demand is small. At 500,000 tokens/s stored as 4-byte token IDs, ingestion is only 2 MB/s. The pressure falls instead on the host CPU cores that read, decode, shuffle, and batch examples, on the PCIe paths that data shares with gradient and checkpoint traffic ([[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/01 The Fleet/02 Compute Infrastructure/V2-02.06 The Node#^sec-compute-infrastructure-pcie-hierarchy-within-node-c56f|section 2.5.1.1]]), and on shared storage that every node reads at once.

GPUDirect Storage can remove the host-memory bounce copy for data that needs no host-side preprocessing, and staging data on local NVMe can absorb synchronized bursts that would otherwise hit a shared filesystem. For node design, the requirement is end-to-end sustained bandwidth at the intended concurrency. Storage servers, network paths, host preprocessing, host memory, and accelerator transfer must all keep pace, because a local benchmark that satisfies one node says little about a shared path serving the fleet. Keeping that node productive over a multi-week run then turns on how often a component fault inside the chassis interrupts execution.

### Node Health and Reliability

^sec-compute-infrastructure-node-health-reliability-56a6

When a single GPU suffers an uncorrectable HBM error or a rack power shelf trips, it does not fail in isolation; it takes its entire eight-GPU tensor-parallel group or multi-node rack out of the synchronous training step. A **failure domain** is the set of resources that a single fault takes out of service together, and the physical hierarchy nests these domains inside one another. A failed GPU breaks its tensor-parallel group, which usually spans its node. The node sits in a rack whose shared power shelf, top-of-rack switch, and cooling loop can take every node in it down at once. Racks share row-level power and coolant distribution, and halls share a building’s utility feed, cooling plant, and fire compartment.

For a synchronous training job the node is the smallest domain that matters. An eight-way tensor-parallel group within a node becomes unusable when any one GPU fails, because its collectives require all eight participants. The node’s effective MTBF depends on the combined failure exposure of every component whose loss stops the job, as well as shared power and cooling failures. The node’s figure is therefore an MTBF, the expected interval between successive failures of a unit that keeps running through replacements, while each GPU contributes an MTTF, the distinction drawn with the reliability wall in [[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/01 The Fleet/02 Compute Infrastructure/V2-02.02 The Infrastructure Walls#^sec-compute-infrastructure-infrastructure-walls-4f2c|section 2.1]].

Failure exposure spans memory, interconnects, and the electrical and mechanical systems that support them. An uncorrectable error-correcting code (ECC) fault in an HBM stack forces the accelerator out of service and aborts the active training step. High-speed links degrade as repeated thermal cycles stress connectors and cables, which shows up as link retraining or a fatal communication drop. Power-supply components and the pumps and fans of the cooling path wear out under sustained load, and a loss of power or cooling can take several accelerators down together. The accelerator itself usually dominates the count. In Meta’s report on the Llama 3 405B pretraining run, faulty GPUs and HBM3 memory errors together caused nearly half of the unexpected interruptions, ahead of software bugs and network switches and cables ([[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/01 The Fleet/02 Compute Infrastructure/V2-02.14 Source References#^ref-dubey2024llama|Dubey et al. 2024]]). The ordering in a given fleet still depends on its equipment and operating conditions, so local incident data should confirm it before it guides spare capacity or preventive maintenance.

Health monitoring can flag a node for investigation before another long job lands on it. A rising rate of corrected ECC errors, rising junction temperature (the temperature at the transistors) at constant load, and intermittent link retraining are the most useful early signals, although no single signal establishes a cause or a time to failure. The fleet telemetry that collects them is developed in **[[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/03 The Fleet in Production/12 ML Operations at Scale/V2-12.07 Monitoring at Scale#^sec-ops-scale-fleet-telemetry|Fleet telemetry and hardware observability]]**.

↪ Layered fault detection, from ECC and error-rate monitoring to known-answer stress tests on nodes pulled from production, is developed in **[[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/02 Distributed ML/07 Fault Tolerance/V2-07.05 Hardware Faults at Fleet Scale#^sec-ft-hardware-fault-detection-mitigation-8f7f|Fleet-scale detection and mitigation]]**.

For our 175B model training across 128 nodes, a composite node-level MTBF assumption of 1,000 hours, which includes GPUs, host components, power, cooling, and network links, predicts about 43 node failures over a run of 336 hours, roughly 3.1 failures/day. The estimate multiplies the node count by the run length and divides by the composite MTBF. A GPU-only bound from the per-GPU MTTF of 50,000 hours would predict only 6.9. The node, not the GPU, is therefore the unit that spare capacity and recovery tooling must be sized for. The same accounting, applied to every component of a facility and then to every GPU of a fleet, reappears at larger scope in the facility reliability controls of the rack and the hours-scale fleet failure interval of [[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/01 The Fleet/02 Compute Infrastructure/V2-02.08 The Pod#^sec-compute-fleet|section 2.7.1]].

For our 175B model, a single DGX H100 node provides 640 GB of aggregate HBM, enough to hold the FP16 model weights but not the full Adam training state without sharding and offload. Training also requires processing hundreds of billions of tokens, and a single node would need more than a year of compute to do so ([[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/01 The Fleet/02 Compute Infrastructure/V2-02.08 The Pod#^sec-compute-pod|section 2.7]] works through the numbers), so completing training in weeks requires tens to hundreds of nodes. Stacking those nodes into a physical enclosure defines the next level of infrastructure, where the constraints shift from bandwidth and capacity to raw power and heat.
