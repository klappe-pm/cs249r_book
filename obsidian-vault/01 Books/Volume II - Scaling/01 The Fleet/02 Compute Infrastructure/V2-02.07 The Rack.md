# V2-02.07 The Rack

[[Home|Home]] · [[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/Volume 2 Overview|Volume Overview]] · [[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/01 The Fleet/02 Compute Infrastructure/V2-02 Compute Infrastructure|V2-02 Compute Infrastructure]]

^sec-compute-rack

A standard 42U server rack in a traditional data center draws about 12 kW and can be cooled by room-temperature air pushed through perforated floor tiles. The chapter’s four-node DGX H100 rack scenario draws 33.5 kW, a figure that includes the rack’s allocated cooling overhead. At this density, the engineering constraints shift from silicon and signal integrity to power delivery and thermodynamics. The rack is where the power wall and heat transfer become dominant design forces.

For our 175B model, training across 1,024 GPUs requires 32 racks (4 nodes of 8 GPUs each per rack). Across the cluster, the modeled rack power, which includes each rack’s allocated cooling overhead, sums to approximately 1.1 MW. Delivering this power reliably and removing the resulting heat without allowing any component to exceed its thermal limit is a multidisciplinary engineering challenge spanning electrical, mechanical, and civil engineering. A failure at any point in the power delivery chain, from the utility substation to the individual GPU voltage regulator, can halt the entire training run, wasting hours of computation and potentially corrupting the training state.

**Definition 2.5: Rack**

***Rack*** is the physical infrastructure unit, a standardized 42U enclosure, that houses multiple compute nodes, a top-of-rack (ToR) switch (the first network aggregation point connecting all nodes in the rack to the broader cluster fabric), power distribution units, and cooling distribution manifolds, defining the granularity at which power and cooling capacity must be provisioned.

1.  **Significance**: AI rack power density has grown dramatically. The chapter’s four-node DGX H100 scenario contains 32 GPUs at 700 W each and draws 33.5 kW once host, network, conversion, and allocated cooling overhead are included. This exceeds the roughly 12 kW typical of general-purpose data center racks and requires purpose-designed electrical distribution and cooling capacity. Whether that cooling is engineered air or liquid depends on the system configuration and facility design.
2.  **Distinction**: A node packages compute; a rack integrates shared power, cooling, and network distribution. Components and nodes remain individually serviceable, but their maintenance and replacement must respect the capacity and failure boundaries of those shared systems.
3.  **Common pitfall**: A frequent misconception is that rack placement is a logistical afterthought. Nodes within the same rack share a ToR switch and have single-hop connectivity; nodes in different racks cross at least two switch hops. Placing pipeline stages within a rack can reduce inter-rack traffic when the actual paths and available capacity support the stage-boundary exchanges.

### Power and Thermal Envelopes

^sec-compute-power-wall

Sustaining the compute rate that HBM and Tensor Cores make possible requires a power-delivery and cooling system sized for the workload. Delivery itself adds heat before any computation does, because each of the five voltage-conversion stages between the medium-voltage utility grid and the sub-volt GPU die loses part of the power it passes on ([[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/01 The Fleet/02 Compute Infrastructure/V2-02.07 The Rack#^fig-power-path|figure 12]]).

![[90 Assets/Volume II/02 Compute Infrastructure/Power Path 26f02f7b0a.svg]]

Figure 12: **The Power Delivery Path**: Energy passes through five conversion stages between the medium-voltage grid and the sub-volt supply at the GPU die, and each stage loses a few percent as heat; the band’s height is drawn proportional to the power it carries. The per-stage losses shown are illustrative round values, not measurements of a particular facility; compounded, they lose about a fifth of the power, so delivering 1 MW to the dies draws about 1.3 MW before cooling. PUE uses an IT-equipment boundary and includes facility conversion losses as well as cooling; these losses must not be counted twice. The critical engineering challenge is the rack power distribution unit (PDU) to voltage regulator module (VRM) transition, where 10–40 kW of power must be delivered within a single cabinet.

^fig-power-path

The illustrative conversion losses in [[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/01 The Fleet/02 Compute Infrastructure/V2-02.07 The Rack#^fig-power-path|figure 12]] require roughly 1.3 MW upstream to deliver 1 MW to the dies, before heat removal is counted. Facilities summarize that overhead with power usage effectiveness (PUE), total facility power divided by IT-equipment power, and because the IT boundary already contains some of these conversion losses they must not be counted twice. **[[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/04 The Responsible Fleet/15 Sustainable AI/V2-15.05 Energy Measurement and Modeling#^sec-sustainable-ai-facilitylevel-power-metrics-5558|Facility-level power metrics]]** defines the ratio and draws its boundary.

Every floating-point operation dissipates energy as heat. Dense Tensor Core activity during large matrix multiplications drives heat proportional to the switching activity of billions of transistors. Fleet design must pair each accelerator’s sustained power envelope with any documented transient excursion requirement. At dense fleet scale, this produces the power wall, the point at which facility power delivery and heat removal, rather than available arithmetic units, determine how much compute can be installed and sustained.

The thermodynamic limit (principle [[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/01 The Fleet/V2 Fleet Principles#^pri-thermodynamic-limit|4]]), which holds that every joule a computation draws ends as heat and that a facility can run only as fast as it removes that heat, makes heat removal part of usable compute capacity. A facility can reach its power or cooling envelope while arithmetic capacity is still available to purchase, so the scarce quantity becomes watts per unit of floor area rather than FLOP/s. The power density wall (principle [[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/01 The Fleet/V2 Fleet Principles#^pri-power-density-wall|3]]) adds that spatial constraint, bounding cluster scaling by the watts each rack can dissipate rather than only by silicon area or floor space. If a proposed rack exceeds the engineered airflow’s heat-removal capability, the design must lower its density or move to liquid, which at the 100 kW class of high-density training racks is part of the rack design rather than an option.

**Definition 2.6: Thermal design power (TDP)**

***Thermal design power (TDP)*** is a vendor-defined sustained thermal design target in watts for a specified product configuration and operating condition.

1.  **Significance**: The H100 SXM5 configuration used in this chapter carries a 700 W design target. Sustaining the intended operating point requires a thermal path and facility loop qualified for that target under the vendor’s inlet and coolant conditions.
2.  **Distinction**: TDP does not denote one universal instantaneous-power maximum. Total graphics power (TGP), board power, configurable limits, and transient specifications describe related electrical constraints under conventions that differ by vendor and product.
3.  **Common pitfall**: A frequent misconception is that the advertised wattage alone specifies rack power and cooling. Fleet design must combine the sustained thermal target, configured power limit, workload behavior, component overhead, and transient-power requirement.

The trajectory of TDP records a scaling regime reaching its limits. For three decades, Dennard scaling[[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/01 The Fleet/02 Compute Infrastructure/V2-02.15 Footnotes#^fn6|⁶]] allowed chip designers to shrink transistors while maintaining approximately constant power density. Smaller transistors required lower voltage, so packing more transistors into the same area did not increase heat output per unit area. As voltage scaling and leakage limits ended that regime, architects had to treat energy, cooling, and parallelism as first-order constraints rather than free byproducts of process shrinks ([[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/01 The Fleet/02 Compute Infrastructure/V2-02.14 Source References#^ref-dennard1974|Dennard et al. 1974]]; [[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/01 The Fleet/02 Compute Infrastructure/V2-02.14 Source References#^ref-hennessy2019|Hennessy and Patterson 2019]]). Since then, each generation of accelerator has had to trade performance against power and heat. The era of “free” performance improvements through process shrinks is over; every gain in throughput now has to be paid for in watts, cooling capacity, or specialization.

The V100 (2017) operated at 300 W TDP, the A100 (2020) increased to 400 W, the H100 (2022) reached 700 W, and the B200 (2024) draws 1000 W. In seven years, TDP has more than tripled, growing at approximately 19 percent per year.

If this growth rate continued as a planning scenario on a two-year product cadence, a generation after Blackwell would approach 1,400 W per accelerator, and the generation after that would reach about 2,000 W. At that level, a single 8-GPU node would consume 16 kW of GPU power alone, requiring liquid cooling infrastructure ([[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/01 The Fleet/02 Compute Infrastructure/V2-02.07 The Rack#^sec-compute-cooling|section 2.6.2]]) that can remove heat at rates approaching those of industrial process cooling equipment. The applicable sustained power envelope is not merely a specification on a data sheet; it propagates through every level of the infrastructure stack. The power-delivery chain, cooling system, rack design, and facility electrical infrastructure must accommodate deployed accelerators, their documented transients, and the plausible envelope of future refresh cycles.

The propagation is visible at both ends of the stack in published data. Vendors publish a power rating for each accelerator package, and [[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/01 The Fleet/02 Compute Infrastructure/V2-02.07 The Rack#^fig-chip-vs-cluster-power|figure 13]] (a) collects those ratings across 14 NVIDIA, Google, AMD, and Intel parts. The ratings follow each vendor’s own convention (TDP, maximum board power, or maximum TGP), so they bound design envelopes rather than measure consumption, but they climb steadily from one generation to the next. Panel (b) plots the facility power of supercomputers that Epoch AI tracks for AI work, together with the largest one operating at each date ([[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/01 The Fleet/02 Compute Infrastructure/V2-02.14 Source References#^ref-epochai2026supercomputers|Epoch AI 2026a]]; [[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/01 The Fleet/02 Compute Infrastructure/V2-02.14 Source References#^ref-pilz2025supercomputers|Pilz et al. 2025]]). From 2013 through 2021 that record stayed between 20 MW and 30 MW, a level set by national high-performance computing systems. The Frontier supercomputer raised it to 40 MW in 2022, and AI training clusters then lifted it to about 350 MW by 2025, a 9× increase in three years. Over the same three years the rating of one package rose from the H100’s 700 W TDP to the B300’s 1,400 W maximum TGP, roughly a 2× increase across the two rating conventions. Hotter packages explain only a small part of the jump. The rest comes from the number of accelerators a single system joins together and the facility built to feed them, which is why the power wall at fleet scale is a provisioning problem for the building as much as a thermal problem for the chip.

Accelerators implement power management to operate within their TDP envelope. When the workload does not fully use all SMs (for example, during the communication phase of a training step when the GPUs are waiting for AllReduce to complete), the GPU firmware can disable the clock signal to idle circuitry through **clock gating**. Clock gating eliminates dynamic switching power and frees thermal headroom for the active SMs. A separate mechanism, dynamic voltage and frequency scaling (DVFS), adjusts the chip’s clock frequency and supply voltage to stay within power and thermal limits. During a large matrix multiplication that activates all SMs simultaneously, DVFS reduces the clock frequency to keep total power within TDP. When some SMs are idle, the active SMs can boost frequency because total chip power is below TDP, so the actual clock, and therefore the actual throughput, varies throughout a training step.

Dynamic power management means that the actual power draw of a GPU varies continuously during training. A typical training step might see power swing from 400 W (during communication phases when most SMs are idle) to 700 W (during the forward and backward pass when all Tensor Cores are active) and back, with each transition occurring in microseconds. The voltage regulator modules (VRMs) on the baseboard, which convert the board supply to the sub-volt core voltage, must respond to these transitions fast enough to maintain a stable supply voltage despite the rapidly changing current draw.

DVFS also couples software optimization to the power envelope. A kernel that achieves high SM occupancy (many active warps, or scheduled thread groups, per SM) draws more power, potentially triggering a frequency reduction that partially offsets the occupancy benefit. Conversely, a kernel with moderate occupancy may run at a higher clock frequency, achieving comparable throughput at lower power. Power-normalized throughput (TFLOP/s per watt) is therefore a more robust comparison metric than raw TFLOP/s, and it explains why the same GPU can show different sustained clock frequencies depending on the workload and the cooling solution.

At fleet scale, DVFS also affects power planning. Average workload draw can fall below the configured limit because communication phases and incomplete occupancy leave circuitry idle, but averages do not replace sustained and transient design cases. Some operators deliberately lower accelerator power caps to fit more devices within a fixed facility budget, accepting workload-dependent throughput loss in exchange for lower per-device draw. Power capping is a form of the compute-efficiency trade-off at the operational level.

The physical challenge of 700 W lies in its heat flux. An H100 die measures approximately 814 mm² (roughly $29{\times}28$ mm). Dissipating 700 W from this area produces a heat flux of approximately 86 W/cm². For comparison, the surface of an electric stovetop burner at high heat produces approximately 10 W/cm². An H100 die generates 8.6× the heat flux of a stove burner, concentrated on an area the size of a large postage stamp.

The B200’s 1,000 W envelope spans a dual-die package. In this comparison, the average heat flux remains comparable while the package heat-removal rate increases by 43 percent. Cooling must keep junction temperature and memory temperature within the applicable product limits under the specified inlet and load conditions. A single temperature threshold cannot stand in for those separate requirements.

The thermal interface material (TIM) between the package and its cooling assembly is one part of that heat-transfer path, and it is the part that ages. Training alternates compute-dense phases with communication phases, so the die heats and cools many times per minute; under that cycling, a phase-change or paste TIM can pump out (migrate away from the contact surface) or crack. Increased thermal resistance can raise temperature at the same load, but the performance effect depends on the remaining thermal margin and the device’s control policy. A temperature trend alone does not identify the cause; inlet conditions, flow, contact, and workload also require inspection. When thermal limits reduce clocks without stopping execution, the result is a gray failure, a performance degradation that monitoring must distinguish from an application or network bottleneck.

Arithmetic per watt offsets only part of rising TDP, and a larger capability target can increase total work despite improved efficiency, so facility planning must compare the proposed device count, power envelope, and completion time together. Packing accelerators close together shortens communication paths but concentrates heat. A four-node DGX H100 rack already draws 33.5 kW including its allocated cooling overhead, several times the load of a conventional server rack. The accelerators of a 10,000-GPU cluster, the round fleet size this chapter uses for warehouse-scale examples, alone draw 7 MW before hosts, network, and cooling are counted. Rack and pod designs must reconcile that proximity with enough space and infrastructure to deliver power and remove heat across all participating nodes.

Power-envelope implications recur throughout the infrastructure hierarchy. At the node level, configured limits constrain how many accelerators can share a chassis ([[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/01 The Fleet/02 Compute Infrastructure/V2-02.06 The Node#^sec-compute-node|section 2.5]]). At the rack level, aggregate sustained load and heat flux help determine whether engineered air or liquid cooling can meet the design point ([[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/01 The Fleet/02 Compute Infrastructure/V2-02.07 The Rack#^sec-compute-cooling|section 2.6.2]]). At the pod level, coincident demand and transient behavior shape megawatt-scale power delivery ([[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/01 The Fleet/02 Compute Infrastructure/V2-02.08 The Pod#^sec-compute-pod|section 2.7]]). Every level above the die must therefore manage both sustained heat and electrical variation. The facility envelope also moves slowly, because upgrading transformers, switchgear, and the cooling plant is a construction project that can take longer than delivering the accelerators. Accelerator selection must compare useful workload throughput within that facility envelope, rather than treating power as a cost added after the compute decision.

#### Power Delivery Networks and Transient Dynamics

^sec-compute-power-delivery-transients

Fitting redundant AC power supply units inside every eight-GPU chassis consumes enclosure volume needed for cooling manifolds and high-speed interconnects, especially as rack loads climb toward 100 kW in liquid-cooled clusters. Many dense-rack designs therefore replace per-chassis AC supplies with rack-level DC power distribution. In this architecture, a centralized power shelf converts mains AC to a 48V DC busbar that runs the height of the rack. Power reaches the server backplane through the 48V bus, and local DC-to-DC converters supply the lower voltages required by the components. Consolidation can recover chassis volume for cooling loops and high-bandwidth interconnects, but it does not by itself establish fewer conversion stages or a fixed efficiency gain. The comparison must account for each design’s actual stages and conversion efficiency across the intended load range. Shared power shelves and busbars also create a rack-level failure boundary whose redundancy and serviceability must be qualified.

A training step makes each accelerator’s current draw jump. When a collective barrier releases a node’s GPUs from a communication phase into dense arithmetic, each one steps from a few hundred watts toward its full power envelope within microseconds, and the barrier correlates those steps across the node. The power delivery network (PDN) must hold the supply voltage steady through that step, and the difficulty grows as the voltage falls from the 48V busbar to the sub-1V rail at the die.

At a fixed power, current scales inversely with voltage, so the core rail carries far more current than the busbar; the voltage regulators transform voltage and current between those boundaries. What disturbs the supply is the rate of change of that current, its slew rate $d{i}/d{t}$. Every conductor loop has inductance, a property that resists changes in current, so a fast current step produces a momentary voltage dip, or droop, even when average power remains within the facility budget.

**Definition 2.7: Power delivery network (PDN) and transient dynamics**

***Power delivery network (PDN)*** is the electrical distribution hierarchy that supplies the processor through regulators, interconnects, and decoupling capacitors. Parasitic loop inductance contributes to voltage fluctuations during changes in current.

1.  **Significance**: Synchronized workload transitions can correlate accelerator demand. Supply excursions outside the device’s specified limits can cause timing errors, protective throttling, or resets.
2.  **Distinction**: Local electrical transients require a faster response than facility power-control loops can provide. Regulators and nearby charge storage address different timescales within the same hierarchy.
3.  **Common pitfall**: A frequent misconception is that the full core-current slew acts on a single inductance summed from rack to die. Regulation and decoupling change the current waveform at each stage; the relevant inductance and current must belong to the same path.

For a particular inductive path, the voltage excursion contributed by its inductance follows [[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/01 The Fleet/02 Compute Infrastructure/V2-02.07 The Rack#^eq-compute-pdn-droop|equation 2]]:

$$
{\Delta} {V}= {L_{\text{parasitic}}}\frac{d{i}}{d{t}} \tag{2}
$$

^eq-compute-pdn-droop

Here ${L_{\text{parasitic}}}$ is the loop inductance of that path, and $d{i}/d{t}$ is the current slew through it. Local capacitors initially supply part of a load-current increase, so the current slew seen upstream need not equal the slew at the core. A complete rail model also accounts for resistance, capacitance, and regulator response.

A worked estimate for one accelerator shows why the hierarchy matters. When an H100 moves from its communication-phase draw of about 400 W to its 700 W envelope on a 0.8 V core rail, its core current rises by about 375 A. Suppose the board loop between the voltage regulator and the package has 50 pH of inductance. If package capacitors supply the fast edge so that this loop sees the step over 1 μs, [[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/01 The Fleet/02 Compute Infrastructure/V2-02.07 The Rack#^eq-compute-pdn-droop|equation 2]] gives a droop of about 19 mV, or 2.3 percent of the rail. If the same step reached that loop in 100 ns, the droop would be about 190 mV, roughly 23 percent of the rail and far outside a 5 percent operating band. The inputs are scenario assumptions, but the tenfold sensitivity to rise time is physical. The same current step stays inside tolerance or corrupts timing depending on which charge store answers it first.

The collective barrier that aligns transitions from communication to arithmetic raises the correlation of demand across a rack, but it does not imply that every accelerator switches on the same nanosecond or that their core currents flow through one shared low-voltage loop. Engineers must evaluate both each device’s local voltage excursion and the aggregate demand seen by upstream supplies. A rising load can produce voltage droop, where droop below the specified operating minimum can invalidate timing margins, while an abrupt load reduction can produce overshoot that must remain within the device’s specified limits as well. Neither the magnitude nor the resulting failure mode follows from workload synchronization alone. The mitigation is a hierarchy of charge storage and control, in which each tier answers the transients too fast for the tier above it ([[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/01 The Fleet/02 Compute Infrastructure/V2-02.07 The Rack#^tbl-pdn-decoupling-tiers|table 13]]), with placement and parasitic impedance determining which frequencies each tier can address.

| **Tier**     | **Charge store or control**                 | **Response timescale**  | **Transients it absorbs**                                     |
|:-------------|:--------------------------------------------|:------------------------|:--------------------------------------------------------------|
| **On-die**   | On-die capacitance                          | Sub-nanosecond          | Switching activity within a kernel                            |
| **Package**  | Package and interposer capacitors           | Nanoseconds             | Kernel launches and streaming-multiprocessor activation edges |
| **Board**    | Bulk capacitors, regulator control loop     | Microseconds            | Communication-to-compute phase changes of one accelerator     |
| **Facility** | Uninterruptible power supply (UPS), utility | Milliseconds to seconds | Synchronized power swings across racks and the cluster        |

Table 13: **Decoupling Tiers in the Power Delivery Network**: Each tier supplies charge on a timescale the slower tier above it cannot reach, so a fast edge that bypasses a tier lands on a larger inductance.

^tbl-pdn-decoupling-tiers

The facility row of [[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/01 The Fleet/02 Compute Infrastructure/V2-02.07 The Rack#^tbl-pdn-decoupling-tiers|table 13]] is where the barrier’s correlation appears in aggregate. For an illustrative calculation, assume 1,024 GPUs change together from 400 W to 700 W each over 100 μs. The assumed aggregate step is 307 kW, giving a ramp rate of 3.07 GW/s. This is a load-side scenario, not a measured utility input. No single regulator carries this step, because each accelerator draws its share through its own board, yet the shared upstream supplies see the sum.

↪ How energy buffering presents this ramp to the grid as a smoother, more easily connected load is developed in **[[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/04 The Responsible Fleet/15 Sustainable AI/V2-15.07 Data Center Energy and Resource Consumption#^sec-sustainable-ai-power-delivery|Power delivery]]**.

Workload-level controls complement these hardware tiers. Power ramping can reduce abrupt demand changes where the hardware and firmware support it, but a design must verify those controls rather than assume that every kernel launch receives automatic smoothing. The fleet-level lesson is that average power capacity and transient voltage integrity require separate checks.

### Cooling Class and Heat Removal

^sec-compute-cooling

The power path ends in heat. Under the thermodynamic limit (principle [[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/01 The Fleet/V2 Fleet Principles#^pri-thermodynamic-limit|4]]), nearly every joule an accelerator draws leaves as heat through switching, leakage, and resistive losses. During a transient some of that energy warms the chip and its surroundings, but in steady operation the stored thermal energy stops rising, so cooling must remove heat at about the rate the rack consumes electricity. When removal falls behind generation, temperature climbs through the available thermal margin until throttling lowers clocks and throughput, and sustained overheating can damage the hardware. The rack’s cooling class, air or liquid, is the choice of fluid that carries this heat away, and it bounds how dense the rack can be.

![[90 Assets/Volume II/02 Compute Infrastructure/Compute Infrastructure Thermal Throttle Knee 1fee011c3c.svg]]

*Schematic of chip temperature over time: a flat line at steady temperature, a vertical arrow marking the margin up to a dashed red thermal limit, then a rise that levels off just under the limit where throttling holds it.*

*When cooling falls behind, temperature climbs through the thermal margin until throttling caps it.*

Heat transfer explains why density reshapes the rack. Air carries far less heat per unit volume than liquid, so an air-cooled rack needs enough airflow, pressure, and temperature rise to carry away its heat. How much a hall can supply depends on the server and facility design rather than on one rack-power threshold.

Air cooling, the dominant technology for decades, works by blowing room-temperature air across heat sinks attached to the chips. The air absorbs heat at a rate determined by its specific heat capacity, roughly 1.0 kJ/kg/K. The heated air is exhausted from the rear of the rack, typically 15–20 degrees Celsius warmer than the inlet, and directed to a computer room air conditioning (CRAC) unit that cools it before recirculating.

Air is a poor thermal conductor. Its thermal conductivity is only 0.026 W/m/K, compared to 0.6 W/m/K for water and 400 W/m/K for copper. Removing a large heat load with air requires substantial volumetric flow. Fan power depends on that flow, pressure drop, and fan efficiency; rack power alone does not specify a fixed cooling fraction.

The heat that a coolant stream removes depends on how much fluid flows, how much heat each kilogram absorbs per degree of warming, and how far the fluid warms ([[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/01 The Fleet/02 Compute Infrastructure/V2-02.07 The Rack#^eq-compute-heat-removal|equation 3]]):

$$
{P_{\text{thermal}}} = {\dot{m}}\times {c_p}\times {\Delta}{T} \tag{3}
$$

^eq-compute-heat-removal

where ${P_{\text{thermal}}}$ is the heat removed (watts), ${\dot{m}}$ is the mass flow rate (kg/s), ${c_p}$ is the specific heat capacity (J/kg/K), and ${\Delta}{T}$ is the temperature difference between outlet and inlet. For air with ${c_p}$ of 1,005 J/kg/K and a typical ${\Delta}{T}$ of 15 K (inlet at 20 degrees C, outlet at 35 degrees C), removing 100 kW requires a mass flow rate of 100,000 $\div$ (1,005 $\times$ 15) $\approx$ 6.6 kg/s. At sea-level air density of 1.2 kg/m³, this corresponds to a volumetric flow rate of 5.5 m³/s, or approximately 11,713 CFM (cubic feet per minute). Moving this much air through the confined space of a server rack requires powerful fans that themselves consume substantial power.

At higher power densities, the airflow and pressure required to cool a rack raise fan energy and can make air cooling impractical before the IT load itself reaches a power limit. The exact crossover depends on rack geometry, inlet temperature, pressure drop, and fan-control policy rather than on one universal rack-power threshold.

Water has a specific heat capacity of 4.18 kJ/kg/K, over four times that of air, and a thermal conductivity roughly 23× higher. Its much greater density also lets a small fluid volume carry substantial heat. The difference appears in how much fluid must flow to remove 700 W from a single GPU. Air at a 15-degree temperature rise requires tens of liters per second of airflow (a small wind tunnel). Water at the same temperature rise requires only about 0.01 liters per second (a thin stream). This thousands-fold difference in volumetric flow permits compact liquid paths, but it does not determine pumping energy. Pressure drop through cold plates, pipes, and fittings, together with pump efficiency, determines the power needed to sustain that flow.

At rack scale the same equation sizes the liquid loop. Removing the modeled 120 kW load of a GB200 NVL72 rack through water that warms by 10 °C takes about 170 liters per minute, and a smaller temperature rise needs proportionally more flow. A real design must use the heat fraction the liquid loop actually captures and account for residual air cooling; flow capacity, pressure, leak containment, and loss-of-flow response are separate requirements, which the facility controls of [[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/01 The Fleet/02 Compute Infrastructure/V2-02.07 The Rack#^sec-compute-infrastructure-physical-security-environmental-controls-7132|section 2.6.4]] treat as failure domains.

Direct-to-chip liquid cooling routes coolant through cold plates mounted on GPU packages. The cold plate contacts the heat spreader through thermal interface material; the resulting thermal resistance depends on the package, interface, plate design, and flow. The coolant carries heat through manifolds and pipes to a coolant distribution unit (CDU) at the rack or row level. The permissible supply temperature depends on the equipment, so liquid cooling need not require chilled water.

In a two-loop design, the CDU transfers heat from the chip-level coolant to the facility water loop, which carries it toward cooling towers or dry coolers. Isolation lets each loop maintain its required fluid chemistry and pressure. The chip-level coolant must satisfy the equipment’s compatibility and cleanliness requirements to control deposits, corrosion, and blockage; purity alone does not establish suitability. Liquid cooling can reduce server airflow requirements, although memory modules (DIMMs), voltage regulator modules (VRMs), and other components without cold plates may still need fans, and lower fan demand can also reduce noise.

A more aggressive approach, immersion cooling, submerges entire server boards in a tank of nonconductive dielectric fluid. The fluid absorbs heat from wetted surfaces, replacing server airflow; high-heat-flux components may still need heat sinks or directed flow, and flow, local heat flux, material compatibility, and heat-exchanger capacity still determine component temperatures. Single-phase immersion cooling uses a fluid that remains liquid throughout the process, with heat carried away by convection currents in the tank. The heated fluid rises to the surface, is pumped through a heat exchanger to reject the heat to the building’s chilled water loop, and returns to the bottom of the tank. Two-phase immersion cooling takes this further. Fluid boils at heated surfaces, carries latent heat to a condenser, and returns as liquid, with heat removal depending on the fluid, pressure, condenser capacity, and operating conditions. The complete facility still spends energy on heat rejection and electrical distribution, so the cooling mechanism alone does not settle facility energy.

The trade-off is serviceability. Accessing a failed component requires handling fluid-wetted hardware, and the dielectric fluids themselves are expensive (\$20–\$50 per liter). A single immersion tank holding four server boards may contain 500–1,000 liters of fluid, representing \$10,000–\$50,000 in coolant cost alone. The operational procedures for immersion-cooled facilities differ sharply from air-cooled ones, requiring specialized training for technicians and different approaches to cable management, since all wetted materials must be compatible with prolonged fluid exposure. Depending on the fluid and its additives, metals can corrode, while polymer insulation and seals can swell or degrade. Qualifying compatible materials adds engineering work and can constrain component choices. These serviceability costs enter the design decision alongside the heat paths, binding limits, and parasitic loads of the air and liquid regimes ([[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/01 The Fleet/02 Compute Infrastructure/V2-02.07 The Rack#^tbl-cooling-limits|table 14]]).

### Rack Design Considerations

^sec-compute-rack-design

Packing 33.5 kW of compute and thermal hardware into a standard 42U frame leaves almost no room for conventional server layouts. In an enterprise 42U rack, servers are installed as independent 1U or 2U pizza-box units, each with its own fans, power supplies, and cable connections. A single DGX H100 instead occupies 8U and contains 8 GPUs, the NVSwitch fabric, multiple power supplies, and (in liquid-cooled configurations) coolant manifolds with quick-connect fittings. Four DGX units fill 32U of a 42U rack, leaving only 10U for networking switches, cable management, and PDUs.

The transition from traditional server form factors to ML-optimized designs is driven by the same power density challenge that motivates engineered airflow or liquid cooling. A traditional 1U server dissipates 300–500 W and requires only modest airflow for cooling. A DGX H100 node dissipates approximately 8.4 kW across its 8 GPUs, NVSwitches, and supporting components, requiring either high-volume forced airflow (in air-cooled configurations) or liquid cooling plumbing (in liquid-cooled configurations). The form factor must accommodate both the compute hardware and the thermal management infrastructure, which is why ML nodes are substantially taller (8U vs. 1U) than traditional servers.

Cable management is a nontrivial engineering challenge. Each DGX H100 node has eight InfiniBand cables (one per GPU), two Ethernet management cables, power cables, and, for liquid-cooled units, coolant hoses. A fully populated rack contains over 40 high-speed cables whose layout is governed by three strict physical constraints: airflow clearance, minimum bend radius, and electromagnetic interference. In air-cooled designs, cables must not obstruct intake or exhaust airflow, because even a localized blockage creates hot spots that trigger thermal throttling. High-speed cables simultaneously enforce a minimum bend radius (for installed fiber optic cable, typically 10–15$\times$ the outer diameter, per the manufacturer’s specification ([[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/01 The Fleet/02 Compute Infrastructure/V2-02.14 Source References#^ref-foa2020bendradius|The Fiber Optic Association 2020]])), below which optical fibers suffer attenuation and induce bit errors or complete link drops. Densely bundling high-speed copper cables can also induce electromagnetic crosstalk that degrades signal quality. In large installations, the cable plant alone can take weeks to install and test, and cable routing errors are one of the most common causes of post-installation debugging delays.

The rack’s network attachment needs the same density-driven rethinking, starting with the top-of-rack (ToR) switch. In traditional data centers, a ToR switch provides 1–10 Gb/s Ethernet connectivity to each server, aggregated into uplinks to higher-level switches. In an ML rack, the ToR switch is an InfiniBand or high-speed Ethernet switch providing 400 Gb/s per port, and it must handle the bursty, synchronized traffic patterns of distributed training. In a conventional design all eight GPUs of a node share one ToR switch, so a data-parallel AllReduce converges on that switch and its uplinks. A rail-optimized design instead gives each GPU index its own leaf switch, the first switch tier that endpoints attach to (GPU 0 of every node on leaf 0, GPU 1 on leaf 1, and so on), so same-index collectives stay on one switch. The price is extra switches and cabling, and the benefit depends on how the job’s process groups map onto these per-index rails.

↪ How rail-optimized topologies align cabling with collective traffic is developed in **[[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/01 The Fleet/03 Network Fabrics/V2-03.06 Level 3 Switch and Topology#^sec-network-fabrics-rail-optimized|Rail-optimized topology]]**.

### Facility Reliability and Environmental Controls

^sec-compute-infrastructure-physical-security-environmental-controls-7132

A coolant leak or electrical fault in a single DGX H100 rack threatens \$1.4 million in hardware and can halt a multi-rack training job across the upper levels of the failure-domain hierarchy introduced in [[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/01 The Fleet/02 Compute Infrastructure/V2-02.06 The Node#^sec-compute-infrastructure-node-health-reliability-56a6|section 2.5.5]]. Data centers housing ML infrastructure therefore pair physical security with continuous environmental monitoring that becomes part of the compute design rather than a facilities afterthought. Beneath raised floor tiles and along liquid cooling piping, water sensors identify leaks within seconds and close automatic coolant isolation valves, while humidity controls keep the air within a moderate band, dry enough to prevent condensation and humid enough to limit static discharge. Inside the cabinets, aspirating smoke detectors sample rack air continuously to catch combustion byproducts at concentrations far below what sprinkler-activating detectors can sense. Vibration sensors on the building structure and rack frames flag seismic events or construction shocks before they can damage disk drives or loosen cable connections.

Cooling failures threaten both hardware and useful fleet work. When coolant flow stops, a dense rack loses heat-removal capacity and must throttle or shut down before thermal runaway. N+1 redundancy in CDUs (one spare beyond the number the load requires), temperature monitoring, and automatic load shedding limit the affected capacity. An emergency air path can support reduced-power operation where the facility design provides one.

Serviceability also changes the failure budget. Leaks can damage equipment and drain cooling capacity, which is why the leak sensors above drive isolation valves, while quick-disconnect fittings allow node replacement without draining the entire loop. Their seals and coolant compatibility therefore belong in the maintenance plan. The fleet-level consequence depends on how much synchronized work shares the failed cooling domain.

The economics of cooling reliability shift when moving from independent inference servers to tightly coupled training clusters. In a distributed training run using synchronous parallelism, a cooling-loop failure that idles one slice of the cluster can halt the entire job. Consider a cooling failure that triggers a thermal shutdown of the 256 GPUs served by one coolant distribution unit within a 10,000-GPU cluster, about ten times the 1,024-GPU reference cluster. The example counts lost compute capacity, excluding repair and replacement costs. If the repair time for a CDU pump is 4 hours, the immediate loss of 256 GPUs at the fleet’s reference in-service rate of \$2 per GPU-hour is \$2,048. Assuming the job cannot continue elastically and the remaining 9,744 GPUs remain unavailable for other work, the synchronous blast radius inflates the cost to \$80,000 in lost compute time. Adding checkpoint retrieval and rollback of 30 to 60 minutes of lost computation brings the illustrative lost-compute total to \$90,000–\$100,000 before any additional recovery delay. The energy the rollback repeats belongs to the facility energy account of **[[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/04 The Responsible Fleet/15 Sustainable AI/V2-15.07 Data Center Energy and Resource Consumption#^sec-sustainable-ai-cooling|Cooling energy, heat rejection, and water]]**. Whether N+1 cooling redundancy is worth its price follows from comparing these expected outage losses and recovery behavior with that price, rather than from treating redundancy as an unconditional requirement.

Coolant condition is another reliability input. **Deionized water** and compatible inhibitors control **galvanic corrosion**, the damage caused by electrochemical differences between metals in the loop. **Biofilm** on heat-transfer surfaces impedes cooling and increases pumping demand. Maintenance must preserve coolant chemistry and heat-transfer performance, because a fouled or corroding loop loses capacity before it fails outright.

Fire containment and power isolation bear directly on reliability and uptime. A fire in a single rack can shut down an entire data center hall for days while the fire suppression system is recharged and the affected area is decontaminated. The 2021 OVHcloud fire in Strasbourg shows how a single-building incident, absent the right compartmentalization, escalates into a site-wide failure domain.

**War Story 2.1: The data center that became the failure domain (2021)**

**Context**: OVHcloud operated four data centers at its Strasbourg site, serving customers whose systems depended on the site’s physical power, fire, and recovery design ([[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/01 The Fleet/02 Compute Infrastructure/V2-02.14 Source References#^ref-ovhcloud2021strasbourg|OVHcloud 2021]]).

**Mechanism**: On March 10, 2021, a fire broke out in building SBG2. Uncontained fire spread and shared power pathways destroyed building SBG2, damaged adjacent SBG1, and forced emergency shutdown of SBG3 and SBG4.

**Impact**: Thousands of servers were physically destroyed, taking over 3.6 million websites offline worldwide and destroying unbacked-up customer data.

**Fix**: OVHcloud rebuilt the site with independent physical fire barriers, isolated power distribution per building, and mandatory free off-site backup storage.

**Systems lesson**: Compute infrastructure extends beyond GPUs and network topology. Fire compartments, power isolation, backup geography, and disaster-recovery assumptions determine whether a rack-scale incident becomes a site-scale outage. Multi-node ML training clusters inherit this physical dependency. A single data-center power or cooling domain failure halts active training runs and destroys un-replicated checkpoint states unless recovery paths cross physical facility boundaries.

Once rack-level controls are in place, the reliability question moves up to facility redundancy, where the same failure-domain logic that isolates a leaking rack also decides how much duplicate power, cooling, and recovery capacity the site should buy. Data center reliability is commonly classified using the Uptime Institute Tier system, with Tier III and Tier IV representing common options for mission-critical compute ([[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/01 The Fleet/02 Compute Infrastructure/V2-02.14 Source References#^ref-uptimeInstituteTiers|Uptime Institute 2026]]). A Tier III facility is concurrently maintainable: redundant capacity components and distribution paths let operators maintain or replace equipment without shutting down IT operations. Tier IV adds fault tolerance through independent, physically isolated systems, so a single equipment failure or distribution-path interruption should not affect IT operations. The practical difference is not a magic availability number; it is how much failure prevention the building buys in electrical and mechanical infrastructure before software-level resilience takes over.

For ML training, the calculus differs from traditional enterprise computing. A training run is a long-running batch process, not a real-time transaction system. A facility outage during a two-week run therefore usually costs a restart from the last checkpoint rather than permanent data loss, provided a valid checkpoint survives in an accessible failure domain and usable compute becomes available. The restart still incurs lost work, recovery time, and potentially a missed deadline. Many training deployments therefore pair concurrently maintainable (Tier III) facilities with aggressive application-level fault tolerance. Rather than buying every layer of building-level redundancy, they invest in checkpointing, job restart, and storage that can absorb rare facility interruptions, and redirect the avoided resiliency spend toward accelerators, networking, or storage. Facility redundancy and application-level recovery address different parts of the same risk. A design can trade some preventive infrastructure for checkpointing and recovery capacity only after checking which failures the recovery path survives and whether the resulting completion-time risk is acceptable.

The rack concentrates power and heat into a physical volume where thermodynamics, not software, sets the limits.
