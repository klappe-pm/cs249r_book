# V2-04.13 Summary

[[Home|Home]] · [[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/Volume 2 Overview|Volume Overview]] · [[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/01 The Fleet/04 Data Storage/V2-04 Data Storage|V2-04 Data Storage]]

^sec-storage-summary

Storage in ML systems is an active, multi-tiered pipeline that supplies accelerator inputs, preserves restorable state, and records the lineage of training and serving artifacts. The hierarchy exists because workloads need different combinations of bandwidth, capacity, latency, durability, and cost, and the demand on it grows with the scaling laws that drive the fleet. Larger models call for more training tokens and carry larger checkpoints, so storage pressure rises at every step up the model-size curve. The data pipeline throughput equation, ${\text{BW}_{\text{required}}} = {N_{\text{GPU}}} \times {\eta_{\text{target}}}\times {D_{\text{vol}}^{(\text{batch})}} / {T_{\text{compute}}}$, sizes delivery for a chosen training rate when applied to the representation and distinct input consumers at each tier. Measured against the Fleet Law, storage succeeds when it contributes no exposed time. Prefetching preserves the Fleet Law’s assumption that the next batch is already resident. Tiered checkpoint staging shrinks the save pause that surfaces in ${T_{\text{sync}}}({N})$ to the local write and takes the slow shared-storage copy off the step entirely, so that copy costs step time only when it contends with collectives or input reads. Both spend communication (${C}_2$) and coordination (${C}_3$) capacity to protect compute (${C}_1$).

Treating the hierarchy as a cache makes that sizing workload-specific. The hit rate and the service times of the hot and cold paths determine mean effective throughput, while the tail and correlation of misses determine whether a finite prefetch buffer drains. At the durable tiers, reliability also separates into three contracts: data survival, service response, and the version a reader may observe. These are durability, availability, and consistency, respectively; improving one does not automatically establish the others.

The diagnostic task is to identify which service contract fails before changing the storage tier. Low accelerator utilization can arise from metadata-intensive loading, a depleted prefetch queue, an exposed checkpoint write, or host-memory traffic crossing a contended NUMA link. Measure input wait separately from communication and synchronization before attributing it to storage. Full-corpus scans favor aggregated shards, while graph retrieval and live features require random access and explicit consistency. A depleted input queue can reflect slow storage, metadata contention, or insufficient decoding capacity. For checkpoint writes, evaluate the shorter training pause together with the time until a complete generation becomes durable and publishable.

**Key Takeaways: Feed the accelerators or waste them**

- **Workload demand sizes the delivery path**: Use ${\text{BW}_{\text{required}}} = {N_{\text{GPU}}} \times {\eta_{\text{target}}}\times {D_{\text{vol}}^{(\text{batch})}} / {T_{\text{compute}}}$ for the chosen training rate, then check every stage’s service rate and buffering, because delivery is the minimum over stages and faster compute against a fixed input path plateaus at that rate (principle [[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/01 The Fleet/V2 Fleet Principles#^pri-io-wall|7]]). GDS removes the host payload copy, not CPU control of the I/O, and helps when that copy, or the CPU time it takes from other stages, limits delivery.
- **Workload determines the storage contract**: Full-corpus scans favor sequential throughput and aggregated shards, and metadata operations can limit delivery before byte bandwidth does. Disk-backed graph retrieval must fit its random I/O within a serving deadline, and synthetic-data pipelines must budget verification traffic separately from retained capacity while keeping the versioned lineage of every accepted sample.
- **A tier ratio is not a feed requirement**: Tiers quote bandwidth per accelerator, per node, or as a shared aggregate, so divide shared bandwidth among active clients and size delivery from input bytes rather than from the gap between HBM and storage (principle [[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/01 The Fleet/V2 Fleet Principles#^pri-storage-hierarchy|8]]).
- **Tail latency sets the cache budget**: A high hit rate is not enough when misses are orders of magnitude slower. Served serially, a 95 percent hit rate with misses 100× slower yields only about one-sixth of the all-hot rate. Use [[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/01 The Fleet/04 Data Storage/V2-04.04 The ML Storage Hierarchy#^eq-storage-cache-service-time|equation 1]] as a mean baseline, then size prefetch buffers from p99 I/O latency and test correlated miss bursts against the compute window.
- **Checkpoint recovery is a storage protocol**: Local NVMe staging can shorten pause time, but a restorable checkpoint also needs durable publication, a committed manifest, and verified shards; durability, availability, and consistency remain distinct contracts.
- **Pipeline correctness lives at commit boundaries**: Replay preserves processing completeness only when stable identifiers and idempotent or transactional writes prevent duplicate effects. Event-time watermarks then make the separate freshness-completeness trade-off explicit.
- **Total delivery cost, not price per byte, decides tiering**: Capacity charges scale with bytes stored while transfer charges scale with bytes read, so a dataset read many times across a billing boundary can cost more to read than to store (principle [[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/01 The Fleet/V2 Fleet Principles#^pri-storage-hierarchy|8]]). Compare total delivery cost, including transfer charges and idle accelerators, before deciding where to stage data.

^callout-takeaways--4-12-64d3bcc9

Checkpoint staging shows the C$^3$ trade-off in one design decision. Writing first to local NVMe shortens the compute pause, but asynchronous replication adds communication traffic and requires coordination to publish only complete generations. Once the shared-file-system write leaves the critical path, the bottleneck can move to local flash endurance, peer-replication bandwidth, or manifest commit latency. Measuring only the shorter training pause would miss the new recovery boundary.

**What’s Next: From storage to communication**

Compute silicon, network fabrics, and storage hierarchies establish the physical resources of the machine learning fleet. Coordinating those resources into one training computation remains a separate problem. This chapter’s 175B running model already exceeds, in its FP16 weights alone, the 80 GB device of the HBM placement example, so its state and work must span devices, and every division of that work obliges workers to exchange gradients, activations, or parameters across the fabric. Part II moves from physical infrastructure to distributed execution. It begins with the collective operations that carry those exchanges and the cost of each, then partitions neural networks across data, tensor, pipeline, and sequence dimensions, before turning to the hardware failures that a fleet must survive.
