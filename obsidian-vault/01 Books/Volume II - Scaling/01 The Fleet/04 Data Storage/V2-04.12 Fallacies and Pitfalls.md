# V2-04.12 Fallacies and Pitfalls

[[Home|Home]] · [[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/Volume 2 Overview|Volume Overview]] · [[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/01 The Fleet/04 Data Storage/V2-04 Data Storage|V2-04 Data Storage]]

^sec-storage-fallacies

Storage design errors are among the most expensive mistakes in ML infrastructure because they are discovered late, when training begins, and remediation requires either expensive hardware upgrades or time-consuming data reformatting. The following fallacies and pitfalls capture the most common errors that experienced storage engineers make when first encountering ML workloads.

**Fallacy**: *NVMe is fast enough to feed GPUs directly without pipelining.*

A device can have enough sustained bandwidth and still expose stalls when transfers are serialized with computation. Without pipelining and prefetching to hide the latency of loading from NVMe to host DRAM to HBM, every batch transfer introduces a stall equal to the transfer time. Comparing a four-drive RAID-0 stripe with HBM, a gap of about 120×, does not show whether NVMe can feed the job, because model state is reused many times for each batch loaded from storage. The relevant test compares measured input delivery with batch bytes divided by compute time, then checks whether buffering hides transfer latency and jitter.

**Pitfall**: *Relying on prefetching to hide object storage latency.*

Prefetching hides latency only within the time covered by ready batches; it cannot hide an arbitrarily long interruption. With ${N_{\text{prefetch}}}$ batches ready and constant compute time, a complete interruption of supply exhausts the queue after ${N_{\text{prefetch}}} \times {T_{\text{compute}}}$. A slow request need not stall computation if other completed work remains available, but correlated delays can deplete many workers’ queues together. Provision buffer depth from measured delivery tails, test correlated interruptions, and verify that the sustained refill rate meets consumption. Local staging can reduce remote exposure when the required shards fit and remain valid.

**Fallacy**: *Millions of small training files are harmless at scale.*

Storing each training sample as an individual file (one JPEG per image, one JSON per text sample) creates repeated per-file work through operations such as `open()`, `stat()`, and `close()`. Metadata becomes the bottleneck when the resulting request rate exceeds the service’s capacity, even if byte bandwidth remains available. Aggregating samples into sequential shards amortizes that work ([[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/01 The Fleet/04 Data Storage/V2-04.04 The ML Storage Hierarchy#^sec-storage-data-formats|section 4.3.5]]). Packaging 10,000 to 100,000 samples per shard can reduce file-level operations by a corresponding 10,000 to 100,000$\times$ when readers consume whole shards. The loader must still implement the intended sampling policy and avoid repeated enumeration.

**Pitfall**: *Ignoring egress costs when designing the storage architecture.*

Cloud egress costs are invisible during development but can dominate at scale. Under a representative \$0.09/GB cross-boundary egress fee, a training job that reads a 100 TB dataset from object storage 10 times incurs \$90,000 in egress fees, far exceeding the annual storage cost of \$24,000 under the object-storage price assumption in [[01 Books/Volume II - Scaling/01 The Fleet/04 Data Storage/V2-04.04 The ML Storage Hierarchy#^sec-storage-tier4-object|section 4.3.7]]. These fees apply when reads cross a region, provider, or internet boundary; same-region reads may be free or discounted, which is exactly why the cost stays hidden until a cross-region or multi-cloud topology makes it unavoidable. Teams that prototype with small datasets on object storage and then scale up discover that their storage architecture is economically unsustainable. The fix is to budget egress costs explicitly and to design the data pipeline to minimize cross-tier transfers, typically by staging data on local NVMe at job start rather than streaming from object storage each epoch.

**Fallacy**: *Average write bandwidth is enough for checkpoint design.*

Checkpoints are written by all nodes simultaneously, creating bursts that can exceed steady-state bandwidth by 10$\times$ or more. When the PFS write lies on the training critical path, sizing it only for average write load extends ${T_{\text{write}}}$ during bursts and reduces effective training throughput. A synchronous PFS-direct design must provision enough burst bandwidth to meet its pause budget. With local staging, the first tier absorbs the burst and the PFS drains it in the background; the shared tier must then meet the durability deadline and keep up with repeated saves without an unbounded backlog.

**Pitfall**: *Assuming faster accelerators automatically improve training throughput.*

When an organization upgrades from A100 to H100 GPUs, they expect training time to decrease proportionally to the compute improvement. If the storage pipeline was already marginal on A100s, delivering data just fast enough to avoid stalls, the faster H100 compute hits the I/O wall sooner. The accelerator upgrade reduces the compute time ${T_{\text{compute}}}$ without reducing the input-pipeline time ${T_{\text{IO}}}$, which means the data stall ratio increases. Every accelerator upgrade must be accompanied by a storage pipeline audit to ensure that the faster compute does not expose a previously hidden storage bottleneck.

**Fallacy**: *RAID configurations optimized for databases are suitable for ML training.*

RAID selection follows the tier’s failure contract. A disposable local cache can use RAID 0 to aggregate drives without parity, provided a durable source remains available and the fleet can tolerate restaging after a drive failure. Redundant layouts can still reduce disruption when restaging is slow or the cache supports an availability objective. A local checkpoint awaiting durable replication is not yet disposable merely because it lives on the same NVMe drives. Treating all ML storage as replaceable input data confuses capacity placement with recovery policy.

**Pitfall**: *Creating full copies for each dataset snapshot.*

Copying an entire dataset for every version duplicates unchanged content. For a 10 TB dataset with 5 percent of its bytes changed, a full copy retains 9.5 TB of redundant data. Content-addressable storage or delta encoding can reuse unchanged objects or blocks. If the storage granularity isolates the changed bytes, the new payload is roughly 500 GB, a 20× reduction before metadata and redundancy overhead. Rewriting large compressed shards can reduce that saving because a small sample change may replace a much larger stored object. Versioning must therefore preserve lineage while choosing a reuse granularity compatible with the dataset format.

**Fallacy**: *Compression always helps because it reduces the amount of data to read.*

Compression reduces stored and transferred bytes but adds decoding work. If decoding further saturates an already limiting host processor, it makes that bottleneck *worse*, not *better*. If transfer is limiting and decoding has headroom, compression can improve delivery until another stage becomes the limit. Compare candidate representations using output-equivalent stage rates, including CPU or GPU decoding, rather than select the strongest codec from an I/O-bound label alone. The same choice can reduce capacity cost without improving training throughput.

These fallacies and pitfalls all return to the same accounting exercise: where each byte lives, how often it moves, and which tier pays the cost.

**Self-Check: Question**

1.  A team selects RAID 5 for a disposable local training-data cache whose immutable source is durably stored elsewhere. The fleet can tolerate restaging after drive failure. Why might RAID 0 be a better throughput-and-capacity trade-off under this failure contract?

    1.  RAID 5 cannot be formatted with modern Linux filesystems such as ext4 or XFS.
    2.  RAID 5 disables hardware DMA transfers, preventing the GPU from accessing host DRAM.
    3.  The cache can be reconstructed from its durable source, so RAID 0 can aggregate drives without parity overhead when restaging disruption is acceptable.
    4.  RAID 5 increases drive endurance wear by $10\times$ compared to single-drive configurations.

2.  A training team upgrades its cluster from A100 to H100 GPUs, expecting a $3\times$ end-to-end training speedup. However, wall-clock epoch time barely improves, and GPU utilization drops from $85\%$ to $35\%$. Traces show shorter uninterrupted compute time, unchanged measured input-delivery rate, and increased time waiting for ready input batches, while communication and checkpoint pauses remain unchanged. Diagnose the measured bottleneck and explain why faster compute exposed it.

3.  A team replaces a lighter codec with a denser codec on local-NVMe training shards. On this corpus, illustrative measurements give the lighter codec a 3:1 ratio and 1.5 GB/s decoded output per core, versus 4:1 and 0.5 GB/s for the denser codec. Before the change, worker profiles show decoding and augmentation saturating the host CPU and the ready-batch queue repeatedly draining. Afterward, decompression consumes more CPU time, the queue drains more often, and training step time increases although stored bytes decrease. What caused this measured regression?

    1.  Compressed shards cannot be read by NVMe controllers because flash drives require raw uncompressed binary blocks.
    2.  The pipeline was already CPU-bound on data decoding and augmentation, so adding heavy decompression work saturated the host CPU, starving the GPU of assembled batches.
    3.  The denser codec introduces non-deterministic bit flips that cause stochastic gradient descent to diverge.
    4.  The parallel file system rejects compressed shards larger than 1 GB due to POSIX file locking constraints.

4.  True or False: Sizing a data loader’s prefetch buffer depth based on average storage read latency ensures that training runs will not experience data stalls during multi-week training jobs on shared object storage.

5.  A team creates full $10\text{ TB}$ copies of a pretraining dataset for every snapshot version, creating 20 versions over six months ($200\text{ TB}$ total storage). Explain how content-addressable storage or delta encoding optimizes this workflow when successive versions differ by only $5\%$.
