# V1-04.04 Requirements and Data Budget

[[Home|Home]] · [[01 Books/Volume I - Foundations/Volume 1 Overview|Volume Overview]] · [[01 Books/Volume I - Foundations/01 Foundations/04 Data Engineering/V1-04 Data Engineering|V1-04 Data Engineering]]

^sec-data-engineering-framework-application-keyword-spotting-case-study-c8e9

A wake-word detector answers to two numbers at once. It must catch nearly every spoken wake word, and it must almost never wake for anything else, while listening continuously in tens of kilobytes of memory on a microcontroller or the low-power core of a phone or smart speaker. The chapter’s illustrative smart speaker sets those numbers, and every later stage of the pipeline is measured against them.

In the wake-word interaction sequenced in [[01 Books/Volume I - Foundations/01 Foundations/04 Data Engineering/V1-04.04 Requirements and Data Budget#^fig-keywords|figure 4]], an always-on detector traps “Alexa…” before the smart speaker acknowledges the utterance and opens the command channel for “dim the lights.” Behind that brief exchange sits the data pipeline this chapter builds: the recordings the detector learned from, how they were labeled and checked, and how the result was verified before it shipped.

![[90 Assets/Volume I/04 Data Engineering/Data Engineering Kws 165dfd7446.png]]

Figure 4: **Keyword Spotting in Action**: An Amazon Echo Dot exchange shows the user saying “Alexa,” the device acknowledging the wake word, and the user issuing “dim the lights.” A lightweight, always-on wake-word detector listens continuously and triggers the main voice assistant only after the keyword is spoken.

^fig-keywords

Turning that brief exchange into an engineering specification requires targets that reflect how the device actually listens. Overall accuracy is the wrong target, because almost every second of audio contains no wake word, and a detector that never fires would score nearly perfectly. The scenario therefore sets a **keyword detection rate** of 98 percent, the fraction of spoken wake words the detector catches, measured at a detection threshold that keeps false wakes within the budget the next notebook derives. It also requires a response within 200 ms and a model small and frugal enough for the device.

**Napkin Math 4.1: False positive targets**

**Problem**: An always-on KWS system budgets at most one expected false wake-up per month. Under a model that counts one nonkeyword classification window each second around the clock, how strict does the per-window false positive rate need to be?

**Variables**:

- **Duty cycle**: Always-on (24 hours/day).
- **Window size**: One-second classification windows.
- **Windows per month**: One window per second, 24 hours/day, over 30 days gives 2,592,000 windows/month.

**Math**:

- **False positive rate (FPR)**: 1 expected false wake divided by the monthly nonkeyword-window count gives approximately $3.9 \times 10^{-7}$
- **Nonkeyword rejection requirement**: 99.99996 percent rejection of nonkeyword windows.

**Systems insight**: This calculation bounds the expected monthly count, not the maximum number of false wakes in every month. The expected count holds even though neighboring windows are correlated, because expectations add across windows whether or not the windows are independent. Aggregate accuracy (for example, “99 percent accuracy”) is insufficient here. Evaluation must report false accepts per hour (FA/Hr) together with the corresponding false-rejection behavior. Real detectors score overlapping windows every few tens of milliseconds and smooth the scores, so the per-window target depends on the detector, while the per-hour rate is the quantity to measure. A budget of one false wake per month is deliberately strict; commercial assistants typically pass each candidate wake to a second, larger check rather than asking the always-on detector to meet such a budget alone ([[01 Books/Volume I - Foundations/01 Foundations/04 Data Engineering/V1-04.17 Source References#^ref-apple2017heysiri|Siri Team 2017]]).

![[90 Assets/Volume I/04 Data Engineering/Vol1 Data Engineering Margin 001 7db4aa28c9.svg]]

*Two red horizontal bars on a log scale show expected monthly false wakes: 2,592 at 99.9 percent per-window rejection and 26 at 99.999 percent; a red dashed limit line marks 1 wake per month, and both bars extend past it.*

*Even 99.999 percent per-window rejection multiplies into 26 false wakes a month.*

Evaluation therefore reports the two rates together, as a detection/error trade-off curve that plots false accepts per hour against false rejection rate on streaming audio representative of deployment ([[01 Books/Volume I - Foundations/01 Foundations/04 Data Engineering/V1-04.17 Source References#^ref-nayak2022|Nayak et al. 2022]]). The detection target is read off that curve at the false-wake budget, and operational metrics complete the contract by bounding response time from the end of the keyword to the system’s reaction alongside the average power drawn while listening.

The device sets hard boundaries on any design that meets those targets. Memory limitations require lightweight models, often in the tens-of-kilobytes range ([[01 Books/Volume I - Foundations/01 Foundations/04 Data Engineering/V1-04.17 Source References#^ref-zhang2017hello|Zhang et al. 2017]]), to fit in the always-on island of the system-on-chip (SoC).[[01 Books/Volume I - Foundations/01 Foundations/04 Data Engineering/V1-04.18 Footnotes#^fn1|¹]] This budget must accommodate both model weights and the feature extraction buffers and preprocessing state. Limited computational capabilities (often a few hundred MHz of clock speed) demand aggressive model optimization. On phones and wearables the detector runs from a battery, and even a plugged-in smart speaker must keep its idle power low. KWS systems therefore target sub-milliwatt consumption during continuous listening.

Data quality and diversity determine whether these targets can be met inside those limits. The dataset must cover speakers of different accents, ages, and genders, the many ways people pronounce the same wake word, and background conditions from quiet bedrooms to noisy kitchens. The model cannot grow to compensate for missing coverage, so on this device the data carries the burden that a larger model would carry in a data center.

### KWS Design Space

^sec-data-engineering-kws-design-space-eadd

Doubling the sampling rate of a wake-word recording from 8 kHz to 16 kHz captures sharper consonant sounds and raises the detection rate, but it also doubles the raw-audio window and the input-processing work on the device. Every data engineering choice for the keyword spotter pulls detection rate, false-wake tolerance, latency, energy, and memory against one another, and [[01 Books/Volume I - Foundations/01 Foundations/04 Data Engineering/V1-04.04 Requirements and Data Budget#^tbl-kws-design-space|table 1]] prices those trade-offs. Each row is a D·A·M coupling of the kind drawn in **[[01 Books/Volume I - Foundations/00 Introduction/01 Introduction/V1-01.06 Defining ML Systems#^fig-dam-axis-triad-intro|The D·A·M Axis Triad]]**, because a data choice such as sampling rate or coefficient count changes what the algorithm receives and what the machine must hold in always-on memory.

One row of the table uses mel-frequency cepstral coefficients (MFCCs)[[01 Books/Volume I - Foundations/01 Foundations/04 Data Engineering/V1-04.18 Footnotes#^fn2|²]] ([[01 Books/Volume I - Foundations/01 Foundations/04 Data Engineering/V1-04.17 Source References#^ref-davis1980comparison|Davis and Mermelstein 1980]]), which reduce each frame of roughly 25 ms of audio to 13 to 40 numbers that emphasize the frequencies speech uses. The coefficient count sets the size of the model’s input and how much acoustic detail it keeps; [[01 Books/Volume I - Foundations/01 Foundations/04 Data Engineering/V1-04.08 Data Processing#^sec-data-engineering-systematic-data-processing-aebc|section 4.7]] shows how they are extracted.

| **Design Choice**                                        | **Quality Impact**           |      **Latency Impact**      |               **Cost Impact**                |        **Memory Impact**         |
|:---------------------------------------------------------|:-----------------------------|:----------------------------:|:--------------------------------------------:|:--------------------------------:|
| **8 $\rightarrow$ 16 kHz sampling**                      | +2–4% detection rate         | 2× input-processing workload |             2× raw-audio storage             |       2× raw-audio window        |
| **13 $\rightarrow$ 40 MFCC coefficients**                | +3–5% detection rate         |     3× model-input size      |                   Minimal                    |        3× feature memory         |
| **1M $\rightarrow$ 10M training examples**               | +5–8% detection rate         |      10× training time       |              10× labeling cost               |           10× storage            |
| **Clean $\rightarrow$ noisy training data**              | +10–15% real-world           |           Minimal            |              3× collection cost              |             Minimal              |
| **Cloud $\rightarrow$ local inference**                  | up to 2% detection-rate risk |  100 ms $\rightarrow$ 10 ms  |    \$0.001/query $\rightarrow$ \$0/query     | cloud-scale $\rightarrow$ 256 KB |
| **Real only $\rightarrow$ added synthetic augmentation** | +3–5% robustness             |           Minimal            | 10× cheaper per example than real collection |             Minimal              |

Table 1: **KWS Data Engineering Design Space**: Illustrative scenario inputs price each design choice along four axes (quality, latency, cost, and memory); the entries are not measurements from one system and should not be combined as additive effects. Each row moves from a baseline (left of the arrow) to an alternative, and every entry gives the effect of that move. Higher sampling rates double raw-audio storage and processing, while more training examples multiply labeling and storage requirements. Actual quality and latency must be measured on the target pipeline.

^tbl-kws-design-space

Because the device runs inside a milliwatt power envelope and a tens-of-kilobytes SRAM budget, an engineering team cannot pick the most accurate option in every row of [[01 Books/Volume I - Foundations/01 Foundations/04 Data Engineering/V1-04.04 Requirements and Data Budget#^tbl-kws-design-space|table 1]] at once. Instead, the team must divide a fixed data budget across labeling, storage, and governance so the detector reaches its accuracy target without outgrowing the hardware. Notebook [4.2](https://mlsysbook.ai/vol1/04_data_engineering/04_data_engineering.html#nbk-data-engineering-optimizing-kws-design-space) allocates the data budget and proposes a candidate design for a smart-speaker deployment: how many labels the budget buys, which feature representation the device can afford, and which data options are meant to close the remaining gap. The acquisition, labeling, processing, and delivery decisions that follow supply the evidence needed to accept or reject it.

**Napkin Math 4.2: Allocating the KWS data budget**

**Scenario**: A KWS system for a smart speaker has these constraints:

- **Target**: 98 percent keyword detection rate at a threshold that keeps the expected false-wake budget below 1 false wake/month
- **Budget**: \$150K total data engineering budget
- **Memory**: 256 KB model size limit (always-on island), with a separate 16 KB budget for preprocessing state
- **Timeline**: 6 months to production

**Step 1**: Choose conservative options for the constrained device.

For this scenario, the team chooses two conservative options:

- 13 MFCC coefficients, because every MFCC frame lives in the always-on island’s SRAM as model input and buffered state, so the 40-coefficient option triples a cost the device pays continuously
- Local inference, because streaming every second of audio to a server is ruled out by bandwidth, privacy, and offline operation; the detection-rate risk in the table is the price of a model small enough for the always-on island

The choice rests on where each cost lands. Data collection is paid once, from a central budget, whereas the 40-coefficient representation triples the model’s input, and with it the first layer’s work and the feature buffer, on every device for every second it listens. The table suggests a 3–5 percent detection gain from 40 coefficients. The team gives up that gain on the device and expects broader training coverage to recover it. The acceptance test of [[01 Books/Volume I - Foundations/01 Foundations/04 Data Engineering/V1-04.12 Accepting the Pipeline#^sec-data-engineering-accepting-the-kws-pipeline|section 4.11]] decides the choice.

**Step 2**: Calculate budget allocation.

Labeling is priced at a scenario rate per crowdsourced example, set above the image-classification crowd rates that [[01 Books/Volume I - Foundations/01 Foundations/04 Data Engineering/V1-04.07 Data Labeling#^sec-data-engineering-labeling-economics-3e91|section 4.6.2]] later tabulates because a KWS contributor must record the utterance as well as label it. The \$150K budget splits across three cost categories:

- **Labeling** (~60 percent): \$90K available
- **Storage/Processing** (~25 percent): \$37.5K
- **Governance/Other** (~15 percent): \$22.5K

At \$0.10/label with 20 percent review overhead: \$90K ÷ \$0.12/label = 750K labeled examples

This yields roughly 0.75M labeled examples, just below the 1M anchor in the design space. The 1M-to-10M row in [[01 Books/Volume I - Foundations/01 Foundations/04 Data Engineering/V1-04.04 Requirements and Data Budget#^tbl-kws-design-space|table 1]] therefore starts above this budget; it shows how detection rate scales with data volume, not a gain this plan buys. Counting examples also hides what limits a wake-word model in practice, which is the number of distinct speakers and the hours of ordinary, nonkeyword audio available to measure false wakes. The false-wake target alone calls for about three months of representative listening with no false wake, by the rule of three in [[01 Books/Volume I - Foundations/01 Foundations/04 Data Engineering/V1-04.12 Accepting the Pipeline#^sec-data-engineering-accepting-the-kws-pipeline|section 4.11]], and this allocation has not yet counted that evaluation cost.

**Step 3**: Identify candidate quality improvements.

The quality plan has three components:

- The 750K real examples the budget buys
- An illustrative starting detection rate of ~90 percent, which the team assumes those examples reach, because the design table does not price a gain below its 1M row
- A gap to the 98 percent target that the options below must close

Three options from the design space are meant to close the gap. Their listed gains overlap (noisy data and synthetic augmentation both buy robustness to acoustic conditions), so they cannot simply be added to the starting rate, and each option’s collection, storage, processing, and deployed-footprint costs must fit the allocations above:

- 16 kHz sampling: a detection-rate gain of 2–4 percent, 2× raw-audio storage (on the device only the short window of raw samples feeding each MFCC computation doubles, because every frame still reduces to 13 coefficients)
- Noisy training data: a field detection-rate gain of 10–15 percent, 3× collection cost, for a targeted noisy slice
- Synthetic augmentation: a robustness gain of 3–5 percent, 10× cheaper than real data in the illustrative row

**Step 4**: Final configuration.

The scenario combines these choices with 13 MFCCs and the computed 750K real-label budget in [[01 Books/Volume I - Foundations/01 Foundations/04 Data Engineering/V1-04.04 Requirements and Data Budget#^tbl-data-engineering-kws-configuration|table 2]]. Because the quality effects overlap, the table records a configuration to test, not a solved optimum.

**Result**: The budget buys approximately 750K human-labeled examples, 13 MFCCs, local inference, and a noisy slice plus synthetic augmentation to close the quality gap. Whether that configuration reaches the 98 percent detection target inside the 256 KB model limit is what acceptance decides.

**Systems insight**: Design-space analysis turns “we need more data” into a costed plan. The stated labeling assumptions buy about 750K real examples, and the acceptance test, not the design table, decides whether that plan succeeds.

[[01 Books/Volume I - Foundations/01 Foundations/04 Data Engineering/V1-04.04 Requirements and Data Budget#^tbl-data-engineering-kws-configuration|Table 2]] records the resulting configuration, pairing each design choice with the constraint that motivates it: sampling and augmentation draw on the stated allocations, while the always-on island sets the deployed-footprint constraint. Actual costs and working-state memory still require validation.

The candidate configuration fixes the targets but not the data. Meeting them starts with acquisition: finding recordings that cover the speakers, accents, devices, and rooms the detector will meet, from sources whose cost and consent terms the budget can bear.

**Self-Check: Question**

1.  An always-on Keyword Spotting (KWS) system on an embedded voice assistant continuously evaluates 1-second audio classification windows ($24\text{ hours/day}$ over a $30\text{-day}$ month). Treat each window as nonkeyword audio. The product budgets at most 1 expected false activation per month. An engineer reports 99% aggregate accuracy and, separately, a measured 1% false positive rate on background noise. How many false activations does that FPR imply in expectation, and what per-window FPR meets the expected-count budget?

    1.  A 1% FPR implies an expected count of 720 false activations per month; the expected-count budget requires a per-window FPR of $\le 1.38 \times 10^{-5}$.
    2.  A 1% FPR implies an expected count of ~25,920 false activations per month (~36 false wakes/hour); the expected-count budget requires a per-window FPR of $\le 3.86 \times 10^{-7}$ (\>99.9999% non-keyword rejection).
    3.  A 1% FPR implies an expected count of ~2,592 false activations per month; the expected-count budget requires a per-window FPR of $\le 1.0 \times 10^{-4}$.
    4.  A 1% FPR satisfies the expected-count budget because accuracy is averaged over the total number of audio hours across the entire device fleet.

2.  The chapter specifies its keyword spotter by a keyword detection rate measured at a threshold that holds the expected false-wake budget, rather than by overall accuracy. Why is overall accuracy the wrong target for an always-on detector?

    1.  Almost every one-second window contains no wake word, so a detector that never fires scores near-perfect overall accuracy while missing every command; the meaningful targets are the fraction of spoken wake words caught and the false-wake rate at the chosen threshold.
    2.  Overall accuracy cannot be computed for a model that runs on a microcontroller, so the target must use a metric the device can calculate itself.
    3.  Overall accuracy rises automatically with more training data, so it cannot distinguish one data plan from another.
    4.  Detection rate and overall accuracy are the same quantity for a binary classifier; the scenario only renames it.
