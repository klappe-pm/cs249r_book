# V1-03.11 Stage 6 Monitoring and Maintenance

[[Home|Home]] · [[01 Books/Volume I - Foundations/Volume 1 Overview|Volume Overview]] · [[01 Books/Volume I - Foundations/01 Foundations/03 ML Lifecycle/V1-03 ML Lifecycle|V1-03 ML Lifecycle]]

^sec-ml-workflow-monitoring-maintenance-stage-e79a

Consider an illustrative case six months after a DR screening system launches. A clinic upgrades its fundus cameras, and the new equipment produces images with different color profiles. No code changed, yet the pixel distributions the model learned during training no longer match the images it receives, and in this scenario the model’s sensitivity at that site drops. The degradation equation from **[[01 Books/Volume I - Foundations/00 Introduction/01 Introduction/V1-01.07 When ML Systems Fail Silently#^sec-introduction-ml-vs-traditional-software-e19a|When ML Systems Fail Silently]]** (**[[01 Books/Volume I - Foundations/00 Introduction/01 Introduction/V1-01.07 When ML Systems Fail Silently#^eq-degradation|equation 1.1]]**) models accuracy after deployment as the starting accuracy minus a fitted coefficient ${\lambda}$ times the divergence between production and training data; the coefficient measures how strongly accuracy responds to divergence and is not the clinical sensitivity metric. The new color profile raises that divergence, ${\mathcal{D}(P_t \| P_0)}$, so, given a coefficient fitted on local drift and outcome data, accuracy falls by roughly their product.

The monitoring team does not see the accuracy loss. It sees only the camera change and the shifted colors, because nothing in the serving logs records sensitivity, and the loss stays unconfirmed until labeled outcomes arrive. *ML systems can degrade through data drift even when their artifacts remain untouched.*

Silent degradation means that deployment is not the end of the lifecycle; it starts an ongoing operational phase. Monitoring provides the statistical telemetry to detect degradation, while continuous maintenance pipelines update models when evidence warrants it. The infrastructure that runs this phase in production is developed in **[[01 Books/Volume I - Foundations/04 Deployment/14 ML Operations/V1-14 ML Operations#^sec-ml-operations|ML Operations]]**.

In this illustrative scenario, monitoring tracks performance across clinics to detect whether changing patient demographics, camera technology, or equipment degradation affects accuracy. Three feedback pathways guide update decisions: performance evidence can motivate targeted data collection, data-quality findings can prompt preparation changes, and confirmed model degradation can justify retraining or another intervention. Because clinics upload only diagnostic summaries in normal operation, those pathways need a data path of their own: a sampled set of images, sent during off-hours and labeled centrally, supplies the outcome evidence and the retraining data that summaries cannot. Drift thresholds initiate review; they do not select the remedy automatically. Targeted data collection, preparation changes, and retraining count as maintenance, whereas adding a new imaging modality such as optical coherence tomography would be a product change requiring new data, validation, workflow integration, and any applicable regulatory review.

↪ Divergence metrics and threshold conventions for measuring distribution drift are collected for reference in **[[05 Reference/Volume I - Foundations/V1 Data Foundations#^sec-appdx-data-foundations-measuring-drift-divergence-a6dd|Measuring drift]]**.

### Production Monitoring

^sec-ml-workflow-production-monitoring-dynamic-systems-49ea

A clinic camera upgrade that shifts image color profiles, or a site whose older equipment and cataract-heavy patient cohorts degrade accuracy, exposes blind spots across the lab-to-field gap ([[01 Books/Volume I - Foundations/01 Foundations/03 ML Lifecycle/V1-03.07 Stage 2 Data Collection and Preparation#^sec-ml-workflow-bridging-laboratory-realworld-data-e5b6|section 3.6.1]]).[[01 Books/Volume I - Foundations/01 Foundations/03 ML Lifecycle/V1-03.17 Footnotes#^fn23|²³]] When that happens, neither technical teams tracking system health nor clinical staff relying on referrals can wait months of specialist grading to learn that inputs have changed. Monitoring infrastructure is therefore a deployment prerequisite rather than a post-launch addition, pairing fast operational and statistical signals with delayed outcome evaluations so its findings can drive targeted data collection and model revisions.

Bridging that delay between immediate clinic traffic and slow ground-truth labels requires quantitative thresholds for latency, accuracy, and data distribution stability. Lightweight statistical tests that compare production input distributions against the development data[[01 Books/Volume I - Foundations/01 Foundations/03 ML Lifecycle/V1-03.17 Footnotes#^fn24|²⁴]] flag input shifts as they happen, triggering responses ranging from automated alerts to retraining reviews while labeled performance evidence accumulates.

↪ Drift detection pipelines that run these tests against training baselines in production are developed in **[[01 Books/Volume I - Foundations/04 Deployment/14 ML Operations/V1-14.06 Production Monitoring#^sec-ml-operations-feature-distribution-monitoring-b8a0|Feature distribution monitoring]]**.

A production DR system can track four metric categories at different timescales. The following thresholds illustrate one scenario policy; clinical and operational teams must validate them for the intended product, population, and deployment environment.

- **Model performance metrics** (requiring ground truth, available with delay): sensitivity (target above 90 percent, alert if the rolling average, pooled across sites until enough confirmed cases accumulate, falls below 88 percent). Specificity (target above 80 percent, alert if it drops below 78 percent). Subgroup performance (alert if any demographic drops more than 5 percentage points below baseline).
- **Proxy metrics** (available immediately, without ground truth): prediction confidence distribution (alert if mean confidence drops more than 10 percent relative to baseline). Referral rate (alert if rate changes more than 15 percent from baseline). Image quality rejection rate (alert if more than 20 percent of images fail quality checks).
- **Operational metrics**: Inference latency (p95 below 50 ms, alert if above 100 ms), consistent with the scenario’s well-under-a-second on-device time; throughput (alert if queue depth exceeds 50 images); and error rate (alert if more than 0.1 percent of requests fail).
- **Data stability metrics**: Feature and prediction distributions compared with a baseline, with alerts when recent traffic moves outside the expected range.

The targets and the alerts trigger different responses. A measured miss of an acceptance target opens a review of the affected site and system version. Crossing one of the displayed alert thresholds, which sit further from the target, escalates that review as urgent. Neither threshold alone chooses retraining. The hierarchy matters because operational metrics surface immediate problems, proxy metrics flag possible model issues without ground truth, and labeled performance evidence arrives later.

For the camera upgrade, an immediate color-distribution alert supplies a signal and a site identifier, but until representative labels arrive the team holds evidence of changed inputs, not a measured sensitivity loss, and applies the previously approved exposure limits or alternative screening workflow. Reviewed referrals alone cannot supply those labels, because the routing policy sends only flagged or borderline cases to a specialist. Patients the model cleared as normal are never graded in routine care, so any disease cases the model missed among them remain unseen unless the team also labels a sample of unreferred images. Whether the remedy turns out to be restoring a validated camera configuration or returning to data collection and model development, the fix re-enters the lifecycle at the stage that owns the defect. It leaves only after passing the same release checks, with a revised release manifest, before a controlled rollout.

That return path is the lifecycle loop, in which every production finding has an owning stage and a validation to pass again. Running the loop in production is a separate discipline, the operations loop that detects, diagnoses, decides, releases, and recovers. Its incident workflow, from severity classification to postmortem, is developed in **[[01 Books/Volume I - Foundations/04 Deployment/14 ML Operations/V1-14.08 Incident Response and Governance#^sec-ml-operations-incident-response-ml-systems-c637|Incident response for ML systems]]**, and **[[01 Books/Volume I - Foundations/04 Deployment/14 ML Operations/V1-14.07 Retraining Decisions#^sec-ml-operations-retraining-decision-framework-e33d|Retraining decision framework]]** weighs a retraining against its compute and validation cost.

### Maintenance at Scale

^sec-ml-workflow-maintenance-scale-554c

Operating that update loop across hundreds of clinics rather than a single pilot site introduces a fleet-scale coordination problem, because a candidate update that improves aggregate sensitivity can still regress a specific clinic whose camera optics or patient demographics differ from the network average. For a regulated device such as the DR screener, staged rollouts of any approved update must follow a predetermined change-control plan (the changes to the model that regulators approved in advance), and rollback mechanisms[[01 Books/Volume I - Foundations/01 Foundations/03 ML Lifecycle/V1-03.17 Footnotes#^fn25|²⁵]] limit the damage when a release misbehaves. At this scale, monitoring must track global metrics and site-specific behavior together and maintain data lineage, the metadata trail linking production logs to data, code, and model versions. Lineage lets a team correlate a site-specific accuracy drop with the training experiment and data version behind it instead of reconstructing that link by manual forensic analysis, and regulators may require it.

Correlating production evidence with its causes sends work back upstream. [[01 Books/Volume I - Foundations/01 Foundations/03 ML Lifecycle/V1-03.11 Stage 6 Monitoring and Maintenance#^fig-ml-lifecycle-feedback|Figure 5]] maps those return paths across stages 2 through 6, drawing data collection and data preparation as separate boxes and omitting problem definition, which sits upstream of every path shown. In the diabetic retinopathy deployment, when model evaluation uncovers accuracy drops on older fundus cameras, the *Data gaps* path triggers targeted image collection from those specific clinics. Similarly, when validation across patient cohorts reveals lower sensitivity for patients with cataracts, the *Validation Issues* path motivates retraining with synthetic lens-opacity augmentations. In production, runtime telemetry routes along the *Data Quality Issues* and *Performance Insights* paths back into data preparation and collection whenever clinic hardware upgrades change how images look.

Data-quality and distribution gaps detected during evaluation may prompt targeted collection or augmentation, while operational constraints and performance drift can motivate model, data, or pipeline changes after investigation.

^fig-ml-lifecycle-feedback

Proactive maintenance closes the lifecycle loop. Operational signals identify potential problems, review determines whether newly validated data or another change is needed, and production evidence feeds back to refine problem definitions, data-quality standards, and architectural decisions. When retraining is selected, scheduled or evidence-triggered pipelines can incorporate approved data and return the candidate through validation. With the loop closed, the coupling among stages stops being a design-time concern and becomes a property of the running system, one whose consequences no single stage’s metrics reveal.
