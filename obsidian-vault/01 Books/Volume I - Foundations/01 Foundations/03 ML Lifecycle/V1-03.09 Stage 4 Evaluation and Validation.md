# V1-03.09 Stage 4 Evaluation and Validation

[[Home|Home]] · [[01 Books/Volume I - Foundations/Volume 1 Overview|Volume Overview]] · [[01 Books/Volume I - Foundations/01 Foundations/03 ML Lifecycle/V1-03 ML Lifecycle|V1-03 ML Lifecycle]]

^sec-ml-workflow-evaluation-validation-stage-b47d

Suppose the DR team’s packaged candidate artifact matches the study’s AUC of 0.99 on curated research data. The team then tests it on images from a rural clinic where a nurse with two weeks of training operates a five-year-old fundus camera, and sensitivity falls well below the 90 percent target. The software executes without throwing an exception or returning an error code; the model’s learned features simply encounter pixel distributions distorted by optical blur, low illumination, and variable pupil dilation that never appeared in the training distribution. High accuracy on curated test partitions does not guarantee operational reliability in production. Before deployment, a reproducible candidate artifact must pass through systematic evaluation and validation checks that verify accuracy, latency, and robustness across realistic operating conditions, turning a repeatable build into an approved release.

**Definition 3.3: Model validation**

***Model validation*** is the evidence-based check that decides whether a trained model is suitable to deploy for a specified use. It tests the model against deployment constraints such as latency targets, subgroup performance targets, cost budgets, and robustness under distribution shift, but cannot certify safety under every possible condition.

1.  **Significance**: Validation adds dimensions that test-set accuracy ignores. A model achieving 95 percent accuracy on a static test set may miss a latency target, such as 100 ms in a web service, on its slowest requests. It may underperform a required subgroup threshold, such as a maximum five percentage-point performance gap across demographic groups. It may also lose more than ten percentage points of accuracy under common image-quality problems such as blur and glare. Each unchecked dimension is a deployment risk that compounds silently in production.
2.  **Distinction**: Evaluation measures model behavior using chosen datasets, metrics, and experiments, which may include out-of-distribution and subgroup tests. Validation assembles the broader evidence needed for a specified deployment decision, including system latency, throughput, robustness, operational readiness, and cost.
3.  **Common pitfall**: Treating validation as “one more test.” A model can pass the accuracy test and still fail validation on latency, subgroup reliability, or cost.

Evaluation supplies measurements; validation uses them to decide whether the integrated software and hardware pipeline meets its intended deployment requirements. The word *validation* names several different things in ML practice. A validation set is the data partition used to guide model selection. Data validation is the check on incoming data that stage 2 runs. Model validation is the release decision this stage makes, and the system-validation block of [[01 Books/Volume I - Foundations/01 Foundations/03 ML Lifecycle/V1-03.02 The Lifecycle as a Loop#^fig-ml-lifecycle|figure 2]] is its test of the integrated pipeline. That decision is broader than any evaluation on the validation set. A separate test set supplies evidence after model selection is finished; repeatedly using it to choose a model weakens its independence. Accuracy on held-out examples supplies only part of the release evidence, alongside tests of execution, robustness, and operational readiness. Metric thresholds, controlled production exposure, environmental stress tests, and any required regulatory qualification address complementary risks, with the evidence and sequence determined by the intended use. The measurement methodology behind these checks is developed in **[[01 Books/Volume I - Foundations/03 Optimization/12 Benchmarking/V1-12 Benchmarking#^sec-benchmarking|Benchmarking]]**.

### Evaluation Metrics and Thresholds

^sec-ml-workflow-evaluation-metrics-thresholds-ca81

A trained classifier outputs a continuous probability score for each retina, but the clinic workflow needs a binary referral decision that satisfies the $>90$ percent sensitivity and $>80$ percent specificity floors set against the current referral path during problem definition. Lowering the classification threshold catches more disease (raising sensitivity) at the cost of more false-positive referrals (lowering specificity), while raising the threshold does the reverse. Neither metric alone establishes adequate clinical performance or referral capacity; that judgment also depends on disease prevalence, screening volume, and available care. Sweeping the threshold across all candidate cutoffs traces the receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve, a plot of how sensitivity trades against specificity, and the first evaluation check must verify that at least one operating threshold satisfies both floors simultaneously under the intended operating conditions.

A red dashed horizontal line marks sensitivity above 90% and a red dashed vertical line marks specificity above 80%; the upper-right corner beyond both lines is shaded gray and labeled Pass. An illustrative dark curve falls from high sensitivity at low specificity to low sensitivity at high specificity, and briefly passes through the Pass corner.*

*Sweeping the threshold traces one illustrative curve; it must reach the corner where both floors hold.*

Even separated into sensitivity and specificity, overall numbers can hide local failures. Stratified evaluation splits the test set across patient age brackets, chronic conditions, and clinic camera models to check whether accuracy holds across every subgroup. A DR model with 94 percent overall sensitivity may fall below 80 percent on images captured from patients with cataracts or under low clinic lighting. Those local drops stay hidden in overall averages and surface only as missed diagnoses after launch unless automated evaluation checks each subgroup slice before promoting a candidate checkpoint.

Beyond label accuracy, evaluation must measure probability calibration:[[01 Books/Volume I - Foundations/01 Foundations/03 ML Lifecycle/V1-03.17 Footnotes#^fn20|²⁰]] whether a model’s predicted confidence score matches how often it is actually right. Modern deep models are often poorly calibrated, reporting high confidence ($>90$ percent) even on wrong predictions or on inputs unlike their training data ([[01 Books/Volume I - Foundations/01 Foundations/03 ML Lifecycle/V1-03.16 Source References#^ref-guo2017calibration|Guo et al. 2017]]). When automated triage routes borderline cases to human specialists based on confidence cutoffs, overconfident errors send the wrong cases through without review and erode clinician trust.

↪ How calibration is measured, and the recalibration methods that improve it, are developed in **[[01 Books/Volume I - Foundations/03 Optimization/12 Benchmarking/V1-12.10 Model and Data Evaluation#^sec-benchmarking-calibration-confidence-scores-matter-3669|Confidence calibration]]**.

### Offline and Online Evaluation

^sec-ml-workflow-offline-online-evaluation-3a6b

Clearing sensitivity, subgroup, and calibration checks on a static test partition establishes predictive behavior, but it does not exercise the conditions of a running service. Live requests arrive in bursts, each request and result must be serialized into bytes for transmission, and logging telemetry adds its own overhead. Offline load tests and replay can expose such bottlenecks when they reproduce the relevant conditions; live traffic supplies additional evidence about the deployed environment. Validation therefore progresses from static offline partitions toward controlled production exposure.

For a centralized service, online evaluation extends validation to live traffic through staged rollout.[[01 Books/Volume I - Foundations/01 Foundations/03 ML Lifecycle/V1-03.17 Footnotes#^fn21|²¹]] Staged rollout exposes one candidate to live traffic in steps, unlike the cross-tier compression pattern called progressive deployment in **[[01 Books/Volume I - Foundations/01 Foundations/02 System Paradigms/V1-02.11 Hybrid Architectures#^sec-ml-systems-integration-patterns-5935|Splitting work across tiers: Three integration patterns]]**. In shadow mode the candidate sees live requests but its predictions are only logged, a canary returns its predictions to a small fraction of traffic, and an A/B test splits traffic between candidate and baseline to compare outcomes. Each step adds a different kind of evidence ([[01 Books/Volume I - Foundations/01 Foundations/03 ML Lifecycle/V1-03.09 Stage 4 Evaluation and Validation#^tbl-ml-workflow-validation-stages|table 3]]).

↪ Shadow and canary releases, the production form of this staged rollout, are developed alongside blue-green switching in **[[01 Books/Volume I - Foundations/04 Deployment/14 ML Operations/V1-14.05 Releasing Models#^sec-ml-operations-model-deployment-b9b9|Model deployment]]**.

| **Validation stage**   | **Typical evidence it adds** |
|:-----------------------|:-----------------------------|
| **Offline evaluation** | Algorithmic behavior         |
| **Shadow mode**        | Integration behavior         |
| **Canary deployment**  | Limited-traffic behavior     |
| **A/B testing**        | Comparative outcomes         |

^tbl-ml-workflow-validation-stages

These staged checks must also run where the model runs. In the DR scenario, intermittent connectivity and local execution move them to clinic devices and pilot sites, where a team compares a candidate against recorded clinic inputs and then introduces an approved update at a small group of clinics. Each step keeps its evidence requirements and its exposure limit, the share of patient traffic the candidate may serve. An A/B split of patients between models usually counts as a clinical study and needs ethics review.

### Production-Condition Validation

^sec-ml-workflow-validation-production-conditions-a351

Whereas the rollout stages in [[01 Books/Volume I - Foundations/01 Foundations/03 ML Lifecycle/V1-03.09 Stage 4 Evaluation and Validation#^sec-ml-workflow-offline-online-evaluation-3a6b|section 3.8.2]] bound how much live traffic a candidate sees at each step, production-condition validation specifies which real-world stresses those offline and pilot checks must exercise before exposure widens. Because of the lab-to-field gap ([[01 Books/Volume I - Foundations/01 Foundations/03 ML Lifecycle/V1-03.07 Stage 2 Data Collection and Preparation#^sec-ml-workflow-bridging-laboratory-realworld-data-e5b6|section 3.6.1]]), a model evaluated on one clinic’s clean held-out images says little about the other cameras, capture conditions, and patient populations specified during problem definition. Validation must therefore sample those field conditions across three dimensions: site heterogeneity, input-level perturbations, and temporal drift.

External and multisite validation test whether the model has learned generalizable disease features or merely memorized the quirks of the development hospital’s cameras. In retinal screening, sensor sensitivity, lens coatings, and flash color differ across camera vendors. A DR model trained exclusively on images from high-end fundus cameras in research hospitals can fail when deployed to community clinics using older hardware. Multisite validation therefore benchmarks candidate models on data collected across independent clinic sites with different cameras, lighting conditions, and operator experience levels.

Robustness testing shifts focus to individual inference requests, checking how the model handles realistic image flaws and edge cases. For computer vision pipelines, these flaws include sensor noise, optical blur, uneven contrast, and partial pupil occlusions. As in the Thai clinics of the running case, dim room lighting and undilated pupils produce images the system rejects as ungradable. Testing models against both synthetic and real-world degraded images reveals whether the serving pipeline needs an upstream image-quality check to reject unusable frames before spending accelerator cycles on inference.

Temporal validation tests whether the model keeps its accuracy as the clinic environment changes over weeks and months. Test sets drawn from a single historical window miss later shifts in patient demographics, disease prevalence, or clinic referral habits. When the input distribution changes while the relationship between inputs and labels stays the same, the system faces *covariate shift* ([[01 Books/Volume I - Foundations/01 Foundations/03 ML Lifecycle/V1-03.16 Source References#^ref-quinonero2009dataset|Quiñonero-Candela et al. 2009]]); when the relationship between input features and clinical diagnoses itself changes, it faces *concept drift* ([[01 Books/Volume I - Foundations/01 Foundations/03 ML Lifecycle/V1-03.16 Source References#^ref-gama2014|Gama et al. 2014]]). Both can cause silent model degradation and motivate the continuous telemetry and drift detection pipelines described in [[01 Books/Volume I - Foundations/01 Foundations/03 ML Lifecycle/V1-03.11 Stage 6 Monitoring and Maintenance#^sec-ml-workflow-monitoring-maintenance-stage-e79a|section 3.10]].

↪ Statistical tests that detect covariate shift and concept drift in production are developed in **[[01 Books/Volume I - Foundations/01 Foundations/04 Data Engineering/V1-04.13 Operational Data Health#^sec-data-engineering-detecting-responding-data-drift-509a|Data drift detection and response]]**.

### Regulatory Validation

^sec-ml-workflow-regulatory-domainspecific-validation-f8ab

Passing multisite, robustness, and temporal stress tests shows how the model behaves on degraded inputs, but in a high-stakes domain such as healthcare, validation must also prove that the system fails safely when it is uncertain. For diabetic retinopathy screening, clinical validation studies run the automated system alongside licensed ophthalmologists, comparing model classifications against ground truth established by specialist consensus panels. Systems that *fail safely*, by routing ambiguous or low-confidence images to human specialists, fit into clinical care, whereas systems that *fail silently* produce missed diagnoses that no one catches until patients lose vision. When U.S. Food and Drug Administration (FDA) premarket review is required, this domain-specific safety and performance evidence must also match the device and its regulatory pathway.[[01 Books/Volume I - Foundations/01 Foundations/03 ML Lifecycle/V1-03.17 Footnotes#^fn22|²²]]

Human factors validation tests how the model fits into the clinic staff’s daily decisions. Even an accurate model will be turned off or bypassed if its interface confuses nurses or it slows patient intake, so validation must measure how staff and system work together alongside statistical accuracy.

### Deployment Readiness

^sec-ml-workflow-validation-deployment-readiness-207c

Passing validation produces an immutable release manifest recording the evidence and approval for a particular model version, intended use, and operating conditions. This manifest records the exact model checkpoint hash, preprocessing container configuration, calibration parameters, benchmark scores across designated demographic and equipment slices, and any required regulatory authorization. The manifest ties the release to that evidence; it does not itself confer external authorization or establish performance outside the evaluated conditions. If a model fails to clear an established threshold, such as p99 serving latency (the latency that 99 percent of requests stay under) exceeding the service level agreement (SLA) budget or sensitivity on low-light fundus images falling below the safety floor, the release is held and deployment halts. Diagnostics then route backward to the responsible lifecycle stage: data curation for targeted data collection, model training for a loss re-weighted toward the missed cases, or runtime optimization to reduce execution cost.

That evidence and approval cover one particular system configuration, so deployment must install and sustain exactly that configuration at each clinic, including its preprocessing routines, compiled model, telemetry collectors, and rollback triggers. Rollout checks confirm that the installed serving path matches the evaluated conditions and that local integration has not invalidated the release evidence.
