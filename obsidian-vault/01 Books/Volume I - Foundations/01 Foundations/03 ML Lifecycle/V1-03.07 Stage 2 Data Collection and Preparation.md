# V1-03.07 Stage 2 Data Collection and Preparation

[[Home|Home]] · [[01 Books/Volume I - Foundations/Volume 1 Overview|Volume Overview]] · [[01 Books/Volume I - Foundations/01 Foundations/03 ML Lifecycle/V1-03 ML Lifecycle|V1-03 ML Lifecycle]]

^sec-ml-workflow-data-collection-preparation-stage-ae99

On paper, the problem definition asks for 90 percent sensitivity across rural clinics; in the field, that target immediately meets smudged camera lenses, missing patient records, and a 2 Mbps clinic internet link. Moving from problem definition to data collection is where abstract accuracy targets meet those physical realities. This stage determines dataset size $({D})$, the number of examples, and through it the iron law’s byte volume $({D_{\text{vol}}})$ for a training run, the bytes that storage systems, networks, and memory buses must move. The two grow together but are not the same quantity, since image resolution and encoding set how many bytes each example costs.

The deployment constraints established during problem definition become technical data requirements. If the model must run on edge devices, the collection pipeline must produce inputs compatible with edge preprocessing; if it must achieve 90 percent sensitivity across diverse populations, the dataset must sample those subpopulations sufficiently to support model training.

For diabetic retinopathy screening, these data requirements demand a collection strategy far more stringent than standard computer vision benchmarks. The pipeline must assemble roughly $10^5$ retinal fundus photographs across diverse patient cohorts and imaging hardware, with each image graded by multiple ophthalmologists and disagreements resolved to establish consensus ground-truth labels capable of withstanding clinical and regulatory scrutiny. The annotation protocol must capture fine pathological features (microaneurysms, hemorrhages, and hard exudates) across the entire disease severity continuum. Curation also governs computational efficiency, because not all captured data contributes equally to training, and deliberate selection (**[[01 Books/Volume I - Foundations/03 Optimization/09 Data Selection/V1-09.04 Static Pruning#^sec-data-selection-case-smaller-datasets-215e|The case for smaller datasets]]**) can eliminate redundant samples while preserving the statistical coverage required for generalizable performance.

Collecting those images also strains the clinic’s infrastructure, because each retinal photograph spans several megabytes and clinical protocols capture multiple views per patient. A busy rural facility generates gigabytes of raw imaging data each day, a volume that would overwhelm an intermittent 2 Mbps uplink during operating hours. The training set therefore cannot arrive as a live stream of every capture. It starts as an initial curated corpus of graded images, and later additions travel as sampled images sent during off-hours. Once the screener is deployed, routine traffic shrinks further. Rather than saturating limited wide-area bandwidth with raw uploads, the deployed system can execute inference locally on the edge accelerator and transmit only a compact edge summary, a structured record containing the diagnostic classification and clinical metadata. Local inference reduces uplink traffic from tens of megabytes to kilobytes per patient.

**Napkin Math 3.2: Bandwidth vs. compute**

**Problem**: Quantify the uplink window occupancy for raw image uploads from a busy rural clinic capturing retinal images for diabetic retinopathy screening, and evaluate architectural alternatives to relieve link contention.

**Math**:

1.  Daily data: At 100 patients/day, 4 photos/patient, and 5 MB/photo, the clinic produces 2 GB/day.
2.  Upload time: The uplink is 2 Mb/s, or 0.25 MB/s. Dividing 2,000 MB by that rate gives 8,000 s, approximately 2.2 h.
3.  **Constraint**: If the clinic requires same-shift upload during its 8 h operating window, this transfer alone occupies the link for 27.8 percent of that window and may contend with other traffic.

**Systems insight**: On a good day the raw upload fits the shift, but it ties up a large share of the link, and on a day the link drops it does not happen at all. A screening result that must reach the nurse during the visit therefore cannot depend on the uplink, which is the case for local inference. Uploading detection summaries (10 KB/patient) instead of images also cuts uplink traffic by 2,000×. Overnight batching of images for training remains possible and is not compared here.

Running inference at the clinic (napkin math [3.2](https://mlsysbook.ai/vol1/03_ml_lifecycle/03_ml_lifecycle.html#nbk-ml-workflow-bandwidth-vs-compute)) applies the data locality invariant (definition [2.3](https://mlsysbook.ai/vol1/02_system_paradigms/02_system_paradigms.html#dfn-ml-systems-data-locality-invariant)) to the clinic uplink. The invariant rules out any remote path whose network transfer alone exceeds the deadline. Edge accelerators such as the NVIDIA Jetson family[[01 Books/Volume I - Foundations/01 Foundations/03 ML Lifecycle/V1-03.17 Footnotes#^fn10|¹⁰]] make it practical, though they introduce a hardware trade-off between using a smaller model that fits a low-power edge budget and provisioning more capable on-premise devices at higher cost. The bandwidth constraint makes this infrastructure choice a data-collection decision rather than a late implementation detail. The scenario architecture combines edge devices for local inference and preprocessing, clinic aggregation servers for data management and buffering, and cloud training infrastructure for periodic model updates. It targets a result within seconds of capture and operation that does not depend on connectivity.

Privacy constraints impose a similar architectural decision. Patient privacy regulations may motivate local retention or distributed training, but they generally specify protections and permitted uses rather than one required training topology. Workflows that keep raw clinic data local while sharing only approved updates or summaries add complexity to both data collection and model training infrastructure.

### Lab-To-Field Data Gap

^sec-ml-workflow-bridging-laboratory-realworld-data-e5b6

Wherever the data is stored, the model can be no better than what each clinic’s camera captures. When Google’s DR screening system moved from curated benchmark images into Thai clinics ([[01 Books/Volume I - Foundations/01 Foundations/03 ML Lifecycle/V1-03.16 Source References#^ref-beede2020human|Beede et al. 2020]]), images arrived from diverse camera equipment operated by staff with varying expertise, often under suboptimal lighting and inconsistent patient positioning ([[01 Books/Volume I - Foundations/01 Foundations/03 ML Lifecycle/V1-03.02 The Lifecycle as a Loop#^sec-ml-workflow-case-study-diabetic-retinopathy-screening-7d71|section 3.1.2]]). That divergence between controlled development datasets and real-world inputs is the **lab-to-field gap** introduced with the running case. A model trained exclusively on sharp, well-lit photographs from standardized research cameras can fail on blurry or dim captures from older clinic hardware, not because the algorithm is wrong but because the input distribution has shifted beyond the training envelope. Accuracy on a curated test set therefore does not show readiness for the clinic, and validation must sample these field conditions before release ([[01 Books/Volume I - Foundations/01 Foundations/03 ML Lifecycle/V1-03.09 Stage 4 Evaluation and Validation#^sec-ml-workflow-validation-production-conditions-a351|section 3.8.3]]). Closing that gap across many clinics is an infrastructure problem as much as a modeling one.

### Distributed Data Infrastructure

^sec-ml-workflow-data-infrastructure-distributed-deployment-23ec

If a deployment grows from a handful of clinics to hundreds, the data infrastructure becomes a distributed system whose design follows from the constraints set during problem definition. Connectivity decides transmission. Facilities with reliable broadband can stream diagnostic summaries in near real time, whereas clinics with intermittent satellite links require **store-and-forward architectures** that buffer data locally and synchronize opportunistically during connectivity windows. That choice propagates through the physical stack, because store-and-forward clinics need larger local storage buffers, edge inference that runs without the network, and a way to reconcile local preliminary classifications with later central review. Storage placement matters at the central end as well, because training data, validation data, and the regulatory archive are read at very different rates.

↪ The cost and throughput of each storage tier, and why ML systems combine them, are measured in **[[01 Books/Volume I - Foundations/01 Foundations/04 Data Engineering/V1-04.11 Storage and Delivery#^sec-data-engineering-ml-storage-requirements-performance-1b0d|Storage tiers and cost]]**.

Data heterogeneity grows alongside byte volume. Divergent camera optics, uneven clinic illumination, and variable operator training give each clinic a distinct input distribution, so each clinic operates as a distinct data source.[[01 Books/Volume I - Foundations/01 Foundations/03 ML Lifecycle/V1-03.17 Footnotes#^fn11|¹¹]] The dataset this stage hands forward must therefore carry provenance metadata that records camera model, capture settings, and operator ID for every image. That provenance trail is what isolates a site-specific accuracy regression and satisfies a regulatory audit. Heterogeneity also argues for validation at the point of capture, because a miscalibrated camera discovered weeks later during a central training run has already wasted storage, compute, and patient follow-up opportunities.

### Quality Assurance and Validation

^sec-ml-workflow-quality-assurance-validation-f923

A blurry retinal image that slips past quality checks does not merely waste storage. If such images enter training with unreliable labels or appear frequently in production, they distort the statistical evidence available to the model and degrade clinical safety. Quality assurance ensures that incoming data satisfies the invariants downstream stages depend upon. In the diabetic retinopathy scenario, automated quality-scoring models deployed at the fundus camera immediately flag out-of-focus or improperly framed captures, prompting clinical staff to retake the photograph while the patient is still present rather than discovering the defect weeks later during model training.

↪ Data cascades, in which uncaught collection defects compound into downstream training and evaluation failures, are developed in **[[01 Books/Volume I - Foundations/01 Foundations/04 Data Engineering/V1-04.03 How Data Fails#^sec-data-engineering-data-cascades-systematic-foundations-matter-2efe|Data cascades]]**.

Validation extends beyond image quality to verify proper labeling, patient association, and privacy compliance. Local validation catches capture problems on the spot, whereas centralized validation detects distributional anomalies across the full clinic network, for instance by flagging when a particular site’s images skew toward a narrow demographic range that would bias the training set. Together with the bandwidth and privacy limits set earlier in the stage, these quality checks settle what data the training set contains and what clinic-to-clinic variations the model must handle. Even a curated, validated dataset can still starve the next stage, however, if it cannot reach training as fast as training consumes it.

### Sample Order and Delivery Rate

^sec-ml-workflow-erosion-determinism

Training reads the DR dataset, on the order of $10^5$ fundus photographs, many times over and in a fresh random order on each pass, which scatters reads across storage and makes fast delivery harder. The data loader (the software that reads and batches examples for training) must therefore deliver images at least as fast as training consumes them. When delivery is slower, the accelerator sits idle however fast it is. The hand-off is therefore complete only when the workflow has measured that delivery rate and routed any shortfall to the stage that owns it. Closing such a gap is the feeding problem, which data engineering develops in **[[01 Books/Volume I - Foundations/01 Foundations/04 Data Engineering/V1-04.10 Physics of Data#^sec-data-engineering-feeding-problem|The feeding problem]]**.

↪ How training overlaps data delivery with computation so that the accelerator stays busy is developed in **[[01 Books/Volume I - Foundations/02 Development/08 Model Training/V1-08.07 Pipeline Optimizations#^sec-model-training-data-prefetching-pipeline-overlapping-e984|Data prefetching and overlapping]]**.

Data collection therefore does not end when training begins. Feedback from evaluation and production keeps revising what is collected, and the delivery test keeps confirming that data arrives as fast as training consumes it. Building the collection and delivery pipelines is the subject of **[[01 Books/Volume I - Foundations/01 Foundations/04 Data Engineering/V1-04 Data Engineering#^sec-data-engineering|Data Engineering]]**, and deciding which collected examples deserve training compute is the subject of **[[01 Books/Volume I - Foundations/03 Optimization/09 Data Selection/V1-09 Data Selection#^sec-data-selection|Data Selection]]**. What this stage hands forward is a versioned dataset, its preprocessing pipeline, and a measured delivery rate, which together become the raw material that model development turns into trained models.
