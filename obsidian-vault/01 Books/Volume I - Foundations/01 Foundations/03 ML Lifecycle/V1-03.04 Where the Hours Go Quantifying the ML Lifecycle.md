# V1-03.04 Where the Hours Go Quantifying the ML Lifecycle

[[Home|Home]] · [[01 Books/Volume I - Foundations/Volume 1 Overview|Volume Overview]] · [[01 Books/Volume I - Foundations/01 Foundations/03 ML Lifecycle/V1-03 ML Lifecycle|V1-03 ML Lifecycle]]

^sec-ml-workflow-quantifying-ml-lifecycle-bd69

The DR team lost five months to one late constraint. Conceptual models of the ML lifecycle describe which stages exist, but planning a workflow requires knowing where its hours go, and that time rarely concentrates where research literature spends its pages.

Practitioner surveys show a gap between where research attention centers and where practitioners report spending their time, with about 79 percent of data scientists in CrowdFlower’s 2016 survey naming data collection or cleaning as the activity that consumed most of their time ([[01 Books/Volume I - Foundations/01 Foundations/03 ML Lifecycle/V1-03.16 Source References#^ref-crowdflower2016data|CrowdFlower 2016]]). In the full distribution ([[01 Books/Volume I - Foundations/01 Foundations/03 ML Lifecycle/V1-03.04 Where the Hours Go Quantifying the ML Lifecycle#^fig-ds-time|figure 4]]), algorithm refinement and training-set construction together draw fewer responses than data collection alone.

![[90 Assets/Volume I/03 ML Lifecycle/Generated Figure 47dfaa51b6.svg]]

Figure 4: **Data Scientist Most-Time Responses**: In CrowdFlower’s 2016 survey, 60 percent of respondents selected cleaning and organizing data as the activity where they spent the most time, with 19 percent selecting data collection. Model-focused responses such as pattern mining, training set construction, and algorithm refinement together represent roughly 16 percent of responses. Adapted from CrowdFlower ([[01 Books/Volume I - Foundations/01 Foundations/03 ML Lifecycle/V1-03.16 Source References#^ref-crowdflower2016data|2016]]).

^fig-ds-time

The survey supports one conclusion, that data work is a major time sink for these practitioners, which is reason enough for data engineering, developed in **[[01 Books/Volume I - Foundations/01 Foundations/04 Data Engineering/V1-04 Data Engineering#^sec-data-engineering|Data Engineering]]**, to receive explicit planning rather than treatment as setup work. The survey does not measure how all engineering hours were divided, so a team must still find its own bottleneck rather than set priorities from response shares. In the DR project, each image must be graded by multiple ophthalmologists before it can train anything, so collection and labeling can delay useful experiments more than training does. Improving that pipeline can then reduce iteration time before model or training optimizations become the limiting concern.

Wherever the hours concentrate, iteration velocity governs project progress. The feedback arrows in [[01 Books/Volume I - Foundations/01 Foundations/03 ML Lifecycle/V1-03.02 The Lifecycle as a Loop#^fig-ml-lifecycle|figure 2]] (the *Data fixes* loop from curation back to collection, the *Data needs* loop from validation back to curation, and the *Online Performance* path from production deployment back to collection) are not rare exceptions; they are paths teams traverse repeatedly. Drifting clinic images or wrong labels send the DR team back to collection, and a candidate that exceeds the clinic device’s memory sends it back to model design. Each loop consumes calendar time and compute budget, so the length of one cycle bounds how much a team can learn.

Time spent waiting on slow training runs or cumbersome validation pipelines reduces how many candidate models a team can test, and that loss is the **iteration tax**. Consider two candidate DR models developed over the same six months: a large ensemble trained in the cloud, which takes a week per training run, and a lightweight edge model that trains in hours.

![[90 Assets/Volume I/03 ML Lifecycle/Vol1 ML Lifecycle Margin 001 5009fd53b0.svg]]

*Two horizontal gray bars: 100 experiments for an hourly cycle and 26 for a weekly cycle, joined by a bracket labeled approximately 3.8 times.*

*Over the same six months, the hourly cycle tests nearly four times as many ideas.*

**Napkin Math 3.1: The iteration tax**

**Problem**: Compare what six months of development buys for a rural diabetic retinopathy screening system. The first option is a large ensemble developed on cloud hardware as a laboratory baseline (training time: 1 week, initial accuracy: 95 percent), which would itself need compression before it could fit the clinic’s memory budget. The second option is a lightweight edge model (training time: 1 hour, initial accuracy: 90 percent). The scenario assumes that each large-model experiment improves accuracy by ~0.15 percentage points and each small-model experiment by ~0.1 percentage points, that neither model can exceed a 99 percent accuracy ceiling, and that a team can design, run, and learn from only a few experiments per working week. How do the two compare in final accuracy and in the number of hypotheses the team can test?

**Math**: In six months (~26 weeks), the possible experiment count is:

1.  Large model: 26 weeks of calendar time at 1 week per experiment gives 26 experiments.
2.  Small model: 26 weeks of calendar time at 168 h/week gives 4,368 possible experiments at 1 hour each. Machine time does not limit the small model at that cycle length, so the team’s pace of a few experiments per working week sets its count at 100 effective experiments.

**Result**: The small model starts at 90 percent and would reach 100 percent after 100 effective iterations, so the ceiling holds it at 99 percent. The large model starts at 95 percent and reaches 98.9 percent after 26 slower iterations. Both end near the ceiling, but the fast-cycle team tested 3.8× as many ideas, so it also learned which augmentations (input transformations that leave the diagnosis unchanged) and preprocessing choices matter, knowledge the slow-cycle team never acquired. Because the gains, the ceiling, and the experiment pace are assumptions, the calculation demonstrates a difference in search breadth, not an accuracy lead.

**Systems insight**: *Iteration velocity is a feature.* Its value lies in search breadth rather than in a guaranteed accuracy lead. When candidate systems have comparable attainable quality, shorter cycles expand how much of the design space a team can test. Speed alone cannot guarantee that a smaller model will outperform a larger one. In the DR screening scenario, the lightweight model’s rapid iteration cycle enables the team to experiment with augmentations, preprocessing pipelines, and architecture variations far more quickly.

Late discoveries multiply the iteration tax,[[01 Books/Volume I - Foundations/01 Foundations/03 ML Lifecycle/V1-03.17 Footnotes#^fn7|⁷]] because each one sends the team back through cycles it has already paid for. As the constraint propagation principle ([[01 Books/Volume I - Foundations/01 Foundations/03 ML Lifecycle/V1-03.03 How Decisions Cascade the Constraint Propagation Principle#^sec-ml-workflow-decisions-cascade-system-98f4|section 3.2]]) established, a violation caught only after deployment invalidates artifacts produced by earlier stages, and those invalidated artifacts cost another pass through retraining, pipeline adjustment, and renewed validation. Keeping that tax bounded requires assigning each stage clear ownership of its constraints and verifying every artifact before it crosses a stage hand-off ([[01 Books/Volume I - Foundations/01 Foundations/03 ML Lifecycle/V1-03.05 Stage Ownership and Hand-Offs#^sec-ml-workflow-stage-interface-specification-ae3c|section 3.4.1]]).
