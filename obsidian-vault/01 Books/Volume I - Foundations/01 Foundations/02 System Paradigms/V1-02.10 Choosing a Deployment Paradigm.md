# V1-02.10 Choosing a Deployment Paradigm

[[Home|Home]] · [[01 Books/Volume I - Foundations/Volume 1 Overview|Volume Overview]] · [[01 Books/Volume I - Foundations/01 Foundations/02 System Paradigms/V1-02 System Paradigms|V1-02 System Paradigms]]

^sec-ml-systems-comparative-analysis-paradigm-selection-bf66

An architect choosing where to run an ML feature rarely optimizes one dimension. A privacy rule may forbid cloud processing, a latency budget may forbid remote inference, a memory footprint may exceed the mobile device, and a cost target may rule out always-on edge hardware. Cloud, edge, mobile, and TinyML are operating envelopes for those conflicts, so selecting among them requires a unified comparison framework and a structured decision process.

### Weighing Operational Trade-Offs Across Tiers

^sec-ml-systems-quantitative-tradeoff-analysis-56a8

A model that fits comfortably inside a tier’s latency, power, and memory budgets can still fail in production if the team cannot ship updates to it, if a dropped internet link halts the service, or if monthly cloud bills outrun the product’s revenue. Choosing a deployment tier requires weighing latency-vs-throughput paradigm trade-offs across physical and operational budgets at once. The resource envelopes are already on the table: [[01 Books/Volume I - Foundations/01 Foundations/02 System Paradigms/V1-02.02 Four Deployment Regimes Across Nine Orders of Magnitude#^tbl-deployment-paradigms-overview|table 1]] gives each paradigm’s latency, power, and memory regime, and [[01 Books/Volume I - Foundations/01 Foundations/02 System Paradigms/V1-02.05 System Balance and Hardware#^tbl-representative-systems|table 4]] grounds them in concrete platforms. What those tables leave out are the operational dimensions that decide many real deployments: how the system scales, what it exposes, whether it needs a network, what it costs, and how hard it is to build and ship. [[01 Books/Volume I - Foundations/01 Foundations/02 System Paradigms/V1-02.10 Choosing a Deployment Paradigm#^tbl-big-vs-tiny-d3f9a072|Table 9]] contrasts the four paradigms along eight operational dimensions that cover these questions.

| **Aspect**                 | **Cloud ML**                            | **Edge ML**                                    | **Mobile ML**                                     | **TinyML**                              |
|:---------------------------|:----------------------------------------|:-----------------------------------------------|:--------------------------------------------------|:----------------------------------------|
| **Scalability**            | High (provider capacity limits)         | Good (limited by edge hardware)                | Moderate (per-device scaling)                     | Limited (fixed hardware)                |
| **Data Exposure**          | Remote processing expands the surface   | Local processing reduces transmission          | On-device processing reduces transmission         | Raw data can remain local               |
| **Connectivity Required**  | Required for remote requests            | Intermittent                                   | Optional                                          | None                                    |
| **Offline Capability**     | None                                    | Good                                           | Excellent                                         | Complete                                |
| **Real-time Processing**   | Network and execution must fit deadline | Local execution must fit deadline              | Deadline and sustained-power limits               | Deadline and memory-capacity limits     |
| **Cost anchor**            | Service bill (\$1000s+/month)           | Hardware purchase (\$100s–\$1,000s per device) | Device purchase (\$200–\$1000+; often user-owned) | Hardware purchase (\$1–\$10 per device) |
| **Development Complexity** | High (cloud expertise needed)           | Moderate-High (edge+networking)                | Moderate (mobile SDKs)                            | High (embedded expertise)               |
| **Deployment Speed**       | Fast                                    | Moderate                                       | Fast                                              | Slow                                    |

Table 9: **Operational Paradigm Comparison**: Eight operational dimensions complement the resource envelopes of [[01 Books/Volume I - Foundations/01 Foundations/02 System Paradigms/V1-02.02 Four Deployment Regimes Across Nine Orders of Magnitude#^tbl-deployment-paradigms-overview|table 1]] and [[01 Books/Volume I - Foundations/01 Foundations/02 System Paradigms/V1-02.05 System Balance and Hardware#^tbl-representative-systems|table 4]]. Cloud ML scales most easily, while local processing can reduce transmission and centralized-storage exposure without itself guaranteeing privacy; local tiers can operate without a WAN, but real-time response depends on workload and complete-path timing, while development complexity is highest at the two ends of the spectrum. Cost anchors mix recurring service bills with device purchase prices; they are partial illustrations, not a matched lifecycle-cost ranking.

^tbl-big-vs-tiny-d3f9a072

No column of [[01 Books/Volume I - Foundations/01 Foundations/02 System Paradigms/V1-02.10 Choosing a Deployment Paradigm#^tbl-big-vs-tiny-d3f9a072|table 9]] wins every row, because each paradigm buys its strengths with costs elsewhere. Remote processing, for example, widens the network attack surface and requires transport encryption and strict isolation between tenants; local processing keeps raw data on the device, reducing network exposure without eliminating the risk of physical tampering. The workload archetypes from [[01 Books/Volume I - Foundations/01 Foundations/02 System Paradigms/V1-02.04 Analyzing Workloads#^sec-ml-systems-analyzing-workloads-cbb8|section 2.3]] map directly to these boundaries, with each archetype gravitating toward paradigms that relieve its primary bottleneck.

Each paradigm also shifts engineering complexity to a different subsystem. Cloud deployments demand expertise in multi-tenant networking and dynamic cost governance, whereas edge and TinyML deployments trade those cloud concerns for local thermal and memory limits along with distributed updates and site maintenance. Mobile deployments sit between the two, operating on user-owned hardware that avoids infrastructure purchase costs at the price of platform-specific drivers, operating-system background task limits, and shared energy budgets. None of the four categories supplies a workload-independent latency or energy ranking.

A common architectural failure is selecting a deployment target based on model accuracy alone while treating hardware constraints as downstream optimization details. An unquantized model that delivers 99 percent accuracy in an offline evaluation becomes unviable if its memory footprint triggers paging on mobile hardware, or if continuous inference drives thermal throttling within seconds. Physical invariants (memory capacity, thermal dissipation limits, and network transit delay) must serve as early screening criteria before model development begins, pruning invalid deployment targets before training resources are expended.

### Four Screening Checks for Choosing a Tier

^sec-ml-systems-decision-framework-241f

Pruning invalid targets begins one step earlier than choosing between the cloud and a microcontroller. Before the four tier checks that follow, an engineer must verify whether the problem requires machine learning at all. Deep neural networks carry a heavy maintenance burden that deterministic code avoids, and a fifty-line heuristic rule set can often solve the problem at a tiny fraction of the operating cost. The added engineering and operating cost that a learned system carries over such a baseline is its **complexity tax**.

**Systems Perspective 2.3: The complexity tax**

Consider a classification task solvable either by deterministic heuristic rules or by a deep learning pipeline. The heuristic requires fifty lines of code, incurs near-zero compute cost, demands approximately one hour per month to update rules, and fails in ways engineers can read in the code. The ML system may require only fifty lines of model definition, but, as Sculley et al. ([[01 Books/Volume I - Foundations/01 Foundations/02 System Paradigms/V1-02.16 Source References#^ref-sculley2015hidden|2015]]) observed of production ML generally, it introduces roughly 2,000 lines of supporting infrastructure for feature ingestion, telemetry logging, drift monitoring, and GPU driver management. It also requires 40 hours per month diagnosing data drift and maintaining pipelines.

An ML model that increases accuracy from 90 percent to 95 percent while multiplying operational maintenance by, say, 40× requires an economic comparison. The accuracy and maintenance ratios alone do not establish the return; the value of the avoided errors must justify the additional engineering cost. When the ongoing maintenance cost outstrips the value of marginal statistical gains, deterministic software remains the correct systems choice.

↪ Framing the problem against the existing baseline, and retaining that baseline when the expected benefit does not justify the added cost, is developed in **[[01 Books/Volume I - Foundations/01 Foundations/03 ML Lifecycle/V1-03.06 Stage 1 Problem Definition#^sec-ml-workflow-problem-definition-stage-5974|Stage 1: Problem Definition]]**.

When this complexity tax outweighs the operational value of improved statistical predictions, the ML deployment costs the system more than it returns. Once a learned system is justified, however, paradigm selection relies on a tiered decision framework ordered by how rapidly a constraint invalidates an architecture. The screening hierarchy evaluates four successive checks: regulatory privacy, physical latency, local resources, and lifecycle economics. The last check compares matched lifecycle budgets, costs counted over the same lifetime, the same availability target, and the same scope of expenses, because a comparison that counts more for one option than for another favors the option it undercounts. Privacy leads the order because a legal prohibition on moving data eliminates an option before anything is measured, whereas the latency and resource checks require numbers. Each check passes only its survivors to the next, so the screening sequence acts as successive filters on a candidate set ([[01 Books/Volume I - Foundations/01 Foundations/02 System Paradigms/V1-02.10 Choosing a Deployment Paradigm#^fig-mlsys-playbook-flowchart|figure 11]]).

Figure 11: **Deployment Decision Screening**: Apply privacy, latency, resource, and cost checks to the remaining candidate set. Every check retains upstream exclusions. An empty set requires redesign or partitioning; surviving candidates still need complete-path validation.

^fig-mlsys-playbook-flowchart

The flowchart carries a remaining candidate set through all four checks. A tier excluded at one check stays excluded at every later check. If regulatory rules or data sovereignty forbid off-device transmission, the privacy check excludes remote cloud processing, and it stays excluded at the resource check however much compute it offers. If a network round-trip lower bound exceeds the deadline, additional remote compute cannot restore that path. The resource check asks whether a tier can run a model that still meets the task’s quality requirement, so a variant shrunk to fit a smaller tier fails it when its accuracy falls below that requirement. Cost compares only the survivors, and an empty set calls for changing the workload or partitioning it across tiers. Passing a lower-bound latency screen still requires complete-path validation. In automotive emergency braking, the latency check rules out options before compute capacity or cost are even considered.

**Example 2.1: Autonomous vehicle emergency braking**

**Scenario**: An autonomous vehicle vision system performs real-time pedestrian detection for emergency braking under a strict 100 ms end-to-end latency budget.

**Diagnosis**: At 100 km/h, the vehicle covers 2.8 m during the 100 ms budget, so every millisecond of delay is distance traveled before braking begins. The scenario assumes 50–150 ms of network round-trip delay for cloud offload, which consumes half the budget at best and more than all of it at worst before any detection runs. Local object detection on an automotive edge accelerator takes 10–30 ms; at 10 GFLOPs per frame and 30 FPS, that accelerator must sustain 300 GFLOP/s.

**Systems lesson**: The stated network-delay range cannot guarantee the deadline, so the latency check excludes that cloud path. The local candidates remain subject to the resource check: the pedestrian detector must fit memory and sustain the stated frame rate within its power budget. An automotive edge accelerator remains a candidate under these assumptions; its complete sensing-to-braking path still needs validation. Cloud connections can support offline training and telemetry without reentering the emergency decision path.

^exmp-ml-systems-autonomous-vehicle-emergency-braking

A completed placement decision needs evidence about the surviving paths as well as grounds for rejecting the others. On a factory inspection line, the four checks first eliminate remote and underpowered tiers and then select between two feasible edge designs on matched lifecycle cost.

**Example 2.2: Completing a placement decision**

**Problem**: A fixed factory camera requires 20 FPS inspection with a 100 ms sensing-to-decision deadline. Raw images must remain on premises. Which feasible deployment has the lowest matched lifecycle cost under the following hypothetical evidence?

**Supplied evidence**: A rule-based baseline fails the specified missed-defect criterion, while the learned detector meets both the missed-defect and false-alarm criteria on representative inspection data. The proposed mobile and microcontroller variants fail that same quality requirement, which removes them at the resource check. Local edge candidates A and B preserve the validated detector and preprocessing. Both have a complete peak working set of 2 GB within an available 4 GB, and each must stay within a 25 W sustained system-power budget.

Under the specified peak load and thermal conditions, the supplied complete-path latency bounds are 80 ms for A and 40 ms for B, including acquisition, preprocessing, queuing, inference, and the inspection decision. Their sustained rates are 25 FPS and 40 FPS, at 10 W and 20 W. Both satisfy the same availability and recovery requirements. Annualized budgets over the same service lifetime, including hardware, power, installation, maintenance, and recovery provisions, are \$2,400 for A and \$3,600 for B. These supplied bounds and budgets define an instructional scenario, not benchmarks of named products.

**Result**: Select local edge candidate A. Privacy excludes raw-image cloud offload, task quality excludes the proposed smaller variants, and both edge paths satisfy the timing, throughput, memory, and power requirements. A has the lower matched cost. B’s faster response supplies no required benefit under this specification; a tighter deadline could change the choice.

**Systems lesson**: The final choice follows from task quality and complete-path evidence, not the tier label or peak compute rate. The decision remains valid within the supplied operating conditions; changes to the input stream, detector, load, or thermal environment require reevaluating the relevant checks.

^exmp-ml-systems-completed-placement

The completed comparison depends on operational maintainability as well as execution performance. Its matched budgets include obligations that a hardware-only comparison would omit. Deploying a model across thousands of edge nodes or millions of microcontrollers introduces sustained lifecycle overhead: automated firmware updates, out-of-band telemetry, distributed drift monitoring, and hardware failure recovery. An organization equipped for cloud operations may lack the embedded toolchains and physical testing infrastructure required to support edge or TinyML targets at scale.

**Checkpoint 2.2: System design**

First justify the learned system against simpler baselines; then screen its deployment candidates against the physical and operational constraints.

**Decision checks**

**The baseline**: Can you explain why a learned system must first beat a simpler rule-based baseline, and what its complexity tax adds to the cost of deploying it?

**Physical feasibility**: If a remote path’s propagation floor exceeds the service deadline, can you reject it and identify which local execution and memory measurements are still needed before choosing an alternative?

**The infrastructure cost**: What evidence about error consequences and engineering cost is needed to decide whether a five-point accuracy gain (90 to 95 percent) justifies a hypothetical 40× increase in monthly maintenance hours?

The decision framework screens candidate tiers for physical feasibility and then compares their operational costs. If no single tier meets the requirements of the whole pipeline, the next question is which stages can run separately, and what must cross the boundaries between them.
