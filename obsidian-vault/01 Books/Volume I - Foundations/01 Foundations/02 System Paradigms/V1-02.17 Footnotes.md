# V1-02.17 Footnotes

[[Home|Home]] · [[01 Books/Volume I - Foundations/Volume 1 Overview|Volume Overview]] · [[01 Books/Volume I - Foundations/01 Foundations/02 System Paradigms/V1-02 System Paradigms|V1-02 System Paradigms]]

^footnotes

<sup>1</sup> **Deployment paradigm**: A distinct operating regime defined by latency, power, memory, and connectivity constraints. The Cloud-to-TinyML spectrum changes which resources limit the system; optimizing within an infeasible regime cannot satisfy the application’s deployment budget. Raising a cloud model’s throughput by 5 percent, for example, cannot rescue an application whose 10 ms latency budget the network round trip alone exceeds.

^fn1

<sup>2</sup> **Latency**: The time between issuing a request and receiving a result, corresponding to ${T}$ in the iron law, where propagation delay enters through the fixed-overhead term ${L_{\text{lat}}}$. The light barrier makes that propagation floor irreducible, as [[01 Books/Volume I - Foundations/01 Foundations/02 System Paradigms/V1-02.03 Physical Constraints on Deployment#^eq-latency-physics|equation 1]] quantifies. Every millisecond consumed by distance is a millisecond unavailable for model inference, which is why the light barrier forces paradigm selection rather than mere optimization.

^fn2

<sup>3</sup> **Dennard scaling**: Named after Robert Dennard, whose IBM team described MOSFET scaling relationships under which shrinking devices could reduce voltage and current while keeping power density approximately controlled ([[01 Books/Volume I - Foundations/01 Foundations/02 System Paradigms/V1-02.16 Source References#^ref-dennard1974|Dennard et al. 1974]]). As voltage scaling slowed and energy became a first-order architectural constraint, performance growth shifted toward parallelism and specialization: multi-core processors, GPUs, and TPUs ([[01 Books/Volume I - Foundations/01 Foundations/02 System Paradigms/V1-02.16 Source References#^ref-hennessy2019|Hennessy and Patterson 2019]]; [[01 Books/Volume I - Foundations/01 Foundations/02 System Paradigms/V1-02.16 Source References#^ref-esmaeilzadeh2011|Esmaeilzadeh et al. 2011]]).

^fn3

<sup>4</sup> **Tensor processing unit (TPU)**: An application-specific integrated circuit (ASIC) designed for operations common in ML, especially matrix multiplication. Specialization trades general-purpose flexibility for workload-dependent efficiency; its advantage over a GPU depends on the model, numerical format, and systems being compared ([[01 Books/Volume I - Foundations/01 Foundations/02 System Paradigms/V1-02.16 Source References#^ref-jouppi2023|Jouppi et al. 2023]]).

^fn4

<sup>5</sup> **Memory bandwidth (the memory wall)**: The term “memory wall” comes from Wulf and McKee ([[01 Books/Volume I - Foundations/01 Foundations/02 System Paradigms/V1-02.16 Source References#^ref-wulf1995|1995]]), who predicted that the processor-memory performance gap would eventually dominate system performance, a prediction borne out for ML workloads in which weight or activation movement can limit performance. Whether bandwidth limits a given workload depends on arithmetic intensity, batch size, data reuse, access pattern, and hardware balance rather than model size alone.

^fn5

<sup>6</sup> **Workload archetype**: A classification of ML workloads by their dominant bottleneck rather than by their model family. Misidentifying the archetype wastes optimization effort on the wrong term of the iron law, as when teams add accelerator FLOP/s to a bandwidth-bound inference pipeline and obtain little improvement while memory transfer remains dominant.

^fn6

<sup>7</sup> **Deep learning recommendation model (DLRM)**: Meta’s 2019 architecture that exemplifies the capacity-bound archetype. At that scale, memory capacity and communication ${\text{BW}}$ can constrain execution more than raw ${R_{\text{peak}}}$. These demands motivate specialized cluster designs where memory, not arithmetic, is the scarce resource.

^fn7

<sup>8</sup> **Critical path**: The longest sequential chain of dependent operations in a pipeline. The latency rule stated in the text is strict: if a 200 ms cross-region network call appears anywhere on the critical path, a system with a 100 ms total budget is guaranteed to fail regardless of how fast every other stage runs. Model inference need not be the longest stage; preprocessing and postprocessing can dominate, making the critical path longer than model execution alone suggests.

^fn8

<sup>9</sup> **Neural processing unit (NPU)**: A dedicated hardware block on a mobile system-on-chip whose circuits are designed for low-precision tensor operations. This specialization can improve energy efficiency relative to CPU execution, helping AI workloads fit within mobile power budgets.

^fn9

<sup>10</sup> **ML hardware cost spectrum**: The million-fold cost range makes deployment paradigm selection an economics decision as well as a physics decision. The same accuracy target may be achievable on a low-cost microcontroller only after substantial model reduction, or on an expensive data-center accelerator with a much larger resource budget, and the two designs carry different latency, power, and operational cost profiles.

^fn10

<sup>11</sup> **Power usage effectiveness (PUE)**: Total facility energy divided by IT equipment energy over the same period ([[01 Books/Volume I - Foundations/01 Foundations/02 System Paradigms/V1-02.16 Source References#^ref-thegreengrid2007pue|The Green Grid 2007]]), developed in **[[01 Books/Volume I - Foundations/03 Optimization/12 Benchmarking/V1-12.08 Power and Energy Measurement#^sec-benchmarking-power-measurement-techniques-bcc2|Power and Energy Measurement]]**. It captures facility overhead, including cooling and power distribution. Even a PUE of 1.06 leaves substantial noncompute power at data-center scale. TinyML does not require dedicated facility cooling, although sensing, communication, conversion losses, and sleep power still contribute to its complete system-energy budget. These device-level costs sit outside PUE.

^fn11

<sup>12</sup> **Cloud as utility computing**: A provisioning model in which a provider owns and operates a specialized hardware portfolio that few organizations could economically maintain themselves and rents it on demand. Renting gives direct access to the architecture each workload archetype requires, such as dense accelerator pods for compute-bound workloads, HBM-equipped nodes for bandwidth-bound workloads, and high-memory systems with fast interconnects for capacity-bound workloads, without purchasing and maintaining the entire installation.

^fn12

<sup>13</sup> **Industrial IoT (IIoT)**: A domain where latency constraints can be set by physical safety rather than user perception. Keeping sensing and the stop decision local removes the long-haul network term but leaves the edge system bounded by its local compute capacity $({R_{\text{peak}}})$.

^fn13

<sup>14</sup> **Internet of things (IoT) data wall**: IoT deployments depend on continuous sensor streams from devices distributed across homes, factories, farms, vehicles, and infrastructure ([[01 Books/Volume I - Foundations/01 Foundations/02 System Paradigms/V1-02.16 Source References#^ref-mckinsey2021iot|McKinsey Global Institute 2021]]). In many of those settings, the aggregate ${D_{\text{vol}}}$ from raw streams overwhelms the available uplink budget or latency budget for centralized ingestion, making local edge processing an architectural requirement rather than merely a cost optimization.

^fn14

<sup>15</sup> **Embedded edge constraints**: Across representative edge envelopes, 1–8 GB can hold roughly 1–8 billion INT8 weights before activations, runtime state, and other buffers, so the memory budget, not a fixed parameter count, sets the ceiling. Sustainable compute is further limited by power, cooling, and duty cycle.

^fn15

<sup>16</sup> **Edge fleet coordination**: Managing thousands of distributed edge devices amplifies coordination problems: intermittent connectivity causes model version drift, hardware heterogeneity requires per-target optimization, and limited remote access can make failed firmware rollbacks require a site visit.

^fn16

<sup>17</sup> **Checkout-free retail**: A store design that fuses camera observations with shelf events and transaction state locally, then transmits only selected events or aggregates. Those derived records still fall inside the privacy boundary.

^fn17

<sup>18</sup> **Industry 4.0**: The fourth industrial revolution integrates ML into the sensor-actuator feedback loop on factory floors. In an illustrative welding-robot scenario, defect detection samples at 60 Hz, giving a 16.7 ms sampling interval; if each response must finish before the next sample, the complete sensing-to-actuation path must fit that deadline. This completion requirement is a scenario assumption; the sampling rate alone does not establish the control deadline or guarantee safe operation.

^fn18

<sup>19</sup> **Predictive maintenance**: The use of sensor data such as vibration and temperature to estimate equipment condition and failure risk. Continuous monitoring makes sensing cadence, duty cycle, power, and local storage first-class constraints. Battery-powered monitoring nodes may require sub-watt average operation, whereas mains-powered industrial gateways can use a much larger envelope.

^fn19

<sup>20</sup> **Computational photography**: The use of ML algorithms (for example, multi-frame fusion and neural denoising) to overcome the physical limits of small mobile camera sensors.

^fn20

<sup>21</sup> **Mobile vision model reduction**: MobileNet-style architectures reduce the computation in common vision layers while preserving useful accuracy for mobile tasks. The systems point here is that mobile deployment often requires changing the model family, not merely moving the same model to a phone.

^fn21

<sup>22</sup> **System-on-chip (SoC)**: Mobile SoCs integrate CPU, GPU, and NPU cores with caches and memory controllers to reduce data-movement costs. Mobile DRAM is generally off-die, and feasible model size depends on capacity, precision, working memory, bandwidth, and latency.

^fn22

<sup>23</sup> **Face ID**: Apple performs facial matching within the Secure Enclave and encrypts the stored mathematical representations so that they remain available only to that subsystem and do not leave the device ([[01 Books/Volume I - Foundations/01 Foundations/02 System Paradigms/V1-02.16 Source References#^ref-apple2024facialmatching|Apple 2024a]], [[01 Books/Volume I - Foundations/01 Foundations/02 System Paradigms/V1-02.16 Source References#^ref-apple2024secureenclave|2024b]]). The example shows how local inference and hardware isolation can reduce transmission exposure while supporting low-latency authentication.

^fn23

<sup>24</sup> **The 1 mW threshold**: Ambient energy harvesting can power a device indefinitely only when long-term harvested power exceeds the complete system’s average load. Available power varies across sources and environments. Solar cells the size of a thumbnail may produce ~10 mW outdoors but ~10 µW indoors, thermoelectric generators on warm pipes ~100 µW, and RF energy from nearby transmitters ~10 µW. This crossover can transform the deployment model from “battery-limited lifetime” to “deploy and forget,” but it depends on the source, environment, and workload.

^fn24

<sup>25</sup> **Ubiquitous computing**: Mark Weiser’s ubiquitous-computing vision imagined computation woven into everyday environments until it receded from direct attention ([[01 Books/Volume I - Foundations/01 Foundations/02 System Paradigms/V1-02.16 Source References#^ref-weiser1991computer|Weiser 1991]]). TinyML is one modern path toward that vision, because it brings the cost and power of an intelligent sensor low enough for mass deployment.

^fn25

<sup>26</sup> **Microcontroller (MCU)**: A single-chip computer whose design prioritizes minimal cost and power over performance. Memory fit therefore becomes a deployment prerequisite, because the memory actually available to the target is often orders of magnitude less than a smartphone provides.

^fn26

<sup>27</sup> **TinyML energy gap**: The figure compares different tasks on different hardware and with different energy accounting boundaries; its ratios do not identify the effect of hardware integration alone. A batch-1 ResNet-50 inference on a cloud accelerator consumes about 75.5 mJ of accelerator energy, while an always-on keyword spotter uses about 10 µJ, a gap of ~7,500×. A cloud LLM query at about 1 kJ widens the gap to 100,000,000×, the span plotted in [[01 Books/Volume I - Foundations/01 Foundations/02 System Paradigms/V1-02.07 Edge ML Latency and Privacy#^fig-energy-per-inference|figure 7]].

^fn27

<sup>28</sup> **Coin-cell deployment**: A CR2032 battery (225 mAh at 3 V, ~675 mWh) ideally powers a complete 10–50 µW system for about 1.5–7.7 years. This “deploy-and-forget” operating model drives innovation in intermittent computing, where the device sleeps between inferences to stretch the energy budget across years of unattended operation.

^fn28

<sup>29</sup> **TinyML device range**: This physical range reflects a direct trade-off between deployment context and computational capability. Millimeter-scale systems prioritize minimal power for single-function, long-duration tasks, whereas palm-sized boards offer more sensing, interfaces, and debugging support at higher cost and power. Development-board size and power therefore should not be treated as limits for a finished TinyML product.

^fn29

<sup>30</sup> **On-device training constraints**: Updating a model requires working memory beyond that needed to apply it, including information retained from the prediction computation to determine parameter changes. Intermediate results can be discarded only after their final use, which often occurs later during training than during inference. With only 256 KB–2 MB RAM, many microcontrollers cannot hold the full training state; specialized methods reduce that state by limiting which parameters change. TinyTL, for example, updates only a small part of the model so that the update fits in microcontroller memory ([[01 Books/Volume I - Foundations/01 Foundations/02 System Paradigms/V1-02.16 Source References#^ref-cai2020tinytl|Cai et al. 2020]]). Training elsewhere and delivering an updated model is therefore a common design, although constrained local adaptation remains possible.

^fn30

<sup>31</sup> **TinyML ecosystem fragmentation**: TinyML spans microcontroller families such as Arm Cortex-M, RISC-V, and Xtensa, each with different instruction sets, memory layouts, kernels, and vendor toolchains; cloud and mobile stacks also vary, but their runtimes usually hide more of this hardware diversity. A model optimized for one microcontroller target may therefore require retuning and revalidation for another, multiplying the engineering cost of multi-device deployment.

^fn31

<sup>32</sup> **Always-on wake-word detection**: A small model that listens continuously for the acoustic signature of a wake phrase. The acceptable detector budget depends on the platform, but spending even a few extra milliwatts continuously can materially shorten battery life, and the architecture succeeds only when the always-on path consumes far less average power than the components it prevents from waking.

^fn32

<sup>33</sup> **Train-serve cost asymmetry**: Training usually requires many forward and backward passes, whereas one inference request uses a trained artifact for one or more forward passes. Training is not literally one-time because models may be retrained, and inference cost varies sharply with model size, sequence length, batching, and service overhead. The economic rationale for the split is amortization: repeated use can spread each training cycle’s fixed cost across many served predictions.

^fn33

<sup>34</sup> **Amdahl’s law**: Formalized by Amdahl ([[01 Books/Volume I - Foundations/01 Foundations/02 System Paradigms/V1-02.16 Source References#^ref-amdahl1967|1967]]) for multiprocessor scaling, this principle applies directly to ML deployment pipelines where the model is only one stage among many. Where ML is 30 to 50 percent of runtime, even a 100× model speedup yields only about 1.4–2× end-to-end improvement, because the rest of the pipeline is unchanged. Teams that benchmark model latency in isolation systematically overestimate deployment gains.

^fn34
