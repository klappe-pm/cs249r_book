# V1-02.09 TinyML Ubiquitous Sensing

[[Home|Home]] · [[01 Books/Volume I - Foundations/Volume 1 Overview|Volume Overview]] · [[01 Books/Volume I - Foundations/01 Foundations/02 System Paradigms/V1-02 System Paradigms|V1-02 System Paradigms]]

^sec-ml-systems-tinyml-ubiquitous-sensing-scale-a67b

Instrumenting every pallet in a warehouse, every cable on a suspension bridge, or every beehive in an apiary means putting “eyes and ears” on tens of thousands to millions of physical objects. At that count, each device must cost dollars rather than hundreds of dollars and measure millimeters rather than centimeters. Smartphones are far too expensive and too large for the role; ubiquitous sensing at this scale requires unit costs under ten dollars.

TinyML completes the deployment spectrum with low-cost, low-power ML on deeply constrained embedded devices ([[01 Books/Volume I - Foundations/01 Foundations/02 System Paradigms/V1-02.16 Source References#^ref-warden2020tinyml|Warden and Situnayake 2020]]; [[01 Books/Volume I - Foundations/01 Foundations/02 System Paradigms/V1-02.16 Source References#^ref-janapa-reddi2022-012788b4|Janapa Reddi et al. 2022]]). Devices costing under \$10 and averaging less than one milliwatt[[01 Books/Volume I - Foundations/01 Foundations/02 System Paradigms/V1-02.17 Footnotes#^fn24|²⁴]] of power when duty-cycled make ubiquitous[[01 Books/Volume I - Foundations/01 Foundations/02 System Paradigms/V1-02.17 Footnotes#^fn25|²⁵]] sensing economically practical at large scale. MLPerf Tiny and MCUNet show how such constraints are evaluated and optimized in practice ([[01 Books/Volume I - Foundations/01 Foundations/02 System Paradigms/V1-02.16 Source References#^ref-banbury2021mlperftiny|Banbury et al. 2021]]; [[01 Books/Volume I - Foundations/01 Foundations/02 System Paradigms/V1-02.16 Source References#^ref-lin2020mcunet|Lin et al. 2020]]). TinyML’s first feasibility screen is memory capacity, because weights must fit in flash and working state in SRAM, but once a model fits, power is the limit that shapes the design. This is the domain of the power-bound archetype, where the optimization objective shifts from maximizing throughput to minimizing energy per inference. With duty-cycled execution, a keyword spotting model consuming about 10 µJ per inference on dedicated low-power audio silicon can operate for years on a coin-cell battery, trading model capacity for operational longevity. That anchor is the best case; a general-purpose microcontroller running the same model spends orders of magnitude more per inference.

On an Arduino Nano 33 BLE Sense development board ([[01 Books/Volume I - Foundations/01 Foundations/02 System Paradigms/V1-02.09 TinyML Ubiquitous Sensing#^fig-TinyML-example|figure 9]]), on-board microphone, motion, and environmental sensors share a single substrate with a low-power microcontroller executing quantized TensorFlow Lite Micro models ([[01 Books/Volume I - Foundations/01 Foundations/02 System Paradigms/V1-02.16 Source References#^ref-david2021tensorflow|David et al. 2021]]). The board illustrates integration; its full power draw depends on the sensors, radio, and duty cycle.

![[90 Assets/Volume I/02 System Paradigms/Tiny ML b9b5ad7f2d.png]]

TinyML runs on microcontrollers (MCUs),[[01 Books/Volume I - Foundations/01 Foundations/02 System Paradigms/V1-02.17 Footnotes#^fn26|²⁶]] which omit the power-hungry infrastructure of conventional computing. Mobile and edge processors rely on external dynamic RAM (DRAM), high-speed memory buses, and operating system runtimes that draw milliwatts just to keep DRAM in self-refresh and hundreds of milliwatts once the memory bus is active. Many microcontrollers integrate the processor core, non-volatile flash, and static RAM (SRAM) onto a single silicon die, reducing reliance on external memory buses ([[01 Books/Volume I - Foundations/01 Foundations/02 System Paradigms/V1-02.16 Source References#^ref-banbury2021mlperftiny|Banbury et al. 2021]]; [[01 Books/Volume I - Foundations/01 Foundations/02 System Paradigms/V1-02.16 Source References#^ref-lin2020mcunet|Lin et al. 2020]]; [[01 Books/Volume I - Foundations/01 Foundations/02 System Paradigms/V1-02.16 Source References#^ref-janapa-reddi2022-012788b4|Janapa Reddi et al. 2022]]). Eliminating off-chip signaling lets duty-cycled average power fall below a milliwatt and sleep power reach microwatts, although active inference on boards such as the ESP32-S3 still draws tens to hundreds of milliwatts. Integration is typical rather than universal. Some targets, including ESP32-S3 variants, attach external flash or additional RAM and so pay part of that off-chip signaling cost again.

On-chip integration still imposes a strict internal-memory ceiling. On-chip SRAM is physically constrained by silicon die area: because each SRAM cell requires six transistors ($6\text{T}$), expanding capacity directly inflates manufacturing cost. A TinyML model must therefore execute within tens to hundreds of kilobytes of SRAM, with no virtual memory, paging, or swap space to cushion an overflow. Fitting the entire inference pipeline, from sensor acquisition to classification output, into this kilobyte-scale memory footprint defines the TinyML operating regime.

Energy is the other budget at this scale. Keyword spotting is a small task on low-power hardware, which places it at the bottom of [[01 Books/Volume I - Foundations/01 Foundations/02 System Paradigms/V1-02.07 Edge ML Latency and Privacy#^fig-energy-per-inference|figure 7]].[[01 Books/Volume I - Foundations/01 Foundations/02 System Paradigms/V1-02.17 Footnotes#^fn27|²⁷]] Its average power then depends on duty cycle, the fraction of time the device is awake running inference, and multi-year coin-cell operation requires the complete device load to fit the battery budget.[[01 Books/Volume I - Foundations/01 Foundations/02 System Paradigms/V1-02.17 Footnotes#^fn28|²⁸]]

**Definition 2.5: TinyML**

***TinyML*** is the machine learning domain of always-on sensing constrained by kilobyte-scale memory and milliwatt-scale power.

1.  **Significance**: It requires models whose weights and code fit in flash while activations and runtime state fit in on-chip SRAM, enabling continuous inference on milliwatt power budgets.
2.  **Distinction**: Unlike mobile ML, which uses multi-watt processors and a full OS, TinyML runs on microcontrollers using bare-metal firmware or a real-time operating system.
3.  **Common pitfall**: A frequent misconception is that TinyML is just “small models.” In reality, memory capacity decides whether a model fits at all, and once it fits, TinyML is a power-bound regime whose primary metric is energy per inference (microjoules), not just the parameter count.

This definition reframes the iron law of ML systems. In terms of [[01 Books/Volume I - Foundations/01 Foundations/02 System Paradigms/V1-02.04 Analyzing Workloads#^eq-bottleneck|equation 5]], TinyML imposes a physical memory-capacity precondition that execution time does not capture: the weight footprint ${M_{\text{weights}}}$ and activation footprint ${M_{\text{activations}}}$ must stay within memory capacity ${M_{\text{device}}}$. When total memory is measured in kilobytes, model structure and runtime allocation determine feasibility before latency optimization begins, and every avoidable data movement spends part of the energy budget. The objective therefore shifts from raw throughput toward energy per inference and memory fit.

The same four-branch map applies at the bottom of the spectrum ([[01 Books/Volume I - Foundations/01 Foundations/02 System Paradigms/V1-02.09 TinyML Ubiquitous Sensing#^fig-tiny-ml|figure 10]]). Milliwatt budgets and kilobyte memories favor low-precision execution and duty-cycled sleep, and they move the failure boundary away from latency toward compression-driven accuracy loss and risky firmware updates.

### TinyML Advantages and Operational Trade-Offs

^sec-ml-systems-tinyml-advantages-operational-tradeoffs-2d40

Stripping away the continuous radio uplink and the application processor is what lets TinyML devices shrink from palm-sized developer kits to millimeter-scale chips[[01 Books/Volume I - Foundations/01 Foundations/02 System Paradigms/V1-02.17 Footnotes#^fn29|²⁹]] that cost single-digit dollars and run for months or years on a coin-cell battery or harvested ambient energy. That same hardware austerity, however, removes the memory headroom required to train models on the device,[[01 Books/Volume I - Foundations/01 Foundations/02 System Paradigms/V1-02.17 Footnotes#^fn30|³⁰]] so models are trained elsewhere and deployed through specialized algorithms, aggressive model compression, and tight hardware-software co-design.

The trade-offs are just as distinctive. Microcontrollers commonly provide $10^5$ to $10^6$ bytes of RAM and enforce a strict two-tier memory partition. Static model weights and compiled code reside in non-volatile flash (often a few megabytes). Mutable activations and the runtime buffer (the *tensor arena*, a fixed block of SRAM reserved for them) must fit within the much smaller on-chip SRAM. That SRAM holds a few hundred kilobytes at most, such as the ESP32-S3’s 512 KB, and as little as tens of kilobytes on the smallest parts. As a result, peak intermediate activation volume, rather than static parameter storage, is frequently the barrier that prevents a model from executing. The smart doorbell’s level-1 check ([[01 Books/Volume I - Foundations/01 Foundations/02 System Paradigms/V1-02.04 Analyzing Workloads#^fig-doorbell-scorecard|figure 3]]) shows why that check is only a first screen: its weights fill just 11.9 percent of flash, a figure that says nothing about whether its intermediate results fit in SRAM. Embedded development also demands cross-stack co-design: engineers manage memory pools by hand, debug bare-metal firmware without operating-system abstractions, and optimize operator kernels directly for constrained instruction sets.

These bounds also create lifecycle and reliability challenges. An energy-harvesting device can satisfy its long-term average power budget yet fail when stored energy is exhausted before the next harvest. Its duty cycle must therefore respect both energy per inference and the timing of available power. Model quality can also suffer from aggressive quantization and pruning: converting 32-bit floating-point weights to 8-bit integers (INT8) saves memory but can degrade accuracy and robustness on out-of-distribution sensor inputs. Post-deployment flexibility is also sharply constrained, because models are often packaged with firmware and field updates must account for limited storage, interrupted transfers, and safe rollback. Ecosystem fragmentation[[01 Books/Volume I - Foundations/01 Foundations/02 System Paradigms/V1-02.17 Footnotes#^fn31|³¹]] across microcontroller vendors and ML frameworks creates additional overhead and portability challenges.

### Environmental and Health Monitoring

^sec-ml-systems-environmental-health-monitoring-14ad

In consumer devices, always-on wake-word detection uses that milliwatt envelope as a continuous low-power filter that wakes costlier application processors only when a trigger phrase is heard.[[01 Books/Volume I - Foundations/01 Foundations/02 System Paradigms/V1-02.17 Footnotes#^fn32|³²]] In environmental and health monitoring, the same local-filtering pattern governs sensors deployed where unit economics and physical isolation rule out higher tiers altogether. Across large agricultural fields, sparse and expensive connectivity is the first limit; FarmBeats used sensors, cameras, drones, and local gateway processing to reduce raw data movement in precision agriculture ([[01 Books/Volume I - Foundations/01 Foundations/02 System Paradigms/V1-02.16 Source References#^ref-vasisht2017farmbeats|Vasisht et al. 2017]]), and TinyML pushes that filtering down to individual sensing nodes running under milliwatt budgets.

Wildlife conservation shows the uplink arithmetic in an illustrative case. Solar-powered acoustic sensors identify species on-device and transmit periodic detection summaries rather than raw audio, cutting uplink data from 4.3 GB/day to 400 KB/day, a 10,750× reduction that conserves power in off-grid deployments. Medical wearables apply the same logic to physiological monitoring, where runtime remains bounded by sensor draw, radio power, duty cycle, and sleep energy rather than by the model alone.

All four paradigms sit on one continuum bounded by the speed of light, heat dissipation, and memory signaling, and a real feature usually presses against several of those bounds at once. Choosing among the paradigms therefore means mapping an application’s constraints against all of them together.
