# V1-02.03 Physical Constraints on Deployment

[[Home|Home]] · [[01 Books/Volume I - Foundations/Volume 1 Overview|Volume Overview]] · [[01 Books/Volume I - Foundations/01 Foundations/02 System Paradigms/V1-02 System Paradigms|V1-02 System Paradigms]]

^sec-ml-systems-deployment-spectrum-71be

A safety system with a 10 ms reaction budget cannot wait for a cross-country round trip, and a billion-parameter model cannot be squeezed into a microcontroller by better code alone. Both follow directly from the physical laws governing the speed of light, power thermodynamics, and memory signaling. The light barrier limits remote responsiveness, the power wall restricts sustained throughput, and the memory wall caps data transfer. Where a system runs therefore changes the terms of its silicon contract, and with them what the hardware can offer the model.

↩︎ A first sketch of the deployment spectrum and its three walls is established in **[[01 Books/Volume I - Foundations/00 Introduction/01 Introduction/V1-01.10 AI Engineering as a Discipline#^sec-introduction-deployment-spectrum-a38c|Deployment context shapes the lifecycle]]**.

### The Light Barrier

^the-light-barrier

Sending a sensor packet from California to a cloud data center in Virginia (~3,600 km straight-line) requires ~36 ms round-trip in optical fiber before a single floating-point operation begins, and real networks add routing, protocol, queuing, and processing delays on top. Any application that requires a sub-10 ms response *cannot* rely on distant cloud infrastructure, no matter how fast the remote accelerators are. The **light barrier** sets this absolute latency[[01 Books/Volume I - Foundations/01 Foundations/02 System Paradigms/V1-02.17 Footnotes#^fn2|²]] floor. Let $\text{Distance}$ denote the one-way path length and ${c_{\text{fiber}}}$ the propagation speed in optical fiber. [[01 Books/Volume I - Foundations/01 Foundations/02 System Paradigms/V1-02.03 Physical Constraints on Deployment#^eq-latency-physics|Equation 1]] gives the minimum round-trip time:

$$
{L_{\text{lat,min}}}= \frac{2 \times \text{Distance}}{{c_{\text{fiber}}}} \approx \frac{2 \times \text{Distance}}{200{,}000 \text{ km/s}} \tag{1}
$$

 For optical fiber, ${c_{\text{fiber}}}\approx 200{,}000$ km/s, roughly two-thirds of its vacuum value because light travels more slowly through glass than through a vacuum. This propagation floor directly motivates edge ML: when a tight latency budget rules out the round trip, computation must move closer to the data source.

^eq-latency-physics

### The Power Wall

^the-power-wall

Doubling a processor’s clock frequency to finish a model’s arithmetic in half the time does not merely double its power draw. Higher frequencies typically require higher supply voltages to switch transistors reliably, so dynamic power can jump roughly eightfold, and every extra watt becomes heat that the package and chassis must carry away. That thermal ceiling is the **power wall**, which limits how much computation can occur in a given volume. Dynamic CMOS power follows [[01 Books/Volume I - Foundations/01 Foundations/02 System Paradigms/V1-02.03 Physical Constraints on Deployment#^eq-power-scaling|equation 2]], where ${C}$ is capacitance, ${V}$ is supply voltage, and ${f}$ is clock frequency. Under the illustrative assumption that voltage rises proportionally with frequency (${V}\propto {f}$), dynamic power rises cubically ($\text{Power} \propto {f}^3$). The exact voltage-frequency relationship varies by device, but increasing both consumes thermal headroom rapidly.

$$
\text{Power} \propto {C}\times {V}^2 \times {f}\quad \text{where } {V}\propto {f}\implies \text{Power} \propto {f}^3 \tag{2}
$$

^eq-power-scaling

When broad voltage scaling broke down in the mid-2000s,[[01 Books/Volume I - Foundations/01 Foundations/02 System Paradigms/V1-02.17 Footnotes#^fn3|³]] that cubic penalty ended “free” frequency speedups and forced hardware designers toward parallelism (multi-core CPUs) and domain-specific specialization (GPUs and Tensor Processing Units (TPUs)[[01 Books/Volume I - Foundations/01 Foundations/02 System Paradigms/V1-02.17 Footnotes#^fn4|⁴]]). [[01 Books/Volume I - Foundations/01 Foundations/02 System Paradigms/V1-02.03 Physical Constraints on Deployment#^fig-microprocessor-trends|Figure 2]] records that turn across five decades of processors. From the mid-2000s to the early 2020s the largest chips gained about 190× more transistors, yet the fastest clock stayed near 4.7 GHz and single-thread performance rose only 5×, against 21× in the decade before. The added transistors went into more cores and specialized units rather than a faster single thread.

The same limit is tightest on mobile devices, which hit a thermal ceiling at 3–5 W; exceeding it causes “throttling,” where the device reduces performance to prevent overheating, a trace worked through in napkin math [2.9](https://mlsysbook.ai/vol1/02_system_paradigms/02_system_paradigms.html#nbk-ml-systems-thermal-wall). This physical limit gives rise to mobile ML: battery-powered devices cannot simply run cloud-scale models locally.

### The Memory Wall

^the-memory-wall

An accelerator’s matrix units can finish a block of multiplications in nanoseconds, yet spend most of an inference step sitting idle while weights and activations (the intermediate results of each layer) trickle in from off-chip DRAM. The **memory wall** ([[01 Books/Volume I - Foundations/01 Foundations/02 System Paradigms/V1-02.16 Source References#^ref-wulf1995|Wulf and McKee 1995]]) names this widening gap between processor speed and memory delivery, which shows up in ML chiefly as a bandwidth[[01 Books/Volume I - Foundations/01 Foundations/02 System Paradigms/V1-02.17 Footnotes#^fn5|⁵]] shortfall. A rough sketch with representative annual growth factors shows why the gap compounds:

$$
\frac{\text{Compute Growth Rate}}{\text{Memory Bandwidth Growth Rate}} \approx \frac{1.6}{1.2} \approx 1.33 \tag{3}
$$

^eq-memory-wall

In [[01 Books/Volume I - Foundations/01 Foundations/02 System Paradigms/V1-02.03 Physical Constraints on Deployment#^eq-memory-wall|equation 3]], the numerator and denominator are dimensionless annual growth factors. A ratio above 1 means compute capability is pulling away from memory bandwidth, so each hardware generation makes data movement a larger share of the performance problem unless the workload increases locality or arithmetic intensity (operations performed per byte moved).

![[90 Assets/Volume I/02 System Paradigms/System Paradigms Memory Wall Divergence 6b67d65787.svg]]

*Two diverging trend strokes: a steep red compute-growth curve pulls away from a shallow blue memory-bandwidth curve, with the widening gap shaded red.*

*Compute capacity outruns memory bandwidth; the widening gap is the memory wall.*

This illustrative trend comparison assumes that compute capacity doubles every 18 months, about 1.6× per year (the numerator of [[01 Books/Volume I - Foundations/01 Foundations/02 System Paradigms/V1-02.03 Physical Constraints on Deployment#^eq-memory-wall|equation 3]]), while memory bandwidth grows by ~20 percent annually (the denominator). These assumed rates illustrate the widening gap rather than a forecast for every hardware generation. Sustained for a decade, those rates leave compute about 16× further ahead of bandwidth than at the start.

This widening gap makes data movement a frequent bottleneck, in both time and energy, for ML workloads. An on-chip arithmetic logic unit (ALU) operates over short local connections, whereas an off-chip DRAM access also drives memory interfaces and longer interconnects. In representative hardware, fetching operands from DRAM can cost orders of magnitude more energy than the arithmetic performed on them ([[01 Books/Volume I - Foundations/01 Foundations/02 System Paradigms/V1-02.16 Source References#^ref-horowitz2014|Horowitz 2014]]). The ratio depends on the operation, numerical precision, memory technology, and amount of data transferred. The constraint affects all paradigms, and it takes a different form on TinyML devices, where capacity, not bandwidth, is usually exhausted first.

↪ The architectural responses to the memory wall, high-bandwidth memory (HBM) and on-chip SRAM hierarchies, are developed in **[[01 Books/Volume I - Foundations/03 Optimization/11 Hardware Acceleration/V1-11.06 Memory Systems#^sec-hardware-acceleration-memory-hierarchy-1839|Memory hierarchy]]**.

**Checkpoint 2.1: Physical constraints and deployment**

Deployment choices are governed by physics, not just preference. Check your understanding:

**Light barrier**: Can you use the cross-country propagation bound to explain why that distant cloud path cannot meet a $<10\text{ ms}$ safety deadline?

**Power wall**: Can you explain why a workload that exceeds a phone’s sustained heat-dissipation budget cannot maintain its initial performance?

**Memory wall**: Can you explain why data movement is often more expensive (in time and energy) than computation?

These physical laws explain the trade-offs behind the four paradigms. Physical limits decide which designs can work at all; privacy regulation, economic incentives, and data sovereignty requirements further restrict the choices within those limits. No regulation can make the speed of light faster, and no economic model can repeal thermodynamics. The walls also act together, and in practice an ML system is limited by whichever wall it reaches first, which is the wall on the resource the workload exhausts fastest relative to the tier’s supply. Evaluating these limits quantitatively requires analytical tools: the iron law to decompose execution time into its components, the bottleneck principle to identify the rate-limiting stage, and workload archetypes to classify the model’s resource demands.

**Self-Check: Question**

1.  A safety-critical autonomous braking system requires an end-to-end decision latency budget of 10 ms. A cloud data center is located 1,500 km away. Given that light travels through optical fiber at approximately $200{,}000\text{ km/s}$, why is offloading inference to this cloud data center physically impossible?

    1.  The cloud server queue latency always adds at least 25 ms regardless of server load.
    2.  The speed of light in fiber allows a round trip in 5 ms, but serialization of model weights requires 15 ms.
    3.  The minimum speed-of-light round-trip propagation time alone is 15 ms, consuming the entire 10 ms budget before compute begins.
    4.  Wireless transmission protocols require a minimum 50 ms handshake before transmitting sensor packets.

2.  Under dynamic CMOS power scaling where supply voltage scales proportionally with clock frequency ($V \propto f$), doubling the processor clock frequency increases dynamic power dissipation by a factor of 8 ($f^3$).

3.  How does the memory wall manifest in modern computing hardware according to the historical growth rates of compute capacity versus memory bandwidth?

    1.  Memory bandwidth doubles every 18 months while compute throughput increases by only 20% annually, causing compute starvation.
    2.  Both compute throughput and memory bandwidth grow at identical 60% annual rates, but memory capacity limits model size.
    3.  Processors have reached absolute thermal limits while memory signaling energy has dropped to near-zero levels.
    4.  Compute capacity historically doubles roughly every 18 months (~60% annually), whereas memory bandwidth grows by only ~20% annually, causing the compute-to-bandwidth gap to widen compounding over time.

4.  What causes thermal throttling on passively cooled mobile devices running sustained ML inference, and how does it affect continuous model execution?

5.  The historical scaling relationship under which shrinking MOSFET transistor dimensions allowed reducing voltage and current while keeping power density constant is known as \_\_\_\_ scaling.
