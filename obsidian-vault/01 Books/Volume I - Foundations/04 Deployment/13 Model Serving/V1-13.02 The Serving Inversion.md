# V1-13.02 The Serving Inversion

[[Home|Home]] · [[01 Books/Volume I - Foundations/Volume 1 Overview|Volume Overview]] · [[01 Books/Volume I - Foundations/04 Deployment/13 Model Serving/V1-13 Model Serving|V1-13 Model Serving]]

^sec-model-serving-serving-paradigm-9634

When a user uploads a photograph and waits for its label, or a chat interface sends a prompt and waits for the first word of the reply, the request arrives at a moment no scheduler chose and must return within a service-level objective (SLO) of tens to hundreds of milliseconds. Production **serving** answers such live clients, whereas the benchmarks of **[[01 Books/Volume I - Foundations/03 Optimization/12 Benchmarking/V1-12 Benchmarking#^sec-benchmarking|Benchmarking]]** measured models under a load generator that decided when each request arrived. Moving from offline training to live traffic therefore triggers the **serving inversion**, a reversal of engineering priorities. Offline training packs large batches to amortize weight transfers from high-bandwidth memory (HBM) and maximize work completed per hour, whereas online serving is judged by whether each individual request completes before its deadline while live traffic tests the operating envelope continuously.

That priority shift restructures the balance of terms in both forms of the iron law of ML systems, the D·A·M product of factors and the unpacked sum of time terms. Shrinking a task from a large training batch to small batches or individual inputs (${B}=1$) slashes the Data factor ($\text{Samples/Task}$ in **[[01 Books/Volume I - Foundations/00 Introduction/01 Introduction/V1-01.08 Iron Law of ML Systems#^eq-intro-dam-iron-law|equation 1.2]]**) and inflates the Machine factor ($\text{Seconds/FLOP}$), because a batch too small to keep the matrix units busy lowers hardware efficiency. The same shift appears across the unpacked terms of **[[01 Books/Volume I - Foundations/00 Introduction/01 Introduction/V1-01.08 Iron Law of ML Systems#^eq-intro-iron-law|equation 1.3]]** (${T} = \frac{{D_{\text{vol}}}}{{\text{BW}}} + \frac{{O}}{{R_{\text{peak}}} \cdot {\eta_{\text{hw}}}} + {L_{\text{lat}}}$). For weight-dominated operations, small batches reduce arithmetic intensity and can shift execution from compute-bound matrix multiplications toward memory-bandwidth-bound transfers (${D_{\text{vol}}}/{\text{BW}}$). A small batch therefore exposes fixed costs that a large batch shares, both the weight read and the per-kernel launch overhead, and which one dominates depends on the workload and the runtime. The two services that run through this chapter show both cases. Launch overhead dominates the ResNet-50 service when an eager framework issues its kernels, and the weight read dominates each decode step of the language-model service once the engine captures that step as one graph. Simultaneously, the latency term ${L_{\text{lat}}}$ expands beyond microsecond accelerator kernel launches to include network transit, request serialization, and host orchestration, often becoming the limiting constraint on end-to-end response time.

↩︎ The multiplicative, additive, and pipelined formulations of the iron law of ML systems are established in **[[01 Books/Volume I - Foundations/00 Introduction/01 Introduction/V1-01.08 Iron Law of ML Systems#^sec-introduction-iron-law-ml-systems-c32a|Iron Law of ML Systems]]**.

The inversion reshapes each D·A·M layer at once. In the data layer, the constraint shifts from total dataset volume to the arrival rate, payload freshness, and variable sequence dimensions of live queries. In the algorithm layer, execution runs forward only, so backpropagation state and optimizer memory vanish from the accelerator, even as variable input sizes make execution time differ from one request to the next. In the machine layer, the operating target shifts from maximum hardware utilization to **headroom**. Whereas offline training treats an idle accelerator as wasted capital, online serving treats near-saturation as an operational hazard, because stochastic request arrivals make queue lengths and waiting times diverge nonlinearly as utilization approaches 100 percent. Maintaining adequate headroom preserves the spare capacity needed to absorb transient traffic spikes without violating tail-latency percentiles, so serving optimizes completed requests under a strict latency deadline rather than steady-state hardware occupancy.

Meeting that latency deadline governs how engineering teams partition the end-to-end serving stack. Every stage between the network socket and the response draws on the same single allowance, so any millisecond consumed by unoptimized feature retrieval, host serialization, or queueing delays directly subtracts from the execution time available to the neural network itself. Consequently, the central engineering task in model serving is deciding which operations must remain on the synchronous request path, which tasks can be offloaded to asynchronous background pipelines, and how much headroom the system must reserve before tail-latency guarantees collapse under load. Three conditions therefore separate serving from the forward pass that a benchmark times: a per-request deadline, arrivals that no scheduler controls, and a stack in which the model is one stage among several.

**Definition 13.1: Model serving**

***Model serving*** is the operational phase that provides model predictions to end-users or downstream systems under latency, throughput, availability, and cost constraints.

1.  **Significance**: Online serving turns training’s priority on sustained hardware efficiency (${\eta_{\text{hw}}}$) into a request-latency constraint (${L_{\text{lat,total}}}$), requiring a stack designed around the percentile named by its SLO.
2.  **Distinction**: Unlike model training, which commonly processes scheduled batches, serving may handle stochastic online requests, synchronized streams, or offline batches.
3.  **Common pitfall**: A frequent misconception is that serving is “just the forward pass.” In reality, it is a systems problem, because model execution is only one component of a stack that may include request routing, load balancing, and data transformation.

The SLO[[01 Books/Volume I - Foundations/04 Deployment/13 Model Serving/V1-13.16 Footnotes#^fn1|¹]] turns those three conditions into a single time budget across the end-to-end request pipeline ([[01 Books/Volume I - Foundations/04 Deployment/13 Model Serving/V1-13.02 The Serving Inversion#^fig-serving-inference-pipeline|figure 1]]). Host-side ingress and input preprocessing (network termination, payload deserialization, and tokenization or feature transformation), neural network evaluation on the accelerator, and output postprocessing with safety validation all draw from that allowance. Any of the three stages can become the latency bottleneck, and [[01 Books/Volume I - Foundations/04 Deployment/13 Model Serving/V1-13.04 Request Lifecycle#^sec-model-serving-latency-budget-ef40|section 13.3.1]] shows that host preprocessing alone can take longer than the forward pass.

↩︎ SLOs, service level agreements (SLAs), and the tail-latency percentiles they constrain are established in **[[01 Books/Volume I - Foundations/03 Optimization/12 Benchmarking/V1-12.07 Inference Benchmarks#^sec-benchmarking-latency-tail-latency-5cde|Latency and tail latency]]**.

Two services carry the chapter, one per half, and each lighthouse arrives in the regime it represents. The first half follows a ResNet-50 image-classification service on one accelerator node. That node hosts one replica, a single running copy of the model and its server, and a service grows by adding replicas. ResNet-50 is the book’s compute-bound lighthouse. Each forward pass performs about 8.2 GFLOP of arithmetic on 102.2 MB of FP32 weights, so a large training batch keeps the matrix units busy. A served request arrives alone, however, and at batch one the same forward pass spends most of its time on kernel launches and other fixed per-kernel costs rather than arithmetic. Recovering the efficiency that training enjoyed without breaking the deadline is the first half’s problem, and the reference request profile introduced with the latency budget carries the service through queuing, batching, runtime selection, and replica startup.

Large language model (LLM) serving then follows a GPT-2 XL chat service, the bandwidth-bound lighthouse whose decoding step **[[01 Books/Volume I - Foundations/02 Development/06 Network Architectures/V1-06.08 Transformers Parallel Sequence Processing#^sec-network-architectures-inference-memory-bandwidth-wall-98d1|Autoregressive decoding]]** traced. Each generated token streams all 3 GB of its FP16 weights from memory, about 0.9 ms on the book’s reference H100, so memory bandwidth sets token latency and the memory each conversation holds sets concurrency. Llama 3 8B, a larger member of the same transformer family, extends that case where the per-conversation cache grows large enough to bound how many requests share the accelerator. DLRM, the capacity-bound lighthouse, appears once as a contrast in which embedding lookups rather than arithmetic dominate a request, and MobileNetV2 appears where the target device cannot hold ResNet-50 at all.

### Static Vs. Dynamic Inference

^sec-model-serving-static-vs-dynamic-inference-e864

A nightly job that precomputes product recommendations for existing users can pack thousands of items into each accelerator batch, whereas a live content-moderation API must score an uploaded image within milliseconds of arrival. Deciding whether to generate predictions before or during a user request sets the trade-off between offline hardware efficiency and online responsiveness ([[01 Books/Volume I - Foundations/04 Deployment/13 Model Serving/V1-13.15 Source References#^ref-google2024staticdynamic|Google 2024]]). It determines whether an accelerator runs in an unconstrained throughput regime or under the strict tail-latency deadlines of an interactive serving loop.

#### Static Inference

^sec-model-serving-static-inference-35f4

Static inference (also called offline or batch inference) precomputes predictions for anticipated inputs and stores them in a retrieval store. Moving model execution entirely out of the user request path alters the underlying hardware constraints. Because batch jobs have no millisecond-level deadlines, accelerators can use large batches (${B}\gg 1$) selected for throughput within memory limits. A large batch reuses each weight across many samples, which raises arithmetic intensity for weight-dominated operations, and a batch-size sweep identifies where throughput stops improving. When a client issues a request (for example, a user opening nightly generated recommendation rankings), the serving system performs a low-latency key-value lookup rather than executing a forward pass on an accelerator. The primary operational constraint shifts from accelerator latency to storage capacity and data freshness. Precomputing predictions across an entire input domain scales combinatorially ($\mathcal{O}({N_{\text{users}}}\times {N_{\text{items}}})$ for ${N_{\text{users}}}$ users and ${N_{\text{items}}}$ items in recommendation systems), and stale predictions persist until the next batch refresh.

#### Dynamic Inference

^sec-model-serving-dynamic-inference-d2d5

**Dynamic inference** (also called online or real-time inference) evaluates inputs on demand as requests arrive. On-demand execution accommodates arbitrary, previously unseen inputs within the model schema and immediately reflects newly deployed model weights without requiring precomputation. However, executing the model directly within the user request path subjects inference to the strict end-to-end latency budget set by its SLO. Because user requests arrive stochastically, the serving runtime cannot delay execution indefinitely to accumulate large batches. Serving requests individually or in small batches (${B}=1$ to ${B}=4$) can expose weight traffic or launch overhead that larger batches amortize. When these costs dominate execution, the device completes fewer queries per unit time, increasing provisioned cost per query. This tension between interactive response time and hardware efficiency can make dynamic serving more expensive per query than offline batching (example [[01 Books/Volume I - Foundations/04 Deployment/13 Model Serving/V1-13.02 The Serving Inversion#^exmp-cost-latency|13.1]]).

Latency constraints directly dictate infrastructure costs. **Systems insight**: Serving one image at a time saves only the difference between 3.8 ms and 3.5 ms of latency, yet multiplies the hardware cost per million queries by 7.5×. The batch-8 latency counts execution only; it leaves out the time the server spends waiting for eight requests to arrive, the formation delay that [[01 Books/Volume I - Foundations/04 Deployment/13 Model Serving/V1-13.06 Throughput Optimization#^sec-model-serving-batching-tax|section 13.5.2]] quantifies. Engineers must quantify whether that speedup generates enough business value to justify the 7.5× cost increase.

^exmp-cost-latency

Consider two contrasting deployment scenarios for a ResNet-50 image classifier. A static batch approach suits a photo management application that preclassifies every image in a user library overnight. Classifying 10,000 photos one at a time, at 3.5 ms per batch-one forward pass on the reference H100, would take 35 s. At the 89,500 images/s that MLPerf Inference ([[01 Books/Volume I - Foundations/04 Deployment/13 Model Serving/V1-13.15 Source References#^ref-reddi2020|Reddi et al. 2019]]) measured for a compiled INT8 engine at large batch on the same accelerator, the same library takes 0.11 s, about 320× faster. Clients then experience instantaneous retrieval upon browsing. Conversely, a dynamic online approach suits a content moderation service that must evaluate user-uploaded media in real time, where each image traverses the complete preprocessing, accelerator execution, and postprocessing pipeline within a 100 ms service-level objective.

These two modes optimize for different operating regimes. Static inference maximizes accelerator utilization and amortizes memory transfers across large, offline batches, trading persistent storage capacity for zero serving latency at query time. Dynamic inference minimizes tail latency under stochastic, concurrent traffic, accepting lower compute efficiency and higher marginal cost per query to evaluate arbitrary, unpredictable inputs. Production architectures frequently combine both paradigms in a tiered design. Static precomputation populates key-value caches with predictions for high-frequency queries, such as catalog items or viral media, while dynamic serving clusters evaluate the long tail of novel or personalized requests. Deciding when predictions are computed resolves only the temporal dimension of serving, however. Equally limiting is the spatial dimension, where the model physically executes, because physical placement dictates memory hierarchy limits, network boundaries, and the hardware budget available to the serving stack.

### The Spectrum of Serving Architectures

^sec-model-serving-spectrum-serving-architectures-8966

A cloud server can pool accelerators behind an HTTP endpoint at the cost of a network round trip and multi-second cold starts, whereas a hearing aid or industrial sensor microcontroller cannot afford a network hop or an operating system at all. Although “serving” often implies a networked server processing API requests, the four deployment paradigms (Cloud, Edge, Mobile, and TinyML) and the physical constraints that give rise to them—the light barrier, the power wall, and the memory wall—shape the serving stack into different forms.

Serving spans a continuum. At the cloud end, **centralized networked microservices** pool accelerators to optimize throughput and cost per query, at the expense of network round-trip overhead and cold-start latency. Further along, **application-embedded edge serving** runs models inside client processes via direct function calls, which eliminates network latency and preserves privacy at the expense of strict device memory and power limits. At the far end, **bare-metal firmware execution** runs on microcontrollers. The four deployment paradigms of **[[01 Books/Volume I - Foundations/01 Foundations/02 System Paradigms/V1-02.02 Four Deployment Regimes Across Nine Orders of Magnitude#^sec-ml-systems-deployment-paradigm-framework-0d25|Four Deployment Regimes Across Nine Orders of Magnitude]]** map onto these three architectures with some overlap. Cloud deployments and on-premises edge servers use networked serving, mobile apps and embedded edge devices run the model inside an application, and TinyML compiles it into firmware. Each tier’s memory and power limits stay in force at serving time, and serving adds a latency SLO and cost pressure on top of them, because a training job can run longer to absorb a limit, whereas a live request has a deadline. The same model may therefore require different serving strategies depending on where it runs.

↩︎ The light barrier, power wall, and memory wall that give rise to the four deployment paradigms are established in **[[01 Books/Volume I - Foundations/01 Foundations/02 System Paradigms/V1-02.03 Physical Constraints on Deployment#^sec-ml-systems-deployment-spectrum-71be|Physical Constraints on Deployment]]**.

The three architectures differ in what limits them. Networked serving runs the model as a standalone service behind HTTP or gRPC, so network transfer and serialization join model execution as candidate bottlenecks. Data-center accelerators support batching and concurrency, but a new replica’s cold start can stretch from seconds to minutes because container startup, model loading, and warmup sit outside the steady-state request path. Application-embedded serving runs the model inside the user’s application process on a phone or embedded board, behind a function call rather than a network interface. With no shared server to batch for, optimization targets energy and responsiveness (the pattern MLPerf calls SingleStream), and a passively cooled envelope of a few watts throttles sustained inference once the chassis heats. Bare-metal serving compiles the model into microcontroller firmware, often without an operating system or a dynamic allocator, so weights execute in place from flash memory and activations fit a preallocated tensor arena of a few hundred kilobytes of SRAM. Batching is impossible on a single sensor stream.

↩︎ The MLPerf SingleStream, MultiStream, Server, and Offline scenarios are established in **[[01 Books/Volume I - Foundations/03 Optimization/12 Benchmarking/V1-12.05 Benchmark Suites#^sec-benchmarking-mlperf-execution-scenarios-89f0|MLPerf execution scenarios]]**.

Embedded execution enables **zero-copy inference**. When data moves through a system, each copy consumes CPU cycles and memory bandwidth. In cloud serving, separate physical memory spaces force repeated transfers. A camera frame passes from a network socket buffer to user space, through a preprocessing buffer into page-locked host DRAM, and across PCIe into accelerator memory. A mobile pipeline can eliminate these intermediate copies when its camera, image signal processor (ISP), CPU, and NPU exchange shared buffer descriptors in unified system memory. In unified-memory system-on-chip (SoC) architectures, such as Apple A- and M-series processors or Qualcomm Snapdragon, all compute engines share a single physical DRAM pool. When software passes buffer handles rather than duplicating bytes, inference latency drops and memory bus power decreases, although format conversions may still require CPU or ISP intervention.

The deployment tiers differ first in their operating envelopes (latency targets, memory, and power), which **[[01 Books/Volume I - Foundations/01 Foundations/02 System Paradigms/V1-02.03 Physical Constraints on Deployment#^sec-ml-systems-deployment-spectrum-71be|Physical Constraints on Deployment]]** derived and tabulated for each paradigm. What serving adds is the model each tier can actually run. Applying those envelopes to one image-classification workload, [[01 Books/Volume I - Foundations/04 Deployment/13 Model Serving/V1-13.02 The Serving Inversion#^tbl-resnet-serving-spectrum|table 2]] shows why cloud and mobile can adapt ResNet-50 while TinyML must substitute MobileNetV2. The cloud column runs the model through TensorRT, NVIDIA’s ahead-of-time inference compiler,[[01 Books/Volume I - Foundations/04 Deployment/13 Model Serving/V1-13.16 Footnotes#^fn2|²]] and the mobile column through the TensorFlow Lite runtime. The chapter uses these engines as given until [[01 Books/Volume I - Foundations/04 Deployment/13 Model Serving/V1-13.07 Inference Runtime Selection#^sec-model-serving-inference-runtime-selection-5eef|section 13.6]] examines what such runtimes do to a model.

**Systems Perspective 13.1: ResNet-50 across the serving spectrum**

**Systems insight**: It is tempting to read [[01 Books/Volume I - Foundations/04 Deployment/13 Model Serving/V1-13.02 The Serving Inversion#^tbl-resnet-serving-spectrum|table 2]] as one model deployed three ways. That reading is misleading, because the cloud and mobile tiers share the ResNet-50 graph but use different precision and runtimes, while the TinyML tier cannot run ResNet-50 at all and substitutes an architecture designed for its constraints. Treating these as one model hides the work that makes each deployment possible.

Whichever tier hosts it, a networked deployment runs the model inside a server process, and each such replica must turn an irregular stream of requests into work its accelerator can execute efficiently. That conversion is the job of the inference server’s internal architecture.
