# V1-16.04 Thirteen Quantitative Principles

[[Home|Home]] · [[01 Books/Volume I - Foundations/Volume 1 Overview|Volume Overview]] · [[01 Books/Volume I - Foundations/04 Deployment/16 Conclusion/V1-16 Conclusion|V1-16 Conclusion]]

^sec-thirteen-quantitative-invariants-0dd2

Whether an engineer alters an embedding table layout in DLRM, doubles batch size in ResNet-50, or quantizes an attention layer from FP16 to INT8, the outcome follows from physical bounds, empirical trade-offs, and operational feedback rather than guesswork. Reasoning about those cross-layer effects before profiling is the job of the **thirteen quantitative principles** the book accumulated across its four parts ([[01 Books/Volume I - Foundations/04 Deployment/16 Conclusion/V1-16.04 Thirteen Quantitative Principles#^tbl-thirteen-principles|table 3]]).

The thirteen principles are not all the same kind of statement. Three are hard bounds (the Pareto frontier, the arithmetic intensity law, and Amdahl’s Law) and two are decompositions (the iron law and the energy-movement principle). Two are fitted models (statistical drift and bias feedback) and two are policies whose parameters a product decision sets (the verification gap and the latency budget). Four are heuristics (data-as-code, data gravity, the silicon contract, and training-serving skew). The silicon contract is a heuristic because it predicts which resource an architecture saturates first, a prediction that the arithmetic intensity bound then tests. The Pareto frontier, by contrast, is a bound because no feasible design lies beyond it. Each principle box on the part pages names its kind, and each principle is reliable only within its stated assumptions.

*Notation Guide for [[01 Books/Volume I - Foundations/04 Deployment/16 Conclusion/V1-16.04 Thirteen Quantitative Principles#^tbl-thirteen-principles|table 3]]*: The verification-gap row is stated in words; its formal version on the part page (principle [[01 Books/Volume I - Foundations/04 Deployment/V1 Deployment Principles#^pri-verification-gap|9]]) uses the task-specific distance metric ${d_{\text{task}}}$ and the acceptable error tolerance threshold ${\tau_{\text{tol}}}$. Finite tests estimate the deployment success probability with uncertainty; an evaluation must separately state the statistical confidence level of its interval or bound. ${q}$ is the target success probability for latency deadline ${T_{\text{deadline}}}$, and ${\alpha_{\text{fb}}}$ is the amplification factor compounding disparity ${\Delta_g}$ at step ${k}$. The drift row restates the degradation equation (**[[01 Books/Volume I - Foundations/00 Introduction/01 Introduction/V1-01.07 When ML Systems Fail Silently#^eq-degradation|equation 1.1]]**), in which ${\lambda}$ is the sensitivity fitted from labeled outcomes and ${\mathcal{D}(P_t \| P_0)}$ measures how far the current input distribution has moved from the training distribution. ${S_{\text{skew}}}$ is the expected absolute difference between serving-path and training-path outputs on the same input. In the iron-law row, ${D_{\text{vol}}}$ is the bytes moved, ${\text{BW}}$ the memory bandwidth, ${O}$ the operations performed, ${R_{\text{peak}}}$ the peak arithmetic rate, ${\eta_{\text{hw}}}$ the fraction of that peak achieved, and ${L_{\text{lat}}}$ the fixed overhead. Data transfer can overlap with computation, so the time for those two terms lies between their maximum (full overlap) and their sum (no overlap). In the arithmetic-intensity row, ${R_{\text{attain}}}$ is the attainable compute rate and ${I}$ the arithmetic intensity, the operations performed per byte moved. In the energy-movement row, ${E_{\text{total}}}$ is the total energy, ${E_{\text{move}}}$ the energy per byte moved, and ${E_{\text{compute}}}$ the energy per operation. In efficiency bounds, ${G}$ is the speedup factor applied to the parallelizable fraction ${f_{\text{parallel}}}$.

The thirteen principles are not independent axioms. The conservation-of-complexity heuristic[[01 Books/Volume I - Foundations/04 Deployment/16 Conclusion/V1-16.12 Footnotes#^fn1|¹]] introduced in Part I connects them into an integrated framework, because the complexity a decision displaces usually lands under another principle. The heuristic holds that simplifying one part of a system tends to move complexity to another part rather than remove it. In Part III, model compression demonstrated this displacement at the scale of a single model: pruning sacrifices capacity, quantization sacrifices precision, and distillation expends training compute. At the scale of the entire system the discipline is the same. After any local simplification, trace where the displaced complexity has migrated. The heuristic serves as a diagnostic rather than a physical conservation law. Accidental complexity, work the task never required such as an oversized model, can be removed outright by thoughtful design. Systemic complexity, which the task itself demands, often shifts elsewhere instead, as when quantizing a model moves it into validation.

The part pages introduced the principles in the order the book needed them. Read together, they sort more usefully by the three questions the introduction asked of every ML system (**[[01 Books/Volume I - Foundations/00 Introduction/01 Introduction/V1-01.13 Summary#^sec-introduction-summary-385d|Summary]]**): what shapes its predictions, what limits its execution, and what evidence supports its deployment. The three groupings that follow keep each principle’s number, so every principle can still be found on its part page. Only two principles move away from the others of their part. Data gravity (2) joins the execution principles because it decides where computation runs, and bias feedback (13) joins data-as-code because it changes the data the next model learns from.

### What Shapes Predictions (Principles 1 and 13)

^what-shapes-predictions-principles-1-and-13

A single change to a data-cleaning filter can alter what a model predicts without touching a line of model code, and an automated credit model can satisfy every throughput and convergence target while systematically compounding disparities against underserved groups across retraining cycles. The data-as-code principle ([[01 Books/Volume I - Foundations/01 Foundations/V1 Foundation Principles#^pri-data-as-code|1]]) explains the first behavior: because the training dataset dictates learned behavior, a modification to data curation is a behavioral change requiring the same rigorous versioning, code review, and regression testing applied to source code.

The bias feedback model (principle [[01 Books/Volume I - Foundations/04 Deployment/V1 Deployment Principles#^pri-bias-feedback|13]]) extends that dependency past launch, because a deployed model’s predictions shape the data its successor trains on. It treats disparity as a quantity that compounds across retraining cycles at a fitted rate, turning fairness into a stability question about the deployment loop that must be intercepted by disaggregated monitoring rather than discovered in aggregate accuracy. Once the data that shapes predictions is fixed, the next question is what physical bounds govern the work of training and running the model on that data.

↩︎ The bias feedback model, with the fitted factor that projects when a disparity that met its release threshold will exceed it, is established in **[[01 Books/Volume I - Foundations/04 Deployment/15 Responsible Engineering/V1-15.04 Responsible Engineering Across the Lifecycle#^sec-responsible-engineering-monitoring-incident-response-54f4|Operate: Fairness as loop stability]]**.

### What Limits Execution (Principles 2–8)

^what-limits-execution-principles-28

Moving a petabyte-scale dataset between cloud regions costs days of transfer time and substantial egress fees, which readily justifies moving compute to the data rather than the reverse. The data-gravity principle ([[01 Books/Volume I - Foundations/01 Foundations/V1 Foundation Principles#^pri-data-gravity|2]]) formalizes this placement constraint: where data volume resides dictates where computation can affordably execute.

↩︎ The transfer time and egress cost that make a large dataset the gravitational center of an architecture are established in **[[01 Books/Volume I - Foundations/01 Foundations/04 Data Engineering/V1-04.10 Physics of Data#^sec-data-engineering-data-gravity-adcb|Data gravity]]**.

The lighthouse models carry the same placement constraint into execution. In DLRM, embedding tables that outgrow a single accelerator’s memory are sharded across accelerators and memory tiers, and where each shard sits decides how many lookups must cross a slower link, so the execution system is engineered around where the parameters physically reside. Compute-to-data placement recurs across deployment environments precisely because moving large, low-reuse datasets saturates interconnects.

Once placement fixes where the data sits, the iron law (principle [[01 Books/Volume I - Foundations/02 Development/V1 Development Principles#^pri-iron-law|3]]) and the silicon contract (principle [[01 Books/Volume I - Foundations/02 Development/V1 Development Principles#^pri-silicon-contract|4]]) govern how efficiently the work executes there. The iron law’s multiplicative D·A·M form connects data scale ($\text{Samples/Task}$), algorithmic work ($\text{FLOPs/Sample}$), and machine pace ($\text{Seconds/FLOP}$), while its three-term physical decomposition isolates data movement, computation, and overhead. The two forms describe the same execution time, and the sum, which splits that time by what the machine waits on, is the form a bottleneck diagnosis uses. The silicon contract predicts which term limits performance on a given architecture-hardware pair. Measured shapes, kernels, and runtime conditions confirm which regime of [[01 Books/Volume I - Foundations/04 Deployment/16 Conclusion/V1-16.03 Lighthouse Models Constraint Propagation#^tbl-lighthouse-trace|table 1]] actually applies. Diagnosing whether an execution path is compute-bound, bandwidth-bound, or capacity-bound determines which optimizations yield speedup and which waste engineering effort. Execution time drops most when engineers target the limiting term directly rather than distributing effort uniformly.

↪ The bottleneck diagnostic that pairs each limiting iron-law term with the optimizations that work and those that are wasted is collected for reference in **[[05 Reference/Volume I - Foundations/V1 The D·A·M Taxonomy#^sec-appdx-dam-taxonomy-bottleneck-diagnostic-3fc9|Acting on the limiting term]]**.

The remaining four principles of the second question build on one another. The Pareto frontier (principle [[01 Books/Volume I - Foundations/03 Optimization/V1 Optimization Principles#^pri-pareto-frontier|5]]) identifies the designs that no alternative beats on every objective at once, so improving one objective along the frontier requires a trade-off against another within the same feasible design set. The arithmetic intensity law (principle [[01 Books/Volume I - Foundations/03 Optimization/V1 Optimization Principles#^pri-arithmetic-intensity|6]]) then diagnoses, for each candidate design on that frontier, whether compute or bandwidth caps the speed of its kernels on the target machine.

Moving data through memory costs energy as well as time. The energy-movement principle (principle [[01 Books/Volume I - Foundations/03 Optimization/V1 Optimization Principles#^pri-energy-movement|7]]) captures that cost by combining per-event costs with event counts. Under the reference energy constants, one DRAM access costs about 173–582× as much as one arithmetic operation. The DRAM-versus-FP32 ratio from hardware acceleration (**[[01 Books/Volume I - Foundations/03 Optimization/11 Hardware Acceleration/V1-11 Hardware Acceleration#^sec-hardware-acceleration|Hardware Acceleration]]**) sits at the lower end of that range, and the cheaper FP16 operation sets the upper end. Which cost dominates a whole workload still depends on how many of each event occur. Even after memory traffic and kernel execution are tuned, Amdahl’s Law (principle [[01 Books/Volume I - Foundations/03 Optimization/V1 Optimization Principles#^pri-amdahl|8]]) sets the ceiling on any parallelism gain, explaining why data loading and preprocessing can become bottlenecks in highly optimized systems.

↩︎ The compute-versus-bandwidth-boundary and hardware balance (${I_{\text{ridge}}}$) are established in **[[01 Books/Volume I - Foundations/03 Optimization/11 Hardware Acceleration/V1-11.07 Performance Bounds#^sec-hardware-acceleration-hardware-balance|Hardware ridge points]]**.

MobileNetV2 touches all four. Depthwise separable convolutions move the Pareto frontier, buying far fewer operations for a small loss in accuracy ([[01 Books/Volume I - Foundations/04 Deployment/16 Conclusion/V1-16.11 Source References#^ref-sandler2018|Sandler et al. 2018]]). INT8 quantization exploits the arithmetic intensity law by raising arithmetic intensity through reduced memory traffic ([[01 Books/Volume I - Foundations/04 Deployment/16 Conclusion/V1-16.11 Source References#^ref-jacob2018|Jacob et al. 2018]]). The energy it saves follows the energy-movement principle because fewer bytes move, provided the NPU executes the INT8 operators natively, while Amdahl’s Law bounds end-to-end speedup when host-side preprocessing remains unaccelerated. Microcontroller workloads such as KWS compress these trade-offs further, operating under sub-megabyte memories that leave little room for avoidable overhead. Satisfying every execution bound on the bench, however, does not guarantee that the model will behave reliably under live traffic.

↩︎ Compression moves a model along the accuracy-efficiency frontier, as established in **[[01 Books/Volume I - Foundations/03 Optimization/10 Model Compression/V1-10.06 Choosing and Combining Techniques#^sec-model-compression-balancing-tradeoffs-6ae3|The accuracy-efficiency frontier]]**.

### What Evidence Supports Deployment (Principles 9–12)

^what-evidence-supports-deployment-principles-912

A system that meets its throughput, memory, and accuracy targets on the bench can still fail silently in production when live inputs or serving pipelines diverge from the test harness. The verification gap (principle [[01 Books/Volume I - Foundations/04 Deployment/V1 Deployment Principles#^pri-verification-gap|9]]) names the distance between what finite tests can show and correctness on every future input. Because that gap never closes, the principle instead specifies the required success probability over a stated deployment population and error tolerance, and finite testing estimates whether the system meets that requirement at a stated statistical confidence level. Once the model is serving live traffic, the statistical drift diagnostic (principle [[01 Books/Volume I - Foundations/04 Deployment/V1 Deployment Principles#^pri-statistical-drift|10]]) detects when that deployment population has changed, and labeled outcomes measure how much the shift costs in accuracy. The training-serving skew diagnostic (principle [[01 Books/Volume I - Foundations/04 Deployment/V1 Deployment Principles#^pri-training-serving-skew|11]]) catches internal divergence when training and serving paths compute the same features differently. The latency budget (principle [[01 Books/Volume I - Foundations/04 Deployment/V1 Deployment Principles#^pri-latency-budget|12]]) holds the entire request path to the tail quantile the product selects.

↩︎ Reusing training-time transformation parameters at serving time, the consistency imperative that prevents skew, is established in **[[01 Books/Volume I - Foundations/01 Foundations/04 Data Engineering/V1-04.08 Data Processing#^sec-data-engineering-ensuring-trainingserving-consistency-c683|Training-serving consistency]]**.

Monitoring infrastructure, drift detection, feature stores, and disaggregated subgroup metrics exist to make these principles measurable and to trigger corrective action. A recommendation system that achieves high offline accuracy still requires parity checks when training-serving skew corrupts feature pipelines (principle [[01 Books/Volume I - Foundations/04 Deployment/V1 Deployment Principles#^pri-training-serving-skew|11]]) and outcome auditing when user behavior shifts seasonally (principle [[01 Books/Volume I - Foundations/04 Deployment/V1 Deployment Principles#^pri-statistical-drift|10]]).

↩︎ The monitoring infrastructure and drift quantification that surface these silent failures are established in **[[01 Books/Volume I - Foundations/04 Deployment/14 ML Operations/V1-14.06 Production Monitoring#^sec-ml-operations-production-monitoring-5e1b|Production Monitoring]]**.

### The Integrated Framework

^the-integrated-framework

Although the three questions group the thirteen principles by job, a single engineering decision activates principles across groups at once. Quantizing a model from FP16 to INT8 navigates the Pareto frontier (principle [[01 Books/Volume I - Foundations/03 Optimization/V1 Optimization Principles#^pri-pareto-frontier|5]]), trading numerical precision for memory traffic, and its consequences spread across the stack. Quantization alters the model’s silicon contract (principle [[01 Books/Volume I - Foundations/02 Development/V1 Development Principles#^pri-silicon-contract|4]]), shifting its operating point on the arithmetic intensity curve (principle [[01 Books/Volume I - Foundations/03 Optimization/V1 Optimization Principles#^pri-arithmetic-intensity|6]]) and transforming its energy profile (principle [[01 Books/Volume I - Foundations/03 Optimization/V1 Optimization Principles#^pri-energy-movement|7]]). In deployment, the latency budget (principle [[01 Books/Volume I - Foundations/04 Deployment/V1 Deployment Principles#^pri-latency-budget|12]]) dictates whether the resulting speedup meets the service-level objective (SLO), while the verification gap (principle [[01 Books/Volume I - Foundations/04 Deployment/V1 Deployment Principles#^pri-verification-gap|9]]) demands verifying that the compiled serving graph preserves the empirical accuracy measured during compression testing.

The opening fleet failure escaped at exactly this verification step. Compression testing accepted the quantized model on images from the evaluation pipeline, while the firmware path presented a different population. The training-serving skew diagnostic (principle [[01 Books/Volume I - Foundations/04 Deployment/V1 Deployment Principles#^pri-training-serving-skew|11]]), comparing model outputs from the two preprocessing paths on the same captured inputs, is the measurement that would have exposed the mismatch before the affected cohort did. In that comparison the evaluation pipeline, which produced the calibration images, plays the role the diagnostic assigns to the training path, and the firmware path plays the serving path. The verification gap names why the failure escaped; the skew diagnostic names how to catch it.

The trace touched seven of the thirteen principles, and a different decision would have touched a different set. Had the same model moved off the device to a cloud endpoint, data placement (principle [[01 Books/Volume I - Foundations/01 Foundations/V1 Foundation Principles#^pri-data-gravity|2]]) would have entered, because every input image would then cross the network. Had preprocessing stayed on the host CPU while the NPU ran the faster INT8 kernels, Amdahl’s Law (principle [[01 Books/Volume I - Foundations/03 Optimization/V1 Optimization Principles#^pri-amdahl|8]]) would have bounded how much of that speedup the request path could see. The principles activate in turn as a decision moves from model representation to hardware execution to production validation, so a deployment proposal can be checked against the same principles before anyone writes code.

**Checkpoint 16.2: Applying the principles**

A colleague proposes quantizing your model from FP16 to INT8 to reduce serving costs.

**Trace the principles**

**Pareto frontier** (principle [[01 Books/Volume I - Foundations/03 Optimization/V1 Optimization Principles#^pri-pareto-frontier|5]]): What accuracy are you trading for the bandwidth gain?

**Silicon contract** (principle [[01 Books/Volume I - Foundations/02 Development/V1 Development Principles#^pri-silicon-contract|4]]): Does the target hardware efficiently accelerate the required INT8 operators?

**Verification gap** (principle [[01 Books/Volume I - Foundations/04 Deployment/V1 Deployment Principles#^pri-verification-gap|9]]): Does the quantized serving artifact preserve the behavior accepted during compression testing?

**Training-serving skew** (principle [[01 Books/Volume I - Foundations/04 Deployment/V1 Deployment Principles#^pri-training-serving-skew|11]]): Do the calibration images pass through the same preprocessing path that every serving device uses?

**Latency budget** (principle [[01 Books/Volume I - Foundations/04 Deployment/V1 Deployment Principles#^pri-latency-budget|12]]): Does the speedup bring you within SLO, or create headroom for batching?

In the part order of [[01 Books/Volume I - Foundations/04 Deployment/16 Conclusion/V1-16.04 Thirteen Quantitative Principles#^tbl-thirteen-principles|table 3]], the principles form a cycle rather than a pipeline ([[01 Books/Volume I - Foundations/04 Deployment/16 Conclusion/V1-16.04 Thirteen Quantitative Principles#^fig-invariants-cycle|figure 1]]), a property that the three-question grouping hides. The deployment principles (9–13) turn runtime telemetry into evidence that sends work back to the data foundations, through remediations such as canary rollbacks, fallback heuristics, dataset augmentation, model retraining, or kernel retuning, so each production lesson becomes the starting condition of the next design pass. Bias feedback (13) belongs with them because its feedback factor can be fitted only from retraining cycles after the model is serving. The conservation-of-complexity heuristic sits at the center of that cycle because every pass around it asks where the complexity displaced by the previous decision has landed.
