# V1-15.05 Environmental and Cost Awareness

[[Home|Home]] · [[01 Books/Volume I - Foundations/Volume 1 Overview|Volume Overview]] · [[01 Books/Volume I - Foundations/04 Deployment/15 Responsible Engineering/V1-15 Responsible Engineering|V1-15 Responsible Engineering]]

^sec-responsible-engineering-environmental-cost-awareness-0f3e

In 2019, researchers estimated that development-scale training and architecture search for a large natural language processing (NLP) model could emit as much carbon as five cars over their entire lifetimes ([[01 Books/Volume I - Foundations/04 Deployment/15 Responsible Engineering/V1-15.10 Source References#^ref-strubell2019|Strubell et al. 2019]]). Later analysis showed that the most quoted architecture-search estimate was highly sensitive to proxy-task, hardware, data-center efficiency, and grid carbon-intensity assumptions ([[01 Books/Volume I - Foundations/04 Deployment/15 Responsible Engineering/V1-15.10 Source References#^ref-patterson2021carbon|Patterson et al. 2021]]). The correction narrows the estimate without removing the point. Training runs consume megawatt-hours of electricity, inference at scale multiplies per-request inefficiencies into measurable environmental impact, and resource-intensive models exclude organizations that lack large compute budgets. The techniques that improve efficiency therefore also serve as instruments of responsible engineering, tying computational efficiency to environmental sustainability and economic accessibility. On the DLRM ranking service, request volume makes serving rather than training the term that dominates both the bill and the emissions.

### Efficiency as Responsibility

^sec-responsible-engineering-efficiency-responsibility-fb99

Training a single large language model consumes thousands of GPU hours and energy measured in megawatt-hours. The bitter lesson predicts that this footprint will keep growing, because the methods that win are the ones that convert additional compute into capability. Much of this expense, however, is not intrinsic to the learning task but represents *accidental complexity*: training from scratch when fine-tuning would suffice, deploying larger architectures than tasks require, and running hyperparameter searches that explore redundant configurations. Computational cost depends on engineering choices as well as model physics. Green AI treats that efficiency as a primary metric rather than an afterthought.[[01 Books/Volume I - Foundations/04 Deployment/15 Responsible Engineering/V1-15.11 Footnotes#^fn13|¹³]]

↩︎ The bitter lesson, the historical pattern in which methods that scale with compute overtake hand-built knowledge, is established in **[[01 Books/Volume I - Foundations/00 Introduction/01 Introduction/V1-01.05 The Bitter Lesson#^sec-introduction-bitter-lesson-79c7|The Bitter Lesson]]**.

The growth in training compute carries a price, and the price decides who can take part. [[01 Books/Volume I - Foundations/04 Deployment/15 Responsible Engineering/V1-15.05 Environmental and Cost Awareness#^fig-frontier-training-cost|Figure 6]] plots the estimated compute cost of training notable models since 2016 in constant 2023 dollars. Across the models that Epoch AI flags as frontier at release, the cost of a training run rose about 2.9× per year, and the most expensive run in the data, Grok 4 in 2025, cost about \$388M ([[01 Books/Volume I - Foundations/04 Deployment/15 Responsible Engineering/V1-15.10 Source References#^ref-epochai2026notable|Epoch AI 2026]]). Cottier et al. ([[01 Books/Volume I - Foundations/04 Deployment/15 Responsible Engineering/V1-15.10 Source References#^ref-cottier2024rising|2024]]), using their own selection of frontier runs, estimate 2.4$\times$ per year. Either rate concentrates frontier training in the few organizations able to fund it, so efficiency is a question of access as well as of energy. For a frontier model the training run alone is a large cost; for a high-traffic service such as DLRM, as [[01 Books/Volume I - Foundations/04 Deployment/15 Responsible Engineering/V1-15.05 Environmental and Cost Awareness#^sec-responsible-engineering-total-cost-ownership-35c1|section 15.4.2]] shows, serving dominates.

At the hardware level, environmental impact scales with total electrical energy draw across the machine lifecycle, mediated by accelerator utilization, data-center cooling overhead (power usage effectiveness), and the **carbon intensity** of the local electrical grid, the mass of CO<sub>2</sub>eq emitted per kilowatt-hour it delivers. CO<sub>2</sub>eq (carbon dioxide equivalent) expresses every greenhouse gas as the mass of CO<sub>2</sub> with the same warming effect. Within the accelerator package, data movement can dominate energy dissipation. In the 45 nm reference behind the energy-movement principle ([[01 Books/Volume I - Foundations/03 Optimization/V1 Optimization Principles#^pri-energy-movement|7]]), a single off-chip DRAM access costs two to three orders of magnitude more energy than a 32-bit floating-point operation, although total energy also depends on access width, event counts, reuse, and the device. Algorithmic and runtime optimizations reduce environmental burden only when they reduce measured watt-hours for a verified quality target under production load, curbing this physical dissipation at the silicon level.

↩︎ The physical energy disparity between off-chip memory access and arithmetic execution is established in **[[01 Books/Volume I - Foundations/03 Optimization/11 Hardware Acceleration/V1-11.03 Hardware Specialization#^sec-hardware-acceleration-machine-learning-hardware-specialization-09c5|Machine learning hardware specialization]]**.

Physical resource demands also dictate deployment accessibility. Oversized models that need multi-accelerator nodes linked by high-bandwidth interconnects (such as NVLink) only to hold their weights in memory confine deployment to centralized cloud facilities. Compressing models so their working sets fit within commodity DRAM or the unified memory of edge processors opens up who can run them. Running locally removes the need for a constant high-bandwidth network link and recurring cloud API bills, letting client devices run low-latency, private inference right where data is captured.

At production scale, where serving systems process millions of requests daily, per-query inefficiencies compound rapidly across accelerator fleets. Under the iron law of ML systems (${T} = {D_{\text{vol}}} / {\text{BW}} + {O} / ({R_{\text{peak}}} \cdot {\eta_{\text{hw}}}) + {L_{\text{lat}}}$), per-query execution latency dictates the request throughput that an individual accelerator can sustain before violating service-level latency objectives. Reducing execution time allows a smaller provisioned cluster to support the same aggregate traffic, and capacity planning can turn that reduced service demand into lower provisioned-capacity costs. Energy savings are less certain. If the fleet keeps its size, faster queries save only the dynamic energy that accelerators draw while computing, because the idle and baseline power of every provisioned machine continues to flow. A valid comparison therefore measures total energy over the same traffic and quality target and distinguishes that result from savings on a capacity-based bill.

Each optimization developed earlier in this book attacks a specific term of the iron law, and each reduces environmental burden only under the conditions its own chapter states. Quantization and pruning shrink the bytes moved ${D_{\text{vol}}}$ and the operation count ${O}$ when supported kernels retain the smaller representation, and knowledge distillation moves a large model’s behavior into a smaller one (**[[01 Books/Volume I - Foundations/03 Optimization/10 Model Compression/V1-10 Model Compression#^sec-model-compression|Model Compression]]**). Selecting the data a model trains on removes training operations before they run (**[[01 Books/Volume I - Foundations/03 Optimization/09 Data Selection/V1-09 Data Selection#^sec-data-selection|Data Selection]]**), and specialized datapaths and fused kernels raise hardware efficiency ${\eta_{\text{hw}}}$ (**[[01 Books/Volume I - Foundations/03 Optimization/11 Hardware Acceleration/V1-11 Hardware Acceleration#^sec-hardware-acceleration|Hardware Acceleration]]**).

Responsible engineering treats these optimizations as design constraints established before training and deployment, rather than post-hoc remedies applied to an oversized model. System requirements specify explicit upper bounds on latency, memory capacity, and energy consumption alongside target predictive quality, and the task is to find the most compact architecture and execution strategy that satisfies them. In practice this begins by establishing bounded machine envelopes (thermal design power, battery capacity, and peak latency) before choosing or training an architecture. Targeted optimizations such as quantization or kernel fusion come only after the most compact adequate architecture has been selected.

Edge deployment makes these limits immediate, because passive thermal dissipation and battery capacity enforce power budgets that cannot be relaxed after the fact. A wearable with an illustrative 500 mW inference budget shows the consequence for model choice. Under the active-power profiles assumed here, the KWS lighthouse’s DS-CNN (200K parameters at 50 mW) leaves a 10× power margin, while MobileNetV2 (3.5M parameters at 1.2 W) exceeds the same budget by 2.4× before sustained thermals are considered.

Fitting the envelope is necessary but not sufficient. A keyword detector does not substitute for a vision model merely because it fits, and per-inference energy compounds into aggregate emissions and operating cost once the model is deployed at scale. Techniques that lower measured energy per inference reduce lifecycle energy only when their savings outweigh any added training, preprocessing, or other workload costs, while cloud and cluster financial savings depend directly on provisioning and hardware utilization.

↩︎ The power, memory, and latency envelopes that separate cloud, edge, mobile, and TinyML deployment are established in **[[01 Books/Volume I - Foundations/01 Foundations/02 System Paradigms/V1-02.03 Physical Constraints on Deployment#^sec-ml-systems-deployment-spectrum-71be|Physical Constraints on Deployment]]**.

### Total Cost of Ownership

^sec-responsible-engineering-total-cost-ownership-35c1

On the DLRM ranking service, a training cycle that costs \$3,200 is followed by roughly \$500,000 per year in serving compute. That contrast shows how total cost of ownership,[[01 Books/Volume I - Foundations/04 Deployment/15 Responsible Engineering/V1-15.11 Footnotes#^fn14|¹⁴]] the upfront and recurring expense of a system over its lifetime, can be dominated by recurring inference at high traffic. Unlike a cloud-versus-edge comparison, which uses that total to choose a deployment, the lifecycle view splits one deployment’s total into training cycles, recurring inference, and operations to find which term dominates. Other systems may be dominated by training, data, staffing, or idle capacity, so measurement determines where optimization should focus.

![[90 Assets/Volume I/15 Responsible Engr/Responsible Engr Tco Bar 92a8dc97f5.svg]]

*Horizontal stacked bar of three-year total cost of ownership: a thin gray training sliver on the left, a wide orange inference segment dominating the middle, and a gray operations segment on the right. Training is a sliver; inference is most of the total.*

*At production scale, recurring inference can dominate lifetime cost.*

↩︎ Total cost of ownership as a comparison of cloud and edge deployments, weighing capital against operating expense, is established in **[[01 Books/Volume I - Foundations/01 Foundations/02 System Paradigms/V1-02.06 Cloud ML Computational Power#^sec-ml-systems-cloud-ml-tradeoffs-constraints-96ed|Cloud ML trade-offs and constraints]]**.

Walking the DLRM service’s numbers for its 10 million daily users shows why those two terms sit so far apart. A single training cycle appears considerable on its own: data preparation consumes 100 GPU-hours at approximately \$4/hour (\$400), hyperparameter search across multiple configurations requires 500 GPU-hours (\$2,000), and the final training run uses 200 GPU-hours (\$800), for a cycle total of \$3,200.

Recurring inference dwarfs that figure because the 10 million daily users and 20 ranked items per user from the exposure-feedback analysis require 200 million inferences every day. Treating 10 milliseconds per inference as unbatched, non-overlapped dedicated GPU service demand yields approximately 23.1 GPUs running continuously. At \$2.50/GPU-hour, the scenario’s annual GPU cost reaches \$506,944. That rate sits below the \$4/hour the training runs pay because an always-on fleet is bought as continuously reserved capacity rather than on demand.

Over a three-year operational period, twelve quarterly training cycles, the first included, produce training costs of approximately \$38,400, while inference costs over the same period total \$1.5M. The resulting 40:1 ratio is specific to these assumptions, but it dictates where optimization effort yields the highest return: inference latency and serving efficiency.

↩︎ Cost per inference, the serving cost that capacity planning divides among requests, is established in **[[01 Books/Volume I - Foundations/04 Deployment/13 Model Serving/V1-13.11 Economics and Planning#^sec-model-serving-cost-per-inference-27fc|Cost per inference across hardware]]**.

Per-query optimization becomes important when serving billions of requests. Shaving a fifth off the per-query accelerator service demand can reduce required hardware when other workload assumptions remain fixed. Hardware selection among CPUs, GPUs, and Tensor Processing Units changes costs and carbon footprint in workload- and deployment-dependent ways. Model compression through quantization and pruning can reduce high-volume inference costs when it lowers measured service demand without unacceptable quality loss.

The third term of the lifecycle total cost of ownership (TCO) is operations: monitoring, maintenance, retraining, and incident response, all of which scale with system complexity and with the rate of distribution shift in the application domain.

Every one of these terms draws on resources that, once consumed by one ML system, cannot be used for other purposes, so wasteful consumption in one project constrains what other projects can attempt. Engineers should therefore evaluate return on investment (ROI), asking whether the value an ML system delivers justifies its resource consumption. A recommendation system that increases engagement by 1 percent might not justify millions of dollars in computational costs, while a medical diagnosis system that saves lives does. Deployment-to-training cost ratios vary with traffic, utilization, hardware, update cadence, staffing, and incident burden, so the right model choice depends on measured lifecycle cost and task value, not on a fixed ratio.

#### Accounting for Cost and Carbon

^sec-responsible-engineering-tco-calculation-methodology-7cb0

Converting the same scenario into carbon requires measured or estimated energy use, facility overhead, and applicable grid carbon intensity. The accounting keeps the walk-through’s traffic, service demand, and training budget, adds the operations work that the walk-through left unquantified, and converts every accelerator-hour into emissions with one conversion factor. Training recurs with each retraining cycle, inference recurs with every request, and operations accumulate through telemetry, on-call engineering, and incident response, so each enters the three-year total on its own basis. On-call engineering is counted in full-time equivalents (FTE), where one FTE is one engineer working full time.

**Napkin Math 15.4: The carbon cost of compute**

**Problem**: The cost accounting must convert GPU-only electrical energy into “kg CO<sub>2</sub>eq.” What carbon factor does one GPU-hour carry under the scenario baseline, excluding host, facility, and embodied impacts?

**Variables**:

- **Power**: 400 W per GPU (scenario baseline).
- **Intensity**: 0.4 kg/kWh CO<sub>2</sub>eq (rounded grid baseline).

**Math**: [[01 Books/Volume I - Foundations/04 Deployment/15 Responsible Engineering/V1-15.05 Environmental and Cost Awareness#^eq-carbon-footprint|Equation 2]] captures the standard conversion:

$$
\text{Carbon} = \text{Energy (kWh)} \times \text{Carbon Intensity (kg/kWh)} \tag{2}
$$

 Applying the baseline assumptions:

$$
\begin{gather*} \left(\text{0.4 kW} \times \text{1 hour}\right) \times \text{0.4 kg/kWh} = \text{0.16 kg CO}_2\text{eq per GPU-hour} \end{gather*}
$$

 **Systems insight**: This GPU-only conversion factor lets the accounting track a modeled accelerator-carbon subtotal alongside dollar cost. Whole-system and facility emissions require incorporating the host, facility overhead, and embodied energy boundaries.

^eq-carbon-footprint

In [[01 Books/Volume I - Foundations/04 Deployment/15 Responsible Engineering/V1-15.05 Environmental and Cost Awareness#^tbl-tco-summary|table 10]], inference accounts for 73.5 percent of the three-year cost, operations for 24.6 percent, and training for only 1.9 percent. Those proportions turn efficiency from a tuning preference into a responsibility check, because small serving-path reductions compound across every request the fleet handles. The same split decides which reduction saves more. Inference costs 40 times as much as training over the three years, so a 20 percent cut in per-query serving demand saves far more than a 50 percent cut in total training cost. Shortening only the final run would not deliver that training cut, because data preparation and hyperparameter search are untouched.

**Checkpoint 15.4: Efficiency as responsibility**

Total cost of ownership reveals where responsible optimization has the most leverage.

**Inference dominance**: under this scenario’s assumptions, can you compare a 20 percent reduction in total inference cost with a 50 percent reduction in total training cost, and explain why reducing only final-run time does not establish the latter saving?

**Carbon accounting**: can you convert GPU-hours into kg CO<sub>2</sub>eq using power draw and carbon intensity, and explain how workload design, hardware, scheduling, and region affect the result?

**Sufficiency test**: for a given ML system, can you justify that the model size is appropriate for the task, or identify where a simpler model would deliver comparable accuracy at a fraction of the cost?

### Environmental Impact

^sec-responsible-engineering-environmental-impact-0292

Two data centers can run the exact same DLRM serving fleet at the same dollar bill while one emits several times more carbon than the other because its local power grid burns coal instead of hydroelectricity. A team that replaces working servers early to save 10 percent on electricity can raise net lifetime emissions once the carbon spent manufacturing the new silicon is counted ([[01 Books/Volume I - Foundations/04 Deployment/15 Responsible Engineering/V1-15.10 Source References#^ref-gupta2022chasing|Gupta et al. 2022]]). The TCO accounting in [[01 Books/Volume I - Foundations/04 Deployment/15 Responsible Engineering/V1-15.05 Environmental and Cost Awareness#^sec-responsible-engineering-total-cost-ownership-35c1|section 15.4.2]] paired billed compute dollars with a GPU-only carbon subtotal under one baseline grid, but full environmental accounting must include physical overheads that no cloud invoice itemizes.

Operational emissions depend on whole-system energy, facility power usage effectiveness (PUE), and regional grid carbon intensity ($\text{gCO}_2\text{e}/\text{kWh}$), while embodied manufacturing impacts require separate accounting. In power benchmarking, a measurement boundary separates server-plug energy from facility overhead, and PUE, total facility energy divided by IT-equipment energy, is the ratio across that boundary. In carbon accounting, the same ratio becomes the multiplier that converts measured IT energy into the energy the facility actually draws. The same boundary keeps server fans, which cool the chips but draw their power inside the server, in the IT term so that their energy is not counted twice.

↩︎ The power measurement boundary that separates server-plug energy from facility overhead is established in **[[01 Books/Volume I - Foundations/03 Optimization/12 Benchmarking/V1-12.08 Power and Energy Measurement#^sec-benchmarking-power-measurement-boundaries-982c|Power measurement boundaries]]**.

A first-order operational emissions estimate is $\text{Energy}_{\text{hardware}} \times \text{PUE} \times \text{Carbon Intensity}$, where the hardware term covers the full IT-equipment boundary over the accounting window, not GPU-only energy. Operational carbon includes electricity consumed by both active work and idle provisioned equipment. In contrast, embodied carbon encompasses the greenhouse gases emitted across the hardware supply chain (silicon wafer fabrication, packaging, transport, and facility construction) before a chip executes its first cycle. Embodied carbon can account for a large share of lifetime emissions, and on low-carbon grids a majority ([[01 Books/Volume I - Foundations/04 Deployment/15 Responsible Engineering/V1-15.10 Source References#^ref-gupta2022chasing|Gupta et al. 2022]]), so prematurely replacing older servers for slight operational efficiency gains can paradoxically increase net lifetime emissions.

Cost, energy, and emissions are also distinct measurements. An optimization that reduces TCO may not reduce energy or emissions if it changes utilization, hardware, or workload placement. Data-center electricity use makes workload design, hardware, cloud region, and timing part of responsible engineering ([[01 Books/Volume I - Foundations/04 Deployment/15 Responsible Engineering/V1-15.10 Source References#^ref-henderson2020towards|Henderson et al. 2020]]).

The DLRM accounting in [[01 Books/Volume I - Foundations/04 Deployment/15 Responsible Engineering/V1-15.05 Environmental and Cost Awareness#^tbl-tco-summary|table 10]] shows how lopsided the carbon split can be. Under its service-demand assumptions, inference emits 63× as much modeled accelerator CO<sub>2</sub> as all 12 training cycles combined, so for a service of this kind the serving path, not the training run, is where carbon reductions have leverage. Efficiency optimization and environmental responsibility align when measured lifecycle energy and emissions fall for the required workload and quality target. Lifecycle assessment extends that measurement from hardware manufacture through decommissioning, and carbon-aware scheduling acts on grid carbon intensity by shifting batch jobs to lower-carbon hours or regions.

The same physical quantities that govern performance also affect responsibility. Data movement and computation contribute to chip-level energy, while data-center emissions additionally depend on utilization, facility overhead, grid intensity, and embodied impacts. Pareto analysis applies to fairness, latency, and carbon alike, and the first question is always whether the current design lies on the frontier, because a dominated design can improve several objectives at once. *Responsible engineering extends the constrained optimization problem this book has been teaching to objectives that include societal impact alongside throughput and latency.*

Training carbon still matters at frontier scale, even though serving dominates a high-traffic service such as DLRM, because a single frontier run is large enough to count on its own. A scale calculation for training a large foundation model shows how large that term can be. Under the grid assumption of napkin math [15.5](https://mlsysbook.ai/vol1/15_responsible_engr/15_responsible_engr.html#nbk-responsible-engr-carbon-cost-scale), one such run exceeds a hundred passenger-car years of emissions (see the margin).

**Napkin Math 15.5: The carbon cost of scale**

**Problem**: A foundation model is being trained at the scale of GPT-3, consuming 1,287 MWh of electricity ([[01 Books/Volume I - Foundations/04 Deployment/15 Responsible Engineering/V1-15.10 Source References#^ref-patterson2021carbon|Patterson et al. 2021]]). What is the operational carbon impact under the stated grid-intensity assumption?

**Math**:

1.  **Energy consumption**: 1,287 MWh = 1,287,000 kWh.
2.  **Carbon intensity**: This scenario uses $\approx$ 429 g/kWh CO<sub>2</sub> (0.429 kg/kWh) ([[01 Books/Volume I - Foundations/04 Deployment/15 Responsible Engineering/V1-15.10 Source References#^ref-patterson2021carbon|Patterson et al. 2021]]), a US grid-average figure slightly above the rounded 0.4 kg/kWh baseline of the cost accounting. Each calculation states its own grid assumption, and the difference changes the result by less than 10 percent.
3.  **Total emissions**: 1,287,000 kWh $\times$ 0.429 kg/kWh = 552,123 kg CO<sub>2</sub> (552 t).
4.  **Comparison**: The scenario assumes 4.6 t CO<sub>2</sub> per passenger-car year. This per-year basis differs from the lifetime-car basis of the 2019 five-car estimate, so the two car comparisons are not directly comparable.

**Systems insight**: Under this training-energy scenario, the operational emissions equal the assumed annual emissions of 120 passenger cars. A 1 percent reduction in total training energy under the same grid mix would remove the equivalent of about 1.2 cars taken off the road for a year.

Pricing compute in kilowatt-hours, tonnes of CO<sub>2</sub>, and three-year operating dollars places resource consumption alongside fairness and explainability as a measurable boundary of the system. Specification, measurement, documentation, and resource accounting together define what a responsible system must satisfy, but none of those bounds is self-verifying. The Govern stage of the lifecycle makes them provable to someone outside the team through infrastructure that controls data access, bounds privacy leakage, and preserves lineage and audit evidence in production.
