# V1-15.11 Footnotes

[[Home|Home]] · [[01 Books/Volume I - Foundations/Volume 1 Overview|Volume Overview]] · [[01 Books/Volume I - Foundations/04 Deployment/15 Responsible Engineering/V1-15 Responsible Engineering|V1-15 Responsible Engineering]]

^footnotes

<sup>1</sup> **Proxy variable**: Removing one proxy may have little effect when other correlated features retain similar signal. Conversely, correlation alone does not prove discrimination or identify the appropriate intervention. Protected-attribute removal, subgroup evaluation, tests that change one attribute and compare the decision, analysis of what actually caused an outcome, and review of the full decision process provide complementary evidence; no single diagnostic is a complete defense.

^fn1

<sup>2</sup> **Goodhart’s law in feedback loops**: Recommendation feedback loops are the canonical ML manifestation of Goodhart’s law. Gradient descent optimizes watch-time proxies at a speed no human curator can match, and the system’s own outputs reshape the training distribution. Users who consume extreme content generate data that can reinforce extremity and can decouple the proxy from user welfare faster than manual editorial processes would.

^fn2

<sup>3</sup> **Gender Shades**: A 2018 study by Joy Buolamwini (MIT Media Lab) and Timnit Gebru (Microsoft Research) that audited commercial gender-classification systems from Microsoft, IBM, and Face++ using the Fitzpatrick skin type scale, a dermatological classification developed in 1975 for UV sensitivity ([[01 Books/Volume I - Foundations/04 Deployment/15 Responsible Engineering/V1-15.10 Source References#^ref-fitzpatrick1988|Fitzpatrick 1988]]) and repurposed here as a demographic benchmark for algorithmic auditing.

^fn3

<sup>4</sup> **Disparate impact**: In *Griggs v. Duke Power Co.* (1971), the US Supreme Court held under Title VII that an employment practice may be unlawful because of its operation even without discriminatory intent ([[01 Books/Volume I - Foundations/04 Deployment/15 Responsible Engineering/V1-15.10 Source References#^ref-griggs1971dukePower|Supreme Court of the United States 1971]]). Disparate impact is a legal claim with statute- and context-specific elements, not a synonym for any statistical disparity. Model outcomes and proxies can supply relevant evidence, but metrics alone do not establish liability.

^fn4

<sup>5</sup> **Four-fifths rule**: The 1978 Uniform Guidelines on Employee Selection Procedures use the four-fifths rule as a practical employment-selection diagnostic ([[01 Books/Volume I - Foundations/04 Deployment/15 Responsible Engineering/V1-15.10 Source References#^ref-uniformguidelines1978|Equal Employment Opportunity Commission et al. 1978]]). A selection rate below 80 percent of the rate for the group with the highest rate is generally regarded as evidence of adverse impact, but the Guidelines also recognize that smaller differences may matter and that the ratio is not dispositive.

^fn5

<sup>6</sup> **Equalized odds**: Formalized by Hardt et al. ([[01 Books/Volume I - Foundations/04 Deployment/15 Responsible Engineering/V1-15.10 Source References#^ref-hardt2016equality|2016]]), requiring that both TPR and FPR be equal across protected groups; the weaker “equal opportunity” relaxes this to TPR alone. Equalized-odds postprocessing may require randomized, group-dependent decisions rather than deterministic thresholds alone. It also requires protected-group information at decision time and must be evaluated under applicable law; for example, US credit regulation generally restricts using protected bases to assess creditworthiness.

^fn6

<sup>7</sup> **COMPAS**: Correctional Offender Management Profiling for Alternative Sanctions.

^fn7

<sup>8</sup> **Reweighting**: The technique descends from importance sampling in statistics, which corrects an estimate drawn from one distribution by weighting each sample toward a target distribution. Kamiran and Calders ([[01 Books/Volume I - Foundations/04 Deployment/15 Responsible Engineering/V1-15.10 Source References#^ref-kamiran2011|2012]]) showed that appropriately chosen weights can reduce disparate impact from training data.

^fn8

<sup>9</sup> **Adversarial debiasing**: The key differentiating property is representation pressure: the adversary discourages the primary model from encoding protected-attribute information, which can reduce protected-attribute leakage and help satisfy selected fairness criteria under the evaluated distribution ([[01 Books/Volume I - Foundations/04 Deployment/15 Responsible Engineering/V1-15.10 Source References#^ref-zhang2018mitigating|Zhang et al. 2018]]). It does not provide a general fairness guarantee under arbitrary deployment shift, because any guarantee depends on assumptions about invariance, labels, causal structure, and the type of shift. Postprocessing methods such as threshold adjustment may also be appropriate under different assumptions but must be revalidated when deployment demographics or label processes change. The cost is additional training, hyperparameter tuning, and slice-level validation rather than a universal percentage overhead.

^fn9

<sup>10</sup> **Explainability and interpretability**: *Interpretability* usually describes how readily a person can understand a model or its behavior, often through a constrained structure such as a short rule list or sparse linear model. *Explainability* is broader and includes post-hoc methods such as LIME and SHAP. Neither property is automatic: a linear model with opaque features can be difficult to interpret, and a feature attribution is not a causal account of a decision. The systems implication is that intrinsic constraints affect model selection, while post-hoc methods add a computation and validation path whose cost depends on the method, model, and serving workflow. GDPR access and automated-decision provisions use the phrase “meaningful information about the logic involved” for covered processing ([[01 Books/Volume I - Foundations/04 Deployment/15 Responsible Engineering/V1-15.10 Source References#^ref-gdpr2016|European Parliament and Council of the European Union 2016]]), leaving the appropriate technical approach dependent on the decision and governing law.

^fn10

<sup>11</sup> **LIME (local interpretable model-agnostic explanations) and SHAP (SHapley additive explanations)**: LIME ([[01 Books/Volume I - Foundations/04 Deployment/15 Responsible Engineering/V1-15.10 Source References#^ref-ribeiro2016|Ribeiro et al. 2016]]) fits a local interpretable surrogate around each prediction; SHAP ([[01 Books/Volume I - Foundations/04 Deployment/15 Responsible Engineering/V1-15.10 Source References#^ref-lundberg2017unified|Lundberg and Lee 2017]]) adapts Shapley values from cooperative game theory to compute feature contributions under a unified additive framework. Exact Shapley computation requires evaluating a feature’s marginal contribution across all possible feature subsets, scaling exponentially with the feature count ${n_{\text{feat}}}$ ($\mathcal{O}(2^{{n_{\text{feat}}}})$); practical production deployments therefore rely on polynomial-time model-specific algorithms (such as TreeSHAP for decision trees) or sampling approximations (such as KernelSHAP) to avoid unacceptable serving latency. The systems trade-off is that explanation fidelity, latency, and implementation complexity must be budgeted explicitly rather than treated as free.

^fn11

<sup>12</sup> **Model cards**: The primary failure mode model cards address is scope creep, the gradual expansion from “it worked for case A” to “try it for case B” without revalidating intended use. In practice, cards are often written after deployment decisions are made, documenting observed behavior rather than constraining it. The companion “Datasheets for Datasets” ([[01 Books/Volume I - Foundations/04 Deployment/15 Responsible Engineering/V1-15.10 Source References#^ref-gebru2021|Gebru et al. 2021]]) applies the same principle to training data. Without both, the card becomes a historical record rather than an engineering guardrail.

^fn12

<sup>13</sup> **Green AI**: Schwartz et al. ([[01 Books/Volume I - Foundations/04 Deployment/15 Responsible Engineering/V1-15.10 Source References#^ref-schwartz2020|2020]]) contrasted “Red AI” (performance at any cost) with “Green AI” (efficiency as primary metric). The compute-growth anchor comes from AI and Compute’s 2012–2018 trend analysis (the fit that gives a 3.4-month doubling time), which reported a 300,000$\times$ increase in compute used in the largest AI training runs over that six-year window ([[01 Books/Volume I - Foundations/04 Deployment/15 Responsible Engineering/V1-15.10 Source References#^ref-amodei2018aicompute|Amodei and Hernandez 2018]]); comparisons that extend to later frontier models span a longer window and report larger factors. The Green AI proposal (reporting FLOPs alongside accuracy for every published result) reframes efficiency from an engineering preference into a scientific reporting obligation, making the resource cost of marginal accuracy gains visible and comparable across research groups.

^fn13

<sup>14</sup> **TCO (total cost of ownership)**: ML TCO also includes labeling, monitoring, retraining, remediation, energy, audits, and compliance, costs that an estimate of compute alone omits. Utilization and update cadence determine how the balance between training and inference falls for a given service.

^fn14

<sup>15</sup> **Privacy-preserving techniques**: K-anonymity ([[01 Books/Volume I - Foundations/04 Deployment/15 Responsible Engineering/V1-15.10 Source References#^ref-sweeney2002|Sweeney 2002]]) ensures each record is indistinguishable from at least ${k_{\text{anon}}}-1$ others with respect to chosen quasi-identifiers, l-diversity adds attribute variety within equivalence classes ([[01 Books/Volume I - Foundations/04 Deployment/15 Responsible Engineering/V1-15.10 Source References#^ref-machanavajjhala2007ldiversity|Machanavajjhala et al. 2007]]), and t-closeness bounds distribution distance ([[01 Books/Volume I - Foundations/04 Deployment/15 Responsible Engineering/V1-15.10 Source References#^ref-li2007tcloseness|Li et al. 2007]]). These syntactic guarantees address different threats from differential privacy and do not by themselves protect against all ML-specific leakage: a model trained on de-identified data can still memorize examples or reveal membership signal under some conditions ([[01 Books/Volume I - Foundations/04 Deployment/15 Responsible Engineering/V1-15.10 Source References#^ref-shokri2017membership|Shokri et al. 2017]]). Differential privacy instead bounds the influence of one record under a specified mechanism and privacy budget, offering a semantic guarantee designed to remain meaningful in the presence of side information; the methods are therefore not a simple strength hierarchy, and the appropriate protection depends on the release, threat model, and utility requirements.

^fn15

<sup>16</sup> **Differential privacy**: Introduced by Dwork et al. ([[01 Books/Volume I - Foundations/04 Deployment/15 Responsible Engineering/V1-15.10 Source References#^ref-dwork2006calibrating|2006]]), a randomized mechanism ${\mathcal{M}}$ satisfies $({\epsilon}, {\delta})$-differential privacy if for all neighboring datasets ${X}, {X'}$ differing by one record and output sets ${\mathcal{S}}$, $\Pr({\mathcal{M}}({X}) \in {\mathcal{S}}) \leq e^{\epsilon}\cdot \Pr({\mathcal{M}}({X'}) \in {\mathcal{S}}) + {\delta}$. Here ${\epsilon}$ controls the multiplicative bound and ${\delta}$ is additive slack in the inequality; their acceptable values depend on the neighboring-dataset relation, release, and policy. The systems trade-off is utility rather than mere implementation complexity: stronger privacy often requires more noise or tighter sampling, and privacy loss composes across repeated queries or training steps. Engineers must therefore track cumulative privacy loss with a valid accountant and report the assumptions behind the chosen budget.

^fn16

<sup>17</sup> **Membership inference attack**: The attack uses model behavior to infer whether a record appeared in training; overfitting can increase the signal ([[01 Books/Volume I - Foundations/04 Deployment/15 Responsible Engineering/V1-15.10 Source References#^ref-shokri2017membership|Shokri et al. 2017]]). In practice, higher confidence on training samples can help distinguish members from nonmembers. Its measured success depends on model access, data distribution, and defense assumptions.

^fn17

<sup>18</sup> **Federated learning**: McMahan et al. ([[01 Books/Volume I - Foundations/04 Deployment/15 Responsible Engineering/V1-15.10 Source References#^ref-mcmahan2017communication|2017]]) introduced Federated Averaging (FedAvg), in which each client runs several local training steps before the coordinator averages the resulting models. Averaging after many local steps rather than after every gradient cuts the number of communication rounds, which is what makes training over constrained client uplinks practical.

^fn18

<sup>19</sup> **EU AI Act (Regulation 2024/1689)**: The first comprehensive AI legal framework, defining four risk tiers with penalties that vary by infringement category. The Act has extraterritorial reach: non-EU organizations may need to comply when they place systems on the EU market or when system outputs are used in the EU. Systems engineering implications are concrete: high-risk AI requires logging infrastructure for audit trails, human oversight mechanisms built into the architecture, and CE marking (the EU conformity mark), all capabilities that must be designed in from inception, not retrofitted after deployment.

^fn19

<sup>20</sup> **High-risk AI (EU AI Act Annex III)**: Annex III enumerates specified use cases in areas including biometrics, critical infrastructure, education, employment, essential services, law enforcement, migration, and justice. Classification depends on intended use, and model architecture alone does not determine it.

^fn20

<sup>21</sup> **GDPR (General Data Protection Regulation) articles 15 and 22**: Article 22 restricts certain solely automated decisions with legal or similarly significant effects and, for specified exceptions, requires safeguards including human intervention, the ability to express a point of view, and the ability to contest the decision; article 15(1)(h) contains the access right to meaningful information about the logic involved in such automated decision-making ([[01 Books/Volume I - Foundations/04 Deployment/15 Responsible Engineering/V1-15.10 Source References#^ref-gdpr2016|European Parliament and Council of the European Union 2016]]). The European Data Protection Board’s guidance emphasizes that required human oversight must be substantive and not merely a “rubber-stamping” exercise ([[01 Books/Volume I - Foundations/04 Deployment/15 Responsible Engineering/V1-15.10 Source References#^ref-edpb2018automatedProfiling|European Data Protection Board 2018]]).

^fn21

<sup>22</sup> **HIPAA (Health Insurance Portability and Accountability Act)**: Enacted in 1996, with Privacy Rule and Security Rule requirements establishing standards for protected health information ([[01 Books/Volume I - Foundations/04 Deployment/15 Responsible Engineering/V1-15.10 Source References#^ref-hipaa1996|United States Congress 1996]]; [[01 Books/Volume I - Foundations/04 Deployment/15 Responsible Engineering/V1-15.10 Source References#^ref-hhs2003hipaaprivacy|U.S. Department of Health and Human Services 2003]], [[01 Books/Volume I - Foundations/04 Deployment/15 Responsible Engineering/V1-15.10 Source References#^ref-hhs2005hipaasecurity|2005]]); PHI may be used for training under applicable authorization or another permitted pathway. Model outputs may remain PHI when they identify or can reasonably identify individuals, and security-rule documentation retention must be reflected in audit and evidence design.

^fn22

<sup>23</sup> **Data lineage systems**: Systems such as Apache Atlas and DataHub capture metadata about data flows from pipeline execution, creating graphs in which nodes are datasets and edges are transformations. GDPR Article 30 requires records of processing activities ([[01 Books/Volume I - Foundations/04 Deployment/15 Responsible Engineering/V1-15.10 Source References#^ref-gdpr2016|European Parliament and Council of the European Union 2016]]), not automated model-level lineage specifically.

^fn23

<sup>24</sup> **Audit trail**: Audit integrity requires controls against unauthorized alteration, but retention and deletion duties vary by record type and law; append-only object storage with retention controls, or cryptographic hash chains, can provide tamper evidence without creating a universal rule that records are never deleted. A large platform may log billions of events daily; HIPAA’s Security Rule also imposes multi-year documentation-retention obligations for required policies and procedures ([[01 Books/Volume I - Foundations/04 Deployment/15 Responsible Engineering/V1-15.10 Source References#^ref-hhs2005hipaasecurity|U.S. Department of Health and Human Services 2005]]). Retention planning must distinguish records that must be preserved from personal data that must be minimized or deleted.

^fn24
