# V1-15.07 Fallacies and Pitfalls

[[Home|Home]] · [[01 Books/Volume I - Foundations/Volume 1 Overview|Volume Overview]] · [[01 Books/Volume I - Foundations/04 Deployment/15 Responsible Engineering/V1-15 Responsible Engineering|V1-15 Responsible Engineering]]

^sec-responsible-engineering-fallacies-pitfalls-61b9

Teams can still fail after assembling assessment frameworks, fairness metrics, explainability mechanisms, efficiency analyses, and governance infrastructure. A team may retrofit fairness after benchmark success, trust aggregate accuracy, or treat compliance evidence as paperwork rather than system behavior, drawing on intuitions from traditional software engineering where bugs are local and testing is deterministic.

**Fallacy**: *Responsibility can be addressed after the system achieves technical objectives.*

Teams assume fairness constraints can be retrofitted once models demonstrate strong benchmark performance. In production, early architectural decisions constrain what interventions remain feasible. Amazon’s recruiting tool, abandoned after remediation failed ([[01 Books/Volume I - Foundations/04 Deployment/15 Responsible Engineering/V1-15.03 Engineering Responsibility Gap#^sec-responsible-engineering-optimization-succeeds-systems-fail-1a22|section 15.2.1]]), is the case in point. Integrating fairness constraints early can be less costly than retrofitting them after data contracts, monitoring, and release criteria are fixed.

**Pitfall**: *Relying on aggregate metrics to assess fairness.*

Engineers assume high overall accuracy indicates the system works well for all users. The flaw of averages ([[01 Books/Volume I - Foundations/04 Deployment/15 Responsible Engineering/V1-15.04 Responsible Engineering Across the Lifecycle#^sec-responsible-engineering-testing-across-populations-9f20|section 15.3.2]]) shows why this intuition fails, because aggregate metrics can conceal large subgroup disparities. The credit-service analysis in [[01 Books/Volume I - Foundations/04 Deployment/15 Responsible Engineering/V1-15.04 Responsible Engineering Across the Lifecycle#^sec-responsible-engineering-worked-example-fairness-analysis-loan-approval-2c72|section 15.3.2.1]] showed a 30 percentage-point TPR gap, with qualified minority applicants rejected at 4× the majority-group rate. These disparities can persist undetected when standard monitoring tracks only aggregates. Production systems require disaggregated evaluation with application-specific thresholds, such as the 5-percentage-point TPR threshold used for the credit service.

**Fallacy**: *Removing sensitive attributes from training data eliminates bias.*

Teams remove gender, race, and other protected attributes expecting this ensures fairness. Other features can act as proxy variables when they correlate with sensitive characteristics. ZIP codes, purchase patterns, browsing history, college names, and language choices can carry indirect demographic signal. Amazon’s recruiting tool kept encoding sex through college and organization names after explicit gender terms were removed ([[01 Books/Volume I - Foundations/04 Deployment/15 Responsible Engineering/V1-15.03 Engineering Responsibility Gap#^sec-responsible-engineering-optimization-succeeds-systems-fail-1a22|section 15.2.1]]), and the care-management algorithm (war story [15.1](https://mlsysbook.ai/vol1/15_responsible_engr/15_responsible_engr.html#ws-responsible-engr-proxy-variable-trap)) reproduced racial disparity through its cost target, a proxy that no attribute filter would remove. Removing protected attributes does not by itself establish fairness.

**Pitfall**: *Treating documentation as sufficient accountability.*

Teams invest effort in model cards, then consider responsibility requirements satisfied. Documentation provides transparency ([[01 Books/Volume I - Foundations/04 Deployment/15 Responsible Engineering/V1-15.04 Responsible Engineering Across the Lifecycle#^sec-responsible-engineering-model-documentation-standards-bef6|section 15.3.3.2]]) but not enforcement. A model card specifying “not validated for high-stakes decisions” has no effect when the system is repurposed for loan approvals without technical restrictions. Accountability requires operational integration: monitoring dashboards, documented subgroup-disparity alert thresholds, incident response procedures, and access controls preventing deployment beyond validated use cases.

**Fallacy**: *Responsible AI is primarily a legal compliance issue.*

Teams treat responsibility as external oversight rather than engineering practice. Engineering decisions made months before legal review constrain the solution space more than any compliance assessment. Architecture selection determines what fairness interventions are feasible, while data pipeline design establishes whether disaggregated evaluation is even possible. As [[01 Books/Volume I - Foundations/04 Deployment/15 Responsible Engineering/V1-15.03 Engineering Responsibility Gap#^sec-responsible-engineering-engineering-leadership-responsibility-e03c|section 15.2.5]] establishes, systems designed with responsibility as an engineering objective enable efficient validation; systems where responsibility is added at late-stage review face redesign or deployment with documented risks.

**Pitfall**: *Measuring the environmental impact of training but not inference.*

Public discourse focuses on training-run carbon, and engineers often follow this framing when assessing environmental responsibility. The illustrative TCO analysis in [[01 Books/Volume I - Foundations/04 Deployment/15 Responsible Engineering/V1-15.05 Environmental and Cost Awareness#^sec-responsible-engineering-total-cost-ownership-35c1|section 15.4.2]] shows why this focus is incomplete. Under its unbatched service-demand assumptions for the DLRM ranking service ([[01 Books/Volume I - Foundations/04 Deployment/15 Responsible Engineering/V1-15.05 Environmental and Cost Awareness#^tbl-tco-summary|table 10]]), the inference-to-training cost ratio is about 40:1, and inference emits about 63× as much CO<sub>2</sub> as training. A model trained periodically but served millions of times daily can have a lifecycle footprint dominated by inference rather than training. These values are scenario-dependent, but they show why lifecycle accounting must include both terms.

**Fallacy**: *Model weights are exempt from data governance and deletion requests.*

Teams often assume that once training data has been compiled into model weights, the data is gone and compliance obligations cannot reach the artifact. In reality, models can memorize training data, and membership inference attacks can reveal whether a sensitive record appeared in the training set ([[01 Books/Volume I - Foundations/04 Deployment/15 Responsible Engineering/V1-15.10 Source References#^ref-shokri2017membership|Shokri et al. 2017]]). Unlike a database delete, a neural network diffuses each training point’s statistical influence across its parameters through gradient updates, so verifiably purging that influence forces systems trade-offs. Exact retraining from scratch on a cleaned dataset provides a reference for removing the excluded records’ contribution, but it re-expends the training budget (potentially thousands of accelerator-hours) and its carbon emissions. It also does not erase other copies or facts inferable from retained data. Machine unlearning, introduced with audit infrastructure ([[01 Books/Volume I - Foundations/04 Deployment/15 Responsible Engineering/V1-15.06 Data Governance and Compliance#^sec-responsible-engineering-audit-infrastructure-accountability-669d|section 15.5.5]]), aims to bound that cost, but verifying that an intervention removes statistical influence without degrading model utility remains an open systems challenge. Engineering teams must track data-to-model lineage and evaluate retraining, machine unlearning, or compensating controls with legal and privacy specialists whenever an erasure request affects deployed artifacts.

Across these failures, the recurring mistake is to treat responsibility as something added after the system is built rather than as a system property. Measurable constraints, continuous monitoring, and enforceable governance carry responsibility through the full lifecycle.

**Self-Check: Question**

1.  A deployed automated lending model achieves an impressive 88% overall accuracy on its global test set. However, a disaggregated audit reveals that qualified minority applicants experience a 60% True Positive Rate compared to 90% for majority applicants (a 30 percentage-point gap) and face $4\times$ higher false rejection rates. Which engineering pitfall does this scenario illustrate?

    1.  Relying on aggregate metrics to assess fairness, which conceals severe subgroup disparities behind strong overall averages (the Flaw of Averages).
    2.  Treating documentation as sufficient accountability, assuming a model card automatically prevents operational misuse.
    3.  The belief that model weights are exempt from data governance and right-to-erasure regulations.
    4.  Assuming that edge deployment power budgets scale linearly with dataset sample size.

2.  A team removes race and gender columns from their training dataset, asserting that “the model cannot discriminate on features it cannot see.” Drawing on the Amazon recruiting and Optum healthcare cases, explain why this naive attribute removal creates false confidence, and identify the specific engineering analyses required.

3.  True or False: Because a model card explicitly specifies that a vision model is “not validated for high-stakes medical or security screening,” publishing the model card guarantees operational compliance without requiring technical access controls or deployment release checks.

4.  True or False: For a high-traffic production ML service, measuring and reporting only the electricity consumed during model training runs provides an accurate accounting of the system’s long-term environmental carbon footprint.
