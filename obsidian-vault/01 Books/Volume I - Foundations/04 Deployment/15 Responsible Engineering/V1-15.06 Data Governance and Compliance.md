# V1-15.06 Data Governance and Compliance

[[Home|Home]] · [[01 Books/Volume I - Foundations/Volume 1 Overview|Volume Overview]] · [[01 Books/Volume I - Foundations/04 Deployment/15 Responsible Engineering/V1-15 Responsible Engineering|V1-15 Responsible Engineering]]

^sec-responsible-engineering-data-governance-compliance-bd1a

The keyword spotting system makes the governance problem physical. An always-listening voice assistant samples audio continuously in private homes, stores enrolled speaker profiles, and periodically syncs with cloud pipelines that retrain on population-wide audio. Every guarantee the earlier stages established now therefore depends on what data the system may hold, who may touch it, and what evidence survives.

**Data governance**, the engineering discipline that turns regulatory and ethical obligations into verifiable technical controls, is the enforcement layer that makes accountability operational. Fairness metrics, model cards, and impact assessments have practical force only if the system can prove what data it ingested, which principals accessed it, which legal basis authorized processing, and whether applicable deletion or contestability requests were executed. The legal-basis question alone can decide an enforcement outcome. In 2023, Ireland’s Data Protection Commission found that Meta had relied on an invalid legal basis for personalized advertising and had violated transparency and fairness requirements, a governance failure that involved no security breach at all ([[01 Books/Volume I - Foundations/04 Deployment/15 Responsible Engineering/V1-15.10 Source References#^ref-dpc2023metaIreland|Data Protection Commission 2023]]).

Storage architectures also serve as governance enforcement mechanisms that determine which principals access data, how queries are tracked, and whether pipelines satisfy legal requirements. Every architectural decision, from ingestion strategies through extract, transform, load (ETL) pipelines to persistent storage layout, carries governance implications that surface during regulatory audits, privacy investigations, or model recalls. Data governance translates abstract policy into concrete systems engineering through authenticated access controls on training partitions, immutable audit infrastructure that logs dataset access, privacy-preserving transformations during feature extraction, and deterministic lineage systems that trace training snapshots to deployed model checkpoints.

↩︎ The database, warehouse, and data-lake storage architectures, and the access patterns each one serves, are established in **[[01 Books/Volume I - Foundations/01 Foundations/04 Data Engineering/V1-04.11 Storage and Delivery#^sec-data-engineering-ml-storage-systems-architecture-options-67fa|Storage system options]]**.

**Principle: Compliance as engineering constraint**

**Invariant**: Data governance obligations are engineering requirements, not optional policy overlays.

**Implication**: Systems that process regulated data must map applicable duties to technical and organizational controls. Access control, erasure, contestability, audit, and lineage may be required or useful depending on the jurisdiction, data, actor, and use case. The General Data Protection Regulation (GDPR), California Consumer Privacy Act (CCPA), Brazil’s General Data Protection Law (LGPD, *Lei Geral de Proteção de Dados*), and China’s Personal Information Protection Law (PIPL) differ in scope and duties, so no single control checklist establishes compliance across them.

^pri-responsible-engr-compliance-engineering

Data governance turns security, privacy, compliance, and lineage into mutually dependent engineering constraints, and the KWS system shows where each one applies. Continuous capture, enrolled profiles, and fleet-wide retraining create obligations across consent management, data minimization, access auditing, and deletion rights, and the four domains map directly to hardware and pipeline enforcement points in this architecture. Security comes first by protecting raw features, training partitions, and model artifacts across the ingestion lifecycle through mutual authentication, role-based authorization, encryption at rest and in transit, and hardware-rooted key management. Hardware keystores on edge microcontrollers protect stored speaker templates, mutual TLS authenticates telemetry in transit, and role-based access controls restrict access to centralized training audio in cloud storage.

Because access control alone does not limit what an authenticated consumer can learn from the records it reads, privacy regulates information exposure inside that boundary through differential privacy and automated feature redaction. In the KWS system, the privacy design keeps ambient audio inside a circular SRAM buffer until an on-device trigger fires, and it bounds information leakage during fleet-wide acoustic model retraining.

Where privacy governs what leaves the device, compliance translates jurisdiction-specific statutes (such as GDPR, CCPA, and LGPD) into retention windows, consent verification, and cross-border replication rules once data arrives in the cloud. Compliance thereby determines the legal basis for processing and the scope of any revocation or erasure workflow that removes in-scope voice profiles and acoustic data across primary stores and backups.

Proving that those security, privacy, and retention rules were followed requires lineage and audit systems that render the entire data lifecycle inspectable. Retained lineage linking deployed weights through training snapshots to authorized source records supports verification of security claims and applicable obligations. In the KWS pipeline, such lineage establishes provenance through content hashes and signed manifests that link each deployed DS-CNN binary back through specific training splits and acoustic augmentation seeds to the original raw recordings.

Governance works as an operating model rather than a single control. The framework in [[01 Books/Volume I - Foundations/04 Deployment/15 Responsible Engineering/V1-15.06 Data Governance and Compliance#^fig-data-governance-pillars|figure 7]] arranges eight elements, from organizational oversight and compliance policies to data catalogs, sourcing, quality metrics, runtime operations, and security, around a central governance hub. Technical mechanisms and organizational processes must function as mutually reinforcing subsystems. Encrypted storage without identity-mapped access controls still lets an insider copy data out, and compliant storage without verifiable audit trails cannot pass a regulatory inspection. Within the D·A·M taxonomy, data governance is the control plane of the Data axis. It enforces lawful sourcing, privacy bounds, and lifecycle integrity for the data that feeds the Algorithm, and it runs on Machine-axis infrastructure such as encrypted storage, access control, and audit logs.

### Security and Access Control Architecture

^sec-responsible-engineering-security-access-control-architecture-982a

When a data scientist queries the KWS feature store for training data, she can read aggregated voice features but cannot access the raw audio recordings from which they were derived. The serving pipeline, by contrast, can read online features for inference without writing to the training dataset or modifying source data. That separation reflects a layered security architecture in which governance requirements translate into enforceable technical controls at each pipeline stage. Feature stores enforce role-based access control (RBAC) by mapping organizational policies to authenticated identities and database permissions. These controls operate across distinct storage tiers. Object storage enforces bucket-level access policies, data warehouses implement column-level security and dynamic masking for sensitive fields, and feature stores maintain isolated read and write paths that separate low-latency online inference key-value lookups from high-throughput offline batch training extracts.

↩︎ The dual-storage architecture of offline columnar stores and online key-value caches is established in **[[01 Books/Volume I - Foundations/01 Foundations/04 Data Engineering/V1-04.08 Data Processing#^sec-data-engineering-feature-stores-bridging-training-serving-55c8|Feature stores]]**.

Access control mechanisms remain incomplete without encryption, which protects data at rest and in transit but does not prevent misuse after decryption or authorized access. Training corpora are encrypted under keys held in a dedicated key management service, feature stores encrypt tables at rest, and KWS edge devices encrypt what they send and verify the signature on every model update. Each of these controls is only as strong as its key management and update authorization.

When training and serving run on infrastructure that the data owner does not control, data also needs protection while it is being computed on. **Confidential computing** provides that protection with hardware secure enclaves (trusted execution environments) that isolate memory and execution from the host’s software, though it does not protect data from the application authorized to process it.

Serialized weights and checkpoints require the same protection as training data, because loading an unverified checkpoint in a format that can embed executable code gives an attacker control of training nodes and inference workers. Formats that store only tensors avoid that mechanism, and cryptographic signatures verify a checkpoint’s integrity and provenance before loading, although neither establishes that a model is free of backdoors.

Authorized loading also leaves the model’s behavior to be evaluated. Poisoned training samples can induce backdoors, perturbed inference inputs can induce misclassification (evasion attacks), and systematic queries can extract model functionality ([[01 Books/Volume I - Foundations/04 Deployment/15 Responsible Engineering/V1-15.10 Source References#^ref-tramer2016stealing|Tramèr et al. 2016]]). Access control, cryptographic enclaves, and artifact verification therefore establish *who* can reach data and models and *how* they are protected across storage, transit, and execution, yet controlling access is only half the problem. Even authorized callers can compromise individual privacy if the data or model outputs themselves expose too much. Prediction APIs or raw weights can reveal whether a particular record was present in training ([[01 Books/Volume I - Foundations/04 Deployment/15 Responsible Engineering/V1-15.10 Source References#^ref-shokri2017membership|Shokri et al. 2017]]), so artifact integrity and limits on information exposure require distinct controls.

### Technical Privacy Protection Methods

^sec-responsible-engineering-technical-privacy-protection-methods-4a74

Even when role-based access control works as designed, a data scientist querying a training table does not need, and should not see, individual user records when aggregate statistics suffice, nor should a released checkpoint or prediction API reveal whether a specific customer’s record was in the training set ([[01 Books/Volume I - Foundations/04 Deployment/15 Responsible Engineering/V1-15.10 Source References#^ref-shokri2017membership|Shokri et al. 2017]]). Privacy-preserving techniques[[01 Books/Volume I - Foundations/04 Deployment/15 Responsible Engineering/V1-15.11 Footnotes#^fn15|¹⁵]] close that exposure gap by bounding what queries and model outputs reveal even to authorized callers. Differential privacy bounds how much changing any one record in the dataset can change the probability distribution of an exported output, such as a query answer or a trained model. A parameter called epsilon sets that bound, and a smaller epsilon means stronger privacy.[[01 Books/Volume I - Foundations/04 Deployment/15 Responsible Engineering/V1-15.11 Footnotes#^fn16|¹⁶]]

One training mechanism that provides differential privacy is Differentially Private Stochastic Gradient Descent (DP-SGD) ([[01 Books/Volume I - Foundations/04 Deployment/15 Responsible Engineering/V1-15.10 Source References#^ref-abadi2016deep|Abadi et al. 2016]]). Unlike standard gradient clipping, which caps the batch-averaged gradient for numerical stability, DP-SGD clips each individual sample’s gradient to an $\ell_2$ norm threshold ${C}$ before summing across the batch. Bounding each sample’s contribution is necessary because an unclipped outlier record could shift the batch sum by an arbitrarily large amount, leaving no finite noise scale able to hide whether that record was in the batch. Once every sample’s influence is bounded by ${C}$, DP-SGD adds calibrated Gaussian noise with distribution ${\mathcal{N}}(0, {\sigma}^2 {C}^2 {\mathbf{I}})$ to the sum of clipped gradients, where ${\sigma}$ is the noise multiplier. Each noisy step still reveals a little about the records in its batch, so a privacy accountant accumulates that privacy loss across the sampled training steps and checks the total against the chosen epsilon budget.

Standard backpropagation kernels sum weight gradients across the batch dimension in place as they execute, so they never expose an individual sample’s gradient norm. Clipping needs each sample’s gradient norm before that sum occurs, and the direct way to obtain it materializes one full gradient buffer per sample, so gradient storage grows by the batch size. For the KWS acoustic model (200K parameters), one FP32 gradient occupies 800 KB and a batch of 256 per-sample gradients 204.8 MB. The same approach for the 1.5B-parameter GPT-2 would need 1.5 TB, far beyond the 80 GB of one H100.

Specialized two-pass clipping implementations compute each sample’s gradient norm directly from layer activations and backpropagated output gradients before accumulating the rescaled weight updates. They avoid per-sample weight-gradient buffers but pay compute for the second pass, which lengthens every training step. Small sequential microbatches instead trade the memory back for lost parallelism, which also lengthens each step. The price of DP-SGD is therefore a pair of measurements, peak memory and time per step, taken for the chosen implementation with its clipping, noise, sampling, and accounting assumptions intact.

Empirical attacks complement these formal checks. Membership inference attacks measure whether an observer can tell that a specific record was in the training set, and model inversion attacks use confidence outputs to reconstruct sensitive feature values or a training subject’s likeness ([[01 Books/Volume I - Foundations/04 Deployment/15 Responsible Engineering/V1-15.10 Source References#^ref-fredrikson2015|Fredrikson et al. 2015]]). A successful attack reveals practical leakage, but no empirical attack can prove that an $({\epsilon}, {\delta})$ bound holds.[[01 Books/Volume I - Foundations/04 Deployment/15 Responsible Engineering/V1-15.11 Footnotes#^fn17|¹⁷]] Reducing confidence-score precision or restricting outputs to top-${k}$ classes reduces what some APIs expose, but neither is a complete defense. Their value depends on the attacker’s access and auxiliary information, while differential privacy provides a separate bound under its stated mechanism and accounting assumptions.

While differential privacy bounds information leakage from model outputs, collecting continuous raw sensor streams creates an immediate vulnerability if raw data leaves the capture device. Always-listening edge architectures, such as the lighthouse KWS system, operate under severe privacy constraints because continuous ambient microphone sampling must coexist with strict minimization and retention boundaries. To reduce routine transmission exposure, this design confines acoustic feature extraction and wake-word detection to local low-power microcontrollers or digital signal processors (DSPs). Incoming audio samples stream through a small volatile circular buffer in on-chip SRAM, where older frames are continuously overwritten by newly sampled pulse-code modulation (PCM) data. The policy allows saving or sending audio only after an on-device wake-word trigger. Because detection can produce false wakes, validation must cover both the detector and the buffer and egress controls, including which pre-trigger audio becomes accessible after activation.

Confining raw audio to the device enforces data minimization but starves centralized training of the acoustic variety that improves accuracy. **Federated learning** recovers that variety by moving training to the data. Training-capable clients such as paired phones or home hubs train on authorized local examples and send model updates instead of raw audio, and a coordinator combines those updates into a shared model.[[01 Books/Volume I - Foundations/04 Deployment/15 Responsible Engineering/V1-15.11 Footnotes#^fn18|¹⁸]] The constrained KWS microcontroller only runs the result. The approach exchanges centralization risk for physical bottlenecks. Every round moves parameter updates over constrained wireless uplinks, battery-powered clients drop out mid-round, data that differ from home to home make local updates diverge, and raw updates can still leak training examples through reconstruction attacks ([[01 Books/Volume I - Foundations/04 Deployment/15 Responsible Engineering/V1-15.10 Source References#^ref-zhu2019deep|Zhu et al. 2019]]). Compression of those updates reduces communication but provides no privacy guarantee by itself.

Secure aggregation lets the coordinator learn only the aggregate of participating clients’ updates, under the protocol’s participation, dropout, and collusion assumptions ([[01 Books/Volume I - Foundations/04 Deployment/15 Responsible Engineering/V1-15.10 Source References#^ref-bonawitz2017|Bonawitz et al. 2017]]). What that aggregate and the final model reveal remains outside its protection; differential privacy bounds that release by limiting the effect of any one record or client, provided the training mechanism and privacy accountant support the chosen neighboring relation. A design that needs both must account separately for what a curious coordinator observes during training and what recipients can infer from released checkpoints.

When triggered audio recordings or aggregated model checkpoints must be retained on centralized infrastructure for verification or downstream training, privacy protection shifts to automated lifecycle management. Automated retention workflows apply time-to-live policies across primary stores, caches, training partitions, and backups. Cryptographic erasure destroys the keys needed to decrypt covered copies; physical overwrite is a distinct storage-sanitization mechanism. Neither removes every copy automatically. The workflow must inventory replicas, key copies, and backups, define applicable retention exceptions, and verify that in-scope data is no longer recoverable under the chosen threat model. Differential privacy, on-device minimization, federated training, and automated retention supply the technical mechanisms that bound exposure, while external statutes decide which of those mechanisms are mandatory and what rights affected people can invoke.

### Regulation as Architecture

^sec-responsible-engineering-regulatory-landscape-1ec1

When the credit service denies an applicant who then asks why, contests the decision, and requests access to or deletion of the data used about her, those retention, erasure, and explanation paths stop being internal engineering preferences and become statutory rights. To satisfy them, the system must do more than report an accuracy score. It must produce an explanation tied to the specific decision, preserve the model and data lineage that led to that output, route the dispute to a substantive human review path, retain audit logs, and support deletion or access workflows where data rights apply. Explicit regulatory frameworks turn transparency, oversight, and accountability into non-negotiable architecture requirements.

Regulation first enters the architecture through risk classification. The EU AI Act establishes a comprehensive framework, classifying AI systems by risk level and mandating requirements accordingly.[[01 Books/Volume I - Foundations/04 Deployment/15 Responsible Engineering/V1-15.11 Footnotes#^fn19|¹⁹]] The Act entered into force on August 1, 2024 and applies in phases: prohibited-practice rules began applying in 2025, while high-risk and other operator obligations phase in by system category and implementation guidance ([[01 Books/Volume I - Foundations/04 Deployment/15 Responsible Engineering/V1-15.10 Source References#^ref-euaiact2024|European Parliament and Council of the European Union 2024]]).

For systems engineers, compliance hinges on architectural capabilities rather than phase-in dates. Covered high-risk systems[[01 Books/Volume I - Foundations/04 Deployment/15 Responsible Engineering/V1-15.11 Footnotes#^fn20|²⁰]] must implement risk management, data governance, technical documentation, transparency, human oversight, and accuracy, robustness, and security requirements. A covered credit-decision system therefore needs auditability from inception: model versions, training data provenance, validation evidence, human-oversight design, logging, and postdeployment monitoring must be part of the architecture rather than documents assembled after launch.

Contestability adds a second architectural requirement. GDPR moves the same applicant workflow into data-subject rights. Article 22 grants EU data subjects the right not to be subject to decisions based solely on automated processing that produce legal or similarly significant effects, subject to specified exceptions and safeguards.[[01 Books/Volume I - Foundations/04 Deployment/15 Responsible Engineering/V1-15.11 Footnotes#^fn21|²¹]] Article 15(1)(h) separately gives data subjects access to meaningful information about the logic involved in automated decision-making referred to in Article 22. Engineering teams should determine which decisions are covered and design the required explanation and human-review capabilities accordingly.

Where a substantive review path is required, it must be operationally staffed and supported by summaries, provenance, and audit tools. That staffing scales with decision volume. If 0.1 percent of 1M daily decisions are appealed or escalated, trained reviewers must work through 1,000 cases/day, a workload assumption rather than a model-error rate. The review tooling must also surface each case’s explanation, lineage, and model version fast enough for that queue to clear.

US sectoral law reaches similar capabilities through domain-specific evidence requirements, less unified than the EU AI Act. In the credit example, the Equal Credit Opportunity Act (ECOA) and its implementing Regulation B require specific principal reasons for covered adverse actions, and those reasons must accurately describe the factors actually considered or scored ([[01 Books/Volume I - Foundations/04 Deployment/15 Responsible Engineering/V1-15.10 Source References#^ref-cfpb2023regb|Consumer Financial Protection Bureau 2023]]). Healthcare regulation, including the Health Insurance Portability and Accountability Act (HIPAA)[[01 Books/Volume I - Foundations/04 Deployment/15 Responsible Engineering/V1-15.11 Footnotes#^fn22|²²]] and Food and Drug Administration (FDA) guidance, imposes the same pattern with different artifacts: protected-health-information controls, validation records, audit logs, and incident response. Across domains, the task is to translate each obligation into a concrete capability: explanation, human review, lineage, access control, deletion, monitoring, or incident response. Because the same safeguards recur across regimes, [[01 Books/Volume I - Foundations/04 Deployment/15 Responsible Engineering/V1-15.06 Data Governance and Compliance#^tbl-responsible-engr-regulatory-capabilities|table 11]] organizes them by capability rather than by jurisdiction.

Data-protection law reaches deeper into the pipeline than decision-level duties do, because its obligations attach to the data itself rather than to a decision. When a European user invokes an applicable right to erasure under GDPR, the voice assistant must determine which personal data and downstream artifacts are in scope and execute the appropriate workflow within the regulation’s response deadlines ([[01 Books/Volume I - Foundations/04 Deployment/15 Responsible Engineering/V1-15.10 Source References#^ref-gdpr2016|European Parliament and Council of the European Union 2016]]). These obligations become architecture constraints in pipeline design, storage choices, and operational procedures. GDPR’s data minimization principle requires limiting collection and retention to what is necessary for stated purposes. Article 15 access rights cover personal data undergoing processing and specified information about that processing, not every artifact merely associated with a user.

Voice assistants operating globally face overlapping regulatory regimes because requirements vary by jurisdiction and apply differently based on user age and data sensitivity. GDPR cross-border-transfer rules permit transfers through adequacy decisions, appropriate safeguards, or limited derogations rather than imposing categorical data localization. These rules can still drive regional storage, replication, and processing choices.

A structured data card turns these operational metadata requirements into reviewable fields. The card in [[01 Books/Volume I - Foundations/04 Deployment/15 Responsible Engineering/V1-15.06 Data Governance and Compliance#^fig-data-card|figure 9]], adapted from the Open Images Extended (MIAP) specification ([[01 Books/Volume I - Foundations/04 Deployment/15 Responsible Engineering/V1-15.10 Source References#^ref-pushkarna2022|Pushkarna et al. 2022]]), partitions authorship, primary motivations, explicit safety prohibitions (such as forbidding age or gender classification), and methodological caveats. Although documenting these boundaries does not alone guarantee legal compliance, it provides the verifiable baseline necessary for pipeline ingestion checks.

The engineering response to these regulatory requirements is proactive architectural design. Teams that build documentation, monitoring, explainability, and human oversight into systems from inception demonstrate compliance efficiently. Teams that must retrofit these capabilities face expensive pipeline redesigns or deployment blocks. Nearly every regulatory obligation eventually asks the infrastructure the same two questions: which data produced this model, and who touched that data under what authority. Lineage and audit systems answer them.

### Building Data Lineage Infrastructure

^sec-responsible-engineering-building-data-lineage-infrastructure-3128

When a regulator audits which training data produced a deployed model, or a user invokes an applicable right to erasure, the organization must answer from infrastructure rather than manual investigation. The lineage graph built for reproducibility in **[[01 Books/Volume I - Foundations/01 Foundations/04 Data Engineering/V1-04.09 Dataset Release#^sec-data-engineering-tracking-data-transformation-lineage-3b09|Transformation lineage]]** and **[[01 Books/Volume I - Foundations/04 Deployment/14 ML Operations/V1-14.04 Development Infrastructure#^sec-ml-operations-versioning-lineage-b1cf|Versioning and lineage]]**, a directed acyclic graph (DAG) of artifact versions and transformation tasks, is the same structure an audit queries.[[01 Books/Volume I - Foundations/04 Deployment/15 Responsible Engineering/V1-15.11 Footnotes#^fn23|²³]] Governance adds obligations to that graph rather than a new system. Ingestion records must link each KWS recording to its acquisition method and consent basis, and the use boundaries declared on a data card ([[01 Books/Volume I - Foundations/04 Deployment/15 Responsible Engineering/V1-15.06 Data Governance and Compliance#^fig-data-card|figure 9]]) decide which training jobs may consume which feature partitions.

The obligation that lineage serves most directly is lineage-driven deletion. When an erasure request arrives, traversing the graph from the source recording identifies candidate derived artifacts (cached spectrograms, feature embeddings, training snapshots, and checkpoint weights) for scope determination and possible invalidation, retraining, or legal review. Deployment edges extend the same traversal to the field. Because lineage records which model version each device downloaded, a quality or compliance defect later found in the training data can be traced to the devices running the affected models and recalled there. Lineage captures provenance, but accountable operation also requires access history: who touched the data, when, and under which authority.

↩︎ Transformation lineage, which records the code version, parameters, and processing history behind each dataset, is established in **[[01 Books/Volume I - Foundations/01 Foundations/04 Data Engineering/V1-04.09 Dataset Release#^sec-data-engineering-tracking-data-transformation-lineage-3b09|Transformation lineage]]**.

### Audit Infrastructure and Accountability

^sec-responsible-engineering-audit-infrastructure-accountability-669d

![[90 Assets/Volume I/15 Responsible Engr/Vol1 Responsible Engr Margin 004 68ff2e912a.svg]]

*A flat gray line labeled Dataset, a straight blue line labeled Audit log that rises from near zero, crosses the dataset line, and then crosses a red dashed line labeled Budget, with the overrun shaded red.*

*Audit volume can outgrow the storage budget while the dataset stays flat.*

Recording that access history for HIPAA or Sarbanes-Oxley (SOX, the US law that governs corporate financial reporting) audits can amplify writes. If a production feature store logs the full feature payload on every read, each read triggers a log write far larger than the access it records, and the audit log can then outgrow the training dataset itself and exceed its storage budget while the dataset stays flat. Accountability trails[[01 Books/Volume I - Foundations/04 Deployment/15 Responsible Engineering/V1-15.11 Footnotes#^fn24|²⁴]] record access events to meet legal obligations, and for a fixed event schema, retained volume depends on recorded events per unit time, bytes per event, and the retention window. More accesses or instrumented stages raise the event count, while logging full feature payloads and intermediate activation tensors greatly increases bytes per event. Separating compact audit metadata from raw payload data controls that multiplier.

Production keyword spotting architectures apply that separation through a tiered audit topology, in which each tier logs only what its storage and bandwidth budgets allow. At the edge tier, microcontrollers operating within strict SRAM budgets log local state transitions, wakeup timestamps, and power states to volatile circular ring buffers, and they upload those records in batches to conserve radio energy and uplink bandwidth. The retention requirement determines which events can use this volatile path. Events that must survive power loss require durable local storage until central storage acknowledges durable receipt, adding write energy and storage wear to the device budget. If retention requirements exceed the available storage or uplink budget, the design must reduce optional telemetry or provision additional capacity before deployment.

Once telemetry reaches centralized infrastructure, auditing shifts from bandwidth-constrained edge devices to high-throughput feature stores. At this feature tier, offline and online feature stores emit append-only audit events for every read and write transaction. Rather than duplicating high-dimensional feature vectors, these events record requesting service identities, authorized subject pseudonyms, feature group hashes, and read timestamps. This metadata-only logging strategy enables fine-grained access verification while keeping log volume proportional to access transactions rather than payload sizes.

From feature stores, data flows into training pipelines running across accelerator clusters. At this training tier, cluster schedulers record deterministic manifests mapping training jobs directly to immutable data partition hashes, git commit SHAs, and hyperparameter configurations. While these manifests support checks of pipeline integrity and reproducibility, they reveal a systems boundary. Access logs confirm which records were processed, but they cannot demonstrate whether deleted user data persists within learned model weights. If the applicable erasure obligation covers a deployed model artifact, teams must evaluate retraining, validated **machine unlearning** (removing a training record’s influence from learned weights without retraining from scratch ([[01 Books/Volume I - Foundations/04 Deployment/15 Responsible Engineering/V1-15.10 Source References#^ref-cao2015|Cao and Yang 2015]]; [[01 Books/Volume I - Foundations/04 Deployment/15 Responsible Engineering/V1-15.10 Source References#^ref-bourtoule2021|Bourtoule et al. 2021]])), or other legally appropriate controls. Database access logs alone cannot establish removal of the training contribution.

Regulatory requirements extend audit responsibility from data access to prediction-time inference behavior. Reconstructing why the credit service denied a specific applicant in [[01 Books/Volume I - Foundations/04 Deployment/15 Responsible Engineering/V1-15.04 Responsible Engineering Across the Lifecycle#^sec-responsible-engineering-worked-example-fairness-analysis-loan-approval-2c72|section 15.3.2.1]], or why an edge voice assistant triggered a false wake-word activation, requires auditing the exact operational state at inference time. Without inference-time logging, an audit trail may confirm database access without capturing which model checkpoint, decision threshold, and runtime features produced a given decision.

Saving raw input tensors for every inference request would overwhelm storage write bandwidth; production serving systems instead record compact, purpose-limited decision manifests. These manifests capture the active model version hash, runtime decision threshold, output confidence scores or reason codes, and cryptographic hashes of input features, supporting indexed retrieval of decision evidence. An input hash identifies content but cannot reconstruct it; replay also requires an appropriately retained source or an authorized recoverable representation, along with the relevant execution artifacts.

Across these edge, feature, training, and serving tiers, governance uses Machine-axis logging and access control to prove Data-axis promises. Even with that infrastructure in place, however, a team can still undermine its own safeguards when it applies intuitions from deterministic software to statistical systems.
