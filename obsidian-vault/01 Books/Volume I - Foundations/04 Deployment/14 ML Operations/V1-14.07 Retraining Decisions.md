# V1-14.07 Retraining Decisions

[[Home|Home]] · [[01 Books/Volume I - Foundations/Volume 1 Overview|Volume Overview]] · [[01 Books/Volume I - Foundations/04 Deployment/14 ML Operations/V1-14 ML Operations|V1-14 ML Operations]]

^sec-ml-operations-retraining-decisions-7d3a

A team that retrains the ranking model on every minor PSI spike burns thousands of dollars in accelerator time and risks training on corrupted upstream logs, while a team that waits blindly for a quarterly calendar tick lets accuracy slide 6 points within four weeks, long before the next tick arrives. Retraining is the most expensive response the operations loop can choose, and the easiest to automate badly. Monitoring supplies two kinds of evidence, a divergence that a label-free monitor measures continuously and outcome metrics that arrive later, and the decide step of the operations loop turns them into one of three actions: retrain, roll back, or wait. The decision has three parts, taken in turn below: the candidate strategies and a test of whether a run is worth its cost now, a cadence that sets how often runs should happen, and a policy comparison between a fixed schedule and an evidence trigger. On the ranking service every run is also paid in accelerator-hours on hardware that serves live traffic.

The degradation equation of **[[01 Books/Volume I - Foundations/00 Introduction/01 Introduction/V1-01.07 When ML Systems Fail Silently#^eq-degradation|equation 1.1]]** turns divergence into a forecast of accuracy when outcome evidence supports one:

$$
{\text{Accuracy}}({t}) \approx {\text{Accuracy}_0} - {\lambda}\cdot {\mathcal{D}}({P_t} \lVert {P_0})
$$

 where ${\lambda}$ is fitted per deployment from historical degradation data, as the statistical drift diagnostic (principle [[01 Books/Volume I - Foundations/04 Deployment/V1 Deployment Principles#^pri-statistical-drift|10]]) requires. While not a universal law, tracking distributional divergence ${\mathcal{D}}({P_t} \lVert {P_0})$ can provide an earlier warning than delayed outcomes; it does not establish the direction or size of a quality change.

### Retraining Decision Framework

^sec-ml-operations-retraining-decision-framework-e33d

Because distributional divergence warns early without proving how much accuracy was lost, an automated training pipeline still leaves one operational decision open: when to run. Deciding when to retrain a model means balancing accuracy degradation against compute and validation expense across three candidate policies. [[01 Books/Volume I - Foundations/04 Deployment/14 ML Operations/V1-14.07 Retraining Decisions#^tbl-retraining-schedules|Table 16]] provides illustrative schedules across domains, from daily retraining for rapidly shifting ad click prediction to quarterly updates for stable medical imaging applications:

| **Domain**              | **Illustrative Schedule** | **Rationale**                       |
|:------------------------|:--------------------------|:------------------------------------|
| **Ad click prediction** | Daily                     | User interests shift rapidly        |
| **Fraud detection**     | Weekly                    | Attack patterns evolve continuously |
| **Demand forecasting**  | Monthly                   | Seasonal patterns change slowly     |
| **Medical imaging**     | Quarterly                 | Disease presentations are stable    |

Table 16: **Illustrative Retraining Schedules by Domain**: These represent starting points; actual cadences depend on observed drift rates and business impact, and organizations typically calibrate them through operational experience.

^tbl-retraining-schedules

Those schedules are starting points, not invariant rules. The ranking service sits between the table’s first two rows. Its users’ interests shift daily, yet the economics below put its optimum at a few days.

In a *scheduled retraining* policy, pipeline execution runs on a fixed calendar cadence (daily, weekly, or monthly) regardless of incoming performance telemetry. This strategy is simple to automate and guarantees that freshly accumulated training data is integrated periodically. However, it burns accelerator hours during quiet periods and reacts slowly when distribution shifts strike between scheduled intervals.

A *triggered retraining* policy initiates pipeline execution when monitoring systems detect statistical drift or performance degradation exceeding predefined thresholds. This approach can conserve compute by responding to evidence rather than a fixed schedule, but it relies heavily on low-latency label streams or accurate proxy metrics to prevent delayed responses or false-alarm retraining runs.

A *continuous retraining* policy updates model weights incrementally as labeled records arrive, using streaming micro-batches or online learning algorithms. While this strategy minimizes staleness lag, it amplifies validation risk, because corrupted, unrepresentative, or adversarial data can poison model parameters before automated anomaly detectors or engineering teams can intervene.

The operating choice among these strategies depends on four physical and operational constraints: retraining compute cost, validation pipeline latency, automated rollback capability, and label arrival delay. Re-optimizing a model on multi-accelerator nodes incurs tangible energy and cluster costs. Triggered policies require statistically reliable proxy signals when true labels arrive with long delays. Every continuous deployment pipeline demands robust canary and rollback mechanisms. The policy should follow from these costs and the label delay, not from habit.

The economic and validation constraints become executable in [[01 Books/Volume I - Foundations/04 Deployment/14 ML Operations/V1-14.07 Retraining Decisions#^lst-ops-triggered-retraining|listing 5]]. The decision function schedules a run only when the estimated recovered value exceeds the total cost of a run (compute, validation, deployment, and the expected cost of a bad rollout, the four terms that [[01 Books/Volume I - Foundations/04 Deployment/14 ML Operations/V1-14.07 Retraining Decisions#^eq-retrain-cost|equation 10]] formalizes below). It also schedules a run only when fresh validation data are available.

``` python
quality_loss = baseline_accuracy - current_accuracy
feature_drift = max(population_stability_index(features))
prediction_shift = distribution_distance(
    baseline_predictions, live_predictions
)

benefit = estimate_value_recovered(
    quality_loss=quality_loss,
    feature_drift=feature_drift,
    prediction_shift=prediction_shift,
)
total_cost = (
    compute_cost + validation_cost + deployment_cost + rollout_risk
)

if benefit > total_cost and validation_data_is_fresh():
    schedule_retraining_run()
```

Listing 5: **Triggered Retraining Decision**: The decision combines quality loss, feature drift, prediction shift, and the cost of a run so automatic retraining fires only when the expected benefit exceeds that total cost.

^lst-ops-triggered-retraining

The decision rule separates detecting drift from authorizing retraining. Drift contributes to estimated benefit, while fresh validation data and positive net value determine whether automation acts. Turning that rule into a cadence requires measuring how fast an unretrained model loses value.

### Quantitative Retraining Economics

^sec-ml-operations-quantitative-retraining-economics-1579

To turn the trigger rule of [[01 Books/Volume I - Foundations/04 Deployment/14 ML Operations/V1-14.07 Retraining Decisions#^lst-ops-triggered-retraining|listing 5]] into a concrete schedule, a team must measure how fast an unretrained model loses value and weigh that loss rate against the fixed cost of a run. When outcome data support a locally exponential fit, the fitted decay rate gives a measurable timescale.[[01 Books/Volume I - Foundations/04 Deployment/14 ML Operations/V1-14.15 Footnotes#^fn23|²³]] Its **half-life**,[[01 Books/Volume I - Foundations/04 Deployment/14 ML Operations/V1-14.15 Footnotes#^fn24|²⁴]] the time for fitted accuracy to fall to half its initial value, states that timescale in intuitive units. The half-life puts deployments that decay at different rates on one scale, but on its own it does not fix the economically optimal interval, which balances the cost of staleness against the cost of a run. The derivation takes three steps: a staleness cost, a retraining cost, and the interval that minimizes their sum per unit time.

#### The Staleness Cost Function

^the-staleness-cost-function

Model accuracy can degrade over time, creating a staleness cost. For economic planning, a team may fit the observable impact over a stable local regime as an exponential decay process. Here ${\gamma}$ is a temporal decay rate under the assumption that measured degradation accumulates steadily; it is not determined by distribution divergence alone. The exponential model is a simplification that enables closed-form economic analysis. Let ${\text{Accuracy}}({t})$ represent accuracy at time ${t}$ since last training, and ${\text{Accuracy}_0}$ represent initial accuracy. [[01 Books/Volume I - Foundations/04 Deployment/14 ML Operations/V1-14.07 Retraining Decisions#^eq-accuracy-decay|Equation 8]] captures this fitted scenario, where the rate ${\gamma}$ depends on domain volatility and observed outcomes:

$$
{\text{Accuracy}}({t}) = {\text{Accuracy}_0} \cdot e^{-{\gamma} {t}} \tag{8}
$$

^eq-accuracy-decay

This temporal model is a time-domain summary of the degradation equation (**[[01 Books/Volume I - Foundations/00 Introduction/01 Introduction/V1-01.07 When ML Systems Fail Silently#^eq-degradation|equation 1.1]]**), which predicts accuracy from divergence, ${\text{Accuracy}}({t}) \approx {\text{Accuracy}_0} - {\lambda} \cdot {\mathcal{D}(P_t \| P_0)}$, where ${\mathcal{D}(P_t \| P_0)}$ is the divergence ${\mathcal{D}}({P_t} \lVert {P_0})$ written as one symbol. If divergence grows at a roughly steady rate over an interval, that equation makes accuracy fall linearly in time at rate ${\lambda}$ times the growth rate of ${\mathcal{D}(P_t \| P_0)}$. The exponential of [[01 Books/Volume I - Foundations/04 Deployment/14 ML Operations/V1-14.07 Retraining Decisions#^eq-accuracy-decay|equation 8]] falls at rate ${\text{Accuracy}_0}{\gamma}$ while ${\gamma} {t}$ is small. Both rates describe the same falling accuracy, so they must agree, which makes ${\gamma}$ an empirical stand-in for how fast the input distribution moves, scaled by the fitted sensitivity ${\lambda}$ and divided by ${\text{Accuracy}_0}$.

The retraining interval derived here uses the time-domain ${\gamma}$, because a schedule is set in days. The drift statistics of [[01 Books/Volume I - Foundations/04 Deployment/14 ML Operations/V1-14.06 Production Monitoring#^sec-ml-operations-model-infrastructure-monitoring-3988|section 14.5.2]] (the population stability index, the KS statistic, and KL divergence) estimate the divergence itself and alert on it directly. Steady divergence growth is an assumption rather than a law, so both forms must be checked against observed outcomes.

A longer-horizon fit would decay toward a floor, such as chance-level accuracy, rather than toward zero. The unshifted form in [[01 Books/Volume I - Foundations/04 Deployment/14 ML Operations/V1-14.07 Retraining Decisions#^eq-accuracy-decay|equation 8]] suffices for the intervals that retraining exists to enforce, the early, small-loss regime (${\gamma} {t}\ll 1$) on which the square-root approximation below also depends.

The cost of staleness accumulates based on the daily query volume ${Q_{\text{day}}}$ and the value impact ${V}$ of a unit change in accuracy fraction. Integrating the instantaneous accuracy loss $({\text{Accuracy}_0} - {\text{Accuracy}}({t}))$ over the retraining interval ${T}$ yields [[01 Books/Volume I - Foundations/04 Deployment/14 ML Operations/V1-14.07 Retraining Decisions#^eq-staleness-cost|equation 9]]:

$$
\begin{array}{>{\displaystyle}c} \text{Staleness Cost}({T}) = \int_0^T {Q_{\text{day}}}\cdot {V}\cdot ({\text{Accuracy}_0} - {\text{Accuracy}}({t})) \, d{t}= \\ {Q_{\text{day}}}\cdot {V}\cdot {\text{Accuracy}_0} \cdot \left({T}- \frac{1-e^{-{\gamma} {T}}}{{\gamma}}\right) \end{array} \tag{9}
$$

^eq-staleness-cost

The integral accumulates cost over time ${t}$ from 0 to ${T}$, and the closed form follows from substituting [[01 Books/Volume I - Foundations/04 Deployment/14 ML Operations/V1-14.07 Retraining Decisions#^eq-accuracy-decay|equation 8]] for ${\text{Accuracy}}({t})$.

#### The Retraining Cost Function

^the-retraining-cost-function

Each retraining incurs fixed costs including compute, validation, and deployment overhead. [[01 Books/Volume I - Foundations/04 Deployment/14 ML Operations/V1-14.07 Retraining Decisions#^eq-retrain-cost|Equation 10]] decomposes these:

$$
\text{Retraining Cost} = {C_{\text{compute}}}+ {C_{\text{validation}}}+ {C_{\text{deployment}}}+ {C_{\text{risk}}} \tag{10}
$$

 where ${C_{\text{compute}}}$ is the cost of the training run itself, ${C_{\text{validation}}}$ is the cost of evaluating the new model before release, ${C_{\text{deployment}}}$ is the cost of rolling it into production, and ${C_{\text{risk}}}$ is the expected cost of potential regression from the new model. These are the four terms that `total_cost` sums in [[01 Books/Volume I - Foundations/04 Deployment/14 ML Operations/V1-14.07 Retraining Decisions#^lst-ops-triggered-retraining|listing 5]].

^eq-retrain-cost

#### Optimal Retraining Interval

^optimal-retraining-interval

The optimal retraining interval ${T}^*$ minimizes total cost per unit time, as [[01 Books/Volume I - Foundations/04 Deployment/14 ML Operations/V1-14.07 Retraining Decisions#^eq-optimal-interval|equation 11]] shows:

$$
{T}^* = \operatorname{arg\,min}_{T}\frac{\text{Staleness Cost}({T}) + \text{Retraining Cost}}{{T}} \tag{11}
$$

^eq-optimal-interval

Under the assumption ${\gamma} {T}\ll 1$, a Taylor expansion of the exponential yields a closed-form approximation. Specifically, the second-order Taylor approximation $e^{-{\gamma} {T}} \approx 1 - {\gamma} {T}+ \frac{1}{2}({\gamma} {T})^2$ reduces the bracketed staleness term $\left({T}- \frac{1-e^{-{\gamma} {T}}}{{\gamma}}\right)$ to $\frac{1}{2}{\gamma} {T}^2$. Geometrically, when accuracy falls at a nearly constant rate ${\text{Accuracy}_0} {\gamma}$ per day, the loss starts at zero right after a retraining run and reaches ${\text{Accuracy}_0} {\gamma} {T}$ at the end of the cycle, so the average accuracy loss across the cycle is half that end-of-cycle peak. Dividing total costs by ${T}$ therefore yields an average daily cost of $\frac{1}{2} {Q_{\text{day}}} {V}{\text{Accuracy}_0} {\gamma} {T}+ \frac{{C}}{{T}}$, where ${C}$ is the Retraining Cost of [[01 Books/Volume I - Foundations/04 Deployment/14 ML Operations/V1-14.07 Retraining Decisions#^eq-retrain-cost|equation 10]]. The first term rises linearly with ${T}$ as average staleness grows, while the second falls as $1/{T}$ as the fixed cost of a run is spread over more days. Differentiating with respect to ${T}$ and setting the derivative to zero places the minimum where those two terms meet, yielding the square-root economic interval of [[01 Books/Volume I - Foundations/04 Deployment/14 ML Operations/V1-14.07 Retraining Decisions#^eq-optimal-retrain|equation 12]]:

$$
{T}^* \approx \sqrt{\frac{2 \cdot {C}}{{Q_{\text{day}}}\cdot {V}\cdot {\text{Accuracy}_0} \cdot {\gamma}}} \tag{12}
$$

 An exact optimum requires minimizing [[01 Books/Volume I - Foundations/04 Deployment/14 ML Operations/V1-14.07 Retraining Decisions#^eq-optimal-interval|equation 11]] without the expansion. The interval grows with the cost of a run and shrinks with traffic, value, and decay rate, each under a square root.

^eq-optimal-retrain

**Napkin Math 14.6: The optimal retraining interval**

**Problem**: How often should the team retrain the model to maximize profit?

**Physics**: In this fitted scenario, model accuracy ${\text{Accuracy}}({t})$ decays exponentially at rate ${\gamma}$. Each successful update is assumed to restore initial accuracy with negligible training and rollout delay; traffic and value remain constant.

- ${Q_{\text{day}}}$: Daily Query Volume (Traffic).
- ${V}$: Financial value per query for a unit change in accuracy fraction. With this convention, ${V}= \$0.50$ means 1 percentage point of accuracy is worth \$0.005 per query. The Value per Point of [[01 Books/Volume I - Foundations/04 Deployment/14 ML Operations/V1-14.02 Silent Failure in Production#^eq-retrain-decision|equation 3]] is the same quantity accumulated over a decision horizon, ${V}/100$ per query multiplied by the number of queries in that horizon.
- ${C}$: Fixed cost of a retraining run, including compute and operational overhead.

**Formula**: [[01 Books/Volume I - Foundations/04 Deployment/14 ML Operations/V1-14.07 Retraining Decisions#^eq-optimal-retrain|Equation 12]] gives the interval ${T}^*$ that minimizes the sum of staleness losses and training costs per unit time.

**Math**: Consider the ranking service in an illustrative scenario (${\text{Accuracy}_0}$ = 0.94):

- **Traffic** $({Q_{\text{day}}})$: 1,000,000 ranking requests/day.
- **Utility** $({V})$: \$0.50/query for a unit accuracy change.
- **Retraining Cost** $({C})$: \$5,000.
- **Drift Rate** $({\gamma})$: 0.24 percent per day.

$$
T^* \approx \sqrt{\frac{2 \times 5{,}000}{1{,}000{,}000 \times 0.50 \times 0.94 \times 0.0024}} \approx \mathbf{3}\text{ days}
$$

**Systems insight**: High traffic and valuable accuracy shorten ${T}^*$, so label availability, validation requirements, and achievable update delay become the limiting factors. If ${T}^*$ is less than the team’s manual deployment time, the proposed cadence exceeds its operational capacity, and the team must shorten the update path or reconsider the policy and its assumptions.

With the parameters collected in [[01 Books/Volume I - Foundations/04 Deployment/14 ML Operations/V1-14.07 Retraining Decisions#^tbl-retraining-parameters|table 17]], the approximation places the economic optimum near 3 days. The ranking service’s drift, a 0.24 percent daily decay fitted to its observed four-week slide, is slow enough that a daily run would not yet pay for itself at this run cost. Four times the traffic would bring the optimum to about 1.5 days, close to the daily cadence that [[01 Books/Volume I - Foundations/04 Deployment/14 ML Operations/V1-14.07 Retraining Decisions#^tbl-retraining-schedules|table 16]] lists for ad click prediction. A slower-moving domain or a costlier run would push the same formula toward the table’s weekly or monthly rows.

| **Parameter**             |  **Value**   | **Description**                            |
|:--------------------------|:------------:|:-------------------------------------------|
| **${Q_{\text{day}}}$**    |  1,000,000   | Ranking requests per day                   |
| **${V}$**                 | \$0.50/query | Value per query for a unit accuracy change |
| **${\text{Accuracy}_0}$** |     0.94     | Initial accuracy                           |
| **${\gamma}$**            |    0.0024    | Daily decay rate (0.24% per day)           |
| **${C}$**                 |   \$5,000    | Total retraining expense                   |

Table 17: **Retraining Decision Parameters**: Example values for the ranking service processing 1,000,000 requests daily.

#### Sensitivity Analysis

^sensitivity-analysis

Under the square-root approximation, ${T}^*$ scales with the square root of these parameters. [[01 Books/Volume I - Foundations/04 Deployment/14 ML Operations/V1-14.07 Retraining Decisions#^tbl-retraining-sensitivity|Table 18]] shows that a fourfold change in retraining cost, query volume, or decay rate moves the approximated interval twofold. The square root damps error in these inputs, so a rough estimate of run cost or decay rate still yields an interval of the right scale.

| **Change**             | **Effect on ${T}^*$** |
|:-----------------------|----------------------:|
| **4× retraining cost** |    2× longer interval |
| **4× query volume**    |   2× shorter interval |
| **4× decay rate**      |   2× shorter interval |

Table 18: **Retraining Interval Sensitivity**: Under the square-root approximation a fourfold change in an input moves the interval only twofold, and the directions differ. More expensive retraining lengthens the interval, while higher query volume or a faster decay rate shortens it.

^tbl-retraining-sensitivity

#### Accelerator-Hours and Contention

^accelerator-hours-and-contention

The run cost ${C}$ bundles the four terms of [[01 Books/Volume I - Foundations/04 Deployment/14 ML Operations/V1-14.07 Retraining Decisions#^eq-retrain-cost|equation 10]], and on the ranking service the accelerator term is the smallest of them. In an illustrative scenario, a run that retrains on the logs gathered since the previous run occupies 4 H100s for 6 hours. Those 24 accelerator-hours cost \$96 at an assumed cloud rental price per training accelerator-hour, about 1.9 percent of the \$5,000 run. Validation, deployment, and rollout risk make up the rest, which is why automating the validation path ([[01 Books/Volume I - Foundations/04 Deployment/14 ML Operations/V1-14.04 Development Infrastructure#^sec-ml-operations-model-validation-a891|section 14.3.2.3]]) shortens the economic interval more than faster training would.

The accelerator-hours matter instead as contention. The serving copy of the tables already holds two accelerators. On an eight-accelerator node, a run that holds its own copy and training state on four more leaves room for exactly one further copy of the tables while it executes ([[01 Books/Volume I - Foundations/04 Deployment/14 ML Operations/V1-14.07 Retraining Decisions#^fig-ml-ops-dlrm-node-day|figure 9]]). That copy can be a canary, a shadow, or the hot standby of [[01 Books/Volume I - Foundations/04 Deployment/14 ML Operations/V1-14.05 Releasing Models#^tbl-ml-ops-release-control-footprint|table 11]], but not more than one of them. A team that releases and retrains on the same node must therefore schedule the two so that a retraining run never evicts the copy that makes rollback immediate.

![[90 Assets/Volume I/14 ML Ops/Cell 18 Output 1 1e02f266b2.png]]

Figure 9: **One Ranking Node’s Day**: Accelerator occupancy on an eight-accelerator H100 node over the day of a retraining run, under one schedule that keeps rollback immediate. The serving copy of the 100 GB tables holds 2 accelerators and the hot standby holds 2 more. The retraining run holds 4 accelerators for 6 h, so while it runs the standby is the only further copy of the tables that fits. After the run, the candidate it produces can go out as a canary on 2 of the freed accelerators. The schedule is illustrative and built from the chapter’s worked numbers.

#### Model Limitations

^model-limitations

This closed-form framework provides a first-order approximation for operational planning, but its utility depends on several simplifying assumptions. First, the exponential decay formulation presumes gradual drift at a steady rate ${\gamma}$; sudden covariate shifts or acute data outages require immediate statistical change-point detection rather than smooth decay models. Second, the formulation assumes a constant marginal value ${V}$ per accuracy point, whereas real-world utility often has nonlinear thresholds or asymmetric penalties. Third, the model treats retraining events as independent resets to initial accuracy with negligible delay, setting aside update failures, time spent training and validating, and transfer learning across cycles. Finally, infrastructure costs are modeled as fixed sums, whereas cloud spot pricing, queue contention, and elastic compute availability introduce dynamic variations. Parameters improve through empirical calibration against historical telemetry as operational experience accumulates, and the framework implements cost-aware automation ([[01 Books/Volume I - Foundations/04 Deployment/14 ML Operations/V1-14.02 Silent Failure in Production#^sec-ml-operations-mlops-3ea3|section 14.1.1]]) with thresholds grounded in measurable business impact.

### Scheduled Versus Triggered Retraining

^sec-ml-operations-scheduled-triggered-retraining-b51e

The interval derived in [[01 Books/Volume I - Foundations/04 Deployment/14 ML Operations/V1-14.07 Retraining Decisions#^eq-optimal-retrain|equation 12]] fixes a calendar schedule from average drift, whereas the decision rule of [[01 Books/Volume I - Foundations/04 Deployment/14 ML Operations/V1-14.07 Retraining Decisions#^sec-ml-operations-retraining-decision-framework-e33d|section 14.6.1]] lets observed evidence decide when to run. Over a 365-day deployment on the ranking service, the two policies trace different sawtooths against a drift threshold of 0.88 accuracy ([[01 Books/Volume I - Foundations/04 Deployment/14 ML Operations/V1-14.07 Retraining Decisions#^fig-rotting-asset-curve|figure 10]]). Under a scheduled policy (orange sawtooth), the model retrains at rigid 90-day intervals, a spacing chosen long enough to make each tooth of the sawtooth visible, repeatedly dipping below the quality floor before each reset. In contrast, a triggered policy (green sawtooth) initiates retraining the moment monitored accuracy touches the threshold, illustrating an idealized instantaneous reset; in practice the trigger is often a divergence or proxy signal calibrated against accuracy. Neither interval is the economic optimum of [[01 Books/Volume I - Foundations/04 Deployment/14 ML Operations/V1-14.07 Retraining Decisions#^eq-optimal-retrain|equation 12]], so the figure compares how the two policies respond to the same decay rather than recommending either interval. Both curves are drawn from the temporal decay model of [[01 Books/Volume I - Foundations/04 Deployment/14 ML Operations/V1-14.07 Retraining Decisions#^eq-accuracy-decay|equation 8]]. In deployment, sample collection, label arrival, training, validation, and rollout delays can carry accuracy below the trigger before recovery; the policy does not guarantee a performance floor.

![[90 Assets/Volume I/14 ML Ops/Cell 19 Output 1 985aad412b.png]]

Figure 10: **The Rotting Asset Curve**: An illustrative exponential accuracy decay under two maintenance policies, both drawn as sawtooths. The scheduled policy resets every 90 days regardless of accuracy and dips below the drift threshold before each reset, while the triggered policy resets on crossing that threshold and holds a higher floor in this zero-delay model. Real response delays and update quality must be measured; neither cadence is universal.

^fig-rotting-asset-curve

In this zero-delay model, the scheduled policy dips below the floor each cycle and the triggered one does not. Scheduled retraining is easy to plan but can retrain too early or too late, whereas trigger-based retraining requires stronger telemetry but aligns intervention with observed degradation. Whichever policy fires, a retrained replacement or a rollback depends on the registry lineage of [[01 Books/Volume I - Foundations/04 Deployment/14 ML Operations/V1-14.04 Development Infrastructure#^sec-ml-operations-versioning-lineage-b1cf|section 14.3.1.2]]. This lineage links each version to the code, data, hyperparameters, and weights that produced it, and so lets a responder return to a known-good checkpoint. Lineage still cannot guarantee bitwise replay if driver versions, CUDA libraries, or nondeterministic reduction kernels vary across the fleet.

Retraining answers gradual drift. A sudden drop with a cause inside the system, such as a broken feature transform, a bad release, or a corrupted upstream table, calls for a different response. The failing layer must be found and contained, often by rollback, long before a retraining run could finish, and that is the work of incident response.
