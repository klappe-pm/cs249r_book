# V1-14.10 Case Studies

[[Home|Home]] · [[01 Books/Volume I - Foundations/Volume 1 Overview|Volume Overview]] · [[01 Books/Volume I - Foundations/04 Deployment/14 ML Operations/V1-14 ML Operations|V1-14 ML Operations]]

^sec-ml-operations-case-studies-641d

Because the physical envelope of the serving hardware and the consequences of an incorrect prediction govern what a deployment can observe and change, a cloud MLOps playbook fails immediately if applied unmodified to a battery-powered voice trigger that cannot stream audio off the device, or to a clinical decision-support loop where an unreviewed weight update can harm a patient. The chapter’s keyword spotter operates under a milliwatt-scale power budget, kilobytes of on-device memory, and telemetry that must leave the device as aggregates rather than audio. In contrast, regulated AI-enabled medical devices require evidence and change controls appropriate to their intended use. FDA guidance for predetermined change control plans describes planned modifications, validation methods, and impact assessment ([[01 Books/Volume I - Foundations/04 Deployment/14 ML Operations/V1-14.14 Source References#^ref-fda2025pccp|U.S. Food and Drug Administration 2025]]). These contrasting environments dictate how engineering teams manage data pipelines, model artifacts, and hardware execution across the lifecycle. Edge architectures devote most engineering effort to bridging the hardware gap between high-throughput cloud training clusters and microcontrollers, whereas clinical systems devote it to provenance tracking, cohort monitoring, and human-in-the-loop validation reviews.

The edge case in [[01 Books/Volume I - Foundations/04 Deployment/14 ML Operations/V1-14.10 Case Studies#^sec-ml-operations-oura-ring-case-study-2444|section 14.9.1]] follows the keyword spotter from its detection drop through deployment and monitoring, reusing the three-tier partition that a published wearable design makes concrete. The clinical case, ClinAIOps, places clinicians inside the operations loop. Despite their contrasting operating regimes, both deployments must satisfy the same core MLOps disciplines. [[01 Books/Volume I - Foundations/04 Deployment/14 ML Operations/V1-14.10 Case Studies#^tbl-ml-ops-principle-mapping|Table 26]] maps these foundational principles across the two environments, demonstrating how physical hardware limits and regulatory mandates alter implementation mechanisms without altering the underlying operational requirements.

| **Principle**              | **Keyword-spotting edge design**                                                  | **ClinAIOps**                                                                                |
|:---------------------------|:----------------------------------------------------------------------------------|:---------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------|
| **Reproducibility**        | Versioned audio datasets, DSP front end, and firmware alongside model weights     | Audit trails, decision provenance                                                            |
| **Separation of concerns** | Independent data, training, and serving layers with an edge-specific OTA pipeline | Distinct clinical validation and deployment stages with regulatory compliance isolation      |
| **Consistency**            | One feature-extraction kernel shared by training and on-device inference          | Standardized clinical data pipelines ensuring training-serving parity                        |
| **Observable degradation** | Aggregate on-device proxies (wake rate, false-wake reports, duty cycle)           | Cohort-specific monitoring, outcome tracking                                                 |
| **Cost-aware automation**  | Updates held until accuracy gain outweighs radio energy, flash wear, rollout risk | Automated model updates with human-in-the-loop review balancing update cost and patient risk |

Table 26: **MLOps Principles by Case Study**: Side-by-side mapping of the five foundational MLOps principles to the keyword-spotting edge design and the ClinAIOps framework, showing how domain constraints reshape implementation without changing which principles apply.

### Keyword Spotting at the Edge

^sec-ml-operations-oura-ring-case-study-2444

Six months after launch, the keyword spotter’s detection rate had fallen four percentage points below target because of three silent changes in **[[01 Books/Volume I - Foundations/01 Foundations/04 Data Engineering/V1-04.13 Operational Data Health#^sec-data-engineering-data-debt-hidden-liability-3335|Operational Data Health]]**. These were a new microphone revision with a different frequency response, evaluation labels revised under new guidelines, and an unannounced change to the device metadata schema. Operations inherits that failure on the chapter’s power-bound case. The fix must reach devices that no one can touch, the evidence must be gathered without uploading audio, and a bad update must be reversible without disabling the device.

#### Context and Motivation

^sec-ml-operations-context-motivation-df0a

Keyword spotting partitions its computation across three tiers. The microcontroller runs the audio front end and the 200 KB INT8 DS-CNN continuously in its always-on island (the low-power domain of the chip that stays awake while the rest sleeps), a companion device or hub runs a heavier verification model when the detector fires, and the cloud aggregates fleet statistics and retrains.

The partition is not peculiar to keyword spotting. A published sleep-staging wearable, the Oura Ring, uses the same three tiers, and its published evaluation makes the work of each tier concrete ([[01 Books/Volume I - Foundations/04 Deployment/14 ML Operations/V1-14.14 Source References#^ref-altini2021promise|Altini and Kinnunen 2021]]). The ring captures accelerometry, optical photoplethysmography (PPG)[[01 Books/Volume I - Foundations/04 Deployment/14 ML Operations/V1-14.15 Footnotes#^fn26|²⁶]] for pulse rate and heart-rate variability, and skin temperature, and its 15–22 mAh battery rules out continuous Bluetooth Low Energy (BLE) streaming. Sensor preprocessing and lightweight activity classification run in the ring’s microcontroller firmware, the heavier multi-sensor sleep-stage sequence model runs on the companion phone during periodic synchronization, and historical aggregation and retraining run in the cloud. The versioning, telemetry, over-the-air, and lifecycle controls described later are a design for the keyword spotter informed by that partition, not a reported account of any vendor’s production system.

#### Model Development and Evaluation

^sec-ml-operations-oura-model-development-9791

Evaluating an edge model against a reference standard is the same step on which the keyword spotter’s monitoring depends. The ring’s reference labels come from polysomnography (PSG),[[01 Books/Volume I - Foundations/04 Deployment/14 ML Operations/V1-14.15 Footnotes#^fn27|²⁷]] the laboratory-grade multichannel physiological recording used as the clinical reference for sleep staging. The evaluation paired the ring with simultaneous laboratory PSG for 106 participants and 440 nights of data. Under 5-fold cross-validation, four-stage sleep classification reached 57 percent accuracy from movement alone and 79 percent once heart-rate and temperature features were added ([[01 Books/Volume I - Foundations/04 Deployment/14 ML Operations/V1-14.14 Source References#^ref-altini2021promise|Altini and Kinnunen 2021]]), which is the evidence that justified the ring’s heavier phone-tier model. The reference itself is imperfect, because human PSG scorers agree with each other at only about 82 percent to 83 percent. The 22 percentage-point gain closes roughly 84.6–88 percent of the gap between the movement-only baseline and that human agreement.

The keyword spotter faces the same evaluation problem with a weaker reference. Its labels come from reviewed recordings, and because the device uploads audio only when it wakes, a spoken wake word that the detector misses leaves no recording at all (**[[01 Books/Volume I - Foundations/01 Foundations/04 Data Engineering/V1-04.13 Operational Data Health#^sec-data-engineering-detecting-responding-data-drift-509a|Data drift detection and response]]**). Measuring the false rejections behind the four-point drop therefore takes a separate, consented collection, and the evaluation set must be versioned by microphone revision so that a hardware change appears as a slice rather than being averaged away. Deploying the retrained model across the tiers then adds requirements that no offline study covers: reproducible quantization, numerical validation of the converted model on the device, and a runtime energy budget.

#### Key Operational Insights

^sec-ml-operations-oura-operational-insights-b335

Following offline validation, the systems challenge shifts from statistical accuracy to deployment safety and runtime efficiency. The toolchain for the keyword spotter needs an automated quantization pipeline that converts FP32 weights to INT8 with calibrated scale factors, deterministic cross-compilation for the firmware that runs the model, and cryptographically signed release packages for each hardware revision. It also needs fault-tolerant OTA delivery through the dual-bank scheme of [[01 Books/Volume I - Foundations/04 Deployment/14 ML Operations/V1-14.05 Releasing Models#^sec-ml-operations-edge-ai-deployment-patterns-dc1d|section 14.4.4]], whose cost is a second 200 KB flash bank ([[01 Books/Volume I - Foundations/04 Deployment/14 ML Operations/V1-14.05 Releasing Models#^tbl-ml-ops-release-control-footprint|table 11]]).

Edge MLOps operates under physical constraints distinct from server-side pipelines. Model accuracy must be co-designed with battery life, radio bandwidth, and on-device storage, and real-time ground-truth labels are nearly absent. In the power-bound row of [[01 Books/Volume I - Foundations/04 Deployment/14 ML Operations/V1-14.02 Silent Failure in Production#^tbl-monitoring-archetype-strategy|table 5]], health monitoring relies on operational proxies (wake rate, duty cycle, false-wake reports, and confidence distributions) rather than verified outcomes. Recovering the four-point detection drop in the field requires configuration management across every execution target (the microphone hardware revision, the DSP front end, the firmware, and the quantized model weights) so that each fielded device’s combination can be traced and receive the right update.

These constraints map directly onto the five principles of [[01 Books/Volume I - Foundations/04 Deployment/14 ML Operations/V1-14.10 Case Studies#^tbl-ml-ops-principle-mapping|table 26]]. Versioned audio datasets provide reproducibility by linking every deployed model to the recordings, labeling guidelines, and microphone revisions that trained it. Tiered software architecture enforces separation of concerns, isolating the device’s audio drivers and feature extractor from the hub’s verification model and the cloud’s aggregation, so that firmware, model, and fallback behavior can be updated independently. A feature-extraction kernel shared between training and the device reduces implementation divergence, while parity tests verify numerical agreement between the Python pipeline and the device’s DSP routine across firmware versions. Privacy-preserving telemetry keeps degradation observable by sending aggregate diagnostics (per-band energy histograms, wake counts, duty cycle, and battery drain) without streaming audio off the device. Finally, cost-aware automation applies to the over-the-air update, because every update must show that its accuracy gain outweighs the radio energy of reception, the flash rewrite cycles, and the risk of altering software on a device that is always listening.

The keyword spotter shows how milliwatts, kilobytes, and radio bandwidth dictate MLOps design. Moving machine learning from consumer devices into regulated healthcare shifts the primary constraint from hardware physics to patient safety, clinical validation, and algorithmic accountability, the operational domain of ClinAIOps.

### ClinAIOps Case Study

^sec-ml-operations-clinaiops-case-study-5d5d

In regulated medical care, an unreviewed model update or an uncalibrated sensor stream in a blood-pressure monitoring system can recommend an unsafe dose before a physician sees the chart, shifting the governing operational constraint from device physics to patient safety and clinical liability. In continuous therapeutic monitoring (CTM),[[01 Books/Volume I - Foundations/04 Deployment/14 ML Operations/V1-14.15 Footnotes#^fn28|²⁸]] wearable sensors stream physiological telemetry to guide personalized medical interventions. General-purpose MLOps frameworks provide lifecycle automation (orchestrating pipelines, tracking artifacts, and monitoring availability). Clinical use adds intended-use-specific evidence, oversight, and authorization requirements because predictions may influence treatment. General-purpose MLOps can incorporate human review steps; a clinical operations framework makes their responsibilities explicit. The case is read here as an operations loop with clinicians inside it, specifying who may change a model, on what evidence, and how a change is reversed. Whether the objectives such a system pursues, and their effects on patients, are the right ones is a question of responsibility that **[[01 Books/Volume I - Foundations/04 Deployment/15 Responsible Engineering/V1-15 Responsible Engineering#^sec-responsible-engineering|Responsible Engineering]]** develops.

The proposed ClinAIOps framework ([[01 Books/Volume I - Foundations/04 Deployment/14 ML Operations/V1-14.14 Source References#^ref-chen2023|Chen et al. 2023]]) adapts MLOps principles to these constraints by formalizing the boundaries between algorithmic inference and clinical authority. Rather than treating human oversight as an external obstacle, ClinAIOps builds multi-party feedback loops directly into the serving architecture. The framework partitions operational control across three actors (patients, clinicians, and machine learning engineers), each operating on distinct timescales with explicit approval steps.

#### Feedback Loops

^sec-ml-operations-feedback-loops-3cdd

Three interlocking feedback loops organize the proposed integration of machine learning into clinical practice; their effectiveness and safety require validation in the intended setting. [[01 Books/Volume I - Foundations/04 Deployment/14 ML Operations/V1-14.10 Case Studies#^fig-clinaiops|Figure 13]] models these three interlocking feedback loops as a triangular protocol among the patient, clinician, and AI developer. Each pair of actors exchanges different information in both directions. The patient streams wearable sensor telemetry and receives clinician-approved therapy alerts. The AI developer provides longitudinal monitoring summaries while accepting physician-defined dosing bounds. The clinician directly governs patient therapy regimens while establishing safety boundaries for algorithmic actuation. The outer cycle connecting all three nodes forms the overarching clinical governance framework.

Figure 13: **ClinAIOps Feedback Loops**: The cyclical framework coordinates patients, clinicians, and AI developers to support continuous model improvement and safe clinical integration. Patients and clinicians use AI outputs in care workflows, while AI developers receive feedback from both groups to refine models and operations. ^fig-clinaiops

Each feedback loop operates on a distinct timescale to balance responsiveness against clinical verification. The **patient treatment loop** executes continuously in real time, capturing sensor telemetry and delivering bounded adjustments for patient self-management. At an asynchronous supervisory cadence, the **clinician oversight loop** reviews aggregated trends, sets operational boundaries, and validates treatment recommendations before interventions occur. Finally, the **developer feedback loop** aggregates telemetry and clinician override patterns back to engineering teams, driving offline retraining and interface calibration.

##### Patient Treatment Loop

^sec-ml-operations-patientai-loop-09c1

The patient treatment loop executes localized inference over high-frequency physiological streams captured by wearable devices, such as continuous glucose monitors or optical pulse sensors. The edge serving runtime evaluates these incoming feature vectors against recent patient history from electronic health records. In this proposed design, recommendations follow a tiered execution model as one safety control. Minor adjustments that fall strictly within clinician-defined bounds are surfaced directly to the patient. Significant changes, and recommendations generated when model uncertainty exceeds an acceptable margin, are blocked from automated delivery and escalated to the clinician. This structure enables rapid adaptation to dynamic physiological states within validated, authorized operating limits; those limits and the escalation mechanism must be tested.

##### Clinician Oversight Loop

^sec-ml-operations-clinicianai-loop-8d56

The clinician oversight loop provides supervisory control over automated inference. Rather than burdening medical staff with continuous raw sensor telemetry, batch aggregation pipelines distill time-series streams into longitudinal summaries, such as circadian variance, multi-day trends, and signal-quality metrics. When the system generates a therapy recommendation, such as lowering the dose of a blood-pressure medication (an antihypertensive), the clinician evaluates the proposal alongside diagnostic factors unobserved by the wearable model and may approve, modify, or reject the recommendation. This decision is written to an audited event log, which enforces clinical accountability at the point of care and records expert feedback for later review. Approval or override is not by itself a verified patient outcome and using it as training supervision requires a defined label interpretation and validation. Clinicians also update the operational thresholds that govern the patient treatment loop, recalibrating the boundary between automated guidance and mandatory review.

##### Developer Feedback and Patient-Clinician Coordination

^sec-ml-operations-patientclinician-loop-3f3a

Offloading continuous data logging and low-level trend detection to the AI pipeline changes the patient-clinician relationship itself. Consultations shift from routine measurement gathering to targeted clinical evaluation and goal setting, an outcome the patient and clinician loops produce together, while the third loop, developer feedback, links that clinical practice back to engineering infrastructure. Operational telemetry generated during these interactions provides engineering teams with direct visibility into production behavior. Overrides, rejection frequencies, and reported adverse events motivate investigation of model behavior, workflow fit, patient mix, and sensor quality, even though they do not uniquely diagnose covariate shift. Engineers use the resulting evidence to evaluate targeted retraining, refine data validation schemas, and eliminate usability failure modes that induce alert fatigue in clinical staff.

#### Hypertension Case Example

^sec-ml-operations-hypertension-case-example-7b1f

In the proposed framework, the three operational loops could coordinate care for hypertension management. Blood pressure fluctuates continuously under exertion, stress, and circadian cycles, requiring individualized, long-term pharmacological adjustments that benefit from continuous therapeutic monitoring.

##### Data Infrastructure

^sec-ml-operations-data-infrastructure-b481

Research systems estimate blood pressure indirectly from wearable pulse signals such as photoplethysmography (PPG) ([[01 Books/Volume I - Foundations/04 Deployment/14 ML Operations/V1-14.14 Source References#^ref-zhang2017|Zhang et al. 2017]]). In the illustrative workflow, these signals are augmented by accelerometer streams to identify motion artifacts and self-reported medication logs. Clinical use requires rigorous calibration and prospective validation for the intended task, together with any applicable regulatory authorization; authorization alone does not establish accuracy for every cohort. When properly validated for the target cohort, this multimodal data stream is merged with electronic health records to supply feature context for inference.

##### Loop Implementation

^sec-ml-operations-loop-implementation-2556

The hypertension workflow labels each of its three panels by the two parties in the exchange ([[01 Books/Volume I - Foundations/04 Deployment/14 ML Operations/V1-14.10 Case Studies#^fig-interactive-loop|figure 14]]), so the panels do not line up one-to-one with the three loops of [[01 Books/Volume I - Foundations/04 Deployment/14 ML Operations/V1-14.10 Case Studies#^fig-clinaiops|figure 13]]. The patient-AI panel is the patient treatment loop, the clinical-AI panel is the clinician oversight loop, and the patient-clinical panel is the coordination those two loops produce together. The developer feedback loop operates out of band, as described in [[01 Books/Volume I - Foundations/04 Deployment/14 ML Operations/V1-14.10 Case Studies#^sec-ml-operations-feedback-loops-3cdd|section 14.9.2.1]], and does not appear.

Across the top of the figure, the two AI-assisted loops operate concurrently. The patient-AI panel (upper left) captures continuous blood-pressure telemetry and issues recommendations for bounded dose titrations, while the clinical-AI panel (upper right) alerts the physician to severe hypertensive excursions and accepts updated titration boundaries. Below them, the patient-clinical panel captures the resulting clinical relationship, where routine telemetry offloaded to the AI loop frees consultation appointments for longitudinal trend review, adverse-event evaluation, and shared lifestyle goal setting.

The three panels illustrate a proposed boundary for clinical automation, not an established treatment protocol: routine monitoring and bounded dose recommendations operate within validated, authorized clinician-defined thresholds, while acute escalations, adverse events, and regimen changes require explicit human authorization. That boundary is the point where ordinary MLOps practices require the additional clinical coordination summarized next.

#### MLOps Vs. ClinAIOps Comparison

^sec-ml-operations-mlops-vs-clinaiops-comparison-94be

Healthcare deployments such as the hypertension case differ operationally from standard software pipelines. General-purpose MLOps provides technical lifecycle controls such as automated testing, continuous integration, model registries, and drift monitoring. ClinAIOps extends this foundation into regulated clinical environments where outputs directly influence medical interventions. [[01 Books/Volume I - Foundations/04 Deployment/14 ML Operations/V1-14.10 Case Studies#^tbl-clinical-ops-5057b6e8|Table 27]] compares the two approaches across eight operational dimensions.

|                         | **General-purpose MLOps emphasis**               | **ClinAIOps extension**                                 |
|:------------------------|:-------------------------------------------------|:--------------------------------------------------------|
| **Focus**               | Technical model lifecycle                        | Human and AI decision-making                            |
| **Stakeholders**        | ML, data, platform, and operations teams         | Adds patients, clinicians, and clinical governance      |
| **Feedback loops**      | Monitoring, retraining, and release              | Adds treatment, clinician, and developer feedback       |
| **Objective**           | Reliable, governed ML operations                 | Safe, effective, and accountable care support           |
| **Processes**           | Automated pipelines and operational controls     | Integrates clinical workflows and human review          |
| **Data considerations** | Lineage, quality, freshness, and access control  | Adds consent, clinical provenance, and protected data   |
| **Model validation**    | Predictive, operational, and slice-level metrics | Adds clinical utility, safety, and cohort outcomes      |
| **Implementation**      | Technical integration and operational ownership  | Adds clinical accountability and stakeholder incentives |

Table 27: **Clinical AI Operations**: General-purpose MLOps supplies technical lifecycle controls; ClinAIOps extends them with clinical workflows, evidence, human oversight, and accountability.

^tbl-clinical-ops-5057b6e8

The table’s central distinction is that clinical deployment alters risk ownership. Statistical accuracy is a prerequisite, but it does not protect against liability when an erroneous prediction drives a medical intervention. The ClinAIOps framework therefore changes the governing constraint from device efficiency to clinical accountability. The model participates in care delivery, but it cannot own clinical decisions. Every recommendation and subsequent clinician action must be traceable to the underlying sensor inputs, model version, and inference metadata; otherwise the system cannot support regulatory audit or retrospective incident analysis. Separation of concerns shifts from an architectural preference to a safety invariant: sensor ingestion, inference scoring, clinical diagnosis, and parameter updates must be decoupled by explicit software interfaces and human approval steps.

The same accountability requirement transforms monitoring. Standardized clinical data pipelines support training-serving parity, but clinical validation requires evaluating model recommendations against standard-of-care outcomes and cohort-specific effects. Observable degradation extends beyond statistical loss and latency to clinical metrics: blood pressure control rates, adverse events, clinician overrides, and subgroup disparities. In this regime, multi-tier feedback loops are not unwanted operational latency; they are deliberate architectural controls that balance automated edge adaptation against legal and ethical accountability. Cost-aware automation operates strictly inside those approval limits. Model refreshes occur only when validated clinical benefits outweigh validation costs and patient risks, and low-confidence predictions trigger deterministic fallback to human clinical review.

### Case Study Synthesis

^sec-ml-operations-case-study-synthesis

The keyword spotter and ClinAIOps cases separate stable MLOps principles from the deployment constraints that reshape their implementation. Copying an edge OTA pipeline into a hospital workflow or requiring clinician sign-off on every consumer wake-word update fails because it mistakes a domain-specific tool for the underlying principle. The keyword spotter is a resource-envelope case. Its operations must preserve reproducibility, consistency, and observable degradation while battery, telemetry, and weak ground truth limit what can be measured and updated on the device. ClinAIOps is an accountability-envelope case. The same principles apply, but validation, audit trails, and human review dominate because the model influences clinical action.

The shared engineering lesson is that MLOps maturity is not tool accumulation. It is the ability to identify the governing constraint, choose the operational controls that match it, and preserve evidence when the model changes. Production ML systems fail when teams apply code-focused operational intuitions without accounting for statistical behavior and shifting distributions, exposing common fallacies and operational pitfalls.
