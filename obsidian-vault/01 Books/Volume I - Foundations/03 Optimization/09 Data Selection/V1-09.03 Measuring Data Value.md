# V1-09.03 Measuring Data Value

[[Home|Home]] · [[01 Books/Volume I - Foundations/Volume 1 Overview|Volume Overview]] · [[01 Books/Volume I - Foundations/03 Optimization/09 Data Selection/V1-09 Data Selection|V1-09 Data Selection]]

^sec-data-selection-measurement-framework-733b

A ResNet-50 trained on an aggressively filtered ImageNet subset can hold its aggregate accuracy while failing on rare classes that the filter discarded as low-density noise. A GPT-2 run on a filtered corpus can cost less per epoch while needing more epochs to reach the same loss. A measurement framework is therefore needed to show what a reduction actually saves. Every data selection technique claims to improve efficiency, but only measurement separates genuine training savings from work that has merely moved to another pipeline stage, or from degradation on safety-critical tail distributions. The measures build from the information-compute ratio through a diagnostic of whether a run is data-limited, metrics that tie sample reduction to accuracy, and checks that a reduced set still covers the cases deployment needs, to the selection inequality that every training-time claim must satisfy.

### The Information-Compute Ratio

^sec-data-selection-informationcompute-ratio-8c0b

Those examples show what pruning too far costs, and keeping every example fails in the opposite direction. Between the two failures lies a Pareto frontier (principle [[01 Books/Volume I - Foundations/03 Optimization/V1 Optimization Principles#^pri-pareto-frontier|5]]) between accuracy and cost, the curve of the best accuracy achievable at each cost. Retaining every example can sit off that curve when it spends FLOPs on redundant signal without improving quality, whereas pruning too far can sacrifice rare-slice recall. A better-chosen subset may preserve that recall at the same cost. Locating a run on that frontier requires measuring how much learning signal each sample yields per unit of hardware work. Data engineering (**[[01 Books/Volume I - Foundations/01 Foundations/04 Data Engineering/V1-04 Data Engineering#^sec-data-engineering|Data Engineering]]**) already divided a record’s task-relevant signal by the cost of moving its bytes (data gravity), a storage-side ratio it calls the data selection gain (**[[01 Books/Volume I - Foundations/01 Foundations/04 Data Engineering/V1-04.10 Physics of Data#^eq-data-selection-gain|equation 4.3]]**). Selection puts training compute in the denominator instead, because a sample that is cheap to move still costs a full forward and backward pass. That ratio is the **information-compute ratio** (ICR).

↪ Locating a model’s operating point on the accuracy-efficiency frontier, and moving along it with compression, is developed in **[[01 Books/Volume I - Foundations/03 Optimization/10 Model Compression/V1-10.06 Choosing and Combining Techniques#^sec-model-compression-balancing-tradeoffs-6ae3|The accuracy-efficiency frontier]]**.

Let ${\Delta}{\mathcal{I}}$ be the learning signal a sample or batch adds and ${\Delta}{O}$ the training operations spent processing it. In practice ${\Delta}{\mathcal{I}}$ is measured as the gain in a fixed evaluation metric, such as percentage points of validation accuracy, so ICR carries units of metric gain per FLOP. The ratio relates the two:

$$
\text{ICR} = \frac{{\Delta}{\mathcal{I}}}{{\Delta}{O}}
$$

A higher ICR indicates that each FLOP of training yields more measured learning signal over the stated interval. Validation gain and training FLOPs can be observed for a specified experiment with a fixed evaluation protocol; the causal contribution of an individual example generally cannot. ICR is therefore measured for a batch, a subset, or an interval of a run, and the value of a sample means the value of the samples in that measured set. Per-example scores built from the model’s uncertainty or its gradients, developed with the coreset algorithms of [[01 Books/Volume I - Foundations/03 Optimization/09 Data Selection/V1-09.04 Static Pruning#^sec-data-selection-coreset-selection-algorithms-2c74|section 9.3.4]], instead rank candidate examples before their downstream benefit is known. Those rankings require validation against measured quality, compute cost, and deployment-critical slice coverage. The core metrics in [[01 Books/Volume I - Foundations/03 Optimization/09 Data Selection/V1-09.03 Measuring Data Value#^sec-data-selection-core-metrics-32e9|section 9.2.4]] supply complementary evidence for that evaluation rather than interchangeable measurements of per-example information, and they also track how ICR decays as the dataset grows.

### Diminishing Returns and the ICR Frontier

^sec-data-selection-icr-frontier

As a training run adds more examples from the same source, a growing share of those examples repeat patterns the model has already seen, and the information-compute ratio falls. The **ICR frontier** is the dataset size at which adding more of that data stops paying. Beyond it, the marginal ICR, the learning returned by the next FLOP spent on more data, drops below what that FLOP could return elsewhere, such as a larger model, a different data source, or an earlier stop. More data from this source then raises cost faster than accuracy, so the run falls further below the accuracy-cost Pareto frontier of the previous section. Despite the shared name, the ICR frontier is one stopping point for one data source, not a curve like the Pareto frontier. The compute-optimal frontier of [[01 Books/Volume I - Foundations/03 Optimization/09 Data Selection/V1-09.03 Measuring Data Value#^sec-data-selection-computeoptimal-frontier-24ba|section 9.2.3]] answers a third question, how a fixed compute budget should be split between model size and training data.

To illustrate diminishing returns, let ${\mathcal{I}}({D})$ be the information content of a dataset of size ${D}$ and assume ${\mathcal{I}}({D}) \propto \log {D}$. This is a simplified analytical model rather than a universal law. If compute cost scales linearly with the per-sample operation count ${O_{\text{sample}}}$, then total operations are ${O} = {O_{\text{sample}}} \cdot {D}$, and the resulting marginal ICR follows [[01 Books/Volume I - Foundations/03 Optimization/09 Data Selection/V1-09.03 Measuring Data Value#^eq-icr-decay|equation 1]]:

$$
\text{ICR}({D}) = \frac{{d}{\mathcal{I}}/d{D}}{d{O}/d{D}} \approx \frac{1/{D}}{{O_{\text{sample}}}} = \frac{1}{{O_{\text{sample}}}\cdot {D}} \tag{1}
$$

^eq-icr-decay

The derivative ratio in [[01 Books/Volume I - Foundations/03 Optimization/09 Data Selection/V1-09.03 Measuring Data Value#^eq-icr-decay|equation 1]] describes the return on an infinitesimal increase in dataset size; for two finite dataset sizes, the ratio is the difference in evaluation gain divided by the difference in training FLOPs. Under this illustrative model, marginal ICR falls as $1/({O_{\text{sample}}} \cdot {D})$, so doubling the dataset halves the learning each additional FLOP yields. Inverted, the compute spent per unit of learning grows linearly with ${D}$. ICR never reaches zero in this model and the curve has no knee of its own, so the frontier is located by comparing marginal ICR with a threshold, not read off the curve. The threshold is the opportunity cost named in the definition, what the same FLOP would return in its best alternative use. A team estimates that threshold by measuring, over the same FLOP interval, the gain from the best alternative it can actually run, such as a larger model trained on the current data, and stops adding data from this source once the data’s marginal gain falls below it.

That saturation is distinct from the data wall (a limit on how much new, high-quality data exists), but the two compound. When new data is scarce *and* marginal data is weak, selection becomes the main lever. Beyond the frontier, additional data still adds compute but returns less learning than the threshold demands. In this regime additional data inflates the ${O}$ term of the iron law without a commensurate gain in accuracy. Measured in dollars rather than FLOPs, the same excess lowers the return on compute (RoC) of **[[01 Books/Volume I - Foundations/00 Introduction/01 Introduction/V1-01.09 Three Dimensions of ML Efficiency#^eq-intro-roc|equation 1.6]]**, the accuracy gained per added dollar of infrastructure.

↩︎ Return on compute, the economic lens that measures accuracy gains against infrastructure spending, is established in **[[01 Books/Volume I - Foundations/00 Introduction/01 Introduction/V1-01.09 Three Dimensions of ML Efficiency#^sec-introduction-roc-invariant|Return on compute: Weighing accuracy against cost]]**.

Data selection turns the total operations $({O})$ term in the iron law from a fixed constant into a variable. A 2$\times$ improvement in measured ICR halves the FLOPs required to reach the same measured gain. It produces the same ideal time reduction as a 2$\times$ throughput increase only when the workload is compute-bound and the saved work lies on the critical path. FLOPs saved on the accelerator shorten the run only when the accelerator’s arithmetic sets its pace. When the run is instead waiting on storage or host decoding, it shortens only by the work the reduction removes from that slower stage ([[01 Books/Volume I - Foundations/03 Optimization/09 Data Selection/V1-09.08 Selection Engineering#^sec-data-selection-selection-engineering-a4eb|section 9.7]]). ICR focuses specifically on compute; the cost model of [[01 Books/Volume I - Foundations/03 Optimization/09 Data Selection/V1-09.09 Choosing a Selection Strategy#^sec-data-selection-quantifying-data-costs-roi-f0b4|section 9.8.1]] extends the same reasoning to acquisition, labeling, and storage costs.

The ratio becomes concrete on a *coreset*, a deliberately selected subset intended to preserve the full dataset’s learning signal. Computing ICR for an ImageNet coreset used to train the ResNet-50 lighthouse shows how large the efficiency gap could become once dataset size, per-sample model cost, and selection strategy meet concrete FLOP budgets. The comparison averages gain over one epoch of each dataset rather than measuring the incremental return on the next data slice, and it uses assumed accuracy gains to illustrate the arithmetic rather than a reported result for the scoring method (EL2N) that [[01 Books/Volume I - Foundations/03 Optimization/09 Data Selection/V1-09.04 Static Pruning#^sec-data-selection-coreset-selection-algorithms-2c74|section 9.3.4]] develops.

**Napkin Math 9.1: Computing ICR: Coresets**

**Problem**: Training the ResNet-50 lighthouse model on ImageNet for one epoch costs 3.2 × 10¹⁶ FLOPs. Under the scenario’s assumed accuracy gains, how would retaining 50 percent of the data change the information-compute ratio? The calculation holds the per-sample work fixed and does not include selection overhead.

**Setup**:

- Dataset: ImageNet (1.28M)
- Model: ResNet-50 lighthouse (~8.2 GFLOP per forward pass, roughly 24.6 GFLOP for a forward plus backward training step, depending on implementation)
- One epoch: 1.28M $\times$ 24.6 GFLOP = 3.2 × 10¹⁶ FLOPs
- Assumed accuracy improvement per epoch (early training): 5 percentage points

**Random selection (baseline)**:

- Process all 1.28M samples uniformly
- Accuracy gain: 5 percentage points
- $\text{ICR}_{\text{random}}$ = 5 percentage points / (3.2 × 10¹⁶ FLOPs) = $1.6 \times 10^{-16}$ per FLOP

**Difficulty-scored coreset** (EL2N, a training-dynamics score defined in [[01 Books/Volume I - Foundations/03 Optimization/09 Data Selection/V1-09.04 Static Pruning#^sec-data-selection-coreset-selection-algorithms-2c74|section 9.3.4]]; 50 percent of data):

- Process 640.6K selected samples
- Assumed accuracy gain: 4.5 percentage points (90 percent of the full-data gain)
- Compute: 640.6K $\times$ 24.6 GFLOP = 1.6 × 10¹⁶ FLOPs
- $\text{ICR}_{\text{coreset}}$ = 4.5 percentage points / (1.6 × 10¹⁶ FLOPs) = $2.9 \times 10^{-16}$ per FLOP

**Systems insight**: Under these assumptions, the selected subset achieves 1.8× higher ICR with a 0.5 percentage points smaller accuracy gain. The comparison is learning per FLOP over one epoch of each dataset. It does not show that the coreset reaches the full-data run’s final accuracy, which must be checked separately at equal quality, and a real decision also requires measured gains, selection overhead, and coverage checks.

**Checkpoint 9.1: Data selection efficiency**

The goal is to improve ICR subject to target quality and required coverage, then verify that the resulting pipeline reduces the cost it is intended to address.

**Metric checks**

**ICR application**: Given two training runs with identical accuracy gains but different compute budgets, determine which achieved higher ICR.

**Data efficiency**: The coreset notebook reports 1.8× higher ICR but a smaller per-epoch gain. Explain what would have to change for a 50 percent subset to match the full-data gain, which would give it 2× higher ICR and so deliver the same measured gain for half the training FLOPs.

**Pipeline checks**

**The three stages**: Map static pruning, dynamic selection, and synthetic generation to the training lifecycle.

A reduction in sample count remains a claim until it is shown to translate into end-to-end efficiency without silently compromising generalization. Notebook [9.1](https://mlsysbook.ai/vol1/09_data_selection/09_data_selection.html#nbk-data-selection-computing-icr-coresets) holds per-sample work fixed and assumes its accuracy gains. Testing the claim on a real workload first requires knowing whether the run is limited by data at all, and then measured metrics that weigh arithmetic savings, data movement overhead, and task quality across the complete pipeline.

### The Compute-Optimal Frontier

^sec-data-selection-computeoptimal-frontier-24ba

Discarding unique, useful tokens when the model is already starved for them hurts accuracy rather than saving time, whereas collecting more data for a model that has not yet trained long enough to use the data it has wastes money on collection. Before evaluating any selection technique, a team must therefore test whether the training run is limited by data or by compute. Neural scaling-law experiments ([[01 Books/Volume I - Foundations/03 Optimization/09 Data Selection/V1-09.14 Source References#^ref-kaplan2020scaling|Kaplan et al. 2020]]; [[01 Books/Volume I - Foundations/03 Optimization/09 Data Selection/V1-09.14 Source References#^ref-hoffmann2022chinchilla|Hoffmann et al. 2022]]) fit power-law relationships over particular model families, datasets, and compute ranges. Controlled sweeps can use those fitted relationships to compare model and data allocations, though no single ratio diagnoses every training run.

The Chinchilla study[[01 Books/Volume I - Foundations/03 Optimization/09 Data Selection/V1-09.15 Footnotes#^fn3|³]] ([[01 Books/Volume I - Foundations/03 Optimization/09 Data Selection/V1-09.14 Source References#^ref-hoffmann2022chinchilla|Hoffmann et al. 2022]]) fit a compute-optimal balance between model size and training data within its experimental regime. Allocations on either side of that fitted balance used the study’s compute budget less effectively. The optimal balance defines a **compute-optimal frontier**: the best achievable performance at each compute budget when data and model size are properly balanced. Plotted over normalized training compute, the frontier separates the optimal operating envelope from the compute-starved and data-starved allocations in the suboptimal region below it ([[01 Books/Volume I - Foundations/03 Optimization/09 Data Selection/V1-09.03 Measuring Data Value#^fig-compute-optimal-frontier|figure 2]]). A run is *data-starved* when it trains on too few unique, useful tokens for its parameter count, so its tokens-per-parameter ratio sits well under the fitted balance, and the same compute would yield more accuracy with more or better data. A run is *compute-starved* in the opposite case, when it holds more useful data than its compute budget lets it train on, so more training compute, not more data, moves it toward the frontier.

![[90 Assets/Volume I/09 Data Selection/Cell 5 Output 1 38548f3adb.png]]

Figure 2: **Illustrative Compute-Optimal Frontier**: The green curve is a conceptual frontier over normalized compute, not a fitted result from Chinchilla. Points below it illustrate allocations that a controlled model-size and token-count sweep might identify as data- or compute-limited.

^fig-compute-optimal-frontier

The frontier answers one allocation question, whether the next unit of compute at a given budget should go to more parameters or to more training tokens. Against a frontier fitted from controlled sweeps, an operating point’s location motivates further experiments. The directional vectors charted in [[01 Books/Volume I - Foundations/03 Optimization/09 Data Selection/V1-09.03 Measuring Data Value#^fig-compute-optimal-frontier|figure 2]] illustrate two candidate interventions: additional training compute and improved data selection at fixed compute. Controlled changes to model size, data, and training allocation determine which intervention helps; a point below the frontier does not identify a unique remedy. Points near a fitted frontier remain specific to the model family, dataset, objective, and compute range used to estimate it.

#### Interpreting the Chinchilla Result

^interpreting-the-chinchilla-result

Within the fitted Chinchilla regime, compute-optimal model parameters and training tokens grew at roughly equal rates.[[01 Books/Volume I - Foundations/03 Optimization/09 Data Selection/V1-09.15 Footnotes#^fn4|⁴]] Training a dense transformer costs approximately $6{P}{D}$ FLOPs for ${P}$ parameters and ${D}$ tokens, the forward-plus-backward accounting derived for the GPT-2 lighthouse in **[[01 Books/Volume I - Foundations/02 Development/08 Model Training/V1-08.02 From One Step to a Training Run#^sec-model-training-running-example-training-gpt2-19cd|Running cases: GPT-2 XL and ResNet-50]]**. Under an equal scaling regime where parameters and tokens grow proportionally (${P} \propto {D}$), total compute scales quadratically (${O} \propto {D}^2$), directly implying that optimal token count scales as the square root of the compute budget (${D_{\text{opt}}} \propto \sqrt{{O}}$). Doubling compute therefore corresponds to about $\sqrt{2} - 1$, or 41.4 percent, more tokens under those assumptions. The study’s fitted balance, commonly quoted as about 20 tokens per parameter, is the reference value for the diagnostic that follows, not a universal optimum.

GPT-2 sits well short of that reference. WebText supplies about 6.7 unique tokens per parameter, a third of the fitted balance, and the original run made up the difference by repeating those tokens about 17 times ([[01 Books/Volume I - Foundations/03 Optimization/09 Data Selection/V1-09.02 The Data Wall#^sec-data-selection-data-selection-fundamentals-e839|section 9.1]]). Repetition keeps the arithmetic budget but not the learning per FLOP, which is the regime where better tokens, rather than more compute, move the run toward the compute-optimal frontier.

#### Applying the Diagnostic

^applying-the-diagnostic

If a training run underperforms expectations, extending one run can reveal whether additional optimization steps still help, but a plateau does not identify data starvation by itself. Learning-rate schedules, optimization limits, model capacity, and data quality can all produce a plateau. A reliable diagnosis compares controlled runs that vary model size, token count, and compute allocation while holding the evaluation protocol fixed.

When controlled runs do point to the data, the remedy that keeps compute fixed is better selection. For a data-starved run, selection means raising the value of each token, never shrinking the pool of unique, useful tokens. It removes repeats and weak tokens, then spends the freed compute on better or new data, such as a cleaner source or synthetic examples ([[01 Books/Volume I - Foundations/03 Optimization/09 Data Selection/V1-09.06 Synthetic Data Generation#^sec-data-selection-synthetic-data-generation-415c|section 9.5]]), or on a curriculum that orders what remains. Pruning to save time pays in the opposite case, when the run holds more useful data than its compute can use. Either way, selection changes the shape of the learning curve itself, and seeing that change requires switching axes, from the frontier’s training compute to dataset size, where the core metrics measure what a reduction preserves.

### Core Metrics

^sec-data-selection-core-metrics-32e9

Reporting accuracy at a single subset size hides whether a selection method learned faster early on, plateaued lower, or never reached the full dataset’s target quality. Three metrics track those distinct behaviors across the learning curve and complement the measured information-compute ratio ([[01 Books/Volume I - Foundations/03 Optimization/09 Data Selection/V1-09.03 Measuring Data Value#^sec-data-selection-informationcompute-ratio-8c0b|section 9.2.1]]). Performance-per-data (PPD) measures average accuracy gain per sample, which becomes gain per FLOP once per-sample cost is fixed. The area under the learning curve (AULC) summarizes performance across a common dataset-size range. The data compression ratio (DCR) measures retained sample reduction at a fixed target accuracy. None of the three establishes the causal value of an individual example.

#### Performance-Per-Data

^performance-per-data

The most direct metric, **performance-per-data (PPD)**, measures accuracy gain per sample:

$$
\text{PPD}({n}) = \frac{\text{Accuracy}({n}) - \text{Accuracy}(0)}{{n}}
$$

 where ${n}$ is the number of training samples. A higher PPD indicates greater average improvement per sample over the stated interval. Tracked across sample counts, PPD traces a curve whose shape must be measured, because diminishing returns and their onset are workload-dependent.

#### Area Under the Learning Curve

^area-under-the-learning-curve

Rather than comparing at a single point, the **area under the learning curve** (AULC) integrates performance across all dataset sizes:

$$
\text{AULC} = \int_0^{D}\text{Accuracy}({n}) \, d{n}
$$

 where ${n}$ is the dataset size and ${D}$ is the total dataset size.

For the same metric and integration range, a higher AULC means stronger integrated performance across dataset sizes, though one strategy need not outperform the other at every size. Comparisons must use the same upper limit ${D}$ or normalize the integral. Measuring AULC requires training at several dataset sizes, so the metric itself spends compute, and when it guides a selection decision that cost belongs to the selection overhead.

In the illustrative curves of [[01 Books/Volume I - Foundations/03 Optimization/09 Data Selection/V1-09.03 Measuring Data Value#^fig-ppd-curve|figure 3]], efficient selection bends into an accuracy plateau near 25 percent of the data while uniform random sampling climbs gradually across the entire budget. Over the same range, the efficient curve therefore encloses the larger area. The horizontal separation between the curves is the reduction in required samples, and at an identical 20 percent data budget the vertical gap is the accuracy that selection adds without any additional compute.

#### Data Compression Ratio

^data-compression-ratio

For coreset methods, the **data compression ratio** (DCR) measures how much data reduction is achieved at a target accuracy:

$$
\text{DCR} = \frac{{D_{\text{total}}}}{{D_{\text{subset}}}} \text{ at } \text{Accuracy}_{\text{target}}
$$

A DCR of 5× means the coreset achieves target accuracy with 20 percent of the data. Applied to the coreset notebook, the three metrics behave differently. Over one epoch, the full ImageNet set gains 5 percentage points while the half-size coreset gains 4.5 percentage points, so the coreset’s PPD is higher by the same 1.8× as its ICR, because per-sample work is fixed. DCR cannot be computed from that notebook at all, because the two runs never reach a common target accuracy. Exposing that missing equal-quality check is exactly the job DCR is designed for. Even when a reduced dataset matches the full dataset’s aggregate accuracy, those learning-curve metrics cannot show whether the retained subset still covers every slice deployment needs.

### Validating a Reduced Dataset

^sec-data-selection-validating-reduced-dataset

When the half-size ImageNet coreset drops 640,000 images, it can hold top-1 test accuracy while quietly stripping out a rare class, shifting the training distribution away from production traffic, or concentrating mislabeled examples in the retained subset. Validating a curated dataset therefore requires checking coverage, distribution alignment, and label quality alongside aggregate accuracy. Coverage metrics check whether coreset selection kept enough examples from each class and demographic group, the minimum that [[01 Books/Volume I - Foundations/03 Optimization/09 Data Selection/V1-09.04 Static Pruning#^sec-data-selection-coreset-selection-algorithms-2c74|section 9.3.4]] sets as a coverage floor. Even when class counts hold, the retained subset can skew toward atypical examples, so distribution alignment metrics (such as KL divergence and the population stability index, the drift statistics of **[[01 Books/Volume I - Foundations/01 Foundations/04 Data Engineering/V1-04.13 Operational Data Health#^sec-data-engineering-detecting-responding-data-drift-509a|Data drift detection and response]]**) test whether the curated training set has drifted from the deployment distribution. Where selection also guides annotation, label quality metrics (inter-annotator agreement and confident learning, which flags examples whose given label disagrees with a model’s confident out-of-sample prediction) verify that active learning did not introduce systematic labeling errors. A 50 percent dataset reduction is worth keeping only when all three checks pass alongside target accuracy, calibration, and robustness.

↪ Coverage, label-quality, and distribution-alignment checks are developed into a full data-benchmarking protocol in **[[01 Books/Volume I - Foundations/03 Optimization/12 Benchmarking/V1-12.10 Model and Data Evaluation#^sec-benchmarking-data-benchmarking-22f9|Data benchmarking]]**.

The validation target can itself be unreliable. Benchmark accuracy can overstate generalization when test sets fail to capture operational distribution shifts, so evaluation benchmarks demand the same scrutiny as curated training sets.

**Example 9.1: The benchmark replication gap**

**Scenario**: Classifiers evaluated on newly collected test sets experienced 3 to 15 percentage-point accuracy drops on CIFAR-10 and 11 to 14 points on ImageNet ([[01 Books/Volume I - Foundations/03 Optimization/09 Data Selection/V1-09.14 Source References#^ref-recht2019imagenet|Recht et al. 2019]]).

**Diagnosis**: The replicated collection produced slightly harder images than the original test sets. The study found that benchmark adaptivity did not explain the gap, even though repeated use of a fixed test set remains a general evaluation risk.

**Systems lesson**: Evaluation-set construction affects measured generalization. Data-selection pipelines should validate curated subsets on independently collected and deployment-relevant test sets rather than trusting one long-reused benchmark.

^exmp-data-selection-benchmark-replication-gap

An efficiency gain measured against a single benchmark can evaporate under distribution shift, so a curated subset must demonstrate consistent quality across multiple held-out distributions before its reported reduction ratio is trusted. Even when quality holds across those distributions, the time spent selecting the subset must still be weighed against the training time it saves.

### The Selection Inequality

^sec-data-selection-selection-inequality-3b7e

The efficiency measures so far count learning against training compute alone, yet a selection method also spends time choosing, and a claim that it shortens training must count that time. To reduce overall training time at a fixed quality and coverage target, selection plus subset training must take less time than training on the full corpus. Let ${T_{\text{selection}}}$ be total wall-clock selection overhead, including candidate scoring and any prerequisite scorer preparation charged to the training run. Let ${T_{\text{train}}}({D})$ be wall-clock training time for a dataset of size ${D}$, and let ${D_{\text{subset}}}$ and ${D_{\text{total}}}$ be the retained and full sample counts. [[01 Books/Volume I - Foundations/03 Optimization/09 Data Selection/V1-09.03 Measuring Data Value#^eq-selection-inequality|Equation 2]] formalizes this break-even condition as the **selection inequality**:

$$
{T_{\text{selection}}}+ {T_{\text{train}}}({D_{\text{subset}}}) < {T_{\text{train}}}({D_{\text{total}}}) \tag{2}
$$

^eq-selection-inequality

Equivalently, isolating the allowable selection overhead yields ${T_{\text{selection}}}< {T_{\text{train}}}({D_{\text{total}}}) - {T_{\text{train}}}({D_{\text{subset}}})$. The upper bound on selection latency depends strictly on how much training execution the subset eliminates; no arbitrary overhead fraction applies across workloads. When an engineering pipeline can amortize scorer preparation (spread its one-time cost) or checkpoint extraction across multiple downstream training runs ([[01 Books/Volume I - Foundations/03 Optimization/09 Data Selection/V1-09.09 Choosing a Selection Strategy#^sec-data-selection-amortization-across-training-runs-f6b8|section 9.8.3]]), ${T_{\text{selection}}}$ reflects only the marginal scoring pass over the candidate pool. Conversely, if an auxiliary proxy must be trained from scratch for a single isolated run, that preparatory compute adds directly to ${T_{\text{selection}}}$. When the selection function ${f}({x})$, which assigns each candidate example a score, repeatedly scores the candidate pool with the full target model, those extra scoring passes and their cross-worker coordination can take longer than the training steps they remove, making the run slower overall.

**Example 9.2: Selection inequality in practice**

**Scenario**: A computer vision team selects a 128,117-image coreset (10 percent) from the 1.28 million ImageNet training images to shorten ResNet-50 training on one H100 sustaining 25 percent of its peak, the training chapter’s compute-bound rate. This coreset is deliberately more aggressive than the half-size coreset of notebook [9.1](https://mlsysbook.ai/vol1/09_data_selection/09_data_selection.html#nbk-data-selection-computing-icr-coresets), and the selection bottleneck and the decision process later in the chapter reuse it. The times assume a host that decodes images fast enough to sustain that rate; [[01 Books/Volume I - Foundations/03 Optimization/09 Data Selection/V1-09.08 Selection Engineering#^sec-data-selection-selection-engineering-a4eb|section 9.7]] examines the host that cannot. Pretrained checkpoints for both the target architecture and a lightweight proxy are already available from earlier exploration, so this comparison accounts only for a marginal scoring pass over the candidate pool.

**Diagnosis**: Against a 211-minute full-data run of 100 epochs, marginal full-model scoring (Option A: target ResNet-50) adds 42 seconds of selection overhead and still saves 188.8 minutes (89.7 percent). Marginal proxy-model scoring (Option B: ResNet-18) drops selection overhead to 18 seconds and saves 189.1 minutes (89.9 percent), only 24 seconds more.

**Systems lesson**: Both options satisfy the selection inequality by a wide margin, because a single scoring pass costs at most one forward pass over the dataset (one-third of a full-data epoch for the target ResNet-50) and less with the proxy. Training on the 10 percent coreset also shrinks every one of the 100 epochs. Scorer choice becomes decisive only when selection rescores dynamically every epoch or when the scoring architecture is significantly larger than the target, multiplying selection latency while subset training savings remain fixed.

^exmp-data-selection-selection-inequality-practice

End-to-end wall-clock time divides into selection overhead and subset training, and the split decides the outcome across three regimes ([[01 Books/Volume I - Foundations/03 Optimization/09 Data Selection/V1-09.03 Measuring Data Value#^fig-selection-inequality|figure 4]]). As illustrative shares of the full training run, lightweight proxy scoring limits selection overhead to 5 percent while training on the 40 percent subset, which yields 55 percent net savings. Heavyweight scoring expends 60 percent overhead on selection, which wipes out all subset-training efficiency gains.

![[90 Assets/Volume I/09 Data Selection/Cell 11 Output 1 0bf8c01cae.png]]

Figure 4: **The Selection Inequality**: In this illustrative example, data selection only improves end-to-end training time at matched quality if the overhead of selection plus training on the subset is less than training on the full dataset. A lightweight selection function (proxy model, cached embeddings) keeps selection overhead low; an expensive selection function (full model forward pass) can negate the savings.

^fig-selection-inequality

This overhead compounds when selection runs dynamically across epochs rather than once offline. If per-epoch rescoring approaches the training work saved by the pruned subset, the net systems advantage vanishes. The retained fraction fixes how much training time the subset removes, which is the most that selection may spend. How much it actually spends is fixed by the size of the scorer and by how often it rescores the pool, so those two choices, not the retained fraction, decide whether selection is a net saving. Measured this way, a selection strategy is judged against its stated objective, counting the overhead of scoring, filtering, and staging samples alongside the training, labeling, or reuse savings it claims at target quality. Teams that optimize selection algorithms against isolated benchmarks rather than end-to-end pipeline constraints find that intuitive heuristics backfire. Scoring overhead consumes accelerator cycles, optimization destabilizes when batches skew toward hard or mislabeled examples, or generalization silently degrades.

Every lever in the rest of the chapter must pass these measures, and the levers appear in the order in which they act on a training run. Static pruning comes first because it fixes the subset before target-model training begins, even when its scoring uses a trained proxy.
