# V1-09.06 Synthetic Data Generation

[[Home|Home]] · [[01 Books/Volume I - Foundations/Volume 1 Overview|Volume Overview]] · [[01 Books/Volume I - Foundations/03 Optimization/09 Data Selection/V1-09 Data Selection|V1-09 Data Selection]]

^sec-data-selection-synthetic-data-generation-415c

Real-world data collection encounters physical and legal boundaries: autonomous fleets cannot safely crash vehicles to acquire catastrophic collision trajectories, healthcare systems cannot transmit protected patient telemetry across institutional firewalls, and edge products must handle acoustic environments before any deployed fleet exists to record them. When the source corpus contains few or none of the cases deployment will face, neither static pruning nor dynamic selection can extract signal that is not there. Synthetic data generation, which **[[01 Books/Volume I - Foundations/01 Foundations/04 Data Engineering/V1-04.05 Data Acquisition#^sec-data-engineering-scalability-cost-optimization-b9b3|Scaling acquisition]]** introduced as one acquisition channel among several, becomes here a deliberate lever for constructing the training distribution directly. The strategy shifts from *curation*, choosing among existing records, to *creation*, making new inputs, perturbations, or supervisory targets.

### Data Augmentation

^sec-data-selection-data-augmentation-transformationbased-synthesis-3aa7

A vision model trained repeatedly on identical images can memorize their exact pixel layouts rather than the object class, yet storing multiple transformed copies of every image on disk would multiply the archive’s storage footprint. **Data augmentation** avoids that storage cost by transforming existing samples on the fly during ingestion; every ResNet-50 epoch on ImageNet sees freshly cropped, flipped, and color-shifted variants of the same images. Label-preserving transformations add diversity without expanding durable storage or acquiring new data, but they move cost into the training pipeline. On host CPU worker threads, decoding and spatial transformations can saturate the cores and starve the accelerator across the PCIe bus. Offloading them to accelerator kernels relieves the CPU, yet inputs still cross the host-to-device link, and the transformations consume HBM and compute cycles that would otherwise perform gradient updates.

For image data, transformations encode invariances required at deployment ([[01 Books/Volume I - Foundations/03 Optimization/09 Data Selection/V1-09.14 Source References#^ref-shorten2019|Shorten and Khoshgoftaar 2019]]). Geometric operations (rotation, flipping, cropping, scaling) encourage viewpoint invariance when they preserve the label, while photometric adjustments (brightness, contrast, color jitter) simulate sensor noise and varying illumination. Stronger methods mask or mix images. Cutout[[01 Books/Volume I - Foundations/03 Optimization/09 Data Selection/V1-09.15 Footnotes#^fn17|¹⁷]] applies random rectangular zero-masks to prevent reliance on localized features. MixUp ([[01 Books/Volume I - Foundations/03 Optimization/09 Data Selection/V1-09.14 Source References#^ref-zhang2018mixup|Zhang et al. 2018]]) constructs virtual samples by taking linear combinations of image pairs and their one-hot labels (${\tilde{x}}= {\lambda_{\text{mix}}} {x}_i + (1 - {\lambda_{\text{mix}}}) {x}_j$, with mixing weight ${\lambda_{\text{mix}}}\in [0, 1]$). CutMix[[01 Books/Volume I - Foundations/03 Optimization/09 Data Selection/V1-09.15 Footnotes#^fn18|¹⁸]] pastes rectangular patches from one image into another while blending labels proportionally to the patch area. Both mixing methods operate through linear combinations and block copies in device memory, so they add negligible FLOP overhead.

Text has no smooth geometric transformations, because tokens are discrete. Back-translation[[01 Books/Volume I - Foundations/03 Optimization/09 Data Selection/V1-09.15 Footnotes#^fn19|¹⁹]] creates new training pairs for an English-to-German translator by running a second model in the opposite direction, pairing each real German sentence with the synthetic English sentence that model produces. Simpler transforms such as synonym substitution, token insertion, or deletion risk corrupting labels unless validated for the task.

For images, the choice of which transformations to apply, and how strongly, can be searched automatically rather than tuned by hand. AutoAugment[[01 Books/Volume I - Foundations/03 Optimization/09 Data Selection/V1-09.15 Footnotes#^fn20|²⁰]] runs an automated search over operations and their strengths, at a search cost of about 15,000 GPU-hours on ImageNet. RandAugment[[01 Books/Volume I - Foundations/03 Optimization/09 Data Selection/V1-09.15 Footnotes#^fn21|²¹]] cuts that search cost by tuning only two values rather than a full policy. Even the best augmentation schedule, however, can only perturb examples the dataset already contains.

### Generative Synthesis

^sec-data-selection-generative-synthesis-creating-new-samples-a43d

Cropping or color-shifting existing images cannot invent a rare object class, an unseen weather condition, or a collision scenario that never occurred in the recorded dataset. When a pipeline needs examples that perturbations of existing records cannot supply, **generative synthesis** constructs new input-label pairs $({x}, {y})$ by sampling from generative models or physical simulators. The three main engines differ in cost per sample and in how realistic their outputs are:

1.  Generative Adversarial Networks (GANs) train a generator network against a discriminator, a second network that learns to tell generated samples from real ones. Once trained, feed-forward generator inference requires a single forward pass, providing low latency and high sample throughput. Samples can look realistic for the cases the generator learned well, but GAN training is vulnerable to mode collapse, in which the generator covers only part of the data distribution and loses the tail variety needed for edge-case coverage.
2.  Diffusion models generate samples by iteratively removing noise over many sequential steps. Systems such as Stable Diffusion[[01 Books/Volume I - Foundations/03 Optimization/09 Data Selection/V1-09.15 Footnotes#^fn22|²²]] can be steered by a text prompt. They typically give more realistic and more varied samples than GANs at a much higher cost per sample. Each of the 20 to 50 denoising steps in a typical run executes the full network once per generated batch, so offline generation is often preferable.
3.  Physical simulation engines (such as CARLA for autonomous driving or Isaac Sim and Unity for robotics) generate data without neural inference, by running physics and rendering pipelines. They produce exact ground-truth annotations (3D bounding boxes, depth maps, velocity vectors) from internal state at no labeling cost, and the bottleneck shifts to host CPU physics and graphics rendering throughput. Their images, however, are only as realistic as the renderer, a limit that opens a domain gap ([[01 Books/Volume I - Foundations/03 Optimization/09 Data Selection/V1-09.06 Synthetic Data Generation#^sec-data-selection-bridging-domain-gap-100b|section 9.5.3]]).

Regardless of the generative engine, synthetic samples inevitably diverge from the statistical distributions of real deployment environments. That divergence determines whether synthetic training transfers to production or fails on the first real-world input.

### Bridging the Domain Gap

^sec-data-selection-bridging-domain-gap-100b

A model trained purely on synthetic data can reach high validation accuracy in the simulator while failing in production because it latched onto rendering or generator artifacts that do not exist in the real world. This **domain gap**[[01 Books/Volume I - Foundations/03 Optimization/09 Data Selection/V1-09.15 Footnotes#^fn23|²³]] is the statistical shift between the synthetic training distribution and production deployment data. Synthetic and real feature densities can sit at different locations in feature space, misaligning the decision boundaries learned from each ([[01 Books/Volume I - Foundations/03 Optimization/09 Data Selection/V1-09.06 Synthetic Data Generation#^fig-domain-gap|figure 8]]). The figure simplifies by drawing each boundary over the overall feature density of its domain. A real boundary depends on how each class is distributed within the domain, so only evaluation on real data establishes how the shift affects classification.

Two complementary strategies address this distribution mismatch. Domain randomization[[01 Books/Volume I - Foundations/03 Optimization/09 Data Selection/V1-09.15 Footnotes#^fn24|²⁴]] takes an aggressive approach. Rather than trying to match the real world precisely, it trains on varied synthetic data by randomizing lighting, textures, backgrounds, and camera parameters during generation. The aim is to make deployment conditions fall within the variation seen during training, though coverage is never guaranteed by randomization alone. The method is effective in robotics and autonomous-driving settings when the simulator spans the relevant physical and visual factors and the result is validated on real data.

Domain adaptation takes the opposite approach by explicitly aligning synthetic and real distributions. Feature alignment methods train on synthetic data while simultaneously minimizing the distance between synthetic and real feature distributions. A common way to minimize that distance is adversarial training, in which a second network learns to tell synthetic features from real ones while the feature extractor learns to fool it, leaving only features both domains share. Fine-tuning offers a simpler path, which pretrains on abundant synthetic data to learn general features and then fine-tunes on a small real dataset to adapt to deployment conditions. Self-training combines these ideas by using a synthetic-trained model to label real unlabeled data with its own confident predictions (pseudo-labels, developed in [[01 Books/Volume I - Foundations/03 Optimization/09 Data Selection/V1-09.07 Learning from Unlabeled Data#^sec-data-selection-semisupervised-learning-using-unlabeled-data-51fc|section 9.6.1]]), then retraining on the combined labeled set. In practice, mixing synthetic and real data provides a real-data anchor while retaining some synthetic coverage. Moving the mix from all-synthetic toward all-real trades a larger domain gap for a higher collection cost. The best ratio depends on the domain, simulator fidelity, model family, and validation protocol rather than on any universal recipe.

Augmentation, noise injection, and simulation can grow a small seed set into a training corpus (**[[01 Books/Volume I - Foundations/01 Foundations/04 Data Engineering/V1-04.04 Requirements and Data Budget#^sec-data-engineering-kws-design-space-eadd|KWS design space]]** works through the keyword spotter’s data budget, including how much synthetic augmentation saves against recording real utterances). The same creation strategy becomes riskier when samples come from learned generators. In recursive training, *model collapse*[[01 Books/Volume I - Foundations/03 Optimization/09 Data Selection/V1-09.15 Footnotes#^fn25|²⁵]] can amplify errors and reduce diversity over generations. Unlike the mode collapse of a single GAN, which happens within one generator’s training, model collapse builds up across successive models trained on each other’s outputs. Synthetic data therefore requires provenance, mixture controls, and validation against real deployment data rather than an unverified assumption that more generated samples help. When the input examples themselves are already on disk, a trained model can instead enrich their labels.

### Knowledge Distillation

^sec-data-selection-knowledge-distillation-compressing-information-ce68

A hard one-hot label such as $[1, 0, 0]$ tells a classifier which class is right, but discards everything a stronger model knows about which wrong classes look similar. Where augmentation ([[01 Books/Volume I - Foundations/03 Optimization/09 Data Selection/V1-09.06 Synthetic Data Generation#^sec-data-selection-data-augmentation-transformationbased-synthesis-3aa7|section 9.5.1]]) and generative synthesis ([[01 Books/Volume I - Foundations/03 Optimization/09 Data Selection/V1-09.06 Synthetic Data Generation#^sec-data-selection-generative-synthesis-creating-new-samples-a43d|section 9.5.2]]) create new inputs, knowledge distillation[[01 Books/Volume I - Foundations/03 Optimization/09 Data Selection/V1-09.15 Footnotes#^fn26|²⁶]] ([[01 Books/Volume I - Foundations/03 Optimization/09 Data Selection/V1-09.14 Source References#^ref-hinton2015distilling|Hinton et al. 2015]]; [[01 Books/Volume I - Foundations/03 Optimization/09 Data Selection/V1-09.14 Source References#^ref-gou2021|Gou et al. 2021]]) creates richer targets for inputs the corpus already holds. A larger trained model (the teacher) labels each training example with its full probability distribution over classes, such as $[0.70, 0.20, 0.10]$ in place of $[1, 0, 0]$. A compact student network then trains against those soft targets, smoothed by a temperature parameter ${T_{\text{distill}}}$. Because each soft target records which wrong classes the teacher found plausible, every example carries more supervision than its one-hot label. **[[01 Books/Volume I - Foundations/03 Optimization/10 Model Compression/V1-10.03 Structural Optimization Changing What the Model Computes#^sec-model-compression-distillation-mathematics-4af6|Distillation mathematics]]** develops distillation as a compression technique, including its loss and temperature mechanics; as a data lever, its cost lies in generating, storing, and reading those targets.

Distillation trades accelerator HBM capacity against storage I/O bandwidth:

- **Online distillation**: The teacher runs beside the student in accelerator memory and produces soft targets as training proceeds, so none are stored. The teacher’s weights and forward passes, however, take memory and arithmetic from the student, which shrinks the student’s batch and lengthens each step.
- **Offline distillation**: The teacher generates predictions across the corpus during an offline precomputation pass, storing dense logit vectors to disk. This frees accelerator memory entirely for student training, but expands dataset storage volume and input pipeline bandwidth. For a classification vocabulary of ${K}$ classes, storing uncompressed float32 logits increases the per-sample label footprint from a single integer index ($\lceil \log_2 {K} \rceil$ bits) to $4{K}$ bytes, an expansion of more than three orders of magnitude once ${K}$ reaches the thousands.

For ResNet-50 on ImageNet the offline footprint is concrete. Storing FP32 logits for 1,000 classes across every training image takes 5.1 GB, against 2.6 MB for the integer labels they replace, a 2,000× expansion that every epoch must read back through the input pipeline. That storage and read traffic, rather than the loss function, is what data selection must budget for. Generation and distillation both supply labeled signal the corpus lacks; a complementary route reduces how many labels training needs in the first place, by extracting supervision from the unlabeled data the corpus already holds.
