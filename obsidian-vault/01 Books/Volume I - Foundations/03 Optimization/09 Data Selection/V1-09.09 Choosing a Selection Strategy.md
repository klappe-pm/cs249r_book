# V1-09.09 Choosing a Selection Strategy

[[Home|Home]] · [[01 Books/Volume I - Foundations/Volume 1 Overview|Volume Overview]] · [[01 Books/Volume I - Foundations/03 Optimization/09 Data Selection/V1-09 Data Selection|V1-09 Data Selection]]

^sec-data-selection-decision-framework-0261

The hospital team in notebook [9.3](https://mlsysbook.ai/vol1/09_data_selection/09_data_selection.html#nbk-data-selection-active-learning-roi) could not label every scan, because doing so would have cost \$5,000,000, 10× over its budget. Allocating capital between human annotation and accelerator compute is a primary systems decision, and a team under a fixed budget must determine whether the next dollar buys more downstream accuracy as specialist annotations or as additional accelerator hours. A cost model settles the question. The choice among levers follows from it in four steps: the total cost of data, the break-even point of a selection pipeline, the amortization of one-time investments across the runs that share them, and a decision process worked on the ResNet-50 vision team of [[01 Books/Volume I - Foundations/03 Optimization/09 Data Selection/V1-09.03 Measuring Data Value#^sec-data-selection-selection-inequality-3b7e|section 9.2.6]].

### Data Costs and Return on Investment

^sec-data-selection-quantifying-data-costs-roi-f0b4

Budgeting a training project from cloud GPU and object-storage invoices alone misses the licensing and human annotation bills that often dwarf compute cost. The **total cost of training data** spans acquisition, labeling, storage retention, and accelerator compute across the full data lifecycle:

$$
{C_{\text{total}}}= {C_{\text{acquire}}}+ {C_{\text{label}}}+ {C_{\text{store}}}+ {C_{\text{process}}}
$$

 Labeling (${C_{\text{label}}}$) represents the expenditure that data selection targets most directly, whereas storage (${C_{\text{store}}}$) and processing (${C_{\text{process}}}$) scale mechanically with retained byte volume and the number of accelerator passes. [[01 Books/Volume I - Foundations/03 Optimization/09 Data Selection/V1-09.09 Choosing a Selection Strategy#^tbl-cost-components|Table 10]] itemizes these four cost components alongside their operational drivers.

| **Component**              |                                    **Formula**                                    |                        **Illustrative Range** |
|:---------------------------|:---------------------------------------------------------------------------------:|----------------------------------------------:|
| **${C_{\text{acquire}}}$** |                          ${D}\times {c_{\text{sample}}}$                          | \$0.001–\$10/sample (web scrape vs. licensed) |
| **${C_{\text{label}}}$**   |                  ${D_{\text{labeled}}}\times {c_{\text{label}}}$                  |        \$0.01–\$200/sample (crowd vs. expert) |
| **${C_{\text{store}}}$**   |   ${D_{\text{vol,store}}}\times {c_{\text{storage}}}\times {T_{\text{months}}}$   |                        \$0.02–\$0.10/GB/month |
| **${C_{\text{process}}}$** | ${D}\times {N_{\text{epochs}}}\times {O_{\text{sample}}}\times {c_{\text{FLOP}}}$ |                Proportional to training FLOPs |

Table 10: **Total Cost of Training Data**: The four cost components span the full data lifecycle. Here ${D}$ is the total sample count, ${D_{\text{labeled}}}$ is the labeled subset, ${D_{\text{vol,store}}}$ is stored byte volume, ${T_{\text{months}}}$ is retention time, ${N_{\text{epochs}}}$ is the number of training passes, and ${O_{\text{sample}}}$ is per-sample training work. The ranges are scenario anchors rather than universal market prices.

^tbl-cost-components

The interplay of these four cost terms becomes concrete when evaluating an ImageNet-scale vision model training run. Calculating each term explicitly shows how easily data acquisition and human labeling outspend the accelerator hours needed to fit the weights.

^tbl-data-selection-imagenet-cost-breakdown

**Systems insight**: Acquisition and labeling can dwarf compute cost in supervised vision workloads, as they do under these assumptions. The compute figure covers the whole training campaign at the compute-bound rate of notebook [9.4](https://mlsysbook.ai/vol1/09_data_selection/09_data_selection.html#nbk-data-selection-pruned-epoch-h100), not one run: a published model is the survivor of sweeps, restarts, and ablations, and costing a single run would understate compute roughly thirtyfold. Even so, acquisition and labeling remain the larger share. The run count is an assumption like any other, so a complete ROI calculation must state it and count both categories rather than assuming which one dominates.

Data selection techniques insert algorithmic filtering into the training pipeline to reduce downstream costs. Scoring consumes accelerator cycles, generates host-device memory traffic, or requires custom pipeline stages, so a strategy is justified only if the downstream savings exceed these overheads. Return on investment (ROI), the savings net of the investment per unit invested, makes that test explicit. Because a selection investment is often reused across a training campaign, the general form counts the ${N_{\text{runs}}}$ runs that share it, the **amortized ROI**:

$$
\text{Amortized ROI} = \frac{{N_{\text{runs}}}\times \text{Per-Run Savings} - \text{One-Time Investment}}{\text{One-Time Investment}} \times 100\% \tag{3}
$$

^eq-data-selection-amortized-roi

A single run sets ${N_{\text{runs}}}= 1$ and recovers the ordinary ratio of savings minus investment to investment. The balance between investment and savings varies across selection strategies, and [[01 Books/Volume I - Foundations/03 Optimization/09 Data Selection/V1-09.09 Choosing a Selection Strategy#^tbl-roi-profiles|table 12]] maps the primary cost drivers and benefit mechanisms across four standard techniques; their net return depends on corpus volume, model scale, and existing pipeline infrastructure.

| **Technique**         | **Investment (Cost)**                                   | **Savings (Benefit)**                                        |
|:----------------------|:--------------------------------------------------------|:-------------------------------------------------------------|
| **Deduplication**     | One-time compute for hashing + infrastructure           | Reduced storage and repeated-sample processing               |
| **Coreset Selection** | Proxy model training + selection compute                | Fewer retained samples if target quality and coverage hold   |
| **Active Learning**   | Inference on unlabeled pool + human-in-the-loop latency | Lower labeling demand when query selection transfers         |
| **Data Augmentation** | CPU/GPU cycles for transforms                           | Effective dataset size increase without new data acquisition |

Table 12: **ROI Profiles for Data Selection Techniques**: The table identifies costs and possible savings. No technique guarantees positive ROI. A training-time claim must satisfy the selection inequality; labeling savings or reusable data investments require their corresponding total-cost comparison at target quality.

^tbl-roi-profiles

The profiles in [[01 Books/Volume I - Foundations/03 Optimization/09 Data Selection/V1-09.09 Choosing a Selection Strategy#^tbl-roi-profiles|table 12]] compare those costs directly. Deduplication requires an upfront investment in hashing or embedding infrastructure, but can pay off across many epochs and runs by eliminating redundant storage and compute. Coreset selection demands training a proxy model to score the dataset; it only breaks even if the compute saved by dropping samples exceeds the cost of that proxy training. Active learning shifts the cost from human labelers to machine inference, generating high ROI when human time is expensive, though it introduces human-in-the-loop latency that can stall automated pipelines. Finally, data augmentation requires zero new data acquisition but shifts the burden to the CPU or GPU, trading compute cycles for effective dataset expansion. Each technique is worth adopting only when its savings exceed what it costs to set up and run, which makes locating that exact crossover threshold the next step.

### Break-Even Analysis

^sec-data-selection-breakeven-analysis-9a38

If candidate scoring is computationally heavy or the unlabeled pool is massive, a selection policy can spend more money scoring candidates than it saves on omitted labels or training steps. The **break-even point** marks the operating boundary where the financial savings from omitted samples equal the computational and operational overhead of selecting them. Suppose labeling costs \$10/sample, active learning starts from 1,000 labeled samples (\$10,000), and each round issues 100 queries at \$50 inference cost (the expense of scoring the candidate pool ${U}$ with the active model). If the random-labeling baseline requires 5,000 samples for target accuracy while active learning reaches the same target accuracy with 2,000 labeled samples, the labels-plus-scoring comparison reports net savings per dollar of the stated active-learning budget:

$$
\begin{gather*} \text{Random labeling cost} = \text{5,000} \times \text{\$10/sample} = \text{\$50,000} \\ \text{Active learning cost} = \text{2,000} \times \text{\$10/sample} + \text{10 rounds} \times \text{\$50} = \text{\$20,500} \\ \text{ROI} = \frac{\text{\$50,000} - \text{\$20,500}}{\text{\$20,500}}= \text{$143.9\%$} \end{gather*}
$$

The ROI divides by the active-learning budget rather than by the one-time investment of [[01 Books/Volume I - Foundations/03 Optimization/09 Data Selection/V1-09.09 Choosing a Selection Strategy#^eq-data-selection-amortized-roi|equation 3]], because here the money spent on labels and scoring is itself the investment being judged. Within this labels-plus-scoring boundary, break-even occurs when avoided labeling costs equal selection overhead. In this scenario, active learning would break even only if its total scoring spend grew from \$500 to the full gap between \$50,000 of random labels and \$20,000 of active-learning labels. A complete dollar comparison must also deduct any additional retraining, engineering, storage, and delay-related expense relative to the random-labeling baseline.

When an unlabeled candidate pool ${U}$ contains millions of uncurated examples, repeatedly scoring it across multiple rounds scales scoring cost with $|{U}| \times {c_{\text{infer}}}$. An algorithmic reduction in labeled samples can then be erased if proxy inference costs are not carefully bounded. When one fixed budget funds both scoring and labeling, every dollar spent scoring the pool is a dollar withdrawn from labels, so practical loops narrow ${U}$ with metadata or a cheap heuristic before the active model scores it. Even a pipeline that loses money on a single run, however, can cross break-even once its output is reused across an entire training campaign.

### Amortization Across Training Runs

^sec-data-selection-amortization-across-training-runs-f6b8

Charging the entire engineering and preprocessing bill of a static curation pipeline to a single training run makes a profitable campaign investment look like a net loss. Filters such as MinHash deduplication, exact ${n}$-gram indexing, and embedding clustering require substantial upfront engineering and compute to build once. Production model development, by contrast, is inherently iterative, because hyperparameter search, architecture sweeps, ablation studies, and seed checks repeatedly train on the same curated corpus. The amortized ROI of [[01 Books/Volume I - Foundations/03 Optimization/09 Data Selection/V1-09.09 Choosing a Selection Strategy#^eq-data-selection-amortized-roi|equation 3]] therefore spreads that upfront curation investment across every run in the campaign, as [[01 Books/Volume I - Foundations/03 Optimization/09 Data Selection/V1-09.09 Choosing a Selection Strategy#^tbl-dedup-costs|table 13]] itemizes for an illustrative MinHash deduplication pipeline.

| **Component**                             |                    **Cost** |
|:------------------------------------------|----------------------------:|
| **Build deduplication pipeline**          | \$50,000 (engineering time) |
| **Compute MinHash signatures (one-time)** |                     \$5,000 |
| **Per-run savings**                       |                \$10,000/run |

Table 13: **Deduplication Infrastructure Cost Components**: The one-time investment covers engineering effort and initial compute; the per-run savings accrue with every subsequent training run on the deduplicated data.

^tbl-dedup-costs

Because the initial infrastructure and signature-generation costs are fixed, each subsequent training run compounds the net financial return. Three operational factors govern whether curation infrastructure reaches break-even:

- **Repeated training runs**: Hyperparameter search, model iterations, and scheduled retraining reuse the same selection infrastructure many times.
- **Shared datasets**: A cleaned or deduplicated corpus can support multiple teams or model architectures.
- **Broadly reusable techniques**: Methods such as deduplication transfer across models, whereas task-specific coresets may not.

[[01 Books/Volume I - Foundations/03 Optimization/09 Data Selection/V1-09.09 Choosing a Selection Strategy#^tbl-amortized-roi|Table 14]] tracks how amortized return scales with campaign run count under these cost parameters. A pipeline that loses money on a one-off experiment crosses break-even within a handful of retraining cycles and compounds its savings across larger campaigns.

| **Number of Runs** |           **Amortized ROI** |
|:-------------------|----------------------------:|
| **1 run**          |           -81.8% (net loss) |
| **5 runs**         |     -9.1% (near break-even) |
| **10 runs**        |           +81.8% (positive) |
| **50 runs**        | +809.1% (highly profitable) |

Table 14: **Amortized ROI over Multiple Training Runs**: Under this scenario’s costs and savings, a deduplication pipeline that loses money on its first use becomes profitable when reused across enough runs. Infrastructure investments should be evaluated over their expected lifetime rather than from one use.

^tbl-amortized-roi

Deduplication achieves high investment leverage because eliminating redundant sequences reduces host-storage footprint and avoids wasted gradient updates across every downstream architecture. Sample-selection methods conditioned on proxy gradients or uncertainty scores transfer within a model family or between related architectures, but their rankings must be revalidated when the target model, task, or data changes, which narrows their amortization window relative to deduplication. For exploratory or one-off training runs, low-overhead methods like uniform random sampling or basic augmentation may offer better ROI. A static scoring pass can still pay for itself in one run if its measured savings exceed its cost at target quality.

**Checkpoint 9.3: Accepting a data intervention**

A smaller training pool is one observation in the acceptance decision. Connect the measurements to the constraint the intervention was meant to relieve.

**Time savings**: Identify the scoring, coordination, and training times needed to compare a selected subset with full-data training at the same target quality.

**Cost objective**: Explain how an active-learning pipeline could reduce total expenditure even if it takes longer to train, and identify the costs that must enter that comparison.

**Coverage**: Decide whether unchanged aggregate accuracy is sufficient when a deployment-critical rare slice regresses, and name the additional validation evidence needed. For the disjoint-class allocation in [[01 Books/Volume I - Foundations/03 Optimization/09 Data Selection/V1-09.04 Static Pruning#^sec-data-selection-coreset-selection-algorithms-2c74|section 9.3.4]], explain why reducing ${K}$ below ${a}+{b}$ requires revising the selection plan before training, even if every remaining example has a high score.

With time, labeling, and amortized costs expressed in one accounting, every technique in the chapter can now be weighed against the claim it makes. A training-time saving must satisfy the selection inequality, a labeling saving must beat the random-labeling baseline after scoring and any other differing costs, and a reusable investment must recover its one-time cost across the runs or tasks that share it. Costing each technique separately, however, does not say which one to reach for first when several of them compete for the same budget.

### Decision Process

^sec-data-selection-decision-process-7a2d

When labeling budget, redundancy, rare classes, privacy, and convergence speed conflict, systems engineering requires identifying the primary limiting constraint. [[01 Books/Volume I - Foundations/03 Optimization/09 Data Selection/V1-09.09 Choosing a Selection Strategy#^tbl-data-selection|Table 15]] collects the three levers with order-of-magnitude ranges drawn from scenarios like those worked in this chapter; each must reduce the cost it targets after counting its overhead and preserving target quality and required coverage. Semi-supervised learning and self-supervised pretraining ([[01 Books/Volume I - Foundations/03 Optimization/09 Data Selection/V1-09.07 Learning from Unlabeled Data#^sec-data-selection-learning-unlabeled-data-5c1e|section 9.6]]) both change the training signal rather than the sample set, and both enter the decision through the labeling-cost branch of the tree that follows; the table lists semi-supervised learning with dynamic selection only because it, too, acts during training. Privacy is best read as a data-scarcity constraint, because privacy requirements that limit direct use of real records leave too little usable data. Synthetic records can then stand in for some of those real ones, provided they are checked for leakage of the records they were derived from.

| **Stage**                | **When Applied** | **Techniques**                                        | **Order-of-Magnitude Scenario Range**               |
|:-------------------------|:-----------------|:------------------------------------------------------|:----------------------------------------------------|
| **Static pruning**       | Before training  | Coreset Selection, Deduplication, Quality Filtering   | 30–50% dataset reduction                            |
| **Dynamic selection**    | During training  | Curriculum Learning, Active Learning, Semi-Supervised | 10–30% faster curricula; 2–100$\times$ fewer labels |
| **Synthetic generation** | On-demand        | Augmentation, Generative Models, Distillation         | 2–10$\times$ effective data expansion               |

Table 15: **Three-Stage Data Selection Pipeline**: The ranges illustrate possible outcomes rather than portable expectations. Actual gains depend on the dataset, model, selection method, and target metric.

^tbl-data-selection

Once the dominant constraint is named, it points to a candidate technique, and the five lighthouse workloads, each bound by a different resource, show the pairing in practice.

**Lighthouse 9.1: Lighthouse data selection**

Data selection principles apply to all five lighthouse models, though the dominant constraint determines the technique. [[01 Books/Volume I - Foundations/03 Optimization/09 Data Selection/V1-09.09 Choosing a Selection Strategy#^tbl-data-selection-lighthouse-priorities|Table 16]] maps each workload to a priority. Compute-bound ResNet-50 training benefits from coreset selection, while GPT-2 XL training relies on deduplication and quality filtering to cut the raw tokens it must process. MobileNetV2, compute-bound in its efficient regime, is limited at deployment by latency and power, constraints that apply at inference, where no choice of training data changes the cost of a prediction. The data lever that matters is therefore the one that helps a small model generalize, augmentation matched to its capacity (compact networks underfit under distortion that a large model absorbs). The work is validating that the augmentation preserves the invariances the deployment environment requires.

DLRM, the capacity-bound lighthouse, stores a learned vector (an embedding) for every user and item ID in tables that reach terabytes, and much of that capacity goes to cold IDs, users or items that appear rarely in training data. It uses interaction deduplication to remove repeated user-item records and embedding pruning to shrink those tables, but removing repeated interactions does not by itself shrink a table, because the table holds one row per ID, not per interaction. It shrinks only when whole IDs leave the data or the team changes how IDs are assigned to rows. Finally, keyword spotting, the power-bound lighthouse, is bound for data selection by a scarce, costly seed set as much as by its power and memory budget. It overcomes that scarcity by using augmentation and synthesis to build robust datasets from minimal seed samples.

| **Lighthouse**       | **Primary Bottleneck**                         | **Data Selection Priority**                                                                                          |
|:---------------------|:-----------------------------------------------|:---------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------|
| **ResNet-50**        | Compute                                        | Coreset selection directly reduces training FLOPs                                                                    |
| **GPT-2 XL**         | Training compute (tokens $\times$ FLOPs/token) | Deduplication and quality filtering cut tokens processed; curriculum may improve token efficiency                    |
| **MobileNetV2**      | Latency/Power                                  | Validate augmentation against the target model and deployment invariances                                            |
| **DLRM**             | Memory capacity                                | Interaction deduplication cuts examples; embedding pruning shrinks tables only when cold IDs are dropped or rehashed |
| **Keyword Spotting** | Data scarcity (small seed set)                 | Augmentation and synthesis create datasets from minimal seeds                                                        |

Table 16: **Data Selection Priorities by Lighthouse**: Primary bottleneck and candidate data-selection priority for each lighthouse workload. The pairing suggests a hypothesis to test; the dominant resource constraint alone does not guarantee that a technique pays off.

^tbl-data-selection-lighthouse-priorities

Across these workloads, data selection functions as a systems optimization tailored to relieve whichever physical resource limits the pipeline.

The lighthouse mapping assumes a single dominant constraint, but real projects face several at once. #### Step 1: Assess the Bottleneck

^step-1-assess-the-bottleneck

The vision team’s stated objective is shorter training turnaround at the existing quality target. The full-data baseline takes 211 minutes, and both candidates retain 10 percent of the images, the aggressive coreset of that example rather than the half-size coreset of notebook [9.1](https://mlsysbook.ai/vol1/09_data_selection/09_data_selection.html#nbk-data-selection-computing-icr-coresets). This places the decision in the compute-cost branch. The retained fraction alone does not settle it; the selection inequality and the slice-coverage check do.

A different limiting constraint would change the comparison. If labels were the main expense, fewer annotations could justify additional training time. If controlled sweeps instead diagnosed a shortage of useful tokens, the data-scarcity branch would be relevant even with idle accelerators, because once repeats and weak tokens are gone the remaining remedy is new data. Naming the objective determines which saving the candidate must actually deliver.

#### Step 2: Check Prerequisites

^step-2-check-prerequisites

Both vision candidates require access to the full image pool, a scoring model, and stable sample indices. The proxy additionally needs a ranking that transfers to the target model; its lower execution cost does not establish that prerequisite. [[01 Books/Volume I - Foundations/03 Optimization/09 Data Selection/V1-09.09 Choosing a Selection Strategy#^tbl-technique-prerequisites|Table 17]] places these requirements alongside those of the alternative branches.

| **Technique**            | **Prerequisites**                                           |
|:-------------------------|:------------------------------------------------------------|
| **Active Learning**      | Access to oracle, unlabeled pool, retraining infrastructure |
| **Coreset selection**    | Proxy model or embedding extractor, full dataset accessible |
| **Curriculum Learning**  | Difficulty scoring method, pacing schedule                  |
| **Semi-Supervised**      | Some labeled data, unlabeled data from same distribution    |
| **Self-Supervised**      | Large unlabeled corpus, pretraining compute budget          |
| **Augmentation**         | Domain knowledge of invariances, augmentation library       |
| **Synthetic Generation** | Generative model or simulator, domain gap mitigation        |

Table 17: **Technique Prerequisites**: Each technique carries specific infrastructure and data requirements that must be verified before implementation. A technique with excellent theoretical gains but unmet prerequisites will fail in practice, making this checklist the first step in technique selection.

#### Step 3: Estimate ROI

^step-3-estimate-roi

For the stated vision scenario, target-model scoring plus subset training takes 21.8 minutes (Option A), while proxy scoring plus subset training takes 21.4 minutes (Option B). These figures evaluate the marginal scoring pass using existing checkpoints. Both options beat the full-data baseline in the cost model. The proxy reduces only the scoring term; the retained training work is identical.

Suppose pilot validation in this hypothetical decision confirms that both runs reach the aggregate target under the modeled schedule, but only Option A meets every deployment-critical slice threshold. Option B regresses on a rare class. Option A is then the acceptable choice despite its more expensive scoring pass, saving 188.8 minutes against the baseline. Option B remains a candidate for repair, not deployment. Repair would enforce the coverage floor from [[01 Books/Volume I - Foundations/03 Optimization/09 Data Selection/V1-09.04 Static Pruning#^sec-data-selection-coreset-selection-algorithms-2c74|section 9.3.4]], rescore or enlarge the subset as needed, and repeat the time-to-quality comparison. Additional examples or passes change its cost, so the original proxy saving cannot simply be carried forward. If both candidates passed all quality checks at the stated times, Option B would win this time comparison.

A time saving must still justify any engineering investment. If the proxy checkpoint does not already exist and its training is charged entirely to a single run, that preparatory training erases Option B’s modest time advantage over Option A. [[01 Books/Volume I - Foundations/03 Optimization/09 Data Selection/V1-09.09 Choosing a Selection Strategy#^sec-data-selection-quantifying-data-costs-roi-f0b4|Section 9.8.1]] accounts for that investment, and [[01 Books/Volume I - Foundations/03 Optimization/09 Data Selection/V1-09.09 Choosing a Selection Strategy#^sec-data-selection-amortization-across-training-runs-f6b8|section 9.8.3]] spreads one-time costs across the ${N_{\text{runs}}}$ planned experiments. Reusing the proxy is economically useful only while its ranking continues to pass the same quality checks.

#### Step 4: Combine Techniques

^step-4-combine-techniques

Data selection techniques are not mutually exclusive, but combining them does not guarantee additive gains. A representative workflow deduplicates the raw corpus, applies a coverage-aware coreset filter, orders retained batches through an empirical curriculum, and applies runtime augmentations during batch collation. Pretrained representations alter the baseline trajectory further. Every additional stage must justify its compute overhead, because cascading filters frequently discard overlapping subsets while their scoring latencies accumulate. Stages compound efficiency gains only when their effects do not overlap and their overhead remains small, so combined savings must be measured rather than assumed.

A choice made at the data layer does not stay there. This decision process weighs a technique by its own scoring cost and the training work it removes, but a smaller or reordered corpus also changes what the model learns to represent, and the compression and deployment stages downstream inherit that representation. A selection that looks optimal in isolation can therefore move cost into those later stages, so the chosen strategy must be checked against the layers it feeds.
