# V1-09.07 Learning from Unlabeled Data

[[Home|Home]] · [[01 Books/Volume I - Foundations/Volume 1 Overview|Volume Overview]] · [[01 Books/Volume I - Foundations/03 Optimization/09 Data Selection/V1-09 Data Selection|V1-09 Data Selection]]

^sec-data-selection-learning-unlabeled-data-5c1e

A hospital archive holding tens of thousands of unannotated chest X-rays or a language-modeling team holding terabytes of raw web text already has its inputs on disk; the bottleneck is that active learning still pays an expert for every example it selects and synthesis still pays for every example it creates. Learning from unlabeled data attacks that label bottleneck from the other side. Semi-supervised learning lets a model trained on a small labeled seed supply provisional labels for the rest of the pool. Self-supervised learning removes task labels from pretraining altogether, and foundation models spread the resulting pretraining cost across many downstream tasks. Each approach trades additional training compute for fewer labels, so each is judged by total cost at matched quality rather than by label count alone.

### Semi-Supervised Learning

^sec-data-selection-semisupervised-learning-using-unlabeled-data-51fc

A single hospital’s chest X-ray archive, smaller than the pool in [[01 Books/Volume I - Foundations/03 Optimization/09 Data Selection/V1-09.05 Dynamic Selection#^sec-data-selection-active-learning-humanintheloop-6932|section 9.4.2]], may hold 50,000 scans of which only 500 have been reviewed and labeled[[01 Books/Volume I - Foundations/03 Optimization/09 Data Selection/V1-09.15 Footnotes#^fn27|²⁷]] by radiologists, a labeling rate of 1 percent. **Semi-supervised learning** puts the remaining 49,500 images to work by shifting the trade-off from human annotation to accelerator compute: it uses the small labeled subset to anchor training on the larger unlabeled pool, extracting supervisory signals from unannotated inputs. Models trained this way can approach fully supervised accuracy with far fewer manual labels, provided the unlabeled pool matches the target distribution and the result is validated on labeled data.

Unlabeled data, although it cannot teach the mapping from inputs to outputs, contains structural information about the input distribution ${p(x)}$ that can constrain the hypothesis space, the set of functions the model could learn. Under the smoothness assumption (nearby inputs share a label) and the cluster assumption (inputs in one dense cluster share a label), a decision boundary that cuts through a dense region assigns different labels to nearby inputs and is less plausible than one in a low-density region. Semi-supervised methods exploit these assumptions, which must be checked against the target task.

Three techniques put these assumptions to work. Pseudo-labeling[[01 Books/Volume I - Foundations/03 Optimization/09 Data Selection/V1-09.15 Footnotes#^fn28|²⁸]] trains on the labeled subset, generates model predictions on the unlabeled pool, and incorporates samples exceeding a confidence threshold as synthetic ground truth for subsequent retraining. The confidence threshold governs a systems trade-off: setting it too low admits label noise that degrades learning, while setting it too high discards informative examples and wastes the unlabeled pool. Confident mistakes can survive either threshold, so confidence must be checked against labeled validation evidence rather than treated as proof of correctness.

Consistency regularization[[01 Books/Volume I - Foundations/03 Optimization/09 Data Selection/V1-09.15 Footnotes#^fn29|²⁹]] enforces that the model produce invariant predictions across perturbed versions of the same input. When transformations such as cropping, rotation, or color shifts preserve the task label, the loss function penalizes prediction divergence between two stochastic views of an unlabeled sample. Hybrid approaches like FixMatch[[01 Books/Volume I - Foundations/03 Optimization/09 Data Selection/V1-09.15 Footnotes#^fn30|³⁰]] unify both mechanisms. They generate a pseudo-label on a weakly augmented view when prediction confidence exceeds a threshold, then train the model to predict that label on a strongly augmented variant of the same input.

Label propagation spreads labels from labeled examples to unlabeled examples whose representations lie close to them. It works when examples form well-separated clusters, but finding each example’s neighbors across a large pool costs substantial memory and search time.

**Example 9.5: FixMatch on CIFAR-10**

**Scenario**: A semi-supervised image classification pipeline trains on CIFAR-10 with only 250 labeled samples (25 per class) alongside unlabeled data ([[01 Books/Volume I - Foundations/03 Optimization/09 Data Selection/V1-09.14 Source References#^ref-sohn2020fixmatch|Sohn et al. 2020]]).

**Diagnosis**: The fully labeled CIFAR-10 training set contains 50K labels. FixMatch generates high-confidence pseudo-labels (\>0.95 threshold) on weakly augmented unlabeled images and enforces consistency on strongly augmented variants, reaching 94.9 percent accuracy in the cited experiment.

**Systems lesson**: Semi-supervised learning trades additional training compute for fewer manual labels. In an illustrative cost scenario at \$1 per label, a supervised baseline with 4,000 labels costs \$4,000 in labels plus \$50 of compute, or \$4,050. FixMatch at 250 labels costs \$250 in labels but 5× the compute, \$250, for \$500. Label-plus-compute cost falls by 8.1×; the end-to-end saving depends on the real unlabeled-data and compute costs.

^exmp-data-selection-fixmatch-cifar-10

The objective of semi-supervised learning is lower total labeling and compute cost at matched quality, which can justify a lower ICR when annotation savings exceed the added training expense. Neither the label reduction nor the compute increase carries over unchanged from one task to another; [[01 Books/Volume I - Foundations/03 Optimization/09 Data Selection/V1-09.07 Learning from Unlabeled Data#^tbl-fixmatch-cifar10|table 4]] shows how far the label count fell on one benchmark.

| **Label Budget**  | **Method**        | **Accuracy** |  **Label Efficiency** |
|:------------------|:------------------|:------------:|----------------------:|
| **50,000 (100%)** | Full training set |      —       | Label-count reference |
| **4,000 (8%)**    | FixMatch          |    95.7%     |  12.5× more efficient |
| **250 (0.5%)**    | FixMatch          |    94.9%     |   200× more efficient |
| **40 (0.08%)**    | FixMatch          |    88.6%     |  1250× more efficient |

Table 4: **FixMatch Label Efficiency on CIFAR-10**: With 250 labels (0.5 percent of the dataset), FixMatch reaches 94.9 percent accuracy while using 200× fewer labels than the full training set contains.

^tbl-fixmatch-cifar10

The efficiency column counts labels only, relative to the full labeled set. It does not claim that FixMatch matches a fully supervised model, which would have to be compared at equal accuracy. These efficiency gains also depend on benign distribution assumptions. Semi-supervised learning assumes that unlabeled data comes from the same distribution as labeled data. It struggles when unlabeled data contains out-of-distribution samples (the model confidently mislabels them), when class imbalance is severe (pseudo-labels amplify majority class bias), or when the labeled set does not cover all classes (preventing label propagation for unseen classes). A held-out set with true labels is the check that catches such a mismatch.

Each technique can reduce dependence on task-specific labels, but none eliminates the need for labeled evaluation. The progression raises a related possibility, that task-specific labels may not be necessary for pretraining. The structure of data itself (cat images resemble other cat images, and coherent sentences follow grammatical patterns) may provide a supervision signal, while downstream quality still requires task-aligned validation.

### Self-Supervised Learning

^sec-data-selection-selfsupervised-learning-7518

GPT was trained to predict the next token in a sequence. BERT was trained to fill in masked tokens. Neither pretraining objective required task-specific human labels. **Self-supervised learning**[[01 Books/Volume I - Foundations/03 Optimization/09 Data Selection/V1-09.15 Footnotes#^fn31|³¹]] (SSL) generalizes this insight. By designing *pretext tasks* that derive supervision from the data’s structure, models can learn reusable representations from unlabeled data at scale. Where active and semi-supervised learning reduce the demand for labels, SSL changes the accounting by making unlabeled structure the pretraining signal. [[01 Books/Volume I - Foundations/03 Optimization/09 Data Selection/V1-09.02 The Data Wall#^fig-data-selection-pipeline|Figure 1]] shows only the three levers; SSL sits beside them and extends the usable pool, so the question shifts from which labeled samples to train on to how unlabeled samples can support pretraining. It responds to one aspect of the data wall introduced in [[01 Books/Volume I - Foundations/03 Optimization/09 Data Selection/V1-09.02 The Data Wall#^sec-data-selection-data-selection-fundamentals-e839|section 9.1]] by broadening what counts as a training signal, while leaving corpus quality, licensing, coverage, and labeled evaluation requirements intact.

Labels are one form of supervision; a pretext task can also derive a learning signal from the structure of the data, as [[01 Books/Volume I - Foundations/03 Optimization/09 Data Selection/V1-09.07 Learning from Unlabeled Data#^tbl-self-supervised-tasks|table 5]] summarizes.

| **Modality**    | **Self-Supervised Task** |                      **Supervision Signal** |
|:----------------|:-------------------------|--------------------------------------------:|
| **Text**        | Masked language modeling |               Predict \[MASK\] from context |
| **Text**        | Next-token prediction    |              Predict next token in sequence |
| **Images**      | Contrastive learning     | Same image (augmented) vs. different images |
| **Images**      | Masked autoencoding      |                  Reconstruct masked patches |
| **Multi-modal** | CLIP-style alignment     |                      Match image-text pairs |

Table 5: **Self-Supervised Pretext Tasks by Modality**: Each task extracts supervision from data structure rather than human labels, enabling pretraining on large-scale unlabeled corpora.

^tbl-self-supervised-tasks

Pretext tasks generate supervision signals automatically. Masked-token objectives can encourage models to learn grammatical and semantic structure; next-token prediction teaches continuation structure; and contrastive image objectives encourage visual features that are invariant to selected transformations. In systems terms, self-supervision removes task-specific annotation as a prerequisite for pretraining without removing data cost. Pretraining can begin on unlabeled corpora without waiting for example-level labels, but collection, filtering, licensing, storage, and processing remain. If the representation transfers, one pretraining run serves many tasks and each task needs far fewer labels; task-specific adaptation and evaluation still carry their own data requirements.

When its pretraining cost is reused across tasks, SSL supports the **foundation model paradigm**[[01 Books/Volume I - Foundations/03 Optimization/09 Data Selection/V1-09.15 Footnotes#^fn32|³²]] ([[01 Books/Volume I - Foundations/03 Optimization/09 Data Selection/V1-09.14 Source References#^ref-bommasani2021opportunities|Bommasani et al. 2021]]). Data selection remains essential at both ends of this paradigm. Pretraining corpus curation applies deduplication and quality filtering at web scale, while downstream selection, through coresets or active learning, decides which fine-tuning examples maximize task-specific transfer.

![[90 Assets/Volume I/09 Data Selection/Vol1 Data Selection Margin 002 23e27de8e8.svg]]

*One red source node at left fans out through arrows to several blue downstream task nodes at right.*

*One pretraining corpus defect can affect many downstream tasks.*

Pretext objectives differ in what they stress on the machine. Contrastive learning trains a model to map two augmented views of the same image to nearby representations while pushing the representations of different images apart. Those other images are the *negatives*, and the objective learns better with more of them. SimCLR ([[01 Books/Volume I - Foundations/03 Optimization/09 Data Selection/V1-09.14 Source References#^ref-chen2020simclr|Chen et al. 2020]]) draws its negatives from the rest of the mini-batch, with batches of up to 8,192 examples, so the negative count is paid for in activation memory and, when the batch spans several accelerators, in communication to share every device’s representations. Momentum Contrast (MoCo) ([[01 Books/Volume I - Foundations/03 Optimization/09 Data Selection/V1-09.14 Source References#^ref-he2020|He et al. 2020]]) instead keeps recent representations in a queue produced by a slowly updated copy of the encoder (the momentum encoder), which decouples the number of negatives from batch size.

Masked autoencoders, a second family of pretext objectives, split an image into small square patches, hide most of them, and train the model to reconstruct what is hidden; because the encoder sees only the visible patches, it spends far less memory and compute per image. Autoregressive pretraining on text has no such discount, because it predicts every token of every sequence, so its cost grows with the number of tokens processed. For data selection, the common point is that pretraining turns raw structure into reusable signal that multiplies the value of scarce labels. Because the pretraining run itself is expensive, however, whether self-supervised pretraining pays off depends on how many downstream tasks reuse it.

### Foundation-Model Amortization

^sec-data-selection-economics-amortization-98bb

Pretraining a self-supervised model on a massive unlabeled corpus can consume thousands of accelerator hours, an upfront bill that rarely pays off if the resulting checkpoint serves only a single downstream classifier. **Cost amortization** makes that investment economical by splitting work into two phases: an expensive pretraining pass run once on unlabeled data, and lightweight task-specific adaptations, usually fine-tuning, reused across many applications ([[01 Books/Volume I - Foundations/03 Optimization/09 Data Selection/V1-09.07 Learning from Unlabeled Data#^tbl-cost-amortization|table 6]]).

↩︎ Fine-tuning’s memory and compute cost on one accelerator, set by which parameters it updates, is established in **[[01 Books/Volume I - Foundations/02 Development/08 Model Training/V1-08.04 Computation and Memory Costs of Training#^sec-model-training-finetuning-memory-one-accelerator-7c2e|Fine-tuning memory on one accelerator]]**.

| **Approach**                   | **Labels per Task** | **Compute per Task**  |     **Data Acquisition** |
|:-------------------------------|:--------------------|:----------------------|-------------------------:|
| **Train from scratch**         | 100K–1M labeled     | 100% full training    | Task-specific collection |
| **Fine-tune foundation model** | 100–1K labeled      | 1–5% of full training | Reuse pretraining corpus |

Table 6: **Illustrative Foundation-Model Amortization**: The label and compute ranges are scenario assumptions. They illustrate why reuse can lower marginal task cost when representations transfer, not a universal fine-tuning ratio.

^tbl-cost-amortization

Take a company building 10 specialized classifiers. Under the scenario assumptions, each task trained from scratch needs 100,000 labels at \$1 per label, for \$1M across all tasks and a modeled compute burden of 10,000 GPU-hours. The fine-tuning scenario instead pays 10,000 GPU-hours once, then assigns 1,000 labels and 50 GPU-hours of compute to each task, bringing the modeled fine-tuning label bill across all 10 tasks to \$10K. Assuming both approaches reach the same target quality on every downstream task, labeling cost drops by 100× and per-task marginal compute by 20× as direct consequences of the chosen scenario values.

The compute-only crossover, where total compute starts to favor fine-tuning, occurs when enough downstream tasks share the pretraining cost, provided the representations transfer at the required quality. A dollar crossover also depends on labeling savings, compute prices, and other costs that differ between the approaches. The side-by-side bar charts in [[01 Books/Volume I - Foundations/03 Optimization/09 Data Selection/V1-09.07 Learning from Unlabeled Data#^fig-amortization-comparison|figure 9]] contrast the cost structures across ten downstream tasks. Training from scratch expends a constant 1,000 GPU-hours per task (totaling 10,000 GPU-hours). The foundation model approach absorbs a 10,000 GPU-hour pretraining bar upfront but collapses subsequent task fine-tuning to 50 GPU-hours per bar.

![[90 Assets/Volume I/09 Data Selection/Cell 23 Output 1 c93a906fb3.png]]

Figure 9: **Cost Amortization in Foundation Models**: In this illustrative scenario, training from scratch (left) requires 1,000 GPU-hours per task (10,000 GPU-hours total for 10 tasks). The foundation model approach (right) pays 10,000 GPU-hours upfront for pretraining but reduces each subsequent task to just 50 GPU-hours. At 10 tasks the totals are comparable (10,000 GPU-hours vs. 10,500 GPU-hours), but the per-task marginal cost drops by 20×, and the compute-only crossover favoring the foundation model occurs around 10.5 tasks. The plotted 10-task snapshot therefore remains just below that break-even point.

^fig-amortization-comparison

Every lever is now in view: pruning, reordering, labeling, creating, and learning without labels. Each has been judged so far by the work it removes; the next question is whether the machine can run it without giving that saving back.
