# V1-09.05 Dynamic Selection

[[Home|Home]] · [[01 Books/Volume I - Foundations/Volume 1 Overview|Volume Overview]] · [[01 Books/Volume I - Foundations/03 Optimization/09 Data Selection/V1-09 Data Selection|V1-09 Data Selection]]

^sec-data-selection-dynamic-selection-edaa

Early in a ResNet-50 run over ImageNet, clean, centered images of common classes provide stable gradients that establish general feature representations, but as parameters converge their gradient norms decay toward zero. Executing forward and backward passes on these mastered samples continues to consume accelerator execution cycles and high-bandwidth memory (HBM) transfers while producing negligible parameter updates, driving their effective ICR toward zero. Dynamic selection mitigates this waste by adapting batch composition to the model’s current state. Adapting batches during training shifts selection from an offline preprocessing step into the active execution loop, modifying the operation count (${O}$) at runtime. The same adaptive logic extends to labels, where active learning decides which examples receive a human label as the model’s uncertainty shifts.

### Curriculum Learning

^sec-data-selection-curriculum-learning-easy-hard-2c4e

Random shuffling feeds hard boundary cases, noisy labels, and outliers to an untrained model before its early features have stabilized, while still spending full epochs on simple examples long after the model has mastered them. **Curriculum learning**[[01 Books/Volume I - Foundations/03 Optimization/09 Data Selection/V1-09.15 Footnotes#^fn15|¹⁵]] ([[01 Books/Volume I - Foundations/03 Optimization/09 Data Selection/V1-09.14 Source References#^ref-bengio2009|Bengio et al. 2009]]; [[01 Books/Volume I - Foundations/03 Optimization/09 Data Selection/V1-09.14 Source References#^ref-soviany2022|Soviany et al. 2022]]) orders the training stream instead of shuffling it uniformly, starting with simpler examples and gradually introducing harder ones as parameters converge. Ordering the stream changes which gradients dominate at each stage of training. Easy examples provide stable early signals, while examples scored as hard can contain useful boundary information, label noise, or outliers. An easy-to-hard schedule can smooth training on some workloads, but its benefit depends on the difficulty score, pacing rule, model, and data; any claimed epoch reduction must therefore be treated as illustrative rather than guaranteed.

Implementing a curriculum requires two components: a difficulty scorer that ranks samples, and a pacing function that controls how quickly hard samples are introduced. A common choice is linear pacing:

$$
\text{samples}_{{n_{\text{epoch}}}} = \texttt{sort\_by\_difficulty}[:{D}\cdot \min(1, {n_{\text{epoch}}}/{N_{\text{warmup}}})]
$$

 where ${n_{\text{epoch}}}$ is the current epoch, ${D}$ is the total dataset size, and ${N_{\text{warmup}}}$ is the number of warmup epochs before the full dataset becomes available. Early epochs train on the easiest fraction ${n_{\text{epoch}}}/{N_{\text{warmup}}}$ of the ${D}$ samples; after warmup, training proceeds on the full dataset. From a systems standpoint, dynamically re-sorting multi-terabyte datasets epoch-by-epoch destroys sequential I/O read-ahead and stalls accelerators on storage latency. Production data loaders implement curricula by pre-sorting data offline into discrete difficulty-tiered shards or buckets. During training, workers stream shards sequentially from storage while maintaining local shuffle buffers within each tier, preserving high sequential storage bandwidth while maintaining batch diversity and gradient stability.

The pacing rule fixes one saving before any benchmark is run. Summed over the warmup, the loader serves $\sum_{{n_{\text{epoch}}}=1}^{{N_{\text{warmup}}}} {D} \cdot {n_{\text{epoch}}}/{N_{\text{warmup}}}= {D} ({N_{\text{warmup}}}+1)/2$ samples in place of ${N_{\text{warmup}}}\cdot {D}$, so the warmup epochs cost roughly half their full-data FLOPs. A curriculum can therefore save compute in two distinct ways, through cheaper warmup epochs and through any reduction in the total epochs needed to reach target quality, and a claimed saving must say which of the two it measured.

The difficulty scorer is a systems choice because it trades scoring compute against ordering quality, as [[01 Books/Volume I - Foundations/03 Optimization/09 Data Selection/V1-09.05 Dynamic Selection#^tbl-difficulty-scoring|table 3]] shows. Loss scoring runs a probe model, a small proxy trained briefly to rank examples, and confidence scoring runs a teacher, so both gain better ordering at the cost of extra inference. Heuristics avoid that compute but require domain knowledge, and self-paced scoring moves adaptation into the training loop itself.

| **Strategy**          | **Difficulty Score**                      | **Best For**                                |
|:----------------------|:------------------------------------------|:--------------------------------------------|
| **Loss-Based**        | Loss from probe model (low = easy)        | General-purpose; requires probe training    |
| **Confidence-Based**  | Teacher model confidence (high = easy)    | When teacher available; distillation setups |
| **Domain Heuristics** | Sentence length, image complexity         | No extra compute; domain knowledge required |
| **Self-Paced**        | Current model’s loss (updated each epoch) | Adaptive; no probe needed                   |

Table 3: **Difficulty Scoring Strategies for Curriculum Learning**: The rows compare each scorer’s signal source with the system prerequisite or trade-off it introduces.

^tbl-difficulty-scoring

To see how curriculum savings translate to systems accounting, consider an illustrative workload where a linear-warmup curriculum reaches target accuracy in 115 epochs compared to 150 epochs for the standard shuffled baseline, an apparent 23.3 percent epoch reduction. Counting epochs alone, however, obscures the actual systems cost. Because linear pacing serves fewer samples during warmup, early epochs consume fewer FLOPs than post-warmup epochs; conversely, difficulty scoring and shard reordering introduce data-loading overhead that must be offset by the training speedup.

Alternative curriculum strategies diverge further in their computational demands. *Anti-curriculum* reverses the usual order, presenting the hardest examples first when boundary definition accelerates early convergence, while *self-paced learning* continuously updates sample difficulty using the current model’s training loss. Because optimal sample ordering depends on the architecture, optimizer, and label noise, curriculum gains cannot be assumed from nominal epoch reductions. They must be verified by measuring end-to-end wall-clock time at the target quality metric. Curricula also still assume that every candidate sample already has a label.

### Active Learning

^sec-data-selection-active-learning-humanintheloop-6932

Ordering a training stream helps only after labels exist, and in domains such as clinical imaging or specialist inspection each annotation requires expert time that can cost more than the training run itself. Active learning[[01 Books/Volume I - Foundations/03 Optimization/09 Data Selection/V1-09.15 Footnotes#^fn16|¹⁶]] ([[01 Books/Volume I - Foundations/03 Optimization/09 Data Selection/V1-09.14 Source References#^ref-settles2012|Settles 2012]]; [[01 Books/Volume I - Foundations/03 Optimization/09 Data Selection/V1-09.14 Source References#^ref-ren2021|Ren et al. 2021]]), which **[[01 Books/Volume I - Foundations/01 Foundations/04 Data Engineering/V1-04.07 Data Labeling#^sec-data-engineering-scaling-aiassisted-labeling-9360|Scaling with AI-assisted labeling]]** introduced as a way to stretch a labeling budget, becomes a selection loop here. In active learning the label comes from an oracle, the human expert who supplies the correct label for a queried sample. Rather than labeling an entire pool upfront, active learning shifts the optimization target from choosing which labeled samples to train on to choosing which unlabeled samples are worth paying the oracle to label.

↩︎ The labeling budget, and what each label costs at scale, are established in **[[01 Books/Volume I - Foundations/01 Foundations/04 Data Engineering/V1-04.07 Data Labeling#^sec-data-engineering-labeling-economics-3e91|Labeling economics]]**.

Active learning replaces one-shot collection with an iterative loop of five stages ([[01 Books/Volume I - Foundations/03 Optimization/09 Data Selection/V1-09.05 Dynamic Selection#^fig-active-learning-loop|figure 6]]). The selection strategy queries the unlabeled pool based on model uncertainty and routes ambiguous instances to a human oracle. The loop then merges newly labeled data into the training set and retrains the model to update uncertainty scores over the remaining pool.

The effectiveness of this loop depends on the query strategy used to select samples for annotation ([[01 Books/Volume I - Foundations/03 Optimization/09 Data Selection/V1-09.14 Source References#^ref-settles2009active|Settles 2009]], [[01 Books/Volume I - Foundations/03 Optimization/09 Data Selection/V1-09.14 Source References#^ref-settles2012|2012]]; [[01 Books/Volume I - Foundations/03 Optimization/09 Data Selection/V1-09.14 Source References#^ref-ren2021|Ren et al. 2021]]). The simplest approach, uncertainty sampling, selects samples where the model is least confident, such as predictions near 0.5 probability for binary classification. Scoring with a single model is inexpensive, yet it can overconcentrate queries near one ambiguous region and miss inputs the model is confidently wrong about.

Query-by-committee addresses that single-model blind spot by training multiple models and selecting samples where they disagree most. That disagreement measures epistemic uncertainty, the uncertainty that comes from the model’s limited knowledge and that more labeled data can reduce, and measuring it costs ensemble inference. When practitioners can invest even more compute per candidate, expected model change selects the samples that would induce the largest gradient update if labeled, trading per-sample gradient evaluations for a more direct estimate of parameter impact. Any purely uncertainty- or gradient-driven rule can still cluster an entire acquisition batch inside one narrow pocket of feature space. Diversity sampling complements these methods by selecting samples that are dissimilar from currently labeled data, which spreads the annotation budget across the input distribution.

Active learning pays off most clearly in domains where every label requires scarce specialist judgment. When a ResNet-50-class image classifier moves from ImageNet to chest X-rays, for instance, every label requires a radiologist, and the model may be confident on common conditions but uncertain about rarer cases. By focusing human annotation on these ambiguous cases, active learning directs expensive expert time toward the examples most likely to move the decision boundary. Because annotation cost and turnaround in such domains dominate the compute needed to score candidates, routing only the examples the model is least sure about to the expert changes the economics of both labeling and per-epoch training work. A saving claimed for active learning is measured against a budget-matched random baseline, the randomly chosen examples the same labeling budget would buy, and only after the compute spent scoring the pool is charged against it.

**Napkin Math 9.3: The active learning ROI**

**Problem**: A hospital team has 1 Million unlabeled scans, a \$5/label specialist-label cost, and a \$500,000 budget. How does uncertainty sampling change labeling cost and per-epoch training work relative to the budget-matched random baseline?

**Scenario A: Naive Labeling**

- **Cost**: Labeling all 1 Million scans would cost \$5,000,000 (10× over budget).
- **Budget-matched baseline**: $\$500,000 \div \$5/\text{label} = 100,000$ random scans.
- **Naive labeling outcome**: Random selection does not guarantee rare-pathology coverage.

**Scenario B: Active Learning**

- **Strategy**: Uncertainty sampling selects 50,000 scans for specialist labeling.
- **Assumption**: These scans reach the accuracy of the 100,000 random scans, a 2× label reduction.
- **Cost**: $50,000 \times \$5/\text{label} = \$250,000$ (50 percent under budget).
- **Training work**: $100,000 \div 50,000 = 2×$ fewer examples per epoch than the budget-matched random baseline.
- **Active learning outcome**: Matching 100,000 random scans requires empirical validation.

**Systems insight**: Against the budget-matched random baseline, selection saves $\$500,000 - \$250,000 = \$250,000$; accuracy still requires empirical validation.

The notebook’s 2× label reduction is an assumption, and the size of that multiplier is what decides the outcome. In the illustrative learning curves of [[01 Books/Volume I - Foundations/03 Optimization/09 Data Selection/V1-09.05 Dynamic Selection#^fig-active-learning-multiplier|figure 7]], uncertainty sampling reaches 90 percent accuracy with roughly 2,197 annotations while uniform random sampling needs about 10,000, an approximately 4.6× gap. These curves are constructed to illustrate label efficiency, not fitted to measured learning performance.

Figure 7: **The Active Learning Multiplier**: Model accuracy is plotted against labeled samples on a logarithmic axis. In this illustrative scenario, active learning (green solid) reaches the same accuracy with fewer labels than random sampling (gray dashed); the arrow connects the curve intersections at 90 percent accuracy, an approximately 4.6× label-efficiency gap. The curves are illustrative, not measured.

^fig-active-learning-multiplier

At a fixed price per label, label count and labeling dollars are the same axis, so the figure and notebook [9.3](https://mlsysbook.ai/vol1/09_data_selection/09_data_selection.html#nbk-data-selection-active-learning-roi) differ only in the multiplier they assume. The notebook plans on the conservative 2×; the figure shows what a more favorable task would look like. Because the realized multiplier depends on the task, the model, and the query rule, a team budgets on the conservative value and measures the real one on its own data. Active learning also incurs scoring, acquisition, and repeated-retraining costs, so end-to-end savings can be smaller than the label-count gap; the break-even analysis of [[01 Books/Volume I - Foundations/03 Optimization/09 Data Selection/V1-09.09 Choosing a Selection Strategy#^sec-data-selection-breakeven-analysis-9a38|section 9.8.2]] adds the scoring cost to the comparison. Beyond saving labeling cost, however, a suitable query rule concentrates review on uncertain, diverse, or failure-relevant examples that uniform sampling rarely surfaces.

**Example 9.4: Hard negative mining in a smart doorbell**

**Scenario**: A smart doorbell vision detector receives continuous video feeds dominated by empty backgrounds (easy negatives) and normal pedestrian poses.

**Diagnosis**: Random frame sampling can miss rare false positives such as statues, laundry piles, and human-shaped shadows. Active learning routes uncertain predictions (“Person: 51%”) and sampled production errors to human reviewers for targeted labeling.

**Systems lesson**: Hard-negative mining can improve labeling efficiency by spending review effort on suspected false positives and other difficult negatives. The query stream still needs diversity and coverage checks so confident but systematically wrong cases are not excluded.

^exmp-data-selection-mining-hard-negatives

Curriculum learning and active learning both choose among examples that already exist. When the pool itself lacks the cases a model needs (rare classes with too few examples, edge cases that never appear in the wild, records that privacy rules keep out of reach), no ordering or labeling policy can supply them. The remaining lever is to create them.
