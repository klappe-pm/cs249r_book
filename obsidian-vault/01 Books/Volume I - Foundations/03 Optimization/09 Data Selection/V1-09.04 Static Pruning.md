# V1-09.04 Static Pruning

[[Home|Home]] · [[01 Books/Volume I - Foundations/Volume 1 Overview|Volume Overview]] · [[01 Books/Volume I - Foundations/03 Optimization/09 Data Selection/V1-09 Data Selection|V1-09 Data Selection]]

^sec-data-selection-static-pruning-a390

Web-scale scraping routinely gathers boilerplate text, duplicate images, and malformed records, and each one costs GPT-2 six FLOPs per parameter for every token, or ResNet-50 a full forward and backward pass, before a single useful gradient is calculated. Static pruning removes these low-value records upstream of the training loop. By dropping redundant and corrupt records before target-model training begins, static pruning shrinks the operation count $({O})$ without changing model architectures or execution kernels. Once empirical learning curves establish that a corpus carries removable redundancy and noise, a static pipeline strips out exact and near duplicates first, filters corrupted or mislabeled records next, and ranks the remaining clean pool with coreset algorithms.

### The Case for Smaller Datasets

^sec-data-selection-case-smaller-datasets-215e

On CIFAR-10, Paul et al. ([[01 Books/Volume I - Foundations/03 Optimization/09 Data Selection/V1-09.14 Source References#^ref-paul2021deep|2021]]) report that EL2N pruning (which scores each example by its prediction error early in training) removed half of the training data without reducing accuracy, and on ImageNet-1K Sorscher et al. ([[01 Books/Volume I - Foundations/03 Optimization/09 Data Selection/V1-09.14 Source References#^ref-ganguli2022|2022]]) report that a prototype metric, which scores each image by its distance from the center of its cluster in an embedding learned without labels, discarded 20 percent of the data without sacrificing performance. These empirical pruning studies confirm that dataset volume alone does not determine model accuracy, because large corpora carry substantial redundancy where many examples contribute almost no gradient signal while still costing a full training step. The pattern extends to language modeling, where web-scraped corpora like The Pile[[01 Books/Volume I - Foundations/03 Optimization/09 Data Selection/V1-09.15 Footnotes#^fn5|⁵]] and C4[[01 Books/Volume I - Foundations/03 Optimization/09 Data Selection/V1-09.15 Footnotes#^fn6|⁶]] contain enough duplicate and templated content to make deduplication a systems issue. In the datasets studied by Lee et al. ([[01 Books/Volume I - Foundations/03 Optimization/09 Data Selection/V1-09.14 Source References#^ref-lee2022|2022]]), deduplicated training reduced memorization while preserving or improving perplexity, a measure of how surprised a language model is by held-out text (lower is better). Still, these reported gains are benchmark-specific, because pruning effectiveness depends on how redundant the dataset is, which selection algorithm is used, and the capacity of the target model, so teams must validate aggressive pruning on their own task before committing to it.

That redundancy means individual data points need not provide equal value for training. This heterogeneity follows in part from how classifiers learn decision boundaries. Many samples lie far from any boundary. The model soon classifies them correctly and confidently, so their loss and gradient shrink and a training step on them teaches little. A picture of a dog in good lighting may become easy in this way once the model has learned its dominant features, while ambiguous and underrepresented cases can remain informative.

Label quality also affects data requirements. Conflicting labels can push updates in opposing directions, so reaching a target error may require more examples or more passes over them. The size of that penalty depends on the task and training procedure. A **data quality multiplier** therefore comes from comparing learning curves at the same held-out error, rather than assigning a universal convergence rate to all noisy data.

**Napkin Math 9.2: The data quality multiplier**

**Problem**: Two hypothetical pilot learning curves reach the same held-out error using different amounts of clean and noisy data. With epoch count and per-sample work held fixed, what training-work multiplier do these sample counts imply?

**Setup**: Consider a representative evaluation where a fixed model architecture and training recipe reach target error ${\epsilon}$ = 0.01 (1 percent) under clean versus noisy training partitions:

- Clean data: ${D_{\text{clean}}}$ = 10,000
- Noisy data: ${D_{\text{noisy}}}$ = 16,000

**Result**: Dividing the noisy-data count by the clean-data count gives 1.6×. Under the fixed training recipe, the noisy dataset therefore requires 1.6× as much training work to reach the same error.

**Systems insight**: The avoided training work sets a budget for cleaning. Cleaning pays for itself only when correction or review costs less than the work it removes, with required coverage preserved. If the two learning curves require different epoch counts, compare total examples processed to the target instead of unique sample counts. The decision depends on measured learning curves and pipeline costs, not on the presence of noise alone.

### Data Deduplication

^sec-data-selection-data-deduplication-6c20

The cheapest samples to remove are the ones that repeat what the dataset already contains. Repeated content costs a full forward and backward pass on every presentation, even when that repetition adds little useful learning signal. Exact hashing and shingle-based near-duplicate detection can identify candidates for removal without training a scoring model. Deduplication therefore comes first among the static levers, but near-duplicate thresholds must be validated so the pipeline does not remove useful distributional signal.

The simplest form of deduplication uses hash-based methods for exact matches. By computing a cryptographic hash (MD5 or SHA-256) for each sample and removing those with identical hashes, practitioners can eliminate the byte-for-byte duplicates that accumulate in large web-scraped corpora. This process is computationally cheap, scaling linearly with dataset size, and can be parallelized trivially.

↩︎ Deduplication as a deterministic data-cleaning step that keeps training and serving pipelines consistent is established in **[[01 Books/Volume I - Foundations/01 Foundations/04 Data Engineering/V1-04.08 Data Processing#^sec-data-engineering-ensuring-trainingserving-consistency-c683|Training-serving consistency]]**.

Near-duplicate detection addresses content that differs at the byte level while retaining substantial token- or character-shingle overlap. For text, MinHash[[01 Books/Volume I - Foundations/03 Optimization/09 Data Selection/V1-09.15 Footnotes#^fn7|⁷]] with Locality-Sensitive Hashing[[01 Books/Volume I - Foundations/03 Optimization/09 Data Selection/V1-09.15 Footnotes#^fn8|⁸]] (LSH) approximates Jaccard similarity[[01 Books/Volume I - Foundations/03 Optimization/09 Data Selection/V1-09.15 Footnotes#^fn9|⁹]] efficiently. In practice, the pipeline converts each document into a set of ${w}$-shingles, which are contiguous spans of ${w}$ characters or words, turning sequence comparison into set overlap. It then compresses each shingle set into a fixed-length MinHash signature, where the probability that two documents agree at a signature position equals their Jaccard similarity.

Comparing two short signatures is fast, yet comparing every pair of signatures across ${D}$ documents would still require $\mathcal{O}({D}^2)$ work. To avoid that all-pairs scan, LSH partitions each signature into ${L}$ bands of ${r}$ rows and hashes each band into a bucket. Documents that match across all ${r}$ rows of any single band collide in the same bucket, restricting pairwise similarity verification to likely candidates across a corpus of ${D}$ web documents numbering in the billions. This pipeline detects lightly edited content with high lexical overlap, though it does not establish semantic paraphrase equivalence.

For images, perceptual hashing produces signatures robust to minor transformations like resizing and compression, identifying visually similar candidates stored in different formats. Embedding-based similarity can retrieve semantic near-neighbor candidates by computing dense representations (CLIP[[01 Books/Volume I - Foundations/03 Optimization/09 Data Selection/V1-09.15 Footnotes#^fn10|¹⁰]] ([[01 Books/Volume I - Foundations/03 Optimization/09 Data Selection/V1-09.14 Source References#^ref-radford2021learning|Radford et al. 2021]]) for images, sentence transformers for text), though proximity does not itself prove duplication and the approach incurs higher computational overhead. Across both modalities, deduplication can reduce repeated-content memorization and avoid spending FLOPs on exact or near duplicates. It can also alter the corpus’s intentional empirical weighting, so thresholds and downstream quality must be validated rather than assuming that every duplicate is harmful.

On GPT-2’s run, deduplication costs little and returns hours of accelerator time. Lee et al. ([[01 Books/Volume I - Foundations/03 Optimization/09 Data Selection/V1-09.14 Source References#^ref-lee2022|2022]]) found that near-duplicate removal affected between 3.04 percent (C4) and 13.63 percent (RealNews) of the corpora they studied. If WebText carried a comparable share, removing it would cut the same fraction of the 167 billion tokens GPT-2 processed. The saving would be about 3 hours to 14 hours of the 4.4 days that one eight-H100 machine needs, or 18 kWh to 80 kWh of accelerator energy. The removed tokens are copies of text the corpus already holds, so the cut does not shrink the unique pool GPT-2 is starved for. A data-starved run spends those freed hours on new or better tokens rather than taking them as a shorter run.

Finding the duplicates reads the 40 GB corpus once, about 6 seconds at the 7 GB/s of the machine’s local NVMe storage. The search also holds a 128-hash MinHash signature for each of WebText’s roughly 8 million documents, about 4.1 GB of host memory. Hashing runs on host CPUs rather than on the accelerators, so the cost of deduplication lands on a different stage of the machine from the work it removes. The open question for a corpus this size is which near-duplicates are safe to remove, not whether the machine can afford to find them. Once those duplicates are gone, the next target is records that are unique but broken.

### Data Pruning by Quality

^sec-data-selection-data-pruning-quality-3d69

A unique training example can still waste compute or actively damage the model if its content is garbled or its label contradicts the true class. **Quality-based pruning** removes samples that carry no usable signal or push gradients in the wrong direction. In supervised datasets, label error detection is often the highest-return form of quality pruning. Tools like Cleanlab identify samples where the assigned label may be incorrect, based on model confidence patterns across training. A sample that the model consistently predicts as class A while its label says class B may be a hard boundary case, a distribution shift, a model blind spot, or an annotation mistake. Because those causes call for different remedies, the score should trigger review rather than automatic deletion, so confirmed errors can be corrected or removed without discarding hard examples.

Where label error detection checks whether the target contradicts the input, outlier removal checks whether the input itself sits far from any cluster center in feature space. Such points can indicate sensor noise, annotation errors, or file corruption. In heavy-tailed domains, however, they can also be the rare edge cases that define deployment risk, so outlier thresholds must stay conservative and the affected slices should be reviewed before removal.

Unlabeled pretraining corpora have no class labels to audit, so low-information filtering instead applies domain-specific heuristics to drop samples that carry too little signal to justify a training step. For text corpora, pipelines remove high-perplexity garbled strings (high perplexity can flag incoherent text, but also valid specialist or rare-language writing) alongside low-perplexity boilerplate or repetitive templates. For image datasets, they filter blurry, corrupted, or near-uniform frames.

Because hand-written heuristics can miss subtler quality differences across billions of documents, industrial pretraining pipelines pair them with *classifier-based filtering*. This approach trains a lightweight linear classifier (such as fastText) with curated reference text (e.g., Wikipedia, books, and scientific papers) as positive examples and uncurated web dumps as negative examples. The classifier assigns each scraped document a continuous quality score, allowing the pipeline to drop millions of low-quality pages at gigabyte-per-second ingestion speeds before ranking the clean examples that remain.

### Coreset Selection Algorithms

^sec-data-selection-coreset-selection-algorithms-2c74

Once duplicates and defective records are gone, the remaining samples are unique and valid but still unequal in value, which is the heterogeneity described in [[01 Books/Volume I - Foundations/03 Optimization/09 Data Selection/V1-09.04 Static Pruning#^sec-data-selection-case-smaller-datasets-215e|section 9.3.1]]. Ranking them requires systematic selection criteria. **Coreset selection**[[01 Books/Volume I - Foundations/03 Optimization/09 Data Selection/V1-09.15 Footnotes#^fn11|¹¹]] turns the static pruning decision into a coverage problem, in which the goal is the smallest subset that preserves the statistical properties of the entire dataset. The systems decision is where to spend the selection budget, whether on cheap coverage metrics that preserve distributional structure or on costlier training-dynamics scores that better target the decision boundary. That decision also needs a guardrail before any score is applied. Classes, demographic groups, time windows, and rare failure modes that matter at deployment each require a coverage floor, a minimum number of retained examples, because the highest-average-ICR subset can still remove the examples that define production risk. Each floor is chosen from evidence about that slice; one defensible choice is the smallest number of examples at which the slice’s held-out accuracy stops improving on its own learning curve ([[01 Books/Volume I - Foundations/03 Optimization/09 Data Selection/V1-09.03 Measuring Data Value#^sec-data-selection-core-metrics-32e9|section 9.2.4]]).

Geometry-based methods select samples that cover a chosen representation without requiring target-model training. The ${k}$-Center algorithm[[01 Books/Volume I - Foundations/03 Optimization/09 Data Selection/V1-09.15 Footnotes#^fn12|¹²]] selects samples so that every point in the dataset lies close to some selected sample, minimizing the maximum distance from any point to its nearest selected center under the chosen embedding and metric. Herding takes a different approach, building the subset one sample at a time so that its feature statistics, such as the mean, stay close to those of the full dataset. The idea adapts Welling’s procedure for constructing representative samples ([[01 Books/Volume I - Foundations/03 Optimization/09 Data Selection/V1-09.14 Source References#^ref-welling2009|Welling 2009]]). These methods are computationally attractive because they operate on feature representations, but their scores are not inherently label-aware.

Training-dynamics methods use a proxy model to identify examples that may be important. GraNd (Gradient Normed) and **EL2N (error L2-norm)**[[01 Books/Volume I - Foundations/03 Optimization/09 Data Selection/V1-09.15 Footnotes#^fn13|¹³]] score samples by gradient magnitude or prediction error early in training ([[01 Books/Volume I - Foundations/03 Optimization/09 Data Selection/V1-09.14 Source References#^ref-paul2021deep|Paul et al. 2021]]). A third training-dynamics signal, forgetting events,[[01 Books/Volume I - Foundations/03 Optimization/09 Data Selection/V1-09.15 Footnotes#^fn14|¹⁴]] tracks how often a sample is correctly classified and later misclassified during training ([[01 Books/Volume I - Foundations/03 Optimization/09 Data Selection/V1-09.14 Source References#^ref-toneva2019empirical|Toneva et al. 2019]]). The exact alternative, influence functions ([[01 Books/Volume I - Foundations/03 Optimization/09 Data Selection/V1-09.14 Source References#^ref-koh2017understanding|Koh and Liang 2017]]), measures how removing a sample changes validation loss. Yet it requires the inverse Hessian ${\mathbf{H}}^{-1}$ of the loss, where the Hessian holds the second derivative of the loss for every pair of the ${P}$ parameters. Storing it takes $\mathcal{O}({P}^2)$ memory and inverting it takes $\mathcal{O}({P}^3)$ arithmetic, which rules it out for models with millions or billions of parameters.

GraNd and EL2N circumvent this barrier with lightweight first-order approximations. GraNd scores each sample by its expected gradient norm ($\|\nabla_{\theta}{\mathcal{L}}\|_2$), which avoids the Hessian but still requires per-sample backpropagation rather than a single batch-averaged backward pass. EL2N removes that backward pass as well. Under cross-entropy loss with a softmax output layer, backpropagation starts from the output residual ${\hat{\mathbf{y}}} - {\mathbf{y}}$, the difference between the predicted probability vector ${\hat{\mathbf{y}}}$ and the one-hot label ${\mathbf{y}}$, so early in training the parameter gradient norm correlates strongly with the output error norm $\|{\hat{\mathbf{y}}} - {\mathbf{y}}\|_2$. Practitioners can therefore score each example from a single forward pass without computing backward-pass gradients.

EL2N error is not the same signal as predictive uncertainty, since a confident prediction of the wrong class produces a large EL2N error despite low predictive uncertainty. High scores identify examples the proxy finds difficult, though they can also flag mislabeled or outlying records and therefore need coverage and quality checks. The EL2N study also reports useful score transfer across several architectures, which allows a small proxy model to score candidates for a larger target model.

Each algorithm in [[01 Books/Volume I - Foundations/03 Optimization/09 Data Selection/V1-09.04 Static Pruning#^tbl-coreset-comparison|table 2]] balances upfront scoring computation against downstream training reduction. Because geometry-based and training-dynamics methods optimize different proxies, their relative ranking depends on dataset scale, feature representations, target model capacity, and scoring budgets; a score is computationally justified only when downstream training savings exceed evaluation overhead.

| **Method**     | **Compute Cost**                               | **Requires Training** | **Best For**          | **Limitation**            |
|:---------------|:-----------------------------------------------|:----------------------|:----------------------|:--------------------------|
| **k-Center**   | $\mathcal{O}({D}^2)$ or $\mathcal{O}({D} {K})$ | No                    | Coverage, exploration | Ignores label information |
| **Herding**    | $\mathcal{O}({D} {K})$                         | No                    | Distribution matching | Matches chosen moments    |
| **GraNd**      | $\mathcal{O}(\text{epochs} \times {D})$        | Yes (few epochs)      | Decision boundaries   | Requires proxy training   |
| **EL2N**       | $\mathcal{O}(\text{epochs} \times {D})$        | Yes (few epochs)      | Error-based scoring   | Can rank noise as hard    |
| **Forgetting** | $\mathcal{O}(\text{full training})$            | Yes (full)            | Hard examples         | Expensive to compute      |

Table 2: **Coreset Selection Algorithm Comparison**: ${D} =$ dataset size, ${K}=$ coreset size. Geometry-based and training-dynamics methods optimize different proxies, so their quality ranking depends on the dataset, representation, target model, and scoring budget.

^tbl-coreset-comparison

Geometry-based methods prioritize coverage in a chosen feature representation, while training-dynamics methods use signals such as error or forgetting. Uncertainty sampling, which selects the examples the model is least confident about and which active learning uses as a query rule ([[01 Books/Volume I - Foundations/03 Optimization/09 Data Selection/V1-09.05 Dynamic Selection#^sec-data-selection-active-learning-humanintheloop-6932|section 9.4.2]]), concentrates the budget near the current decision boundary; EL2N instead ranks prediction error, which can also be high for confidently wrong examples away from the boundary. [[01 Books/Volume I - Foundations/03 Optimization/09 Data Selection/V1-09.04 Static Pruning#^fig-coreset-selection|Figure 5]] contrasts that boundary-focused allocation with uniform random sampling of the same five-point budget. The figure illustrates one family of selection rules. A geometry-based method such as ${k}$-center would instead spread its picks to cover the feature space.

Figure 5: **Illustrative Boundary-Focused Selection**: Both panels select five samples, isolating allocation rather than budget. Random sampling distributes those choices across the feature space; a boundary-focused rule such as uncertainty sampling concentrates them in the shaded band around the current decision boundary.

EL2N with a small proxy model offers a practical balance between scoring cost and selection quality when no pretrained scorer is on hand. The workflow trains a lightweight proxy for a few epochs, computes EL2N scores, and selects a subset subject to coverage and quality checks. The proxy’s training epochs count as selection overhead alongside the scoring pass, and validation must then establish whether the resulting subset preserves target accuracy.

**Example 9.3: Coreset selection in practice**

**Scenario**: Without the pretrained checkpoints assumed in example [[01 Books/Volume I - Foundations/03 Optimization/09 Data Selection/V1-09.03 Measuring Data Value#^exmp-data-selection-selection-inequality-practice|9.2]], the vision team must train a proxy from scratch to select its 128,117-image coreset (10 percent) from the 1.28 million ImageNet training images for ResNet-50.

**Diagnosis**: Naive random subsampling can miss rare classes and boundary examples. Training a lightweight proxy model for 5 epochs calculates EL2N error scores, prioritizing high-error examples while checking rare-class coverage.

**Systems lesson**: Proxy-based coreset selection replaces some full-data passes with targeted scoring compute. It accelerates full-model training only when the proxy ranking transfers and coverage checks preserve rare classes and edge cases.

^exmp-data-selection-coreset-selection-practice

[[01 Books/Volume I - Foundations/03 Optimization/09 Data Selection/V1-09.04 Static Pruning#^lst-el2n-coreset|Listing 1]] turns proxy prediction error into reusable selected indices. The `compute_el2n_scores` function spends a small amount of probe compute to measure which samples still confuse a briefly trained model, and `select_coreset` retains the highest-scoring examples. This sketch keeps the top-${K}$ scores and needs quality and coverage checks before its subset replaces the full training pool. The scoring pass requires an unshuffled dataloader so each score position remains aligned with its dataset index.

``` python
def compute_el2n_scores(model, dataloader, num_epochs=5):
    """Compute EL2N scores.

    Returns L2 norm of (prediction - one_hot_label).
    """
    # Train proxy model for a few epochs to get meaningful predictions
    train_proxy(model, dataloader, num_epochs)

    scores = []
    model.eval()
    for x, y in dataloader:
        logits = model(x)
        probs = softmax(logits, dim=1)
        # One-hot encode labels
        one_hot = zeros_like(probs).scatter_(1, y.unsqueeze(1), 1)
        # EL2N score = L2 distance between probabilities and one-hot target
        el2n = (probs - one_hot).norm(dim=1)  # High = large prediction error
        scores.extend(el2n.tolist())
    return scores

def select_coreset(scores, dataset, fraction=0.1):
    """Select top-k samples with the largest prediction errors."""
    k = int(len(dataset) * fraction)
    # Sort by score descending (largest prediction error first)
    indices = argsort(scores, descending=True)[:k]
    return Subset(dataset, indices)

# Illustrative 10% subset; validate accuracy and coverage before use
scores = compute_el2n_scores(proxy_model, full_loader)
coreset = select_coreset(scores, full_dataset, fraction=0.1)
train_full_model(model, coreset)
```

Listing 1: **EL2N-Based Coreset Selection**: The `compute_el2n_scores` function trains a proxy model for a few epochs, then measures prediction error via L2 distance from one-hot labels. High scores identify samples the proxy finds difficult, including potentially noisy examples. The `select_coreset` function illustrates ranking by this score; production use also requires coverage, noise, and target-quality validation.

^lst-el2n-coreset

Because the unconstrained `select_coreset` slice in [[01 Books/Volume I - Foundations/03 Optimization/09 Data Selection/V1-09.04 Static Pruning#^lst-el2n-coreset|listing 1]] takes the global top-${K}$ scores regardless of class, it can under-sample a rare slice whose examples score lower than the majority class; enforcing a coverage floor changes which indices the ranking retains. Consider disjoint classes A and B, a total coreset budget ${K}$ as in [[01 Books/Volume I - Foundations/03 Optimization/09 Data Selection/V1-09.04 Static Pruning#^tbl-coreset-comparison|table 2]], and task-validated minimum counts ${a}$ and ${b}$ for the respective classes. Call an example eligible when it is still in the candidate pool after deduplication and quality filtering. First reserve the highest-scoring ${a}$ eligible examples from A and the highest-scoring ${b}$ from B. Fill the remaining ${K}-{a}-{b}$ slots from the global ranking, skipping indices already reserved. The allocation is infeasible if ${K}<{a}+{b}$, if either class lacks enough eligible examples to meet its floor, or if the eligible pool is smaller than ${K}$; the pipeline must revise the budget or acquire missing coverage before training.

Suppose the unconstrained top-${K}$ ranking contains only A examples and the budget can meet both floors. Reserving B’s floor replaces the lowest-scoring ${b}$ members of that all-A subset with the highest-scoring eligible B examples, while the remaining slots still follow the score order. Coverage thus has an explicit opportunity cost in the selection score. Overlapping demographic or operational slices require joint constraint handling because one example may satisfy several floors; summing those floors as if the slices were disjoint can reject a feasible budget. Even a feasible allocation establishes only retained counts. Independent evaluation must still establish target quality and performance on the required slices.

Together, these three static pruning techniques (deduplication, quality filtering, and coreset selection) shrink ${O}$ before training begins, subject to the same fixed-schedule caveat as before, since a schedule that adds compensating epochs or steps gives back part of the saving. Static pruning answers a question about *what* to keep, but it treats the answer as fixed, training every epoch on the same retained subset. A sample’s usefulness, however, can change as the model learns, because examples that challenge an undertrained model may become easy after sufficient gradient updates. Dynamic selection techniques address this limitation by adapting the training data at each stage based on what the model has already mastered.
