# V1-09.12 Summary

[[Home|Home]] · [[01 Books/Volume I - Foundations/Volume 1 Overview|Volume Overview]] · [[01 Books/Volume I - Foundations/03 Optimization/09 Data Selection/V1-09 Data Selection|V1-09 Data Selection]]

^sec-data-selection-summary-cf3e

Feeding every available example into a training run wastes accelerator compute, storage bandwidth, and annotation budget on redundant or noisy records because marginal learning signal varies by orders of magnitude across raw samples. Data selection therefore treats training volume as an engineering variable rather than a fixed constraint, minimizing total cost across compute, storage, labeling, energy, and wall-clock time so a curated subset can match or outperform a much larger uncurated corpus.

This systems perspective becomes actionable through three quantitative tools: the ICR, the selection inequality, and the total data cost model. The information-compute ratio quantifies learning gained per floating-point operation, providing a proxy for sample value that must be evaluated alongside coverage and target quality. Controlled model-size and data sweeps diagnose whether a run is data-limited or compute-limited in the first place, and PPD, AULC, and DCR then show whether a reduction delivered what it promised across the learning curve. The selection inequality tests a training-time claim by requiring scoring plus subset training to take less time than full-data training at matched quality, while return-on-investment and break-even analysis determine whether a proposed selection mechanism saves resources or merely shifts expenditure between stages.

The three levers act at different points: static pruning removes redundancy before training, dynamic selection prioritizes informative examples during training, and synthetic generation creates data where none exists. A score ranking alone can drop the rare slices that define deployment risk, so a pruned set must meet coverage floors, which displace higher-scoring examples and can make a budget infeasible. Together, these levers answer the data wall, the gap between rapidly growing compute capacity and slowly growing high-quality data.

Learning from unlabeled data changes the training signal rather than the set of samples, so it works alongside these levers rather than as a fourth one. Semi-supervised learning stretches a small labeled seed across an unlabeled pool, while self-supervised pretraining moves the bulk of representation learning into an unannotated upstream phase so downstream tasks can reach target quality with far fewer task-specific labels. Under the worked amortization scenario’s assumptions, fine-tuning uses 100× fewer labels per task and reaches the compute-only crossover at roughly 10.5 tasks, while a full dollar comparison also weighs labeling savings against compute prices and other differing costs.

In production, the difficult part is rarely the scoring rule itself; it is keeping selection from becoming the next bottleneck. Proxy models and cached embeddings bound scoring time, shard-based loaders keep storage access sequential, and data echoing recovers accelerator cycles when ingestion lags. On one H100, a half-size ResNet-50 coreset saves about 63 seconds and 44 kJ per epoch against a one-time scoring pass of about 42 seconds, and those seconds are realized only when the host keeps the accelerator fed and cross-worker coordination stays off the critical path.

**Key Takeaways: Curate, do not accumulate**

- **Selection optimizes system cost**: The objective is minimizing total cost across compute, storage, labeling, and energy rather than reducing sample counts alone. The information-compute ratio measures learning gained per FLOP spent; total-cost accounting and pipeline measurements establish whether that gain serves the system objective.
- **Start with deduplication**: Exact deduplication is often the lowest-risk first technique because it removes repeated bytes without a learned scoring pass. Apply near-duplicate and semantic deduplication only after validating thresholds against downstream quality metrics.
- **Training-time savings must exceed selection overhead**: At matched quality, ${T_{\text{selection}}}+ {T_{\text{train}}}({D_{\text{subset}}}) < {T_{\text{train}}}({D_{\text{total}}})$. Lightweight proxy models and cached embeddings keep ${T_{\text{selection}}}$ low, whereas unoptimized selection algorithms can consume all the compute they save.
- **Dynamic selection adapts the training mix**: Curriculum learning changes presentation order, while active learning changes which samples receive labels. Either improves efficiency when its scoring rule, coverage, and refresh overhead are validated.
- **Self-supervised pretraining can amortize adaptation cost**: Pretraining once and fine-tuning many times spreads pretraining cost across downstream tasks. The worked scenario assumes a 100× label reduction and a 20× marginal-compute reduction; realized savings depend on transfer quality and reuse.
- **Synthetic data is a supplement, not a replacement**: Synthetic samples augment scarce tail cases effectively, but cannot substitute entirely for empirical data. Unanchored synthetic training introduces domain-gap degradation, and recursive synthetic loops can trigger catastrophic model collapse.
- **Validated workload reduction compounds downstream**: When selection safely eliminates examples or tokens without adding compensating steps, that work never reaches later model or machine optimizations. Dynamic scoring and synthesis add their own costs, so the combined saving must be measured rather than assumed.

^callout-takeaways--9-12-884dc489

Examples differ greatly in how much they teach per byte transferred and per FLOP executed, and selection spends compute on the ones that teach most. Within the D·A·M taxonomy, selection operates at the head of the system, where every redundant sample or uninformative token safely eliminated directly reduces operation count (${O}$) in the iron law of ML systems (principle [[01 Books/Volume I - Foundations/02 Development/V1 Development Principles#^pri-iron-law|3]]) and can avoid downstream training reads. Scoring may still read the entire pool, and shard streaming may read discarded records, so reductions in data volume (${D_{\text{vol}}}$) must count scoring and shard-read traffic separately. When selection overhead satisfies the inequality at matched quality, the reduction removes work before downstream compression or hardware acceleration sees it.

Model-based scoring offers a way to invest additional compute in selection, consistent with the bitter lesson, but a stronger scorer justifies that investment only if its improved rankings save more downstream work than it costs. The Pareto frontier (principle [[01 Books/Volume I - Foundations/03 Optimization/V1 Optimization Principles#^pri-pareto-frontier|5]]) applies upstream of the model as well. Each retained fraction is an operating point that trades coverage and rare-slice accuracy against compute, and no single fraction is optimal for every workload.

**What’s Next: From data to algorithms**

Data selection changes *what* the system learns from. **[[01 Books/Volume I - Foundations/03 Optimization/10 Model Compression/V1-10 Model Compression#^sec-model-compression|Model Compression]]** turns to *how* the model represents what it has learned. It starts from the deployment budget the model must fit and adjusts three things to fit it: the model’s structure through pruning and distillation, its numerical precision through quantization, and the way the remaining operations map onto the hardware that executes them.
