# V1-09.08 Selection Engineering

[[Home|Home]] · [[01 Books/Volume I - Foundations/Volume 1 Overview|Volume Overview]] · [[01 Books/Volume I - Foundations/03 Optimization/09 Data Selection/V1-09 Data Selection|V1-09 Data Selection]]

^sec-data-selection-selection-engineering-a4eb

A naive active learning loop that scans the entire dataset every epoch to select the “best” samples can turn a compute-bound training job into an I/O-bound bottleneck. **Selection engineering** is the work of making a chosen technique deliver its promised speedup on real hardware and real data pipelines. The pipeline must keep scoring cheap, reads sequential, and the accelerator fed.

The machine sets the scale for every one of those patterns. One ImageNet epoch of ResNet-50 on one H100 passes through four stages: images are read from local storage, decoded and augmented on host CPUs, copied across PCIe into accelerator memory, and consumed by the accelerator’s arithmetic. Pruning shrinks the last stage directly; whether the other stages shrink, stay the same, or grow depends on how the selection is engineered ([[01 Books/Volume I - Foundations/03 Optimization/09 Data Selection/V1-09.08 Selection Engineering#^fig-data-selection-resnet-data-path|figure 10]]).

![[90 Assets/Volume I/09 Data Selection/Cell 25 Output 1 670e0b6e3f.png]]

Figure 10: **ResNet-50 Data Path on One H100**: One ImageNet epoch crosses four stages: local storage, host decoding and augmentation, the PCIe link, and the accelerator. Loads under each stage are per full epoch at the compute-bound rate; notes above each stage name the lever that acts there. A materialized coreset halves the storage read and the arithmetic, stream-and-discard filtering halves only the arithmetic, offline distillation targets add bytes to every stage before the accelerator, and a scoring pass adds one third of an epoch of forward arithmetic once. The host stage, outlined in red, must decode and augment every image at the accelerator’s rate.

^fig-data-selection-resnet-data-path

**Napkin Math 9.4: One pruned ResNet-50 epoch on the H100**

**Problem**: ResNet-50 trains on the 1.28 million ImageNet training images for 90 epochs on one H100, and a coreset keeps 50 percent of the images. How much accelerator time and energy does the coreset save once its scoring pass is paid?

**Compute**: Each image costs 24.6 GFLOP forward and backward, so a full epoch is 3.2 × 10¹⁶ FLOPs. At the 25 percent of peak that ResNet-50 sustains in training (**[[01 Books/Volume I - Foundations/02 Development/08 Model Training/V1-08.05 Pipeline Architecture#^sec-model-training-data-pipeline-8e71|Data pipeline]]**), the H100 finishes it in about 126 seconds, which at its 700 W board power is about 88 kJ. The pruned epoch takes about 63 seconds and 44 kJ.

**Selection**: An EL2N-style scoring pass is one forward pass over the full pool, a third of an epoch’s arithmetic, about 42 seconds and 29 kJ, paid once. Over 90 epochs the coreset saves about 4 MJ and 2 hours of accelerator time, 135× the scoring energy.

**Data path**: To stay compute-bound, the accelerator must receive about 10,100 images per second. Reading the 148 GB ImageNet archive from local NVMe at 7 GB/s takes about 21 seconds per epoch, and copying the decoded 8-bit tensors (193 GB) across PCIe at 64 GB/s takes about 3 seconds, both shorter than even the pruned epoch’s compute time. The stage that must keep up is the host CPU, which has to decode and augment 10,100 JPEG images every second. The training chapter’s host supplies only about 5,250 per accelerator, so an epoch actually takes about 244 seconds until decode capacity grows; the seconds above are the compute-bound figures selection can claim once the host keeps up.

**Systems insight**: Pruning halves the Data factor ($\text{Samples/Task}$), cutting total arithmetic ${O}$ and accelerator energy in half. It halves the storage read (${D_{\text{vol}}}/{\text{BW}}$), however, only if the retained subset is written out as its own shards. Streaming the full archive and discarding half leaves storage traffic unchanged. The seconds come from FLOPs, and whether they are realized depends on the slowest stage of the data path, which for ResNet-50 is often host decoding (**[[01 Books/Volume I - Foundations/02 Development/08 Model Training/V1-08.05 Pipeline Architecture#^sec-model-training-staged-pipeline|Pipeline stages and accelerator bubbles]]**). When host decoding is the slow stage, the pruned epoch shortens by the decode work it avoids. A materialized coreset halves that decode work by construction; stream-and-discard halves it only when the filter runs before the decoder.

Each lever also adds memory or storage of its own and compute or I/O of its own, and those costs land on different stages of the same data path ([[01 Books/Volume I - Foundations/03 Optimization/09 Data Selection/V1-09.08 Selection Engineering#^tbl-data-selection-footprint|table 7]]).

| **Lever (lighthouse)**                         | **Memory or storage it adds**                      | **Compute or I/O it adds**                                             | **Work it removes**                                     |
|:-----------------------------------------------|:---------------------------------------------------|:-----------------------------------------------------------------------|:--------------------------------------------------------|
| **MinHash deduplication (GPT-2, WebText)**     | 4.1 GB of signatures in host memory                | One 40 GB read (6 seconds) plus host hashing                           | 3.04% to 13.63% of tokens processed                     |
| **EL2N coreset scoring (ResNet-50, ImageNet)** | One score per image (5.1 MB)                       | One forward pass over the pool (42 seconds, 29 kJ)                     | Half of every epoch (63 seconds, 44 kJ)                 |
| **Coreset materialization**                    | A 74 GB repacked subset, written once              | One read and one partial write of the archive                          | Half of every later epoch’s storage read                |
| **Stream-and-discard filtering**               | None                                               | The full 148 GB read every epoch (21 seconds)                          | Accelerator work only; storage traffic unchanged        |
| **Offline distillation targets (ResNet-50)**   | 5.1 GB of FP32 logits in place of 2.6 MB of labels | One teacher forward pass over the pool, then a 5.1 GB read every epoch | Teacher residency in accelerator memory during training |

Table 7: **Memory and Compute Cost of Each Lever**: Values for the chapter’s two lighthouses on the reference H100 machine. Each lever adds memory or I/O at one stage of the data path while removing work at another, so the stage it loads must not become the new bottleneck.

^tbl-data-selection-footprint

Those added costs surface in four places (scoring, storage reads, host preparation, and coordination across workers), and each has an engineering pattern that keeps it off the critical path.

### The Selection Bottleneck

^sec-data-selection-selection-bottleneck-4d00

Evaluating a selection function ${f}({x})$ over a large candidate pool before forming every batch can easily cost more compute than training on the unselected data. While a conventional loader follows a predetermined sampling plan, active learning and adaptive curricula score candidates dynamically during training, introducing **selection latency**. In the ResNet-50 example of [[01 Books/Volume I - Foundations/03 Optimization/09 Data Selection/V1-09.03 Measuring Data Value#^sec-data-selection-selection-inequality-3b7e|section 9.2.6]], which keeps the aggressive 10 percent coreset rather than the half-size one, one static scoring pass with the target model costs a third of an epoch and is paid once. Rescoring the full ImageNet pool before every one of the 100 epochs instead adds 70 minutes of forward passes to 21 minutes of subset training, more than three times the training it serves. The rescoring cuts the saving from 90 percent to 57 percent. The selection inequality ([[01 Books/Volume I - Foundations/03 Optimization/09 Data Selection/V1-09.03 Measuring Data Value#^eq-selection-inequality|equation 2]]) still holds, but scoring has become the largest term, and a heavier scorer or more frequent rescoring would break it. Each rescoring pass reads the full pool however few examples are kept, so keeping fewer examples cannot shrink that term.

Engineering pipelines keep selection latency off the critical path through proxy models, cached embeddings, and indexed retrieval. A smaller proxy model curtails scoring time provided its sample ranking transfers accurately to the target architecture, and running the scoring pass in reduced numerical precision raises arithmetic throughput and cuts memory traffic when ranking fidelity permits. For similarity-based or diversity-based selection, vector indices such as FAISS[[01 Books/Volume I - Foundations/03 Optimization/09 Data Selection/V1-09.15 Footnotes#^fn33|³³]] replace expensive corpus-wide scans with approximate nearest-neighbor retrieval over precomputed embeddings. These techniques decouple selection evaluation from primary model training and shrink ${T_{\text{selection}}}$, but even zero-cost scoring can still slow down training if fetching the selected indices stalls the storage layer.

### The Random-Access Penalty

^sec-data-selection-hardware-empathy-random-access-penalty-ef16

Selecting a sparse subset of examples scattered across a dataset turns large sequential shard transfers into thousands of nonsequential reads, defeating hardware readahead and bulk controller prefetching and lowering the hit rate of the OS page cache. Training already pays for shuffling through the access factor ${F_{\text{access}}}$ that scales effective storage throughput in the data-pipeline model of **[[01 Books/Volume I - Foundations/02 Development/08 Model Training/V1-08.05 Pipeline Architecture#^sec-model-training-core-components-d28d|Core components]]**, and selection pushes that factor lower by design because the retained records no longer sit contiguously on disk. Because a random read costs far more than a sequential one on every storage tier, two subsets of equal size can differ sharply in wall-clock training time according to their access locality. [[01 Books/Volume I - Foundations/03 Optimization/09 Data Selection/V1-09.08 Selection Engineering#^tbl-io-performance|Table 8]] quantifies this small-read penalty across storage tiers for 4 KB transfers.

↩︎ Random access is measured in IOPS and streaming access in sequential throughput, the two storage metrics established in **[[01 Books/Volume I - Foundations/01 Foundations/04 Data Engineering/V1-04.11 Storage and Delivery#^sec-data-engineering-ml-storage-systems-architecture-options-67fa|Storage system options]]**.

| **Storage Tier** | **Sequential Throughput** | **Random I/O (IOPS)** | **Random Throughput (approx)** | **Random Penalty**  |
|:-----------------|:-------------------------:|:---------------------:|:------------------------------:|:-------------------:|
| **HDD (7.2k)**   |         ~150 MB/s         |       ~100 IOPS       |           ~0.4 MB/s            |        375×         |
| **SATA SSD**     |         ~550 MB/s         |       ~10K IOPS       |            ~40 MB/s            |        13.8×        |
| **NVMe SSD**     |        ~3,500 MB/s        |      ~500K IOPS       |          ~2,000 MB/s           |        1.75×        |
| **Cloud (S3)**   |  Configuration-dependent  |  Not IOPS-comparable  |    Configuration-dependent     | Potentially extreme |

Table 8: **Illustrative Small-Read Penalty**: Registry device anchors compare sequential bandwidth with random 4 KB reads under a simplified IOPS conversion. Real throughput depends on request size, queue depth, concurrency, caching, and service configuration; the cloud row is only a qualitative scenario.

^tbl-io-performance

Data loaders (whose general mechanism **[[01 Books/Volume I - Foundations/02 Development/07 ML Frameworks/V1-07.06 Abstraction Tensors, Devices, and Operations#^sec-ml-frameworks-domainspecific-data-organizations-48d9|Data loading]]** develops) avoid the problem by packing examples into large shards and shuffling inside a memory buffer. Sharded dataset formats, such as WebDataset and FFCV, pack thousands of individual examples into contiguous, sequentially readable archive chunks. Rather than fetching scattered individual samples from disk, the loader streams large sequential shards into host memory and populates a **shuffle buffer**. The training loop samples pseudo-randomly from this in-memory buffer, preserving peak storage read bandwidth while satisfying the statistical requirement of randomized mini-batches.

Converting a logical set of selected indices into sequential physical reads requires choosing between two storage strategies. For static pruning (such as a fixed coreset or a deduplicated corpus), the pipeline can materialize the subset offline by repacking the retained records into new, contiguous shards. This step incurs a one-time sequential read and write cost alongside additional disk storage, but ensures that every subsequent training epoch transfers only retained bytes from storage.

Conversely, when the selection policy updates dynamically across training iterations or active-learning rounds, repeatedly repacking multi-terabyte shards on disk would choke the filesystem on write bandwidth. Data loaders instead stream the original, full-volume shards sequentially into host memory and discard unselected records before populating the shuffle buffer. This stream-and-discard filtering preserves sequential read throughput and eliminates seek penalties, but it leaves physical storage traffic unchanged, because the host continues to read 100 percent of the corpus bytes from disk even when accelerators train on only a 10 percent subset. Systems engineers must count this ongoing read traffic on storage-bound nodes and include offline materialization costs when evaluating the selection inequality. Even after storage reads are streaming at full speed, host-side decoding and augmentation can still starve the accelerator.

### Data Echoing

^sec-data-selection-data-echoing-amortizing-io-costs-b4e0

Even with sequential reads, the host CPU often cannot decode and augment samples fast enough. Heavy data transformations, such as 3D rotations, MixUp, JPEG decoding, or on-the-fly generative synthesis, demand substantial host processing per sample. When the host produces batches slower than the accelerator consumes them, the feeding problem of **[[01 Books/Volume I - Foundations/01 Foundations/04 Data Engineering/V1-04.10 Physics of Data#^sec-data-engineering-feeding-problem|The feeding problem]]** reappears inside the selection loop and the accelerator idles, starved for input. The resulting collapse in accelerator utilization extends training wall-clock time, neutralizing the efficiency gains achieved by pruning the dataset.

↩︎ Accelerator bubbles, which open when the data-loading stage cannot keep pace with the accelerator, are established in **[[01 Books/Volume I - Foundations/02 Development/08 Model Training/V1-08.05 Pipeline Architecture#^sec-model-training-staged-pipeline|Pipeline stages and accelerator bubbles]]**.

Data echoing[[01 Books/Volume I - Foundations/03 Optimization/09 Data Selection/V1-09.15 Footnotes#^fn34|³⁴]] ([[01 Books/Volume I - Foundations/03 Optimization/09 Data Selection/V1-09.14 Source References#^ref-choi2020dataechoing|Choi et al. 2019]]) reuses data multiple times before fetching new samples, trading freshness for accelerator utilization when the input pipeline is slower than training. Echoing before randomized augmentation can produce different transformed inputs on each repetition, while echoing later in the pipeline may repeat identical tensors.

The optimal echo factor depends on the ratio ${r_{\text{pipe}}}$ of upstream processing time to downstream training time, where ${T_{\text{prep}}}$ is the time the data pipeline needs to produce a batch and ${T_{\text{GPU}}}$ is the time the accelerator needs to train on it:

$$
{r_{\text{pipe}}}= \frac{{T_{\text{prep}}}}{{T_{\text{GPU}}}}
$$

In this two-stage rate model, ${r_{\text{pipe}}}> 1$ means the data pipeline takes longer to prepare a fresh batch than the accelerator takes to train on it once. When the loader repeats each prepared batch ${e}$ times, the accelerator spends ${e} \cdot {T_{\text{GPU}}}$ training on the current batch while the host pipeline prepares the next fresh batch in parallel over time ${T_{\text{prep}}}$. An echo factor ${e}< {r_{\text{pipe}}}$ therefore recovers part of the idle gap, while ${e}\ge {r_{\text{pipe}}}$ makes ${e} \cdot {T_{\text{GPU}}} \ge {T_{\text{prep}}}$ so the next batch is ready before the accelerator finishes the current one, removing input waiting entirely provided the repeated samples remain statistically productive. Raising ${e}$ beyond ${r_{\text{pipe}}}$ adds no further throughput and only reduces gradient diversity. When ${r_{\text{pipe}}}< 1$, the input pipeline already outruns the accelerator, so echoing has nothing to recover.

![[90 Assets/Volume I/09 Data Selection/Vol1 Data Selection Margin 003 1013752d80.svg]]

*A line labeled utilization rises against echo factor to a red dot at e equals r_pipe, then runs flat; a red dashed line marks the threshold and the shaded region beyond it is labeled e greater than r_pipe.*

*Echoing raises accelerator utilization until ${e}$ reaches ${r_{\text{pipe}}}$; beyond it, nothing is gained.*

**Napkin Math 9.5: Worked example: Data echoing ROI**

**Scenario**: Training ResNet-50 on ImageNet on one H100 fed by the training chapter’s host, where host preparation is the slow stage (**[[01 Books/Volume I - Foundations/02 Development/08 Model Training/V1-08.05 Pipeline Architecture#^sec-model-training-data-pipeline-8e71|Data pipeline]]**).

**Rates**:

- Data pipeline throughput: 5,250 images/s (the host cores available to one accelerator, each reading, decoding, and augmenting about 375 images/s)
- Accelerator training throughput: 10,100 images/s (forward and backward pass at the compute-bound rate of notebook [9.4](https://mlsysbook.ai/vol1/09_data_selection/09_data_selection.html#nbk-data-selection-pruned-epoch-h100))
- Ratio ${r_{\text{pipe}}}= {T_{\text{prep}}} / {T_{\text{GPU}}}$ = (1/5,250 images/s) / (1/10,100 images/s) = 10,100 images/s/5,250 images/s ≈ 1.92 (accelerator waiting 48.0 percent of the time)

**Without echoing**:

- Effective throughput: 5,250 images/s (limited by data pipeline)
- Training time for 90 epochs: $90 \times 1.28\text{M}$ / 5,250 images/s = 21,963 seconds (6.1 hours)
- Accelerator utilization: 52.0 percent

**With echo factor**: ${e}$ = 2, inserted after the measured reading, decoding, and augmentation stage.

- Each already-augmented batch is processed twice, amortizing the measured upstream work; the repeated pass introduces no fresh augmentation
- Effective throughput: 10,100 images/s, the accelerator’s full rate, because an echo factor above the ratio leaves the accelerator no time waiting
- Unique images per second: 5,250 images/s (unchanged)
- Training time: $90 \times 1.28\text{M}$ / 10,100 images/s = 11,416 seconds (3.2 hours) if echoed data is equally valuable

**Trade-off**: Repeated samples are not guaranteed to be as useful as fresh samples; the data echoing paper evaluates echo factor, insertion point, and shuffling because those choices determine whether reuse preserves predictive performance. In one network-fed ResNet-50/ImageNet configuration, Choi et al. ([[01 Books/Volume I - Foundations/03 Optimization/09 Data Selection/V1-09.14 Source References#^ref-choi2020dataechoing|2019]]) reports a 3.25$\times$ reduction in wall-clock time to the target metric.

**Systems insight**: Data echoing can trade sample diversity for accelerator utilization when the input pipeline is the bottleneck. For the training chapter’s input-bound ResNet-50, overlap alone left the accelerator about half idle; an echo factor of 2 closes that gap without adding host cores. The useful echo factor must be measured for the workload because it depends on the batch size, insertion point, augmentation, shuffling, and target metric.

Echoing can introduce correlations when the same example appears within or across nearby batches, so implementations must preserve adequate shuffling and validate the target metric. Choi et al. ([[01 Books/Volume I - Foundations/03 Optimization/09 Data Selection/V1-09.14 Source References#^ref-choi2020dataechoing|2019]]) observed no harm from repeated examples through batch normalization in their image classification and detection experiments, but that result does not guarantee the same behavior for every workload.

These implementation patterns determine whether data selection yields net systems throughput. Proxy selection curtails probe compute when scoring rankings transfer to target architectures. Sharded file formats and shuffle buffers reconcile pseudo-random batch creation with hardware-level sequential streaming, while data echoing recovers stalled accelerator cycles when ingestion throughput limits the training loop. These patterns still leave a coordination question when several accelerator workers share one host. Each worker scores or trains on its own shard, yet the selected indices and scoring checkpoint must still describe one coherent dataset. Shared storage and host memory do not automatically make each worker’s local view representative of the corpus.

### Selection Across Multiple Accelerators

^sec-data-selection-distributed-selection-d03e

When parallel worker processes divide a dataset into local shards across two to eight accelerators on one host, each worker sees only a fraction of the corpus and may score its shard with a slightly different model checkpoint. Without cross-worker coordination, shard-local coreset rankings miss rare global classes, independent hash tables fail to catch duplicates split across workers, and asynchronous active-learning workers select samples using stale uncertainty scores. A single-process pipeline sorts one global ranking, follows one curriculum schedule, and queries one shared candidate pool. A multi-accelerator pipeline, by contrast, must reconstruct a representative global subset from local shard views while all workers contend for the same host memory capacity, storage bandwidth, and host-to-device links.

Whether distributed selection stays faithful to the global dataset or collapses into local shard heuristics depends on how much cross-worker coordination each technique requires against the bandwidth available to sustain it. On one host, score gathering and index dispatch compete for CPU time and memory bandwidth with decoding and batch preparation, so evaluating selection requires analyzing statistical fidelity alongside these shared resources. Statistical fidelity and coordination cost vary independently across selection algorithms. A coreset can remain representative yet cost too much to coordinate, while a cheap shard-local method can scale but systematically miss rare groups visible only in the global corpus. A distributed design therefore needs two acceptance tests: compare selected-data quality with a centralized or otherwise auditable reference, and compare selection overhead with the end-to-end training time it saves. Passing only one test is insufficient.

#### Strategies for Distributed Selection

^sec-data-selection-strategies-distributed-selection-6119

In a basic data-parallel layout, each worker processes a distinct shard and model synchronization is handled separately. Data selection can add dependencies across those shards ([[01 Books/Volume I - Foundations/03 Optimization/09 Data Selection/V1-09.08 Selection Engineering#^tbl-selection-dependencies|table 9]]).

| **Technique**           | **Single-Process Assumption**   | **Distributed Challenge**                      |
|:------------------------|:--------------------------------|:-----------------------------------------------|
| **Coreset Selection**   | Global view of dataset          | Each worker sees only its shard                |
| **Active Learning**     | Centralized uncertainty scoring | Scoring requires model synchronization         |
| **Curriculum Learning** | Global difficulty ordering      | Workers may have different “hardest” samples   |
| **Deduplication**       | One shared duplicate map        | Shard-local hashes miss cross-shard duplicates |

Table 9: **Selection Dependencies in Distributed Training**: The table starts from a common centralized formulation of each technique and identifies the coordination problem introduced by sharding. Distributed variants may relax rather than reproduce the centralized result exactly.

^tbl-selection-dependencies

The selection dependencies admit two broad architectures, each at a different point in the trade-off between consistency and scalability. Centralized selection keeps selection global while training stays distributed. A coordinator process scores the full dataset and dispatches the selected indices to the workers, which preserves selection quality but makes the coordinator a single bottleneck that can serialize scoring or index dispatch. Whether that overhead is acceptable depends on the selection payload, refresh rate, worker count, and copies through shared host memory, and adding accelerator workers does not increase the host memory available to the coordinator.

Hierarchical selection instead distributes the selection computation itself. Each worker selects candidates from its own shard, and the coordinator merges and reranks them before dispatching the final indices ([[01 Books/Volume I - Foundations/03 Optimization/09 Data Selection/V1-09.08 Selection Engineering#^fig-distributed-coreset-architecture|figure 11]]). Shard-local selection reduces coordinator load but introduces a quality trade-off, because local quotas and score distributions may not preserve the global ranking or rare groups spread unevenly across shards. The merge step therefore needs normalization, coverage constraints, or a second global pass.

![[90 Assets/Volume I/09 Data Selection/Generated Figure 4a7e5a457f.svg]]

Figure 11: **Distributed Coreset Selection Architecture**: Parallel shard-local scoring reduces the work assigned to a centralized rank-merging coordinator. Workers send local EL2N scores upward; the coordinator merges them and broadcasts final coreset indices. All boxes are processes on one host; the coordinator is a selection role, and each worker drives one accelerator. The design is beneficial only when this coordination overhead remains below the training time saved by the coreset.

^fig-distributed-coreset-architecture

Shard locality affects training as well as selection. Each worker maintains an independent shuffle buffer over a non-overlapping shard. Because randomization is local to each worker’s memory buffer rather than global across the corpus, rare classes or boundary cases concentrated in specific shards receive uneven exposure across workers unless the dataset is stratified and shards are balanced prior to distribution.

Cross-shard deduplication needs the same global view as hierarchical selection. Workers can compute MinHash signatures locally, but a shared merge must compare candidates across shard boundaries. Independent local hash tables cannot detect a duplicate whose counterpart belongs to another worker. The merge must fit the host’s memory budget or operate in bounded batches, rather than assuming that more workers supply more host capacity. Both hierarchical coresets and cross-shard deduplication merge scores or hashes computed against a fixed target; coordination becomes harder when the scoring model itself updates while workers query the pool.

#### Consistency Challenges in Active Learning

^sec-data-selection-consistency-challenges-active-learning-59c9

Shard-local selection already needs a coherent score scale, but active learning introduces an additional complication because the model changes during selection. Training workers step together in synchronous data parallelism (**[[01 Books/Volume I - Foundations/02 Development/08 Model Training/V1-08.08 Scaling Training Systems#^sec-model-training-data-parallelism-ad1c|Data parallelism]]**), but scoring often runs asynchronously beside them so that it does not pause training. Consider what happens when Worker A scores samples using the model at step ${t}$ while the training workers advance the shared model to step ${t}+1$. Worker A’s scores are now stale and may select samples that the updated model would rank differently.

The scoring schedule therefore defines the meaning of a query, not merely its cost. A reproducible system versions the model checkpoint and candidate-pool snapshot behind each score. A team can measure how much ranking agreement decays with checkpoint age and choose a refresh interval from that evidence rather than from an arbitrary number of steps.

Several strategies mitigate this staleness problem, each with distinct overhead characteristics:

- **Synchronous scoring**: All workers pause training and score simultaneously, guaranteeing consistency but at substantial cost in accelerator utilization.
- **Periodic score refresh**: Workers re-score every ${k_{\text{refresh}}}$ epochs rather than every batch, trading freshness for reduced overhead.
- **Checkpoint-robust selection**: The system selects samples that show high uncertainty under multiple model checkpoints, favoring decisions that are less sensitive to checkpoint choice. Agreement across past checkpoints is a robustness heuristic; ranking quality still needs validation as the model evolves.

Positive ROI can erode quickly when workers coordinate frequently during training. Selection across workers adds coordination overhead whose size depends on what is synchronized and how often. That overhead must remain smaller than the training time saved, and when it approaches that limit, a simpler strategy or a longer refresh interval restores the margin.

The communication path adds a further constraint. Gradient synchronization may use accelerator collectives over local interconnects, while embedding vectors, score arrays, and coreset indices may pass through host memory and CPU dispatch. Selection can therefore expose host-memory, PCIe, storage, or process-synchronization bottlenecks that the training profile alone does not reveal. Extending the design to independent machines adds network topology and remote-storage constraints; those are a further scaling problem beyond the one-host design developed here.

Operational feasibility, however, does not guarantee economic viability. A deduplication pipeline that costs more to build than one training run saves loses money on a single run and pays off only if enough runs reuse it. Deploying selection techniques therefore requires financial accounting of capital, compute, and human annotation budgets.
