# V1-09.11 Fallacies and Pitfalls

[[Home|Home]] · [[01 Books/Volume I - Foundations/Volume 1 Overview|Volume Overview]] · [[01 Books/Volume I - Foundations/03 Optimization/09 Data Selection/V1-09 Data Selection|V1-09 Data Selection]]

^sec-data-selection-fallacies-pitfalls-f4d0

Scaling a training corpus tenfold can multiply storage, I/O, and accelerator bills while barely moving test accuracy. Yet an uncalibrated selection filter can just as easily spend more time scoring candidates than it saves in training or silently erase a safety-critical rare class. The entries below range from mistaken beliefs about what data selection can achieve to failures that arise when a correct strategy meets a systems bottleneck or when a benchmark result is applied uncritically to a new domain.

**Fallacy**: *More data always improves the model enough to justify its cost.*

More data does not imply proportional accuracy gains because learning curves follow power-law diminishing returns. Test loss diminishes sub-linearly with dataset size (${\mathcal{L}}({D}) \propto {D^{-\beta}}$ for a fitted exponent ${\beta}$), meaning each successive error reduction demands a multiplicatively larger corpus. In an illustrative learning-curve scenario, scaling a dataset tenfold from 1M to 10M samples yields only 4 percentage points of accuracy gain. That 10× volume expansion still costs a tenfold increase in storage I/O, host-to-device transfers, and accelerator FLOPs. Teams should measure the learning curve of their own workload rather than assume that more data delivers proportional value.

**Pitfall**: *Replacing real-data validation with synthetic-only training data.*

Synthetic samples cost almost nothing to acquire, so generative models can look like a replacement for data collection. Synthetic-only training fails in two ways. First, synthetic data can differ from the deployment distribution ([[01 Books/Volume I - Foundations/03 Optimization/09 Data Selection/V1-09.06 Synthetic Data Generation#^sec-data-selection-bridging-domain-gap-100b|section 9.5.3]], [[01 Books/Volume I - Foundations/03 Optimization/09 Data Selection/V1-09.06 Synthetic Data Generation#^fig-domain-gap|figure 8]]), so the model learns features that fail to transfer; only real-data labels reveal the effect. Second, recursive training on model-generated data can trigger model collapse ([[01 Books/Volume I - Foundations/03 Optimization/09 Data Selection/V1-09.14 Source References#^ref-shumailov2024|Shumailov et al. 2024]]), which progressively erodes distribution tails. In this scenario, evaluation accuracy degrades from 95 percent to 78 percent across five recursive generations, suffering a 17 percentage-point collapse. Synthetic mixtures must therefore be validated against curated real data.

**Fallacy**: *Data selection is merely data cleaning.*

A dataset can be completely clean and correctly labeled yet redundant. Once the model has minimized loss on a feature cluster, further identical samples add almost no gradient signal and still consume memory bandwidth and accelerator FLOPs. Cleaning corrects intrinsic defects; selection raises the learning return per FLOP by choosing examples for task-relevant information and coverage ([[01 Books/Volume I - Foundations/03 Optimization/09 Data Selection/V1-09.04 Static Pruning#^fig-coreset-selection|figure 5]]; [[01 Books/Volume I - Foundations/03 Optimization/09 Data Selection/V1-09.04 Static Pruning#^sec-data-selection-coreset-selection-algorithms-2c74|section 9.3.4]]). In the worked coreset scenario of notebook [9.1](https://mlsysbook.ai/vol1/09_data_selection/09_data_selection.html#nbk-data-selection-computing-icr-coresets), the selected subset reached 1.8× higher ICR.

**Pitfall**: *Treating data selection as a budget-only tactic.*

Selection can look like a technique for resource-constrained teams, as if frontier foundation models could brute-force convergence by scaling the cluster. The data wall constrains every scale. At frontier scale, continued growth in training-token demand can encounter limits in the effective supply of human text (**[[01 Books/Volume I - Foundations/01 Foundations/04 Data Engineering/V1-04.05 Data Acquisition#^fig-running-out-of-human-data|Training Data Approaching the Stock of Public Text]]**); at GPT-2 scale, one eight-H100 machine passes over the whole WebText corpus about 17 times in 4.4 days ([[01 Books/Volume I - Foundations/03 Optimization/09 Data Selection/V1-09.02 The Data Wall#^sec-data-selection-data-selection-fundamentals-e839|section 9.1]]). The economic return of selection also scales with cluster expenditure, so a 10 percent efficiency gain on a \$100M frontier training run saves \$10M under these assumptions. Amortizing scores and cached embeddings across repeated runs ([[01 Books/Volume I - Foundations/03 Optimization/09 Data Selection/V1-09.09 Choosing a Selection Strategy#^sec-data-selection-amortization-across-training-runs-f6b8|section 9.8.3]]) strengthens the case further.

**Fallacy**: *Selection overhead is too small to change training economics.*

The selection inequality ([[01 Books/Volume I - Foundations/03 Optimization/09 Data Selection/V1-09.03 Measuring Data Value#^eq-selection-inequality|equation 2]]) requires ${T_{\text{selection}}}+ {T_{\text{train}}}({D_{\text{subset}}}) < {T_{\text{train}}}({D_{\text{total}}})$ at matched quality. Consider a baseline training run requiring 8 hours, where training on an optimized coreset takes 2 hours. If a complex selection algorithm requires 10 hours to compute sample importance, the selection step alone consumes 5× the duration of the accelerated training run and exceeds the full training baseline itself, a net wall-clock loss. In the illustrative comparison, proxy-based EL2N scoring completes in 30 minutes, which is 6.2 percent of the full-training baseline and satisfies the inequality.

**Pitfall**: *Pruning rare classes without a coverage floor.*

Standard selection objectives minimize aggregate loss, which gives tail slices little influence over sampling. In a 1M dataset where rare classes comprise 0.1 percent of the data (1,000 examples), an unstratified 10 percent coreset retains only 100 rare-class examples in expectation, far below the illustrative 150-sample retention floor assumed here. An appropriate floor must be validated from task-specific learning curves and critical-slice performance. Importance sampling can compensate by keeping rare examples with higher probability and weighting each kept example’s loss by the inverse of that probability, but very large weights make training noisy. As recommended in [[01 Books/Volume I - Foundations/03 Optimization/09 Data Selection/V1-09.04 Static Pruning#^sec-data-selection-coreset-selection-algorithms-2c74|section 9.3.4]], production coreset pipelines enforce hard stratification floors per class or critical slice before spending the remaining selection budget.

**Fallacy**: *Deduplicating training data is enough to make evaluation reliable.*

Web-scale pipelines scrape training, validation, and benchmark splits from common crawls, which introduces silent cross-split leakage (**[[01 Books/Volume I - Foundations/01 Foundations/04 Data Engineering/V1-04.02 Data as Code#^sec-data-engineering-data-engineering-dataset-compilation-0496|Data as Code]]**). When an evaluation set contains near-duplicates of training documents, the metric measures memorization rather than generalization. A deduplication study found that train-test overlap affects over 4 percent of validation examples across standard language-modeling benchmarks ([[01 Books/Volume I - Foundations/03 Optimization/09 Data Selection/V1-09.14 Source References#^ref-lee2022|Lee et al. 2022]]). Deduplicating the training partition alone leaves this contamination untouched; cross-partition deduplication is required, using ${n}$-gram matching or MinHash LSH ([[01 Books/Volume I - Foundations/03 Optimization/09 Data Selection/V1-09.04 Static Pruning#^sec-data-selection-data-deduplication-6c20|section 9.3.2]]). For a frozen evaluation set, the fix removes the overlapping training records and preserves the evaluation set and its provenance.

**Pitfall**: *Active learning without considering annotation latency.*

Textbook formulations of active learning assume an oracle that labels queries instantly. Production annotation queues introduce delays such as the illustrative 14-day latency between sample dispatch and label return assumed here. This latency breaks the active learning feedback loop in two ways. If accelerator clusters halt training while awaiting labels, expensive hardware sits idle, destroying cluster compute utilization. Conversely, if training continues asynchronously on background data while annotations are pending, model weights drift across training steps; by the time the labels arrive, the uncertainty scores that justified querying those samples are obsolete. Dispatching large batches to amortize the turnaround adds redundancy within the batch unless diversity penalties are applied.

**Fallacy**: *If a technique works on ImageNet, it will work on my dataset.*

CIFAR-10 and ImageNet are highly redundant across canonical object classes, which often allows 30 to 50 percent coreset pruning without measurable accuracy loss. Histopathology, satellite imagery, and industrial defect detection have heavy-tailed feature distributions in which safety-critical patterns appear in a few rare samples. A 50 percent cut tuned on ImageNet can remove the few examples that separate subtle pathologies. Geometric coreset algorithms also depend on embeddings, and a feature extractor pretrained on natural images may not resolve fine-grained domain distinctions. Teams should measure the redundancy of their own data and validate retention across critical slices before pruning.

**Pitfall**: *Optimizing data selection metrics instead of deployment metrics.*

Selection algorithms optimize aggregate surrogates such as area under the learning curve (AULC) or performance-per-data (PPD, [[01 Books/Volume I - Foundations/03 Optimization/09 Data Selection/V1-09.03 Measuring Data Value#^sec-data-selection-core-metrics-32e9|section 9.2.4]]). A selection policy retaining a 10 percent coreset can achieve superior aggregate PPD by prioritizing dense clusters of common, easy samples that accelerate average convergence, while discarding ambiguous edge cases and rare slices. Production failures, however, occur on tail slices, edge cases, and protected sub-populations rather than on averages. A policy without subgroup constraints can pass global validation and still fail in deployment. As the measurement framework of [[01 Books/Volume I - Foundations/03 Optimization/09 Data Selection/V1-09.03 Measuring Data Value#^sec-data-selection-measurement-framework-733b|section 9.2]] requires, policies must be evaluated on stratified, slice-level benchmarks, even when doing so slightly lowers average PPD.

**Self-Check: Question**

1.  A research paper reports that an EL2N coreset selection method successfully pruned $50\%$ of CIFAR-10 with $0\%$ loss in accuracy. A medical imaging team applies the exact same $50\%$ pruning ratio to a rare tumor detection dataset and suffers a disastrous $28\%$ drop in recall. What fallacy explains this failure?

    1.  The team failed to use GPU acceleration during the inference pass
    2.  The team used float32 precision instead of bfloat16 mixed precision
    3.  CIFAR-10 contains more total classes than medical imaging datasets
    4.  Assuming that benchmark coreset pruning ratios transfer directly to specialized, highly imbalanced production domains with rare failure modes

2.  A team implements an elaborate multi-stage active learning pipeline that reduces training dataset size by $40\%$. However, the continuous clustering, proxy scoring, and cross-worker all-gather synchronization take 3 times longer than the GPU time saved during training. Which pitfall does this represent?

    1.  Violating the Selection Inequality by incurring selection overheads that exceed downstream training savings ($T_{\text{selection}} > \Delta T_{\text{train}}$)
    2.  Encountering model collapse due to recursive generator loops
    3.  Failing to implement 4 KB small-read alignment on host NVMe storage
    4.  Violating the smoothness assumption in semi-supervised consistency regularization

3.  Why is evaluating a data selection strategy solely on aggregate validation accuracy a dangerous pitfall when dealing with imbalanced datasets?

4.  True or False: Because modern high-capacity generative models produce photorealistic images and fluent text, a model trained on recursively generated synthetic data will never suffer performance degradation.
