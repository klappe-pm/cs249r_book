# V1-11.07 Performance Bounds

[[Home|Home]] · [[01 Books/Volume I - Foundations/Volume 1 Overview|Volume Overview]] · [[01 Books/Volume I - Foundations/03 Optimization/11 Hardware Acceleration/V1-11 Hardware Acceleration|V1-11 Hardware Acceleration]]

![[90 Assets/Volume I/11 Hw Acceleration/Hw Acceleration Roofline Elbow fdbc43adf7.svg]]

*Roofline elbow: a blue memory-bound slope rises left to right to a short orange compute-bound ceiling, with a dashed vertical ridge line at the bend and one workload dot sitting low on the blue slope, far below the ridge in the memory-bound region.*

*Low arithmetic intensity places the modeled throughput ceiling on the bandwidth slope.*

The compute units fix how fast an accelerator can calculate, and the memory system fixes how fast it can be fed. A kernel’s performance is bounded by whichever of the two runs out first. Putting numbers on that statement takes three pieces: the roofline’s ceilings for each precision path, the ridge point at which they meet, and the hardware balance that moves the ridge from one generation to the next. The ResNet-50 INT8 workload meets its sharpest lesson in these bounds. The INT8 path doubles the H100’s arithmetic ceiling without moving its bandwidth ceiling, so a layer that cleared the ridge at FP16 can fall below it at INT8 if its activations still move at 16 bits.

### The Roofline Model

^sec-hardware-acceleration-roofline-model-3e1a

Quoting an accelerator’s peak FLOP/s cannot explain why one kernel runs near the hardware ceiling while another stalls below 1 percent of peak on the same chip, because peak compute measures only what execution units can retire when operands are already in registers. With the compute units and memory systems that set the arithmetic and bandwidth ceilings now in place, the **roofline model**[[01 Books/Volume I - Foundations/03 Optimization/11 Hardware Acceleration/V1-11.17 Footnotes#^fn21|²¹]] ([[01 Books/Volume I - Foundations/03 Optimization/11 Hardware Acceleration/V1-11.16 Source References#^ref-williams2009|Williams et al. 2009]]), which sorted training kernels in **[[01 Books/Volume I - Foundations/02 Development/08 Model Training/V1-08.04 Computation and Memory Costs of Training#^sec-model-training-arithmetic-intensity-training-bottlenecks-4446|Arithmetic intensity]]**, combines both limits on a single plot. It graphs attainable performance against arithmetic intensity and identifies the lower modeled ceiling, compute or memory bandwidth, for the chosen execution path before profiling establishes which physical resource limits execution.

Performance is bounded by two ceilings, as [[01 Books/Volume I - Foundations/03 Optimization/11 Hardware Acceleration/V1-11.07 Performance Bounds#^eq-roofline|equation 2]] formalizes; it writes the bound of the arithmetic intensity law (principle [[01 Books/Volume I - Foundations/03 Optimization/V1 Optimization Principles#^pri-arithmetic-intensity|6]]) as an idealized equality that ignores launch and synchronization overheads. Here, attainable performance ${R_{\text{attain}}}$ and peak compute ${R_{\text{peak}}}$ are in FLOP/s (often reported as TFLOP/s), peak bandwidth ${\text{BW}}$ is in bytes/s (often TB/s), and arithmetic intensity ${I}$ is in FLOP/byte:

$$
{R_{\text{attain}}} = \min({R_{\text{peak}}}, {\text{BW}} \times {I}) \tag{2}
$$

^eq-roofline

Arithmetic intensity ${I} = {O}/{D_{\text{vol}}}$ is the ratio of operations to bytes moved across the memory interface being modeled (definition [5.3](https://mlsysbook.ai/vol1/05_nn_computation/05_nn_computation.html#dfn-nn-computation-arithmetic-intensity)), and it depends on the implementation as well as the operation. Under the stated traffic models, a large well-tiled 1024 $\times$ 1024 multiply reaches about 341.3 FLOP/byte, while standalone ReLU is about 0.125 FLOP/byte, so the two meet different ceilings on the same hardware.

The roofline visualization shows performance (TFLOP/s) on the vertical axis and arithmetic intensity (FLOP/byte) on the horizontal axis. At low arithmetic intensity, the upper bound increases linearly along the memory bandwidth slope (${\text{BW}} \times {I}$). Above the ridge point, the upper bound is peak compute (${R_{\text{peak}}}$). A kernel approaching the slope is bandwidth limited, while one approaching the flat roof is compute limited on the specified path. Merely lying below either line does not establish the cause; profiling explains that remaining gap. **[[05 Reference/Volume I - Foundations/V1 The D·A·M Taxonomy#^sec-appdx-dam-taxonomy-bottleneck-diagnostic-3fc9|Acting on the limiting term]]** maps each regime to the optimizations that pay off and the ones that waste effort, so classifying a kernel tells the engineer whether more bandwidth, less traffic, or more arithmetic throughput is the investment to test first.

### Hardware Ridge Points

^sec-hardware-acceleration-hardware-balance

A kernel with 200 FLOP/byte that was comfortably compute bound on a V100 or A100 falls below the compute ceiling when migrated unchanged to an H100, because different devices place the compute-bandwidth boundary at different intensities. The **ridge point**, the balance point between a machine’s memory and compute limits previewed alongside the iron law, is a property of the device. Computer architects call the same ratio the **hardware balance $({I_{\text{ridge}}})$**, peak arithmetic throughput divided by peak memory bandwidth, the intensity at which the roofline’s compute and bandwidth ceilings intersect:

$$
{I_{\text{ridge}}} = \frac{{R_{\text{peak}}}}{{\text{BW}}}
$$

The ratio sets a device-specific boundary. Depending on the chosen precision, the H100 requires about 295.2 FLOP/byte on its dense FP16 path (989 TFLOP/s over 3.35 TB/s) to reach its arithmetic ceiling, and twice that on its INT8 path, whereas a microcontroller may have a much lower ridge point. The same kernel can therefore be bandwidth bound on a compute-dense accelerator and compute bound on an edge processor. Hardware balance does not label either device as universally efficient; it identifies how much reuse a workload needs on that device.

Peak computational capability has expanded faster than memory bus performance across accelerator generations, and the size of that mismatch is what sets how much data reuse a kernel needs to stay fed. Across 2017–2024, NVIDIA’s data center generations multiplied dense FP16 compute by 18× and memory bandwidth by only 8.9×, a 2.0× rise in the reuse a kernel needs on B200 relative to V100.

That ratio measures how far the hardware ridge point, the threshold a kernel must clear to become compute bound, has risen since the V100. [[01 Books/Volume I - Foundations/03 Optimization/11 Hardware Acceleration/V1-11.07 Performance Bounds#^fig-rising-ridge|Figure 10]] plots it generation by generation, leaving out the H200 exception, which pairs the H100’s compute with more memory bandwidth and therefore has a lower ridge. The doubling of the gap appears directly as the ridge point roughly doubling from V100 (139 FLOP/byte) to H100 (295 FLOP/byte) and then holding near that level on B200 (281 FLOP/byte). A kernel that cleared the V100 ridge comfortably can fall below the H100 ridge without any change to its code, so newer hardware demands more operational reuse before execution reaches peak throughput.

**Napkin Math 11.3: The utilization gap**

**Problem**: Why is it harder to get 100 percent utilization on an H100 than on an A100?

**Metric**: The ridge point ${I_{\text{ridge}}} = {R_{\text{peak}}} / {\text{BW}}$ (FLOP/byte), the number of operations the hardware must perform for every byte loaded to keep its compute units busy.

**Result**: The intensity needed to reach the tensor peak has increased. Under this simple HBM roofline, a kernel with ${I}$ = 200 FLOP/byte lies above the A100 ridge but below the H100 ridge. If its intensity and implementation remain unchanged, its idealized gain is about 2.1×, between the 1.6× bandwidth ratio and the 3.2× peak-compute ratio, because the kernel moves from the compute roof on the A100 to the bandwidth slope on the H100.

**Systems insight**: The H100 demands more operations per byte to stay on its compute roof, so unchanged code gains less than the peak-compute ratio. Recovering the rest requires raising the kernel’s intensity. Fusing a low-intensity chain, the kernel fusion of **[[01 Books/Volume I - Foundations/02 Development/07 ML Frameworks/V1-07.04 Execution from Graphs to Kernels#^sec-ml-frameworks-execution-strategy-matters-memory-wall-1ce8|The memory wall and kernel fusion]]**, is one way, because it removes intermediate round-trips, but fusion is not automatically the best optimization for every kernel.

Operations such as depthwise convolution, embedding lookup, LayerNorm, and softmax serve as low-intensity reference points that commonly fall below the HBM ridge on compute-dense accelerators under the stated traffic models. [[01 Books/Volume I - Foundations/03 Optimization/11 Hardware Acceleration/V1-11.07 Performance Bounds#^tbl-roofline-operations|Table 7]] maps common neural network operations to their representative roofline regimes and to the lighthouse workload that exercises each. Classification depends on the implementation, memory interface, and target path; profiling distinguishes a bandwidth ceiling from latency, limited parallelism, or other execution constraints.

| Operation                                  | Arithmetic Intensity  | **Classification**                              | **Lighthouse example**      |
|:-------------------------------------------|:----------------------|:------------------------------------------------|:----------------------------|
| **Conv2D (Dense)**                         | 50–200 FLOP/byte      | Straddles ridge; high-reuse cases compute-bound | ResNet-50                   |
| **Dense MatMul (large batch, well-tiled)** | ~150–1,000+ FLOP/byte | Compute-bound once above the target’s ridge     | GPT-2 (batched projections) |
| **Depthwise conv**                         | 10–20 FLOP/byte       | Below modeled HBM ridge                         | MobileNet                   |
| Attention Softmax                          | Convention-dependent  | Below modeled HBM ridge                         | GPT-2 (Generation)          |
| LayerNorm                                  | 1–2 FLOP/byte         | Below modeled HBM ridge                         | GPT-2/Llama                 |
| Embedding lookup                           | $<1$ FLOP/byte        | Below modeled HBM ridge                         | DLRM                        |

**Napkin Math 11.4: Transformer layer analysis**

For one GPT-2 XL layer during prefill, with hidden dimension ${d}$ = 1600, batch size ${B}$ = 8, sequence length ${S}$ = 1024, and ${N_{\text{heads}}}$ = 25 attention heads:

**Attention QKV projection**:

- FLOPs: 2 $\times$ 3 $\times$ 8 $\times$ 1024 $\times$ 1600 $\times$ 1600 = 125.8 GFLOP
- Bytes: (input + weights + output) = (8 $\times$ 1024 $\times$ 1600 + 3 $\times$ 1600 $\times$ 1600 + 8 $\times$ 1024 $\times$ 1600 $\times$ 3) $\times$ 2 ≈ 120.2 MB
- ${I}$ = 125.8 GFLOP / 120.2 MB = 1046.7 FLOP/byte, which is compute bound on the H100 (above the 295.2 FLOP/byte ridge point)

**Softmax**:

- FLOPs: 8 $\times$ 25 $\times$ 1024 $\times$ 1024 $\times$ 3 ≈ 629.1 MFLOP (exp, sum, div)
- Bytes: 8 $\times$ 25 $\times$ 1024 $\times$ 1024 $\times 2 \times 2$ = 838.9 MB
- ${I}$ = 629.1 MFLOP / 838.9 MB = 0.75 FLOP/byte under this simple operation count, placing it below the reference tensor-path HBM ridge. Counting exponentials and division as multiple operations raises the numerical intensity, but those instructions use different execution units. The relevant vector, reduction, and special-function ceilings, together with profiling, determine whether bandwidth is actually the bottleneck.

**Systems insight**: This analysis explains why FlashAttention focuses on reducing memory traffic in attention rather than reducing FLOPs.

The roofline supplies an initial resource bound; profiling determines which physical subsystem limits the kernel. When a kernel approaches its relevant off-chip bandwidth ceiling, optimization should focus on conserving memory traffic. Fusing adjacent operators keeps intermediate activations in on-chip registers or shared memory (SRAM). Lower-precision numeric formats reduce the physical byte count of memory transfers. Algorithmic refactorings like FlashAttention restructure access patterns to compute within SRAM tiles. When a kernel approaches the compute ceiling of its actual execution path, high data reuse keeps execution units supplied and off-chip bandwidth is no longer the limiting resource. Performance here depends on saturating arithmetic execution pipelines: structuring matrix tiles to sustain Tensor Core throughput, hiding instruction latencies through software pipelining, and scaling batch sizes to distribute work across all available streaming multiprocessors.

↪ How a benchmark determines where a workload actually sits against its roofline ceiling is developed in **[[01 Books/Volume I - Foundations/03 Optimization/12 Benchmarking/V1-12.05 Benchmark Suites#^sec-benchmarking-system-benchmarks-393c|System benchmarks]]**.

### Memory Bandwidth Bounds

^sec-hardware-acceleration-calculating-memory-bandwidth-bounds-7fc0

When a kernel’s arithmetic intensity falls below the ridge point, adding more Tensor Cores cannot speed it up because off-chip memory bandwidth cannot deliver operands fast enough to sustain the target compute rate ${R_{\text{ops}}}$ (FLOP/s). For a workload with arithmetic intensity ${I}$ (FLOP/byte), keeping the compute units fed at rate ${R_{\text{ops}}}$ requires the memory subsystem to transfer data at the rate defined by [[01 Books/Volume I - Foundations/03 Optimization/11 Hardware Acceleration/V1-11.07 Performance Bounds#^eq-required-bandwidth|equation 3]]:

$$
{\text{BW}_{\text{req}}} = \frac{{R_{\text{ops}}}}{{I}} \text{ bytes/s} \tag{3}
$$

^eq-required-bandwidth

When this required bandwidth exceeds the hardware’s peak memory bandwidth ${\text{BW}}$, memory transfers cap attainable throughput according to [[01 Books/Volume I - Foundations/03 Optimization/11 Hardware Acceleration/V1-11.07 Performance Bounds#^eq-attainable-throughput|equation 4]]:

$$
{R_{\text{attain}}} = {\text{BW}} \times {I} \tag{4}
$$

^eq-attainable-throughput

Evaluating an operation against its execution path’s ridge point identifies the lower modeled ceiling. Extensive data reuse can raise arithmetic intensity above that ridge, making compute the tighter bound; profiling must establish whether execution approaches it. ResNet-50’s stage-3 $3{\times}3$ convolution demonstrates this behavior.

**Napkin Math 11.5: Convolutional layer analysis**

Consider the $3{\times}3$ convolution in a stage-3 bottleneck block of ResNet-50, with input shape (batch = 32, channels = 128, height = 28, width = 28) and 128 output channels, on an H100 at FP16:

**Computational requirements**:

- Output size: $32 \times 128 \times 28 \times 28$ = 3.2M elements
- FLOPs per output: $128 \times 3 \times 3 \times 2$ = 2,304 (multiply-add)
- Total FLOPs: 3.2M $\times$ 2,304 = 7.4 GFLOP

**Memory traffic analysis**:

- Input: $32 \times 128 \times 28 \times 28 \times 2$ = 6.4 MB (FP16)
- Weights: $128 \times 128 \times 3 \times 3 \times 2$ ≈ 0.3 MB (FP16)
- Output: $32 \times 128 \times 28 \times 28 \times 2$ = 6.4 MB (FP16)
- Total: 13.1 MB

**Arithmetic intensity**: ${I}$ = 7.4 GFLOP / 13.1 MB = 563.1 FLOP/byte

**Systems insight**: This is well above the H100’s FP16 ridge point of 295.2 FLOP/byte, so the simple roofline model places the operation in the compute-limited region. The dense FP16 tensor peak of ~989 TFLOP/s is therefore the ceiling, but realized performance still depends on tiling, occupancy, instruction mix, and library efficiency.

The convolutional layer’s high arithmetic intensity arises from spatial weight reuse: the same $3{\times}3$ kernel is applied across every spatial location in the feature map, amortizing the cost of loading weights from off-chip memory across millions of multiply-accumulate operations. Keeping active tiles in registers and shared memory reduces repeated off-chip transfers, making high arithmetic throughput possible. Whether a particular kernel then saturates the Tensor Cores depends on tiling, occupancy, and instruction mix, which the roofline does not model. Moreover, the FP16 analysis is only half of the chapter’s workload, because the profile **[[01 Books/Volume I - Foundations/03 Optimization/10 Model Compression/V1-10 Model Compression#^sec-model-compression|Model Compression]]** ended on runs ResNet-50 in INT8. Quantization changes both sides of the roofline at once, and the two changes need not cancel.

**Napkin Math 11.6: When INT8 moves a layer below the ridge**

**Problem**: The H100’s INT8 tensor path offers about 2× the operations per second of its FP16 path from the same 3.35 TB/s of memory bandwidth. Which ResNet-50 layers stay compute bound when the model moves from FP16 to INT8?

**The two ridges**: The ridge rises from 295.2 FLOP/byte at FP16 to 590.7 FLOP/byte at INT8. A layer keeps its position relative to the ridge only if every byte it moves halves along with the operand width.

**Three layers at batch 32** (operations per byte, one read of input and weights and one write of the output):

- **Stage-3 $3{\times}3$ convolution** (the layer of notebook [11.5](https://mlsysbook.ai/vol1/11_hw_acceleration/11_hw_acceleration.html#nbk-conv-analysis)): 563 at FP16, above the FP16 ridge. With INT8 activations it reaches 1,126, above the INT8 ridge. If its activations stay FP16 between layers, as when the kernel reads and writes 16-bit tensors for FP16 neighbors, its intensity rises only to 569, well below the INT8 ridge. At batch 32, the input and output activation tensors together account for roughly 12.8 MB of traffic while the shared $3{\times}3$ weight tensor is only about 0.3 MB in FP16, so halving weight bytes alone barely changes total memory traffic.
- **Stage-2 $3{\times}3$ convolution** ($56{\times}56$ positions, 64 channels in and out): 287 at FP16 and 574 at INT8, just below the ridge in both precisions.
- **Stage-2 $1{\times}1$ expansion** ($56{\times}56$ positions, 64 channels in and 256 out): 51 at FP16 and 102 at INT8, far below the ridge in both.

**Systems insight**: INT8 raises the arithmetic ceiling but leaves the bandwidth ceiling where it was, so the layers that were already bandwidth bound gain only the halved bytes, and a compute-bound layer whose activations stay wide crosses below the new ridge. The INT8 speedup of **[[01 Books/Volume I - Foundations/03 Optimization/10 Model Compression/V1-10 Model Compression#^sec-model-compression|Model Compression]]**’s profile therefore depends on whether activations travel at INT8 between layers, a decision made by the compiler and runtime ([[01 Books/Volume I - Foundations/03 Optimization/11 Hardware Acceleration/V1-11.09 Compilers and Runtimes#^sec-hardware-acceleration-compiler-support-172e|section 11.8]]), not by the quantized weights alone.

The three layers also show what the label compute bound means for ResNet-50. It places the model relative to the other lighthouses; it does not say that every layer clears the ridge. At large batch, its $3{\times}3$ convolutions sit at or near the FP16 ridge, where arithmetic time is close to or above memory time, so a faster arithmetic path pays off, while its $1{\times}1$ layers sit further left and depend more on bandwidth. The traffic counts above also assume that each layer reads its input activations from HBM and writes its output back. When a fused kernel or the L2 cache hands one layer’s output to the next on chip ([[01 Books/Volume I - Foundations/03 Optimization/11 Hardware Acceleration/V1-11.08 Hardware Mapping#^sec-hardware-acceleration-kernel-fusion-7faf|section 11.7.7]]), those bytes never reach HBM and the layer moves right on the roofline. For these convolutions, activations rather than weights dominate the bytes, so keeping activations on chip and at INT8, more than batching, holds the layers near the arithmetic ceiling.

Not all neural network layers reuse data so heavily. Fully connected (dense) layers, such as ResNet-50’s classification head, feed-forward networks, and linear projections in transformer attention blocks, lack spatial dimensions. When evaluated at small batch sizes, their arithmetic intensity drops substantially, shifting execution into the bandwidth-bound regime:

**Napkin Math 11.7: Dense layer analysis**

Consider ResNet-50’s final fully connected layer, which maps 2,048 pooled features to 1,000 class scores for a batch of 32 images on the same H100:

**Computational requirements**:

- Matrix multiply: $(32 \times 2048) \times (2048 \times 1000)$
- Total FLOPs: $2 \times 32 \times 2048 \times 1000$ = 131.1 MFLOP

**Memory traffic analysis**:

- Input: $32 \times 2048 \times 2$ = 131.1 KB (FP16)
- Weights: $2048 \times 1000 \times 2$ = 4.1 MB (FP16)
- Output: $32 \times 1000 \times 2$ = 64 KB (FP16)
- Total: 4.3 MB

**Arithmetic intensity**: ${I}$ = 131.1 MFLOP / 4.3 MB = 30.5 FLOP/byte

This intensity sits below the H100’s ridge point of 295.2 FLOP/byte, making this operation memory bound under the stated traffic model. Attainable performance: ${R_{\text{attain}}}$ = 3,350 GB/s $\times$ 30.5 FLOP/byte = 102.3 TFLOP/s

**Systems insight**: Attainable throughput is only 10.3 percent of peak compute capability, so at batch 32 the memory wall caps this layer long before the Tensor Cores do.

The dense layer’s lower arithmetic intensity stems from limited parameter reuse. While a convolutional kernel is reused across every spatial location in an activation map, a dense weight matrix is reused only across the batch dimension. At a batch size of 32, each fetched weight contributes to 32 sample products in this operation. When the weights stream from HBM on each invocation, as modeled, the bandwidth ceiling caps throughput at 10.3 percent of peak compute capacity. The fraction is a bound rather than a measured stall count, and a layer whose weights stay cached between invocations would do better. The same arithmetic explains why transformer inference at low batch sizes is governed by memory bandwidth rather than arithmetic capability, a regime the throughput-ceiling notebook below quantifies for GPT-2.

While small-batch matrix multiplications suffer from restricted parameter reuse, elementwise and reduction operators represent an even more acute memory bottleneck. Operations such as layer normalization, activation functions, and residual additions perform only a few arithmetic operations per element loaded from memory, driving arithmetic intensity to its physical floor:

**Napkin Math 11.8: LayerNorm analysis**

LayerNorm over one GPT-2 XL layer’s activations, with input shape (batch = 8, sequence = 1024, hidden = 1600):

**Computational requirements**:

- Elements: $8 \times 1024 \times 1600$ = 13.1M
- Approximate operations per element: mean reduction (1 ADD), variance (2 ADD, 1 MUL), normalization and affine transform (2 ADD, 2 MUL) ≈ 8; reciprocal-square-root overhead is amortized across each hidden vector
- Total FLOPs: 13.1M $\times$ 8 = 104.9 MFLOP

**Memory traffic**:

- Input: 13.1M $\times$ 2 = 26.2 MB
- Parameters (scale, bias): $1600 \times 2 \times 2$ = 6.4 KB (negligible)
- Output: 13.1M $\times$ 2 = 26.2 MB
- Total: 52.4 MB

**Arithmetic intensity**: ${I}$ = 104.9 MFLOP / 52.4 MB = 2.0 FLOP/byte

This arithmetic intensity sits severely below the H100 ridge point (148× below). The HBM performance upper bound is: ${R_{\text{attain}}}$ = 3,350 GB/s $\times$ 2.0 FLOP/byte = 6.7 TFLOP/s

**Systems insight**: The simple HBM roofline bounds this standalone LayerNorm at about 0.7 percent of the H100’s tensor peak. That tensor peak is a reference, not the throughput available to LayerNorm’s vector and reduction instructions. Actual latency also depends on their execution ceilings, fusion, launch overhead, and which memory level supplies the data.

Standalone normalization performs minimal arithmetic relative to the memory traffic it generates, as the LayerNorm analysis demonstrates. Under the modeled unfused HBM read/write boundary, the implementation performs approximately 2 FLOP/byte, giving a low bandwidth ceiling relative to the tensor peak. Reaching that ceiling still requires enough parallel work and an efficient reduction. Cache residency changes the physical HBM byte count and can shift the relevant bound to another memory interface. In the D·A·M framework, when the machine constraint is a memory bandwidth wall, the algorithmic response is kernel fusion, which collapses adjacent projections, bias additions, normalization, and activations into one kernel so that intermediate values stay in registers or SRAM. Fusion pays off when the grouping preserves numerical behavior and fits the local register and SRAM budget, and profiling confirms that the saved traffic outweighs any loss of occupancy.

### Optimization by Intensity Regime

^sec-hardware-acceleration-optimization-intensity-regime-cb3a

Tuning thread-block occupancy on a bandwidth-bound LayerNorm kernel wastes engineering effort for zero speedup, just as fusing two already compute-bound matrix multiplications cannot raise throughput past the Tensor Core ceiling. The roofline’s intensity regimes ([[01 Books/Volume I - Foundations/03 Optimization/11 Hardware Acceleration/V1-11.07 Performance Bounds#^tbl-optimization-intensity-regime|table 8]]) match each bottleneck to the lever that can move it.

| **Intensity regime**              | **Typical operations**                         | **Optimization priority**                | **Common techniques**                                                               | **Expected impact**                                                            |
|:----------------------------------|:-----------------------------------------------|:-----------------------------------------|:------------------------------------------------------------------------------------|:-------------------------------------------------------------------------------|
| **Above the target path’s ridge** | Large convolutions                             | Maximize compute utilization.            | Tensor Cores, thread-block tuning, and high occupancy.                              | Raises sustained compute utilization when the kernel already clears the ridge. |
| **Near the target path’s ridge**  | Medium-sized dense layers                      | Balance compute and memory optimization. | Larger batches, register tiling, and fusion with adjacent operations.               | Can move the bottleneck between memory and compute.                            |
| **Below the target path’s ridge** | Small dense layers and element-wise operations | Reduce memory traffic.                   | Aggressive kernel fusion, reduced precision (FP16 → INT8), and algorithmic changes. | Reduces external-memory traffic when fusion or layout changes are legal.       |
| **Low-intensity operator chains** | Normalization layers and activation functions  | Eliminate memory round-trips.            | Fuse with adjacent operations and use in-place computation where legal.             | Removes intermediate round-trips when adjacent operations can be fused.        |

Table 8: **Optimization by Arithmetic Intensity Regime**: Position relative to the target execution path’s ridge guides compute and traffic optimization. Profiling confirms the bottleneck; fusion removes intermediate round-trips when dependencies and local storage permit it.

^tbl-optimization-intensity-regime

For low-intensity operations, kernel fusion is often the decisive optimization: LayerNorm combined with the Gaussian Error Linear Unit (GELU), for example, can become a single fused kernel. Beyond fusion, one of the most accessible levers for moving an operation up and right on the roofline is batching.

**Napkin Math 11.9: Batch size and arithmetic intensity**

Increasing batch size improves arithmetic intensity for matrix operations by amortizing weight loading, the effect measured for a small network in **[[01 Books/Volume I - Foundations/02 Development/05 Neural Computation/V1-05.06 Inference Pipeline#^sec-neural-computation-performance-enhancement-techniques-692f|Optimizing inference throughput and latency]]**. [[01 Books/Volume I - Foundations/03 Optimization/11 Hardware Acceleration/V1-11.07 Performance Bounds#^eq-batch-ai|Equation 5]] formalizes this relationship for an idealized FP16/BF16 dense layer $({B}{\times}{d_{\text{in}}}){\times}({d_{\text{in}}}{\times}{d_{\text{out}}})$, counting one read each of the input and weights and one write of the output, where ${B}$ is batch size and ${d_{\text{in}}}$ and ${d_{\text{out}}}$ are the layer’s input and output widths:

$$
{I} = \frac{2{B}{d_{\text{in}}}{d_{\text{out}}}}{2{B}{d_{\text{in}}} + 2{d_{\text{in}}}{d_{\text{out}}} + 2{B}{d_{\text{out}}}} \approx \frac{2{B}{d_{\text{in}}}{d_{\text{out}}}}{2{d_{\text{in}}}{d_{\text{out}}}} = {B}\quad (\text{when } 2{d_{\text{in}}}{d_{\text{out}}} \gg 2{B}({d_{\text{in}}}+{d_{\text{out}}})) \tag{5}
$$

^eq-batch-ai

Example: Dense layer with ${d_{\text{in}}} = {d_{\text{out}}}$ = 2048 (FP16)

- Batch = 1: ${I}$ ≈ 1 FLOP/byte (memory bound)
- Batch = 32: ${I}$ ≈ 31 FLOP/byte (memory bound)
- Batch = 512: ${I}$ ≈ 341.3 FLOP/byte (compute bound on the H100)

**Systems insight**: This explains why batching can produce large throughput improvements in production inference systems, as MLPerf Inference, the standardized benchmark suite, demonstrates by separating bulk-throughput runs from latency-constrained serving runs ([[01 Books/Volume I - Foundations/03 Optimization/11 Hardware Acceleration/V1-11.16 Source References#^ref-reddi2020|Reddi et al. 2019]]).

↪ The MLPerf Inference execution scenarios that separate throughput runs from latency-constrained runs are developed in **[[01 Books/Volume I - Foundations/03 Optimization/12 Benchmarking/V1-12.05 Benchmark Suites#^sec-benchmarking-mlperf-execution-scenarios-89f0|MLPerf execution scenarios]]**.

The batch size analysis reveals why inference serving systems are designed around batching: it changes the arithmetic intensity regime of bandwidth-bound workloads. However, batching introduces latency trade-offs, since requests must wait in a queue until a batch forms. This tension between throughput (favoring large batches) and latency (favoring small batches) is a central challenge in ML serving systems, explored in depth in **[[01 Books/Volume I - Foundations/04 Deployment/13 Model Serving/V1-13.06 Throughput Optimization#^sec-model-serving-dynamic-batching-latencythroughput-tradeoffs-986d|Dynamic batching latency-throughput trade-offs]]**. When service latency limits batching, batch-1 or small-batch LLM decode often retains low weight reuse and low arithmetic intensity, and the next worked model quantifies that particular regime rather than treating all LLM inference as identical.

**Napkin Math 11.10: The throughput ceiling**

**Problem**: A batch-1 GPT-2 calculation makes this bandwidth ceiling concrete: what is the maximum possible utilization of an NVIDIA H100 when running GPT-2 inference (batch size 1)?

**The hardware constraints (the denominators)**

- **Peak compute**: 989 TFLOP/s (FP16 Tensor Core).

- **Peak bandwidth**: 3.35 TB/s (HBM3).

- **Ridge point $({R_{\text{peak}}}/{\text{BW}})$**: 989 TFLOP/s / 3.35 TB/s = 295.2 FLOP/byte (for FP16 Tensor Core).

  **Interpretation**: Saturating this chip at FP16 precision requires 295.2 FLOP/byte operations for every byte loaded. The ridge point varies by precision: FP32 operations (67 TFLOP/s peak) have a ridge point of only ~20 FLOP/byte.

**The workload characteristics (the numerator)**

- **Model**: GPT-2 XL (1.5 billion parameters).
- **Operation**: Autoregressive generation (1 token at a time).
- **Data movement**: Must load all weights (3 GB @ FP16) for every token.
- **Compute**: Vector-Matrix multiplication. Two FLOPs per parameter, $2{P}$ ≈ 3 GFLOP.
- **Arithmetic intensity**: 3 GFLOP / 3 GB = 1 FLOP/byte

**The prediction (iron law)**

Since the modeled intensity ${I}$ (1) $\ll$ the ridge point ${I_{\text{ridge}}}$ (295.2 FLOP/byte), batch-1 decode is bandwidth bound.

- **Maximum throughput**: 1 FLOP/byte $\times$ 3.35 TB/s = 3.4 TFLOP/s.
- **Utilization ceiling**: 3.4 TFLOP/s (Bound) / 989 TFLOP/s (Peak) ≈ $0.3\%$

**Systems insight**: Without batching, a ~\$25,000 accelerator cannot exceed 0.3 percent of its compute peak in this weight-streaming decode scenario. Batching and weight quantization address that gap; key-value caching instead avoids recomputing prior attention states. The ceiling also assumes that the decode step is launched as a captured graph (**[[01 Books/Volume I - Foundations/02 Development/07 ML Frameworks/V1-07.04 Execution from Graphs to Kernels#^sec-ml-frameworks-hybrid-approaches-jit-compilation-8954|Graph capture and JIT compilation]]**); executed one eager kernel at a time, launch overhead rather than weight streaming would set the floor.

Placed on one roofline, the layers and the decode step analyzed above fall on either side of two ridges, and only the stage-3 $3{\times}3$ convolution reaches a flat ceiling ([[01 Books/Volume I - Foundations/03 Optimization/11 Hardware Acceleration/V1-11.07 Performance Bounds#^fig-hw-acceleration-h100-roofline-lighthouses|figure 12]]). The roofline thus diagnoses whether an operation is compute bound or memory bound. Knowing that batch-1 decode is bandwidth bound at a ceiling of 0.3 percent of peak is only the first step; the next challenge is translating this diagnosis into efficient execution plans that exploit accelerator architectures.
