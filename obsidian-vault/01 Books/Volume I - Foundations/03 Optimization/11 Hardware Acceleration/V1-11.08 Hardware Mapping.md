# V1-11.08 Hardware Mapping

[[Home|Home]] · [[01 Books/Volume I - Foundations/Volume 1 Overview|Volume Overview]] · [[01 Books/Volume I - Foundations/03 Optimization/11 Hardware Acceleration/V1-11 Hardware Acceleration|V1-11 Hardware Acceleration]]

^sec-hardware-acceleration-hardware-mapping-fundamentals-neural-networks-f9a9

Consider one of ResNet-50’s $3{\times}3$ convolutions running on an accelerator tile. The mathematical operation is fixed, but the execution plan is not. One schedule can keep a filter in local registers while many output pixels stream past it; another can advance pixel by pixel and reload the same filter values repeatedly from a slower memory tier. Both schedules compute the same tensor. Only one turns the reuse in the convolution into real bandwidth savings.

**Definition 11.4: Mapping in AI acceleration**

***Mapping in AI acceleration*** is the accelerator-compiler process of assigning the logical computation graph onto the physical hardware topology by deciding which operations execute on which processing elements, which data resides in which memory tier, and in what temporal order.

1.  **Significance**: Within the D·A·M taxonomy, mapping is a machine-axis decision that helps determine how closely an operation approaches the roofline bound $\min({R_{\text{peak}}},\; {\text{BW}} \times {I})$. A poor tiling choice that forces unnecessary DRAM accesses can reduce effective arithmetic intensity enough to move a nominally compute-bound operation into the bandwidth-bound regime.
2.  **Distinction**: Traditional compilation often emphasizes instruction selection, register allocation, and scheduling for general-purpose processors. Accelerator mapping gives equal prominence to spatial placement and explicit data movement across a dataflow architecture. The reason is physical: as quantified by the storage-tier energy ladder in [[01 Books/Volume I - Foundations/03 Optimization/11 Hardware Acceleration/V1-11.03 Hardware Specialization#^fig-energy-hierarchy|figure 4]], an off-chip DRAM access costs about 173× the energy of an FP32 multiply, making operand placement as critical to efficiency as the execution unit that evaluates it.
3.  **Common pitfall**: A frequent misconception is that mapping is automatically handled by frameworks. For general GPU workloads, compilers such as Accelerated Linear Algebra (XLA) can find strong mappings for common kernels. For specialized accelerators (systolic arrays, custom ASICs), compiler-generated mappings may still lag hand-tuned schedules because the compiler’s search space is limited by the time budget at compilation.

The convolution example exposes the three decisions that recur throughout accelerator compilation. Placement assigns the multiply-accumulate work to processing elements so parallelism does not turn into idle time or interconnect congestion. Allocation keeps weights, activations, and partial sums in the memory tier where their next use will occur, rather than letting reuse spill back to DRAM. Scheduling orders loops and kernels so the chosen placement and allocation remain valid over time. A poor choice in any one dimension can collapse a high-arithmetic-intensity operation back into a bandwidth-bound execution. In practice, these choices are too coupled for developers to manage by hand at model scale. Systems such as XLA, TVM, and TensorRT therefore lower high-level models and search or select execution plans within their compile-time and hardware budgets. [[01 Books/Volume I - Foundations/03 Optimization/11 Hardware Acceleration/V1-11.09 Compilers and Runtimes#^sec-hardware-acceleration-compiler-support-172e|Section 11.8]] examines that compiler support in detail.

### Placement and Allocation

^sec-hardware-acceleration-computation-placement-23d2

Spatial and storage mapping decisions pull against each other across the chip. Computation placement spreads operations across processing elements to expose parallelism, but placing producer and consumer operations on distant units increases the interconnect bandwidth required to shuttle intermediate tensors between them. Memory allocation stages operands in fast on-chip buffers to cut access latency, but the small capacity of registers and SRAM limits how many processing elements can hold their working sets concurrently. Getting either side wrong leaves thousands of execution units idle or starved for data.

#### Computation Placement

^sec-hardware-acceleration-computation-placement-e03f

The scale of modern hardware magnifies any spatial imbalance: the NVIDIA H100 contains more than 16,000 CUDA cores and more than 500 Tensor Cores ([[01 Books/Volume I - Foundations/03 Optimization/11 Hardware Acceleration/V1-11.16 Source References#^ref-choquette2023|Choquette 2023]]), TPUs organize thousands of multiply-accumulate units into systolic arrays ([[01 Books/Volume I - Foundations/03 Optimization/11 Hardware Acceleration/V1-11.16 Source References#^ref-jouppi2017|Jouppi et al. 2017]]), and wafer-scale processors such as Cerebras’ CS-2 integrate more than 850,000 cores ([[01 Books/Volume I - Foundations/03 Optimization/11 Hardware Acceleration/V1-11.16 Source References#^ref-Cerebras2021|Systems 2021]]). Across arrays of that size, even a small fraction of idle processing elements or redundant cross-chip transfers wastes substantial time and energy.

The difficulty of placement depends on workload regularity. Convolutional networks and dense transformer operations possess regular access patterns that tile predictably, though transformer architectures mix operations with distinct tensor shapes and reuse profiles. Graph neural networks (GNNs), which compute over the nodes and edges of an input graph, present greater difficulty because sparse, irregular neighborhood aggregations introduce load imbalance and non-uniform communication across processing elements. [[01 Books/Volume I - Foundations/03 Optimization/11 Hardware Acceleration/V1-11.08 Hardware Mapping#^tbl-placement-challenges|Table 9]] summarizes these core placement challenges and their operational impact.

| **Challenge**                      | **Impact on execution**                                                                                            | **Key Considerations for Placement**                                                        |
|:-----------------------------------|:-------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------|:--------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------|
| **Workload imbalance**             | Some processing elements finish early while others remain overloaded, leading to idle compute resources.           | Distribute operations evenly to prevent stalls and ensure full utilization of PEs.          |
| **Irregular computation patterns** | Sparse graphs, routing, and variable shapes can create nonuniform work that complicates static placement.          | Use adaptive placement when workload characteristics vary at run time.                      |
| **Excessive data movement**        | Frequent memory transfers introduce latency and increase power consumption.                                        | Keep frequently used data close to the compute units and minimize off-chip memory accesses. |
| **Limited interconnect bandwidth** | Poorly placed operations can create congestion, slowing data movement between PEs.                                 | Optimize spatial and temporal placement to reduce communication overhead.                   |
| **Model-specific execution needs** | CNNs, transformers, and GNNs require different execution patterns, making a single placement strategy ineffective. | Tailor placement strategies to match the computational structure of each model type.        |

Table 9: **Computation Placement Challenges**: Effective neural network deployment requires strategic allocation of computations to processing elements, balancing workload distribution, data movement costs, and hardware constraints to maximize execution efficiency. These challenges guide the design of mapping strategies that optimize resource utilization and minimize communication overhead.

^tbl-placement-challenges

Static spatial mappings suffice for regular dense operations whose shapes and loop bounds are known ahead of time. Workloads with dynamic shapes, sparsity, or mixture-of-experts routing instead require runtime scheduling to balance execution across processing elements. Yet even an optimal computation placement stalls if the required operands are not staged in time: compute units can execute only as fast as operands arrive from the memory hierarchy.

#### Memory Allocation

^sec-hardware-acceleration-memory-allocation-faec

While computation placement determines where operations execute, memory allocation defines where data resides and how it flows through the hierarchy. The goal is to keep reused data near the processing elements without exceeding faster-tier capacity. GPUs expose global memory, shared memory, caches, and registers that kernels coordinate through tiling ([[01 Books/Volume I - Foundations/03 Optimization/11 Hardware Acceleration/V1-11.16 Source References#^ref-NVIDIA2020|NVIDIA Corporation 2020a]]). TPUs use on-chip SRAM buffers to stage the activations that flow horizontally through the 2D systolic-array dataflow ([[01 Books/Volume I - Foundations/03 Optimization/11 Hardware Acceleration/V1-11.16 Source References#^ref-jouppi2017|Jouppi et al. 2017]]) of [[01 Books/Volume I - Foundations/03 Optimization/11 Hardware Acceleration/V1-11.05 Compute Units#^fig-systolic-array|figure 5]], with partial sums flowing vertically, while wafer-scale processors partition memory and computation to control interconnect traffic ([[01 Books/Volume I - Foundations/03 Optimization/11 Hardware Acceleration/V1-11.16 Source References#^ref-Cerebras2021|Systems 2021]]). General-purpose processors and accelerators both use memory hierarchies, but many accelerators expose more placement decisions to compilers and kernels. When allocation fails to exploit locality, processing elements stall waiting for operands, driving down utilization and dissipating energy across the memory bus.

The severity of these penalties varies by workload. Convolutional networks rely on structured, localized access patterns and benefit from well-defined memory layouts that support predictable reuse ([[01 Books/Volume I - Foundations/03 Optimization/11 Hardware Acceleration/V1-11.16 Source References#^ref-chen2016eyeriss|Chen et al. 2016]]). Transformers require access to large parameter sets and intermediate activations, making them sensitive to capacity and bandwidth. Graph neural networks add irregular sparse structures that complicate allocation and prefetching.

Capacity is only the first allocation test. A tensor can fit in accelerator memory yet still dominate execution if each kernel rereads it from a distant tier; conversely, tiling can keep a larger working set efficient by preserving reuse locally. The allocation diagnostic is therefore traffic, not capacity alone: compilers must track how frequently values cross each memory boundary and keep high-reuse tensors in the fastest feasible tier. Systems combine static memory plans for known shapes with dynamic buffer management when shapes vary, while device capacity limits which models fit without partitioning or offload. [[01 Books/Volume I - Foundations/03 Optimization/11 Hardware Acceleration/V1-11.08 Hardware Mapping#^tbl-memory-allocation|Table 10]] summarizes these allocation challenges across the hierarchy.

| Challenge                            | Impact on Execution                                                                    | **Key Considerations for Allocation**                                                             |
|:-------------------------------------|:---------------------------------------------------------------------------------------|:--------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------|
| **High memory latency**              | Slow data access delays execution and reduces throughput.                              | Prioritize placing frequently accessed data in faster memory locations.                           |
| **Limited on-chip storage**          | Small local memory constrains the amount of data available near compute units.         | Allocate storage efficiently to maximize data availability without exceeding hardware limits.     |
| **High off-chip bandwidth demand**   | Frequent access to external memory increases delays and power consumption.             | Reduce unnecessary memory transfers by carefully managing when and how data is moved.             |
| **Irregular memory access patterns** | Some models require accessing data unpredictably, leading to inefficient memory usage. | Organize memory layout to align with access patterns and minimize unnecessary data movement.      |
| **Model-specific memory needs**      | Different models require different allocation strategies to optimize performance.      | Tailor allocation decisions based on the structure and execution characteristics of the workload. |

Table 10: **Memory Allocation Challenges**: Efficient memory management in AI accelerators balances data access speed with hardware constraints, mitigating performance bottlenecks caused by latency, bandwidth limitations, and irregular data patterns. Complex models such as transformers and graph networks impose variable and demanding memory requirements that amplify these challenges.

### Combinatorial Complexity

^sec-hardware-acceleration-combinatorial-complexity-ea33

Even the convolution that opened this section turns hardware mapping into a combinatorial search problem. Keeping a filter resident in local storage improves reuse only if the assigned processing elements have sufficient storage and if the loop schedule revisits that filter before eviction. Similarly, parallelizing across more processing elements improves throughput only until synchronization overhead and interconnect contention erode the benefit. Because changing one decision alters the feasible and efficient choices for the others, compilers must evaluate placement, allocation, and scheduling jointly rather than independently. [[01 Books/Volume I - Foundations/03 Optimization/11 Hardware Acceleration/V1-11.08 Hardware Mapping#^tbl-combinatorial-complexity|Table 11]] summarizes these recurring trade-offs across the execution stack.

| Dimension                             | **Placement considerations**                                                                             | **Allocation and Scheduling Considerations**                                                         |
|:--------------------------------------|:---------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------|:-----------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------|
| **Computational granularity**         | Fine-grained placement enables greater parallelism but increases synchronization overhead.               | Coarse-grained scheduling reduces synchronization overhead but may limit flexibility.                |
| **Spatial vs. Temporal Mapping**      | Spatial placement enhances parallel execution but can lead to resource contention and memory congestion. | Temporal scheduling balances resource sharing but may reduce overall throughput.                     |
| **Memory and Data Locality**          | Placing data closer to compute units minimizes latency but may reduce overall memory availability.       | Allocating data across multiple memory levels increases capacity but introduces higher access costs. |
| **Communication and synchronization** | Co-locating compute units reduces communication latency but may introduce contention.                    | Scheduling synchronization mechanisms mitigates stalls but can introduce additional overhead.        |
| **Dataflow and Execution Ordering**   | Static placement simplifies execution but limits adaptability to workload variations.                    | Dynamic scheduling improves adaptability but adds scheduling complexity.                             |

Table 11: **Placement-Allocation-Scheduling Trade-Offs**: AI accelerator performance depends on mapping computations to hardware, allocating data to memory tiers, and scheduling execution over time. Careful consideration of these interdependent factors is essential for maximizing throughput and minimizing energy consumption.

#### Counting the Search Space

^sec-hardware-acceleration-ordering-computation-execution-59e5

Evaluating these trade-offs requires navigating an unconstrained search space governed by three structural decisions: loop execution order, spatial parallelization across processing elements, and memory placement across the hierarchy. Counting each dimension on its own shows why exhaustive search is impractical.

Machine learning workloads are expressed as nested loops over tensor dimensions, and the loop execution order dictates which tensor elements remain resident in registers or SRAM between iterations. Ignoring dependences and equivalent orderings, the number of ways to arrange ${n_{\text{loops}}}$ loops has the toy upper bound:

$$
{N_{\text{order}}}= {n_{\text{loops}}}!
$$

 A convolutional layer with seven loop dimensions (batch, output channels, input channels, output height, output width, filter height, and filter width) already has $7! = 5{,}040$ possible execution orders. If each memory level could choose an ordering independently, the toy upper bound would expand as:

$$
({n_{\text{loops}}}!)^{{N_{\text{mem}}}}
$$

 where ${N_{\text{mem}}}$ is the number of memory hierarchy levels. A suboptimal ordering evicts values prematurely and multiplies DRAM traffic, whereas an optimized ordering maximizes reuse in local SRAM and caches ([[01 Books/Volume I - Foundations/03 Optimization/11 Hardware Acceleration/V1-11.16 Source References#^ref-sze2017|Sze et al. 2017]]).

Parallelization multiplies the count again. Parallelizing outer independent loops (such as batch or output feature channels) lets each core execute without interconnect traffic, whereas parallelizing an inner reduction loop requires cross-core synchronization and collective communication. Before legality and capacity constraints are applied, the number of ordered ways to select loops for parallel execution has the upper bound:

$$
{\mathcal{P}_{\text{parallel}}}= \frac{{n_{\text{loops}}}!}{({n_{\text{loops}}}-{k_{\text{parallel}}})!}
$$

 where ${k_{\text{parallel}}}$ is the number of loops selected for parallel execution; in a smaller six-loop nest, choosing three loops already gives $6!/3! = 120$ candidates, each with different demands on synchronization, interconnect bandwidth, and PE utilization.

Memory placement is the third dimension, and physical capacity bounds it. In a toy model where five tensor structures can each be assigned to one of four storage handling choices at a single hierarchy level, a single level already yields $4^5 = 1{,}024$ placement combinations, and choosing independently across three memory levels expands the unconstrained count to $(4^5)^3 = 4^{15}$ placements. Because an off-chip DRAM fetch costs roughly 128× more energy than a read from an 8 KB SRAM array ([[01 Books/Volume I - Foundations/03 Optimization/11 Hardware Acceleration/V1-11.03 Hardware Specialization#^fig-energy-hierarchy|figure 4]]), where each tensor resides directly dictates the achieved arithmetic intensity. Yet an operand tile can remain in on-chip SRAM only if its working set fits the allocated buffer. Larger resident tiles raise reuse but consume scarce capacity and register pressure, potentially reducing thread concurrency or forcing spills to slower tiers.

![[90 Assets/Volume I/11 Hw Acceleration/Vol1 Hw Acceleration Margin 003 13a72df76e.svg]]

*Three gray bars on a log scale from 1, each counting choices at a single memory level using the section's separate toy examples: memory placement at 4 to the 5th, or 1,024; loop ordering at 7 factorial, or 5,040; and parallelization at 6 factorial over 3 factorial, or 120.*

*Per memory level, from the section’s separate toy examples: $4^5 = 1{,}024$ placements, $7! = 5{,}040$ orders, $6!/3! = 120$ parallelizations. The product is raised to the number of levels, so three levels give $4^{15}$ placements alone.*

These structural choices (loop ordering, spatial parallelization across processing elements, tile sizing, and memory placement) are deeply coupled. Choosing an output-stationary schedule, for example, which keeps each output’s running sum in one place while inputs and weights stream past ([[01 Books/Volume I - Foundations/03 Optimization/11 Hardware Acceleration/V1-11.08 Hardware Mapping#^sec-hardware-acceleration-data-movement-patterns-3b06|section 11.7.4]]), pins accumulators in local registers and dictates which loops can be mapped spatially across processing elements without requiring inter-core reduction networks. Conversely, parallelizing across more execution units reduces the per-core SRAM budget available for operand staging, forcing smaller tile dimensions and increasing off-chip memory traffic.

While the unconstrained Cartesian product of these decisions suggests a very large combinatorial space, hardware capacity limits, data dependencies, and symmetry prune the vast majority of candidates as either physically illegal or strictly dominated. Exhaustive search across arbitrary loop permutations and placement choices is therefore both unnecessary and intractable. Production compilers and kernel developers manage this complexity by structuring their search around a small family of canonical dataflow patterns, spatial and temporal schedules engineered to exploit the specific reuse properties of neural network operators.

### Dataflow Optimization

^sec-hardware-acceleration-dataflow-optimization-strategies-ce52

Those canonical patterns exist because two mathematically identical loop schedules for the same convolution can differ by more than an order of magnitude in off-chip weight reads (example [[01 Books/Volume I - Foundations/03 Optimization/11 Hardware Acceleration/V1-11.08 Hardware Mapping#^exmp-hw-acceleration-loop-ordering-small-convolution|11.4]]), and every avoided read that would have reached DRAM saves the most expensive access on the energy ladder. While spatial and temporal mapping establish where operations execute and where operands reside, **dataflow optimization** governs the exact trajectories and schedules of data moving through processing elements and the memory hierarchy to maximize operand reuse.

In a systolic array or tiled vector unit, the temporal schedule and spatial routing of weights, inputs, and partial sums dictate how frequently operands must be fetched from energy-expensive DRAM rather than reused from local registers or scratchpads. By controlling off-chip traffic, the dataflow establishes the effective operational intensity of the kernel, determining whether execution achieves the compute-bound plateau or stalls in the bandwidth-bound regime of the roofline model. Optimizing compilers ([[01 Books/Volume I - Foundations/03 Optimization/11 Hardware Acceleration/V1-11.09 Compilers and Runtimes#^sec-hardware-acceleration-compiler-support-172e|section 11.8]]) and runtime systems ([[01 Books/Volume I - Foundations/03 Optimization/11 Hardware Acceleration/V1-11.09 Compilers and Runtimes#^sec-hardware-acceleration-runtime-support-f94f|section 11.8.3]]) therefore tailor dataflow patterns to match the reuse characteristics of each layer to the hardware constraints of the target accelerator.

Three recurring architectural decisions structure this design space, providing a compact vocabulary for navigating dataflow choices without exhaustive search across billions of candidate schedules:

1.  **Locality**: Stationary operand selection (weight-stationary, output-stationary, or input-stationary) dictates which tensor remains pinned in local registers or processing-element scratchpads, trading off memory traffic against register pressure.
2.  **Organization**: Physical tensor layouts (such as channels-last `NHWC` versus channels-first `NCHW`) govern whether memory accesses match hardware memory controller burst sizes and SIMD vector widths, preventing uncoalesced memory transactions.
3.  **Combination**: Kernel fusion and loop tiling partition iteration spaces and merge adjacent operations to eliminate round-trips to off-chip memory for intermediate tensors.

### Stationary Dataflows

^sec-hardware-acceleration-data-movement-patterns-3b06

The first lever, locality, begins with the reuse already present in the inner loops of a tensor operator. Because fetching an operand from DRAM costs orders of magnitude more energy than the arithmetic operation that consumes it ([[01 Books/Volume I - Foundations/03 Optimization/11 Hardware Acceleration/V1-11.03 Hardware Specialization#^fig-energy-hierarchy|figure 4]]) ([[01 Books/Volume I - Foundations/03 Optimization/11 Hardware Acceleration/V1-11.16 Source References#^ref-chen2016eyeriss|Chen et al. 2016]]), a data movement strategy must hold reused values in local registers or scratchpads rather than refetching them across the memory bus. [[01 Books/Volume I - Foundations/03 Optimization/11 Hardware Acceleration/V1-11.08 Hardware Mapping#^lst-matmul-data-movement-ad570267|Listing 14]] shows the canonical three-loop matrix multiplication, exposing the three distinct reuse streams across weights, inputs, and partial sums that a stationary schedule can pin on chip.

``` python
# Matrix multiplication where:
# weights: [512x256] - model parameters
# input:   [256x32]  - batch of activations
# Z:       [512x32]  - output activations

# Computing each output element Z[i,j]:
for i in range(512):
    for j in range(32):
        for k in range(256):
            Z[i, j] += weights[i, k] * input[k, j]
```

Listing 14: **Naïve Matrix-Multiplication Dataflow**: The loop nest revisits weight rows, input columns, and output accumulators, exposing the reuse that a mapping must capture in local memory.

^lst-matmul-data-movement-ad570267

Every operand in this loop nest is reused, but along a different loop. Each weight `weights[i, k]` is used once for each of the 32 output columns, each input `input[k, j]` is used once for each of the 512 output rows, and each output `Z[i, j]` accumulates 256 products before it is final. A processing element’s local storage holds only a small tile at a time, so the mapping cannot capture all three reuses at once. Fetching from DRAM whichever operand is not held locally, on every use, is what turns a compute-bound kernel into a memory-bound one ([[01 Books/Volume I - Foundations/03 Optimization/11 Hardware Acceleration/V1-11.16 Source References#^ref-han2016|Han et al. 2016]]).

The central dataflow decision is therefore which operand to keep local. The loop order answers it: whatever the innermost loops touch repeatedly without changing stays resident, and everything else streams. [[01 Books/Volume I - Foundations/03 Optimization/11 Hardware Acceleration/V1-11.08 Hardware Mapping#^lst-stationary-dataflows-70777c93|Listing 15]] reorders the same computation three ways, and the three orders are the three classic stationary dataflows.

``` python
# Weight stationary: hold one weight, stream inputs and partial sums past it
for i in range(512):
    for k in range(256):
        w = weights[i, k]  # fetched once, reused 32 times
        for j in range(32):
            Z[i, j] += w * input[k, j]

# Output stationary: hold one partial sum, stream weights and inputs past it
for i in range(512):
    for j in range(32):
        acc = 0  # stays in a local accumulator
        for k in range(256):
            acc += weights[i, k] * input[k, j]
        Z[i, j] = acc  # written once

# Input stationary: hold one input, stream weights and partial sums past it
for k in range(256):
    for j in range(32):
        x = input[k, j]  # fetched once, reused 512 times
        for i in range(512):
            Z[i, j] += weights[i, k] * x
```

Listing 15: **Three Stationary Dataflows**: The same matrix multiplication as the naïve loop, reordered three ways; the operand held fixed across the innermost loop is the one fetched once and reused from local storage, while the other two stream.

^lst-stationary-dataflows-70777c93

A weight-stationary dataflow keeps parameters on chip, so each weight crosses the expensive memory boundary once no matter how many inputs it touches. That pays off wherever one filter or weight tile is applied to many inputs, as in convolutions and batched matrix multiplication, and it is the canonical TPU dataflow traced cycle by cycle in [[01 Books/Volume I - Foundations/03 Optimization/11 Hardware Acceleration/V1-11.08 Hardware Mapping#^sec-hardware-acceleration-tiling-principle|section 11.7.5]]. The saved traffic reappears on the other operands: inputs must arrive fast enough to keep the resident weights busy, and partial sums must move between processing elements as they accumulate. The resident tile is also bounded by on-chip capacity, so a layer whose weights far exceed it must cycle weight tiles through, reloading the inputs for each one.

An output-stationary dataflow keeps each running sum in a local accumulator until all of its contributions have arrived, so every output is written to memory exactly once ([[01 Books/Volume I - Foundations/03 Optimization/11 Hardware Acceleration/V1-11.16 Source References#^ref-chen2016eyeriss|Chen et al. 2016]]). It suits layers with wide fan-in, where each output sums contributions from many inputs, such as fully connected layers and attention projections. In [[01 Books/Volume I - Foundations/03 Optimization/11 Hardware Acceleration/V1-11.08 Hardware Mapping#^lst-matmul-data-movement-ad570267|listing 14]] each output accumulates 256 products, so writing its partial sum out after every step would take 256 writes and rereads where an output-stationary dataflow needs one write. The cost is two operand streams at once, since both weights and inputs must now be delivered from memory. The naïve loop in [[01 Books/Volume I - Foundations/03 Optimization/11 Hardware Acceleration/V1-11.08 Hardware Mapping#^lst-matmul-data-movement-ad570267|listing 14]] already has this order, because its innermost loop runs over ${k}$ while ${i}$ and ${j}$ stay fixed.

An input-stationary dataflow keeps an activation tile local while weights stream past it. It pays off when one activation feeds several computations before eviction, as when a transformer token’s activation feeds the query, key, and value projections. Its costs mirror weight stationary’s, with weights now streaming from memory and partial sums still moving as they accumulate.

**Example 11.4: Loop ordering in a small convolution**

Consider a convolution applying 16 filters of size $3{\times}3$ to an $8{\times}8$ single-channel input. The computation can be expressed as five nested loops iterating over output rows (${H_{\text{out}}}$), output columns (${W_{\text{out}}}$), filter count (${d_{\text{out}}}$), filter height (${K_h}$), and filter width (${K_w}$). The $5! = 120$ permutations are an unconstrained upper bound: legal orderings preserve the mathematical result, although floating-point reduction order can change rounding, and they generate different memory traffic.

**Ordering A (weight-stationary)**: Place the filter loops (${d_{\text{out}}}$, ${K_h}$, ${K_w}$) outermost and the spatial loops (${H_{\text{out}}}$, ${W_{\text{out}}}$) innermost. Each $3{\times}3$ filter is loaded into registers once and then applied across all 36 output positions (a $6{\times}6$ output with no padding) before the next filter is loaded. Total weight loads: $16 \times 9 = 144$ values, each loaded exactly once.

**Ordering B (output-stationary, with filters that do not fit locally)**: Place the spatial loops outermost and the filter loops innermost. For every output position, all 16 filters must be loaded, applied, and their partial sums accumulated before advancing to the next position. If the register file cannot hold all 16 filters simultaneously, filters are repeatedly fetched from cache or DRAM. In the worst case, each of the 36 output positions reloads all 144 filter weights, producing $36 \times 144 = 5{,}184$ weight reads.

**Systems insight**: Under these two limiting assumptions, Ordering A reduces modeled weight reads by 36$\times$ relative to the worst case for Ordering B. Real kernels also account for activation and partial-sum traffic, register capacity, cache behavior, and vectorization. The example nevertheless shows why two mathematically equivalent loop orders can place widely different demands on the memory system. Ordering B loses here not because output-stationary is a poor dataflow but because this layer’s reuse sits in its small filters, which Ordering B cannot keep resident; in a wide fan-in layer such as that of [[01 Books/Volume I - Foundations/03 Optimization/11 Hardware Acceleration/V1-11.08 Hardware Mapping#^lst-matmul-data-movement-ad570267|listing 14]], the comparison can reverse when partial sums must leave the array and become the costly operand.

^exmp-hw-acceleration-loop-ordering-small-convolution

The three strategies form a decision framework rather than a quality ranking. The right choice is the operand with the highest reuse relative to the on-chip storage it would occupy, which depends on layer dimensions and batch size, so compilers select among them per layer. Convolution-specific designs add a fourth label, row-stationary. The Eyeriss accelerator maps one-dimensional rows of filter weights and input activations to processing-element registers and accumulates rows of partial sums locally, a hybrid that captures more of a convolution’s multidimensional reuse than fixing any single operand ([[01 Books/Volume I - Foundations/03 Optimization/11 Hardware Acceleration/V1-11.16 Source References#^ref-chen2016eyeriss|Chen et al. 2016]]).

### Tiling

^sec-hardware-acceleration-tiling-principle

A stationary dataflow decides which operand stays local; tiling decides how large a block of it local storage can hold. Blocks are needed at all because of a mismatch between the *computational graph* (which sees a single 4,096 $\times$ 4,096 matrix multiplication) and the *physical silicon* (which possesses a fixed 128 $\times$ 128 systolic array). Bridging this gap requires **tiling**, which partitions a large tensor operation into tiles that fit exactly into the hardware’s fast local memory (SRAM or scratchpad memory), so that each tile is reused many times before it is evicted.

↩︎ Tiling in its software form, the cache-aware blocking a framework applies to matrix multiplication, is established in **[[01 Books/Volume I - Foundations/02 Development/07 ML Frameworks/V1-07.06 Abstraction Tensors, Devices, and Operations#^sec-ml-frameworks-basic-numerical-operations-5516|Basic numerical operations]]**.

To process the 4,096-wide worked-example layer on a 128-wide systolic array, the compiler decomposes the result into 1,024 output tiles. Each output tile accumulates across 32 reduction-dimension tiles, so the complete matrix multiplication executes 32,768 ${\mathbf{A}}$-tile-by-${\mathbf{B}}$-tile products. This decomposition is a physical requirement imposed by memory hierarchy limits: operand tiles are fetched from high-capacity, high-latency HBM, staged in fast SRAM, and pulsed through the systolic array.

Tile dimensions balance on-chip capacity against arithmetic intensity. A larger tile reuses each loaded byte across more multiply-accumulate operations, raising operational intensity toward the compute-bound regime of the roofline model. The capacity ceiling is how much of ${\mathbf{A}}$, ${\mathbf{B}}$, and ${\mathbf{C}}$ fits simultaneously in fast on-chip memory. Tiling sustains high hardware efficiency $({\eta_{\text{hw}}})$ by ensuring that for every byte loaded from main memory, the data is reused 128× within the systolic grid.

When a layer dimension is not an exact multiple of the hardware tile size, the accelerator pays a *fringe penalty* in idle silicon. Consider a layer where only one output dimension expands from 128 to 129 while the other dimensions remain perfectly aligned to the 128-element tile. Processing that single extra column requires scheduling a second 128-wide tile across the systolic array, leaving 127 execution units idle during the fringe pass. Two tiles execute where one nearly sufficed, so the utilization factor along that dimension falls to $129/256$, or approximately 50 percent, creating the efficiency cliff illustrated in the margin figure. When multiple dimensions misalign simultaneously, these padding losses multiply across the tiled axes: a $129 \times 129$ output block partitioned into four $128 \times 128$ physical tiles yields a useful-area efficiency of $(129/256)^2$, or barely 25 percent. [Algorithm 1](https://mlsysbook.ai/vol1/11_hw_acceleration/11_hw_acceleration.html#algo-tiled-gemm) formalizes the resulting loop nest emitted by the compiler to orchestrate this on-chip staging and accumulation.

$\mathbf{A} \in \mathbb{R}^{M\times K}$

Once operand tiles reside in fast local SRAM, the hardware executes the tile product without further off-chip memory traffic. Systolic arrays achieve computational efficiency by routing operands through a structured 2D grid of processing elements (PEs) that communicate directly with adjacent neighbors across dedicated registers, bypassing shared-memory bus contention. In a weight-stationary design, the canonical TPU arrangement of [[01 Books/Volume I - Foundations/03 Optimization/11 Hardware Acceleration/V1-11.05 Compute Units#^fig-systolic-array|figure 5]], weights from the ${\mathbf{B}}$-tile are preloaded into local PE registers and held stationary. Input activations from the ${\mathbf{A}}$-tile stream horizontally across rows from left to right, while partial sums accumulate vertically down columns. On each clock cycle, every active PE multiplies its incoming activation by its stationary weight, adds the result to the incoming partial sum from above, and passes the activation rightward to its adjacent neighbor for the next cycle.

Because operands propagate one hop per cycle, synchronized execution requires an intentional time skew. Consider a minimal $2{\times}2$ tile product where output element ${C}_{ij} = {A}_{i0}{B}_{0j} + {A}_{i1}{B}_{1j}$ requires two products and one accumulation. The weight matrix ${\mathbf{B}}$ is held stationary across four PEs: $\text{PE}_{00}$ stores ${B}_{00}$, $\text{PE}_{01}$ stores ${B}_{01}$, $\text{PE}_{10}$ stores ${B}_{10}$, and $\text{PE}_{11}$ stores ${B}_{11}$. Activation ${A}_{i0}$ enters the top row and ${A}_{i1}$ the second row, because each row holds the weights for one reduction index.

The second row must start one cycle late, as a cycle-by-cycle trace of the first two outputs shows:

- **Cycle 0**: ${A}_{00}$ enters the top row at $\text{PE}_{00}$, which forms the partial product ${A}_{00}{B}_{00}$.
- **Cycle 1**: That partial product moves down to $\text{PE}_{10}$. That PE holds ${B}_{10}$, so the activation it needs is ${A}_{01}$, which must therefore enter the second row at $\text{PE}_{10}$ one cycle later than ${A}_{00}$ entered the first. In the same cycle, $\text{PE}_{10}$ adds ${A}_{01}{B}_{10}$ to the arriving partial sum and emits ${C}_{00}$. Meanwhile ${A}_{00}$ has moved right to $\text{PE}_{01}$, which forms the product ${A}_{00}{B}_{01}$.
- **Cycle 2**: That product moves down to $\text{PE}_{11}$ just as ${A}_{01}$ arrives there from $\text{PE}_{10}$. As a result, ${C}_{01}$ emerges one cycle after ${C}_{00}$.

The one-cycle delay is the skew, because without it the second row’s activation would arrive before the partial sum it must join. Inputs along each successive row and column are staggered in the same way, so operands meeting at any physical coordinate represent identical reduction indices.

Local reuse amortizes the initial fill and drain overhead: each loaded activation is reused across an entire row of ${N_{\text{tile}}}$ processing elements, while each stationary weight serves an entire sequence of input vectors before eviction. Sustained throughput, however, remains bounded by off-chip memory bandwidth whenever operand replenishment cannot keep pace with array retirement ([[01 Books/Volume I - Foundations/03 Optimization/11 Hardware Acceleration/V1-11.06 Memory Systems#^sec-hardware-acceleration-understanding-ai-memory-wall-3ea9|section 11.5.1]]).

#### Tiling Across the Memory Hierarchy

^sec-hardware-acceleration-memoryefficient-tiling-strategies-9fce

The systolic array is one instance of a general hierarchy rule. The same blocking that fits a graph-level matrix onto a fixed array, together with its fringe penalty and the loop structure of [algorithm 1](https://mlsysbook.ai/vol1/11_hw_acceleration/11_hw_acceleration.html#algo-tiled-gemm), recurs across registers, L1/shared memory, and L2 caches ([[01 Books/Volume I - Foundations/03 Optimization/11 Hardware Acceleration/V1-11.16 Source References#^ref-lam1991|Lam et al. 1991]]). At every tier of the memory hierarchy, tiling[[01 Books/Volume I - Foundations/03 Optimization/11 Hardware Acceleration/V1-11.17 Footnotes#^fn22|²²]] bounds the active working set so that each block is reused locally before eviction rather than refetched from a slower tier.

The naïve loop nest of [[01 Books/Volume I - Foundations/03 Optimization/11 Hardware Acceleration/V1-11.08 Hardware Mapping#^lst-matmul-data-movement-ad570267|listing 14]] shows the cost of leaving reuse to chance. Written as ${\mathbf{C}}= {\mathbf{A}}{\mathbf{B}}$, with the weights as ${\mathbf{A}}$ and the input batch as ${\mathbf{B}}$ (the reverse of the systolic trace above, where the stationary weights formed ${\mathbf{B}}$), it references the same elements of both operands again and again. Without tiling, those repeated accesses consume unnecessary bandwidth. Some references can hit in cache, but large matrices and an unfavorable traversal order can still cause excessive lower-tier traffic. Tiling partitions this multiplication across its three principal dimensions (${M}$, ${K}$, and ${N}$) ([[01 Books/Volume I - Foundations/03 Optimization/11 Hardware Acceleration/V1-11.08 Hardware Mapping#^fig-tiling-diagram|figure 13]]). Each highlighted region represents the working set staged in fast memory at one moment. An ${M_{\text{tile}}}{\times}{K_{\text{tile}}}$ block of ${\mathbf{A}}$ (within the green row band) multiplies a ${K_{\text{tile}}}{\times}{N_{\text{tile}}}$ block of ${\mathbf{B}}$ (within the pink column band) to accumulate one ${M_{\text{tile}}}{\times}{N_{\text{tile}}}$ output tile ($\text{Block}_{{m},n}$, shaded blue) of ${\mathbf{C}}$. Processing all inner-product operations for an active tile before evicting it amortizes off-chip DRAM latency across ${M_{\text{tile}}}\cdot {N_{\text{tile}}}\cdot {K_{\text{tile}}}$ arithmetic operations.

Maximizing register reuse within each tile amortizes slow off-chip DRAM accesses and keeps compute engines operating at peak throughput.

##### Tiling Fundamentals

^sec-hardware-acceleration-tiling-fundamentals-e9e6

In the untiled loop of [[01 Books/Volume I - Foundations/03 Optimization/11 Hardware Acceleration/V1-11.08 Hardware Mapping#^lst-matmul-data-movement-ad570267|listing 14]], each inner-loop step fetches ${A}_{ik}$ and ${B}_{kj}$ across an entire row of ${\mathbf{A}}$ and column of ${\mathbf{B}}$ to update ${C}_{ij}$ before advancing to the next column or row. Once ${N}$ grows large enough that a full row or column evicts earlier entries from local storage, the processor reloads values it touched moments earlier and pays the lower-tier latency and energy penalty on every miss ([[01 Books/Volume I - Foundations/03 Optimization/11 Hardware Acceleration/V1-11.16 Source References#^ref-horowitz2014|Horowitz 2014]]; [[01 Books/Volume I - Foundations/03 Optimization/11 Hardware Acceleration/V1-11.16 Source References#^ref-sze2017|Sze et al. 2017]]).

##### Performance Benefits of Tiling

^sec-hardware-acceleration-performance-benefits-tiling-e7bd

Splitting the three loops into outer tile-stepping loops and inner within-tile loops confines the working set to three `TILE_SIZE` $\times$ `TILE_SIZE` submatrices at a time. [[01 Books/Volume I - Foundations/03 Optimization/11 Hardware Acceleration/V1-11.08 Hardware Mapping#^lst-tiled-matmul-eff4c310|Listing 16]] shows this loop-blocking transformation in Python: the outer loops step in increments of `TILE_SIZE`, while the inner loops complete all $\text{TILE\_SIZE}^3$ multiply-accumulates for the active block before advancing.

``` python
TILE_SIZE = 32  # Choose a tile size based on hardware constraints

# Cache blocking: partition data via loop bounds.
# Loads are implicit through the hardware cache hierarchy.
for i in range(0, N, TILE_SIZE):
    for j in range(0, N, TILE_SIZE):
        for k in range(0, N, TILE_SIZE):
            # Each tile computed independently
            for ii in range(i, min(i + TILE_SIZE, N)):
                for jj in range(j, min(j + TILE_SIZE, N)):
                    for kk in range(k, min(k + TILE_SIZE, N)):
                        C[ii, jj] += A[ii, kk] * B[kk, jj]
```

Listing 16: **Cache-Blocked Matrix Multiplication**: This high-level loop blocking approach divides matrices into smaller index ranges so hardware caches can reuse data within each tile, improving computational efficiency without explicit scratchpad loads.

^lst-tiled-matmul-eff4c310

The restructuring produces a chain of effects. A small tile is revisited while it is still likely to be cached, so most operand references hit in L1/L2 or shared memory instead of DRAM. DRAM traffic falls as a result, and the processing units spend less time waiting for operands. When an untiled access pattern repeatedly fetches operands from lower memory tiers, that traffic can impose a bandwidth ceiling below the arithmetic ceiling.

##### Tiling Methods

^sec-hardware-acceleration-tiling-methods-6257

Tiling implementations differ along one axis, which is whether the hardware or the program decides what stays in fast memory. The two ends are implicit (cache) blocking and explicit (scratchpad) staging, and practical kernels often combine them.

Implicit (cache) blocking restructures only the loop bounds. The tiled matrix multiplication in [[01 Books/Volume I - Foundations/03 Optimization/11 Hardware Acceleration/V1-11.08 Hardware Mapping#^lst-tiled-matmul-eff4c310|listing 16]] issues no explicit loads; its tile-shaped access pattern lets hardware caches reuse nearby values before they are evicted. It suits CPUs and GPUs with capable caches and large tensors that exceed fast-memory capacity.

Explicit (scratchpad) staging copies each tile into shared memory, a scratchpad, or registers and reorganizes the computation around the staged copy. It is the only option on accelerators whose fast memory is software managed, and it guarantees reuse that a cache might lose to eviction. Convolution is the classic case, because staging a filter once lets it serve many input regions without being refetched. [[01 Books/Volume I - Foundations/03 Optimization/11 Hardware Acceleration/V1-11.08 Hardware Mapping#^lst-loop-blocking-4034f452|Listing 17]] loads blocks of ${\mathbf{A}}$ and ${\mathbf{B}}$ into temporary fast storage and reuses them across the inner loops. It includes shortened boundary tiles when ${N}$ is not divisible by `TILE_SIZE`, the software counterpart of the fringe penalty that a fixed-size array pays in idle processing elements.

``` python
# Explicit tile staging: load data into fast
# temporary storage before the inner loops.
for i in range(0, N, TILE_SIZE):
    for j in range(0, N, TILE_SIZE):
        for k in range(0, N, TILE_SIZE):
            # Pseudocode: map these tile objects to fast memory
            A_tile = A[i : i + TILE_SIZE, k : k + TILE_SIZE]
            B_tile = B[k : k + TILE_SIZE, j : j + TILE_SIZE]

            # Reuse loaded tiles for all inner iterations
            for ii in range(A_tile.shape[0]):
                for jj in range(B_tile.shape[1]):
                    for kk in range(A_tile.shape[1]):
                        C[i + ii, j + jj] += (
                            A_tile[ii, kk] * B_tile[kk, jj]
                        )
```

Listing 17: **Explicit Tile Staging Pseudocode**: A backend maps the temporary tile objects to fast storage and reuses them across inner-loop operations.

##### Tiling Challenges and Trade-Offs

^sec-hardware-acceleration-tiling-challenges-tradeoffs-e9c9

Tiling improves performance only when the tile matches the locality budget of the hardware. If the tile is too small, memory fetches still dominate execution time because reuse is too limited. If the tile is too large, it exceeds fast memory and causes cache thrashing or scratchpad spills. Selecting the right tile size therefore directly determines computational efficiency and memory bandwidth usage.

The capacity test counts every buffer that is live at the same time. For the tile dimensions in [algorithm 1](https://mlsysbook.ai/vol1/11_hw_acceleration/11_hw_acceleration.html#algo-tiled-gemm), the operand blocks contain ${M_{\text{tile}}} {K_{\text{tile}}}$ and ${K_{\text{tile}}} {N_{\text{tile}}}$ elements, while the output accumulator contains ${M_{\text{tile}}} {N_{\text{tile}}}$ elements. Suppose a hypothetical accelerator retains all three in one software-managed local store, uses FP16 operands and an FP32 accumulator, and loads only the current operand pair. Element storage size ${s_{\text{elem}}}$ is then 2 bytes for each operand and 4 bytes for each accumulator entry. After reserving space for other live state, the tile must satisfy

$$
(2\text{ bytes}){M_{\text{tile}}} {K_{\text{tile}}}+ (2\text{ bytes}){K_{\text{tile}}} {N_{\text{tile}}}+ (4\text{ bytes}){M_{\text{tile}}} {N_{\text{tile}}}\le \text{available local bytes}. \tag{6}
$$

 Keeping the output dimensions fixed while doubling the reduction tile doubles both operand buffers without changing the accumulator. Reject that larger candidate if the revised left-hand side of [[01 Books/Volume I - Foundations/03 Optimization/11 Hardware Acceleration/V1-11.08 Hardware Mapping#^eq-hw-tile-capacity|equation 6]] exceeds the available capacity, even if its longer reduction amortizes accumulator handling over more products. Double buffering has the same operand-capacity effect at fixed tile dimensions: retaining the next ${\mathbf{A}}$ and ${\mathbf{B}}$ tiles while computing the current pair requires a second copy of each operand buffer, while the running accumulator remains single buffered.

^eq-hw-tile-capacity

Actual storage tiers must be budgeted separately. A GPU kernel may stage operand tiles in shared memory while keeping output accumulators in thread registers; unused register capacity cannot make an oversized shared-memory allocation fit. Its shared-memory test counts the staged operand copies and any other shared state, while its register test counts accumulators, operand fragments, and temporary values across the participating threads. Passing both tests establishes capacity feasibility. It does not establish speed: alignment, bank conflicts (threads contending for the same bank of shared memory, which serializes their accesses), occupancy, synchronization, and measured traffic still determine whether the tile improves execution.

The tile choice also controls load balance. In architectures such as GPUs and TPUs, computations execute in parallel across thousands of processing units. If tiles are not evenly distributed, some units remain idle while others are overloaded, leading to suboptimal utilization of computational resources. Effective tile scheduling keeps parallel execution balanced and efficient.

Data movement remains a cost after tiling, even when another resource becomes the throughput limit. Although tiling reduces the number of slow memory accesses, transferring tiles between hierarchy levels still incurs latency and energy cost, especially when data falls from cache or scratchpad back to DRAM. Efficient memory prefetching and scheduling strategies minimize this residual movement and ensure that data is available when needed.

Hybrid tiling combines implicit blocking and explicit staging when neither alone captures the workload’s reuse, for example relying on caches for an outer tier while staging convolution weights explicitly in a scratchpad. Register blocking, double buffering, and hierarchical tiling extend the same locality principle at smaller and larger memory tiers. AI compilers and runtime systems such as TensorFlow XLA, TVM, and MLIR automatically select these tiling strategies based on hardware constraints, enabling fine-tuned performance optimization without manual intervention. [[01 Books/Volume I - Foundations/03 Optimization/11 Hardware Acceleration/V1-11.08 Hardware Mapping#^tbl-tiling-strategies|Table 12]] compares implicit, explicit, and hybrid tiling.

| Aspect                | **Implicit (Cache) Blocking**                                            | **Explicit (Scratchpad) Staging**                                      | **Hybrid tiling**                                              |
|:----------------------|:-------------------------------------------------------------------------|:-----------------------------------------------------------------------|:---------------------------------------------------------------|
| Who Decides Residency | Hardware cache replacement policy                                        | The program, through explicit copies into software-managed memory      | Caches for some tiers, explicit copies for others              |

AI compilers and runtime systems use these techniques to automatically optimize model execution on diverse hardware.

### Memory-Efficient Tensor Layouts

^sec-hardware-acceleration-memoryefficient-tensor-layouts-e250

Stationary dataflows ([[01 Books/Volume I - Foundations/03 Optimization/11 Hardware Acceleration/V1-11.08 Hardware Mapping#^sec-hardware-acceleration-data-movement-patterns-3b06|section 11.7.4]]) and tiling determine which operands stay close to compute units and in what blocks; tensor layouts determine whether those operands can be accessed efficiently once they arrive. A well-chosen weight-stationary schedule still stalls if weights are laid out in a format that produces strided, non-coalesced memory fetches. Tensor layout is a kernel requirement. The physical ordering of multidimensional data in linear memory must match the memory access stride expected by the accelerator’s execution units, or the processor pays in memory stalls, cache underutilization, and redundant bus transactions.

Hardware accelerators fetch data from device memory in fixed-size transactions, typically 32 to 128 bytes per transaction. When compute units request non-contiguous addresses, each transaction delivers only a fraction of useful payload, degrading effective memory bandwidth. Aligning tensor layout with the execution model lets concurrent threads or SIMD lanes access nearby aligned locations in fewer transactions; the count depends on access width and device granularity ([[01 Books/Volume I - Foundations/03 Optimization/11 Hardware Acceleration/V1-11.16 Source References#^ref-nvidia2021cudnn|NVIDIA Corporation 2021]]).

Machine learning frameworks and compilers often manage this layout selection automatically. Optimization tools such as cuDNN for NVIDIA GPUs, XLA for TensorFlow and JAX, and MLIR-based compiler stacks transform tensor layouts as they lower operations to backend-specific kernels ([[01 Books/Volume I - Foundations/03 Optimization/11 Hardware Acceleration/V1-11.16 Source References#^ref-nvidia2021cudnn|NVIDIA Corporation 2021]]; [[01 Books/Volume I - Foundations/03 Optimization/11 Hardware Acceleration/V1-11.16 Source References#^ref-GoogleXLA|Google 2025]]; [[01 Books/Volume I - Foundations/03 Optimization/11 Hardware Acceleration/V1-11.16 Source References#^ref-lattner2021|Lattner et al. 2020]]). In high-level frameworks, layout transformations are typically applied transparently, but developers authoring custom kernels or targeting low-level APIs such as CUDA, Metal, or OpenCL must manage tensor formats directly.

**[[01 Books/Volume I - Foundations/02 Development/07 ML Frameworks/V1-07.06 Abstraction Tensors, Devices, and Operations#^sec-ml-frameworks-tensor-structure-dimensions-4a14|Rank, layout, and data type]]** introduced strides and the copy that makes a strided view contiguous; in PyTorch it is the distinction between `tensor.permute()` and `tensor.contiguous()` ([[01 Books/Volume I - Foundations/03 Optimization/11 Hardware Acceleration/V1-11.16 Source References#^ref-pytorch2026tensorviews|PyTorch Contributors 2026]]). TensorFlow, for its part, defaults to channels-last (NHWC) for convolutional models on NVIDIA GPUs, warning that NCHW inputs introduce automatic transpose overhead when lowered to GPU kernels ([[01 Books/Volume I - Foundations/03 Optimization/11 Hardware Acceleration/V1-11.16 Source References#^ref-tensorflow2024profiler|TensorFlow Developers 2024]]). Hardware-optimized libraries such as cuDNN on GPUs and oneDNN on CPUs implement tailored memory formats to maximize cache line utilization and SIMD throughput. The governing principle is to treat memory layout as an intrinsic property of the backend execution path: the fastest tensor format is the one that minimizes layout conversion overhead and makes memory accesses contiguous across hardware execution units.

#### Channels-Last Layout

^sec-hardware-acceleration-rowmajor-layout-741f

In an NHWC channels-last layout, the channel index varies fastest in a contiguous tensor, placing the channel values for each spatial coordinate adjacent in memory. The label names the order in which dimensions are laid out in memory, from slowest-varying to fastest-varying, so NCHW and NHWC hold the same values in different linear orders.

Consider an RGB image tensor of shape $(\text{Height}, \text{Width}, \text{Channels}) = (3, 3, 3)$. The elements ${X}({h},{w},{c})$ are laid out in linear memory as follows:

$$
\begin{gather*} X(0,0,0), X(0,0,1), X(0,0,2), X(0,1,0), X(0,1,1), \\ X(0,1,2), X(0,2,0), X(0,2,1), X(0,2,2), \ldots \end{gather*}
$$

Because all three dimensions have size 3, the listing shows which index changes fastest, not which dimension is longest. The last index, the channel, cycles through 0, 1, and 2 first; the middle index, the column, advances next; and the row index changes last. All channel values for a given pixel are therefore adjacent, and memory addresses advance across width before advancing down height. This layout supports vectorized execution when a compute kernel processes multiple channels simultaneously: CPU SIMD registers and GPU Tensor Cores can load contiguous channel vectors directly without intra-register shuffling.

The advantage of channels-last storage depends on the target operator and hardware unit. Modern Tensor Core kernels in cuDNN favor NHWC because a convolution lowered to a matrix multiplication sums over its input channels. The channel dimension becomes the reduction dimension of the matrix multiplication, and keeping it contiguous lets the matrix unit load its operand tiles directly. When kernels require channel-contiguous inputs, passing an incompatible format forces the framework runtime to insert an explicit memory transpose, incurring a memory bandwidth penalty before computation begins.

#### Channels-First Layout

^sec-hardware-acceleration-channelmajor-layout-d6a9

In a contiguous NCHW channels-first layout, the spatial width index varies fastest, and all spatial values across an entire channel are stored contiguously before the next channel begins. Accelerators process data in parallel across thread warps or SIMD lanes; when consecutive threads in a warp access consecutive memory addresses, the memory controller coalesces those requests into fewer memory transactions (**memory coalescing**).[[01 Books/Volume I - Foundations/03 Optimization/11 Hardware Acceleration/V1-11.17 Footnotes#^fn23|²³]]

Consider the same RGB image tensor with dimensions reordered in channels-first order as $(\text{Channels}, \text{Height}, \text{Width}) = (3, 3, 3)$. Retaining ${X}({h},{w},{c})$ as semantic coordinate notation, the data are stored in channels-first order as follows:

$$
\begin{gather*} X(0,0,0), X(0,1,0), X(0,2,0), X(1,0,0), X(1,1,0), X(1,2,0), \ldots, \\ X(0,0,1), X(0,1,1), X(0,2,1), \ldots, X(0,0,2), X(0,1,2), X(0,2,2), \ldots \end{gather*}
$$

Here the column index varies fastest, then the row index, and the channel index changes last. In this format, all red channel values for the entire image are stored first, followed by all green values, and then all blue values. This arrangement allows kernels that parallelize across spatial coordinates $({H}, {W})$ to issue coalesced reads along image rows, which suited earlier GPU convolution algorithms and SIMD execution pipelines ([[01 Books/Volume I - Foundations/03 Optimization/11 Hardware Acceleration/V1-11.16 Source References#^ref-chetlur2014cudnn|Chetlur et al. 2014]]).

The relative advantage of NCHW versus NHWC depends on how the underlying kernel maps computation to hardware threads. Convolutional layers evaluate filters across spatial and channel dimensions simultaneously. In classic direct or FFT-based convolutions, channels-first ordering enabled efficient spatial sliding without stride jumps. However, modern GEMM-lowered convolutions and Tensor Core pipelines map channel reduction directly to matrix multiply accumulators, favoring channels-last structures. When a model operates in NCHW but invokes a Tensor Core-optimized backend, the runtime must either run an unoptimized fallback kernel or dynamically transpose the tensor into NHWC, burning memory bandwidth in both cases.[[01 Books/Volume I - Foundations/03 Optimization/11 Hardware Acceleration/V1-11.17 Footnotes#^fn24|²⁴]]

#### Comparing Channels-Last and Channels-First Layouts

^sec-hardware-acceleration-comparing-rowmajor-channelmajor-layouts-e410

Both channels-last (NHWC) and channels-first (NCHW) layouts address specific hardware access patterns, with their efficiency determined by whether a kernel parallelizes over spatial coordinates or vectorizes across channel dimensions. Layout choice directly governs cache line utilization, memory bus transaction count, and compute unit occupancy. [[01 Books/Volume I - Foundations/03 Optimization/11 Hardware Acceleration/V1-11.08 Hardware Mapping#^tbl-major|Table 13]] contrasts the performance trade-offs and hardware compatibility between these two approaches.

| Feature                     | **Channels-Last (NHWC)**                                       | **Channels-First (NCHW)**                                      |
|:----------------------------|:---------------------------------------------------------------|:---------------------------------------------------------------|
| **Memory storage order**    | Pixels are stored row-by-row, channel interleaved              | All values for a given channel are stored together first       |
| Best for                    | CPU loops, element-wise operations, many channels-last kernels | Many legacy GPU convolution paths and channel-first model code |
| **Cache efficiency**        | High cache locality for sequential row/channel-last access     | Can improve coalescing for channel-first kernels               |
| **Convolution performance** | Often preferred by modern Tensor Core convolution/fusion paths | Efficient for older FP32 cuDNN paths and direct convolutions   |
| **Memory fetching**         | Good when kernels vectorize over adjacent channel data         | Good when kernels process channel-major tiles                  |
| **Default in frameworks**   | Common TensorFlow convention; PyTorch channels-last option     | Common PyTorch tensor shape convention                         |

Table 13: **Data Layout Strategies**: Channels-last (NHWC) and channels-first (NCHW) layouts optimize memory access patterns for different backend kernels. NHWC often suits CPU vectorization and modern Tensor Core convolution and fusion paths, while NCHW remains the default PyTorch tensor shape and suits older FP32 GPU kernel paths. Choosing the appropriate layout directly impacts performance by maximizing cache utilization and memory bandwidth efficiency.

^tbl-major

While compilers such as XLA, TensorRT, and PyTorch Inductor automate layout propagation and insert layout conversions when beneficial, format conversions are not free. Transposing a tensor requires reading every element from memory and writing it back in a different order, which consumes memory bandwidth without executing useful arithmetic. In production deployments, matching the model’s memory layout to the accelerator’s native kernel format eliminates these layout transformations entirely. Even when tensor layout is perfectly aligned with the hardware, however, executing operations as isolated kernels introduces a separate bottleneck: intermediate values must be exposed through global-memory buffers between operations, even when caches absorb some physical traffic. Resolving that bottleneck requires changing how operations are scheduled rather than how tensors are laid out.

### Kernel Fusion on the Accelerator

^sec-hardware-acceleration-kernel-fusion-7faf

At 2.0 FLOP/byte, the standalone LayerNorm kernel of notebook [11.8](https://mlsysbook.ai/vol1/11_hw_acceleration/11_hw_acceleration.html#nbk-hw-acceleration-layernorm-analysis) can reach at most 6.7 TFLOP/s, about 0.7 percent of the H100’s tensor peak, no matter how its internal arithmetic or tensor layout is scheduled. Kernel fusion[[01 Books/Volume I - Foundations/03 Optimization/11 Hardware Acceleration/V1-11.17 Footnotes#^fn25|²⁵]] removes that bottleneck by combining successive operations into a single kernel so intermediate tensors stay in registers or on-chip SRAM instead of making round-trips through device memory. On an accelerator, the two engineering questions are which intermediate transfers fusion eliminates from the memory bus and what the combined kernel costs in register pressure and occupancy (the warps resident at once).

↩︎ Kernel fusion (also called operator fusion), with its LayerNorm, dropout, and ReLU example and the traffic count for unfused versus fused chains, is established in **[[01 Books/Volume I - Foundations/02 Development/07 ML Frameworks/V1-07.04 Execution from Graphs to Kernels#^sec-ml-frameworks-execution-strategy-matters-memory-wall-1ce8|The memory wall and kernel fusion]]**.

The traffic case for fusion carries over unchanged, since each intermediate that stays on chip saves one write to device memory and one read back; for a chain of three operations that cuts idealized external traffic by roughly 3$\times$. On an accelerator the saving matters most for elementwise operators, whose arithmetic intensity sits far below one FLOP per byte, so moving each intermediate across the memory bus takes longer than computing it ([[01 Books/Volume I - Foundations/03 Optimization/11 Hardware Acceleration/V1-11.16 Source References#^ref-Jia2019|Jia et al. 2019]]). Fused execution also shortens the lifetime of intermediate buffers, although an optimizing runtime can often reuse those buffers anyway. What fusion guarantees is the removed write and read for each eliminated intermediate, and how much of that removed traffic would have reached DRAM depends on cache behavior.

#### Performance Benefits and Constraints

^sec-hardware-acceleration-performance-benefits-constraints-1b74

Because high-bandwidth memory is the scarce resource on GPUs and TPUs, eliminating redundant memory transactions translates directly into higher compute utilization ([[01 Books/Volume I - Foundations/03 Optimization/11 Hardware Acceleration/V1-11.16 Source References#^ref-NVIDIA2020|NVIDIA Corporation 2020a]]). Not all operations can be fused arbitrarily, however. Element-wise operations and frozen inference normalization are strong candidates because their computations do not require batch-wide reductions, whereas training-mode batch normalization computes batch statistics and is not purely element-wise. A fused kernel carries each element through the whole chain in one pass. A reduction can break that pass, because its consumer cannot start until every contributing element has been read. Matrix multiplications and convolutions constrain fusion further because they involve reductions and large data movement; compilers routinely fuse them with element-wise epilogues, the final steps a kernel applies before writing its output, such as bias or activation, yet cannot freely merge them with unrelated global operations.

Even when two operators are algebraically compatible, register pressure bounds how aggressively they should be fused. Keeping intermediate values in registers eliminates redundant memory writes, but if a fused kernel exceeds the available registers per thread, the compiler spills excess values to per-thread local memory backed by device DRAM, adding latency that can negate the benefits of fusion. On GPUs, where occupancy is bounded by the register file, excessive fusion can also starve parallelism. Fusion therefore trades on-chip capacity for removed traffic, and the trade has a break-even. Each intermediate the fused kernel holds costs registers in every thread; once those registers lower occupancy below what is needed to hide memory latency, or force values to spill, the kernel gives back some of the time it saved. A fusion that spills as many bytes to device memory as it removes has saved no traffic, although fewer launches can still save time, so the useful test is the net change in execution time at an occupancy that still keeps the units busy.

Different accelerators and compilers therefore strike different balances. NVIDIA GPUs favor warp-level parallelism where element-wise epilogue fusion is straightforward ([[01 Books/Volume I - Foundations/03 Optimization/11 Hardware Acceleration/V1-11.16 Source References#^ref-NVIDIA2020|NVIDIA Corporation 2020a]]). TPUs prioritize systolic-array execution coupled to vector post-processing ([[01 Books/Volume I - Foundations/03 Optimization/11 Hardware Acceleration/V1-11.16 Source References#^ref-jouppi2017|Jouppi et al. 2017]]). Compiler stacks such as TVM, XLA, TensorRT, and MLIR use graph rewrites and engine-building heuristics to weigh memory savings against register and occupancy limits ([[01 Books/Volume I - Foundations/03 Optimization/11 Hardware Acceleration/V1-11.16 Source References#^ref-chen2018tvm|Chen et al. 2018]]; [[01 Books/Volume I - Foundations/03 Optimization/11 Hardware Acceleration/V1-11.16 Source References#^ref-GoogleXLA|Google 2025]]; [[01 Books/Volume I - Foundations/03 Optimization/11 Hardware Acceleration/V1-11.16 Source References#^ref-nvidia2024tensorrt|NVIDIA 2024b]]; [[01 Books/Volume I - Foundations/03 Optimization/11 Hardware Acceleration/V1-11.16 Source References#^ref-lattner2021|Lattner et al. 2020]]). When debugging performance regressions or modifying models frequently, frameworks also allow developers to disable fusion selectively so that memory savings, register usage, and hardware execution constraints can be evaluated independently.

### Applying Mapping Strategies to Neural Networks

^sec-hardware-acceleration-applying-mapping-strategies-neural-networks-3110

Convolutional networks, transformers, and multilayer perceptrons stress the memory hierarchy in different places ([[01 Books/Volume I - Foundations/03 Optimization/11 Hardware Acceleration/V1-11.06 Memory Systems#^sec-hardware-acceleration-model-memory-pressure-f95e|section 11.5.5]]), so each model family requires a different combination of stationary dataflow, tiling, tensor layout, and kernel fusion. Mapping is a systematic search over loop permutations, tiling factors, and stationarity policies that maximizes data reuse under hard SRAM and register constraints. Because each family presents a distinct ratio of weights to activations and differing opportunities for temporal locality, optimal mapping policies diverge across workloads. [[01 Books/Volume I - Foundations/03 Optimization/11 Hardware Acceleration/V1-11.08 Hardware Mapping#^tbl-mapping-strategies|Table 14]] consolidates how dataflow, memory layout, kernel fusion, and tiling align with these three architectural archetypes.

#### Convolutional Neural Networks (ResNet-50)

^sec-hardware-acceleration-convolutional-neural-networks-resnet50-3eb6

For ResNet-50 and similar CNNs, a key mapping opportunity is spatial weight reuse. A small filter is applied across many spatial locations, so its weights participate in many multiply-accumulate operations. Weight-stationary mappings can capture that reuse by retaining filter tiles locally, while row-stationary or output-stationary mappings may better balance activation and partial-sum traffic on other architectures. Favorable convolution shapes can achieve high arithmetic intensity, which makes them well suited to matrix and systolic execution paths. The model family does not determine one universal dataflow: layer shape, batch size, local-storage capacity, and the target’s supported kernels decide which reuse pattern is worth retaining.

^tbl-mapping-strategies

This spatial regularity also enables aggressive fusion and tiling. For inference with frozen BatchNorm statistics, compilers can fold or fuse convolution, normalization, and activation to avoid unnecessary intermediate writes. Training-mode BatchNorm requires reductions over batch statistics and does not have the same element-wise structure that permits fusion. Spatial tiling partitions the feature map into subregions sized to fit within on-chip SRAM, so the fused kernel processes each tile from fast memory before moving to the next.

#### Transformer Architectures (GPT-2/Llama)

^sec-hardware-acceleration-transformer-architectures-gpt2llama-dd9a

Transformer mapping depends on execution phase. During training and prefill, attention creates large intermediate matrices unless a tiled implementation avoids materializing them; during autoregressive decoding, attention reads the growing key-value (KV) cache. Each new query vector must access the stored key and value pairs across the preceding sequence. As sequences grow, the full KV cache usually lives in HBM or device DRAM, while attention kernels tile active blocks through SRAM, registers, or shared memory. Those large intermediate matrices are why fused attention kernels such as FlashAttention ([[01 Books/Volume I - Foundations/03 Optimization/11 Hardware Acceleration/V1-11.16 Source References#^ref-dao2022|Dao et al. 2022]]), the IO-aware attention of **[[01 Books/Volume I - Foundations/02 Development/08 Model Training/V1-08.07 Pipeline Optimizations#^sec-model-training-flash-attention-ioaware-attention-optimization-3da0|FlashAttention: IO-aware attention optimization]]**, deliver large gains during training and prefill; the mapping keeps each attention tile in on-chip SRAM and preserves the $\mathcal{O}({S}^2)$ computation.

#### Multilayer Perceptrons and DLRM

^sec-hardware-acceleration-multilayer-perceptrons-dlrm-7c0d

MLPs present the most straightforward mapping problem because their computation reduces largely to dense GEMM.[[01 Books/Volume I - Foundations/03 Optimization/11 Hardware Acceleration/V1-11.17 Footnotes#^fn26|²⁶]] Each fully connected layer multiplies an activation matrix by a weight matrix. The weight matrix is fixed across samples, so batching reuses each loaded weight across more activations and raises arithmetic intensity. A batch size of one provides little cross-sample weight reuse and often leaves wide matrix engines underused, while larger batches can move execution toward the compute-bound regime.

↪ How GEMM arithmetic intensity scales with matrix dimension is collected for reference in **[[05 Reference/Volume I - Foundations/V1 Algorithm Foundations#^sec-appdx-algorithm-foundations-general-matrix-multiply-gemm-b55d|General matrix multiply (GEMM)]]**.

Because MLP layers are typically followed by activation functions and bias additions, GEMM fusion combines these steps into a single kernel, avoiding intermediate memory writes. Blocked tiling partitions the large matrix multiplications into sub-blocks sized for the accelerator’s shared memory, ensuring high cache utilization throughout computation. The simplicity of the MLP mapping, dominated by a single primitive with predictable access patterns, is precisely why hardware vendors optimize GEMM libraries so aggressively: gains in GEMM performance translate directly to MLP throughput. DLRM is not purely an MLP workload, however. Its large sparse embedding tables and feature-interaction stage can dominate capacity and memory traffic, so the dense mapping described here applies to its bottom and top MLP towers rather than to the entire model ([[01 Books/Volume I - Foundations/03 Optimization/11 Hardware Acceleration/V1-11.16 Source References#^ref-naumov2019deep|Naumov et al. 2019]]).

### Hybrid Mapping Strategies

^sec-hardware-acceleration-hybrid-mapping-strategies-3e8c

Production models rarely consist of a single layer type. A vision transformer, for example, combines a convolutional patch-embedding stage, self-attention layers, and dense MLP blocks within a single forward pass ([[01 Books/Volume I - Foundations/03 Optimization/11 Hardware Acceleration/V1-11.16 Source References#^ref-dosovitskiy2021image|Dosovitskiy et al. 2021]]). Those layers create different reuse patterns: the embedding stage can benefit from weight-stationary mapping, attention emphasizes activation movement and tiling, and MLP blocks demand blocked GEMM tiling and fusion. Because no single dataflow strategy is optimal across all three stages, hardware mapping in practice is hybrid and layer-specific.

Hybrid mapping addresses this heterogeneity by allowing the accelerator to switch strategies at layer boundaries. Each layer presents a different balance of compute intensity, data reuse, and memory access pattern, and the optimal mapping must shift accordingly ([[01 Books/Volume I - Foundations/03 Optimization/11 Hardware Acceleration/V1-11.16 Source References#^ref-sze2017|Sze et al. 2017]]). Rather than committing to one dataflow for the entire model, hybrid approaches consider weight stationary execution when weights can be reused locally, input-stationary execution when activation reuse justifies its storage, and output stationary execution when partial-sum traffic is costly. Layer dimensions and available local memory determine which candidate pays off.

TPU-style systolic arrays, NVIDIA GPUs, and tile-based accelerator designs expose different combinations of local memory, tensor layouts, fusion, and scheduling controls. Compilers and runtimes can therefore choose layer-specific strategies rather than one global dataflow for the whole model ([[01 Books/Volume I - Foundations/03 Optimization/11 Hardware Acceleration/V1-11.16 Source References#^ref-jouppi2023|Jouppi et al. 2023]]; [[01 Books/Volume I - Foundations/03 Optimization/11 Hardware Acceleration/V1-11.16 Source References#^ref-NVIDIA2020|NVIDIA Corporation 2020a]]; [[01 Books/Volume I - Foundations/03 Optimization/11 Hardware Acceleration/V1-11.16 Source References#^ref-chen2018tvm|Chen et al. 2018]]). These implementations depend on programmable memory hierarchies, efficient interconnects, and specialized execution pipelines. However, hybrid mapping remains a design-time optimization: in production workloads, execution conditions change dynamically due to varying input sizes, memory contention, and hardware resource availability. Machine learning compilers and runtime systems extend these static mapping choices by introducing dynamic scheduling, memory optimizations, and automatic tuning, ensuring that deep learning workloads operate efficiently across diverse accelerators and deployment environments.
