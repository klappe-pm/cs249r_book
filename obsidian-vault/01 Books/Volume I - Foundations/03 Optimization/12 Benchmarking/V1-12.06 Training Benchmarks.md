# V1-12.06 Training Benchmarks

[[Home|Home]] · [[01 Books/Volume I - Foundations/Volume 1 Overview|Volume Overview]] · [[01 Books/Volume I - Foundations/03 Optimization/12 Benchmarking/V1-12 Benchmarking|V1-12 Benchmarking]]

^sec-benchmarking-training-benchmarks-96da

Training and inference fail in opposite ways on the same accelerator. A training run stalls when synchronizing gradients across accelerators saturates the interconnect; a serving deployment violates its SLO when request bursts build a queue. The two regimes also measure different resources. Training iteratively refines parameters over billions of samples and must hold parameters, gradients, optimizer state, and the activations retained for the backward pass, a footprint that can far exceed the weights alone ([[01 Books/Volume I - Foundations/03 Optimization/12 Benchmarking/V1-12.02 The Benchmark–Production Gap#^tbl-benchmarking-lighthouse-footprints|table 1]] gives ResNet-50’s). Its figure of merit is wall-clock time to a target accuracy. Inference evaluates fixed parameters against arriving queries, discards the backward graph and optimizer state, and is judged by latency percentiles and throughput under bursty load. A training result and an inference result on the same accelerator therefore measure different resources and cannot stand in for each other, which is why MLPerf maintains separate Training and Inference suites ([[01 Books/Volume I - Foundations/03 Optimization/12 Benchmarking/V1-12.14 Source References#^ref-mattson2020mlperf|Mattson et al. 2020]]; [[01 Books/Volume I - Foundations/03 Optimization/12 Benchmarking/V1-12.14 Source References#^ref-reddi2020|Reddi et al. 2019]]). This chapter measures training on ResNet-50 and serving on MobileNetV2 ([[01 Books/Volume I - Foundations/03 Optimization/12 Benchmarking/V1-12.07 Inference Benchmarks#^sec-benchmarking-inference-benchmarks-2c1f|section 12.6]]).

↩︎ The training memory budget of weights, gradients, optimizer state, and retained activations is established in **[[01 Books/Volume I - Foundations/02 Development/08 Model Training/V1-08.04 Computation and Memory Costs of Training#^sec-model-training-optimizer-memory-training-loop-4383|Reducing optimizer memory]]**.

Because training couples per-step computation with gradient communication and multi-gigabyte state, a team that purchases a larger GPU cluster expecting proportional training-speed gains can discover after procurement that interconnect stalls and memory bottlenecks swallow much of the speedup. Training benchmarks exist to catch that gap before capital is committed. [[01 Books/Volume I - Foundations/03 Optimization/12 Benchmarking/V1-12.06 Training Benchmarks#^tbl-training-metrics|Table 12]] collects the metrics they report in six groups, from time and throughput through reproducibility. One of those measurements is the outcome, the wall-clock time to reach a verified quality target, and the rest diagnose what paced or interrupted the run.

**Definition 12.3: ML training benchmarks**

***ML training benchmarks*** are machine learning system benchmarks that measure the time to reach a target quality metric (for example, a specified validation accuracy or loss threshold) on a fixed dataset and model, quantifying the rate of convergence per unit of resource.

1.  **Significance**: Training benchmarks reveal large gaps invisible to hardware specs. Holding the model and quality target fixed, the time to convergence can vary widely across hardware-software stacks because training performance depends on the full pipeline: data loading $({D_{\text{vol}}}/{\text{BW}})$, compute utilization $({\eta_{\text{hw}}})$, gradient synchronization $({\text{BW}}, {L_{\text{lat}}})$, and fault recovery overhead. A peak FLOP/s spec sheet captures none of these interactions.
2.  **Distinction**: Unlike inference benchmarks, which measure per-query latency and throughput under load, training benchmarks measure time-to-accuracy across the full optimization loop: data loading, forward pass, backward pass, gradient synchronization, and optimizer step. As scale increases, communication $({\text{BW}})$ can displace compute $({R_{\text{peak}}})$ as the limiting constraint; profiling establishes the transition for a particular workload.
3.  **Common pitfall**: A frequent misconception is that training benchmarks measure “how fast the GPU runs.” At large scale, interconnect bandwidth $({\text{BW}})$ for gradient synchronization and fault tolerance overhead (checkpoint I/O, straggler mitigation) often dominate the benchmark result more than peak FLOP/s.

Because training represents the largest capital expenditure in many ML organizations, only time-to-accuracy measurement reveals whether added accelerators and faster kernels deliver proportional value rather than dissipating into scaling inefficiencies, input stalls, or communication overhead. Standardized suites track those gains, and the core training metrics behind them (time-to-accuracy, scaling efficiency, utilization, energy, and checkpoint overhead) can all be measured on one machine with one to eight accelerators.

### Training Benchmark Motivation

^sec-benchmarking-training-benchmark-motivation-f365

MLPerf Training ([[01 Books/Volume I - Foundations/03 Optimization/12 Benchmarking/V1-12.14 Source References#^ref-mattson2020mlperf|Mattson et al. 2020]]; [[01 Books/Volume I - Foundations/03 Optimization/12 Benchmarking/V1-12.14 Source References#^ref-mlperf-training-website-2f25e5be|MLCommons 2024c]]) provides the standardized framework for this time-to-quality measurement. The longitudinal scaling curves in [[01 Books/Volume I - Foundations/03 Optimization/12 Benchmarking/V1-12.06 Training Benchmarks#^fig-mlperf-training-improve|figure 7]] track cumulative relative performance gains across nine model workloads between 2018 and 2024 against the orange Moore’s Law baseline. Every workload curve outpaces cumulative transistor scaling (which reaches 6.6$\times$ by mid-2024), led by Mask R-CNN climbing to 48.8$\times$ and ResNet reaching 34$\times$ as distributed scaling and compiler optimizations compound hardware improvements ([[01 Books/Volume I - Foundations/03 Optimization/12 Benchmarking/V1-12.14 Source References#^ref-tschand2025|Tschand et al. 2024]]). This trajectory documents improvements in the full hardware-software system beyond transistor scaling alone.

Figure 7: **MLPerf Training Progress**: Workload lines report cumulative relative performance across benchmark versions against the plotted cumulative Moore’s Law baseline. Every workload’s latest point exceeds the baseline at the same date, and Mask R-CNN reaches the largest plotted gain at 48.8$\times$; the comparison captures combined hardware and software progress, not hardware scaling alone. Source: ([[01 Books/Volume I - Foundations/03 Optimization/12 Benchmarking/V1-12.14 Source References#^ref-tschand2025|Tschand et al. 2024]]).

^fig-mlperf-training-improve

The gap between the 6.6$\times$ transistor baseline and the 34$\times$ to 48.8$\times$ workload curves in [[01 Books/Volume I - Foundations/03 Optimization/12 Benchmarking/V1-12.06 Training Benchmarks#^fig-mlperf-training-improve|figure 7]] came from exposing and removing bottlenecks that raw silicon TFLOP/s never show, including slow data loading, underutilized accelerators, excessive memory overhead, and communication stalls that erode scaling efficiency. Advertised hardware capability becomes a training speedup only to the extent that an end-to-end training benchmark verifies it under realistic conditions.

Training benchmarks therefore serve four functions. They compare accelerators and frameworks on standardized tasks, which guides hardware selection and quantifies software optimizations such as mixed-precision training[[01 Books/Volume I - Foundations/03 Optimization/12 Benchmarking/V1-12.15 Footnotes#^fn16|¹⁶]] and memory-efficient data loading. They test scalability, because added accelerators should reduce training time proportionally and communication, synchronization, and memory bottlenecks keep them from doing so. They account for cost and energy, because a single large-scale training run can consume megawatt-hours of electricity ([[01 Books/Volume I - Foundations/03 Optimization/12 Benchmarking/V1-12.06 Training Benchmarks#^sec-benchmarking-energy-efficiency-cost-15ca|section 12.5.2.4]] gives the estimate for GPT-3). Their standardized evaluation criteria, controlled randomness, submission rules, and audits also make comparisons more reproducible and constrain implementation-specific shortcuts. Serving all four functions requires a matched set of training metrics, anchored by time to quality and backed by diagnostic measures of scaling, utilization, energy, and fault recovery.

### Training Metrics

^sec-benchmarking-training-metrics-0f1a

No single counter captures all four functions of a training benchmark. A complete evaluation pairs an outcome metric, the wall-clock time required to reach a verified quality target, with diagnostic metrics that explain what paced or interrupted the run, namely sample throughput, multi-accelerator scaling efficiency, compute and memory utilization, total energy, and checkpoint overhead.

#### Time and Throughput

^sec-benchmarking-time-throughput-1252

A training cluster that processes twice as many images per second by doubling the minibatch or dropping precision has not sped up training if the run requires twice as many epochs to converge, or if it never reaches the quality floor at all. That is why the primary metric for evaluating training efficiency is the time required to reach a predefined accuracy threshold. Time to accuracy, time to train, and time to quality all name this one measurement. Training time $({T_{\text{train}}})$ measures how long a model takes to converge to an acceptable performance level, reflecting the overall computational efficiency of the system. Let $\text{Accuracy}_{\text{val}}({t})$ be the model’s validation accuracy at training time ${t}$, and let the target accuracy be the benchmark-specific threshold (for example, 75.9 percent top-1 accuracy for ResNet-50 on ImageNet in MLPerf). [[01 Books/Volume I - Foundations/03 Optimization/12 Benchmarking/V1-12.06 Training Benchmarks#^eq-training-time-benchmark|Equation 1]] formally defines this metric as the earliest time at which validation accuracy reaches the target (the infimum, $\inf$, of all such times), keeping the benchmark focused on how quickly a system achieves meaningful results:

$$
{T_{\text{train}}}= \inf \big\{{t}\geq 0 : \text{Accuracy}_{\text{val}}({t}) \geq \text{target accuracy} \big\} \tag{1}
$$

^eq-training-time-benchmark

A run that never reaches the target has no finite time-to-accuracy, preventing raw training speed from rewarding a system that fails the benchmark’s quality criterion.

Throughput,[[01 Books/Volume I - Foundations/03 Optimization/12 Benchmarking/V1-12.15 Footnotes#^fn17|¹⁷]] often expressed as the number of training samples processed per second, provides an additional measure of system performance. Let ${N_{\text{samples}}}$ be the total number of training samples processed and ${T_{\text{train}}}$ the training time from [[01 Books/Volume I - Foundations/03 Optimization/12 Benchmarking/V1-12.06 Training Benchmarks#^eq-training-time-benchmark|equation 1]]. [[01 Books/Volume I - Foundations/03 Optimization/12 Benchmarking/V1-12.06 Training Benchmarks#^eq-throughput-benchmark|Equation 2]] makes the rate explicit by dividing processed samples by training time:

$$
\text{Throughput} = \frac{{N_{\text{samples}}}}{{T_{\text{train}}}} \tag{2}
$$

^eq-throughput-benchmark

Throughput alone cannot certify system capability. A platform may sustain high sample rates using aggressive low-precision formats or enormous minibatches, yet fail to reach target accuracy because of gradient divergence or poor generalization. In MLPerf Training, for instance, a ResNet-50 benchmark on ImageNet is valid only if the model achieves the required 75.9 percent top-1 validation accuracy ([[01 Books/Volume I - Foundations/03 Optimization/12 Benchmarking/V1-12.14 Source References#^ref-mattson2020mlperf|Mattson et al. 2020]]; [[01 Books/Volume I - Foundations/03 Optimization/12 Benchmarking/V1-12.14 Source References#^ref-mlperf-training-website-2f25e5be|MLCommons 2024c]]). Evaluating throughput strictly as the rate of progress toward convergence ensures that reported gains reflect genuine learning productivity rather than wasted arithmetic cycles.

#### Scalability and Parallelism

^sec-benchmarking-scalability-parallelism-1124

Scalability measures how effectively training performance improves as resources are added. Ideally, doubling GPU count should halve training time. In practice, communication overhead, memory bandwidth limits, and parallelization inefficiencies constrain scaling well below linear.

**Napkin Math 12.3: Scaling efficiency calculation**

**Problem**: A team trains an image classifier on a fixed dataset. Single-GPU training takes 24 hours. With 8 GPUs, training takes 4 hours. Is this good scaling? Where did the efficiency go?

**Step 1**: Define scaling efficiency. For strong scaling (fixed problem size, more processors), let ${T}(1)$ be the training time on a single GPU, and ${T}({N_{\text{GPU}}})$ the training time on ${N_{\text{GPU}}}$ GPUs. [[01 Books/Volume I - Foundations/03 Optimization/12 Benchmarking/V1-12.06 Training Benchmarks#^eq-scaling-efficiency|Equation 3]] defines efficiency:

$$
\text{Eff}_{\text{scaling}}({N_{\text{GPU}}}) = \frac{{T}(1)}{{N_{\text{GPU}}} \times {T}({N_{\text{GPU}}})} \times 100\% \tag{3}
$$

^eq-scaling-efficiency

**Step 2**: Calculate efficiency. $\text{Eff}_{\text{scaling}}(8) = \frac{24\,\text{hours}}{8 \times 4\,\text{hours}} \times 100\%$ = 24/32 = 75 percent

With perfect scaling, 8 GPUs would complete in 3 hours (24 hours/8 GPUs). The actual 4 hours represents 75 percent efficiency.

**Step 3**: Investigate the efficiency loss. The two elapsed times establish the “missing” 25 percent, but do not attribute it. [[01 Books/Volume I - Foundations/03 Optimization/12 Benchmarking/V1-12.06 Training Benchmarks#^tbl-benchmarking-scaling-efficiency-loss|Table 11]] lists illustrative contributions and profiling signals for gradient synchronization, memory copy, load imbalance, and batch-size effects. Strong-scaling comparisons hold total work fixed; time-to-quality comparisons must also account for any change in steps to convergence.

In multi-accelerator data-parallel training (such as 2, 4, or 8 GPUs connected over PCIe or NVLink within a host), each accelerator computes gradients on its own local slice of the minibatch. The AllReduce collective of **[[01 Books/Volume I - Foundations/02 Development/08 Model Training/V1-08.08 Scaling Training Systems#^sec-model-training-data-parallelism-ad1c|Data parallelism]]** must sum these gradients so every worker receives the identical global gradient average before the next step. Gradient tensors have one entry per trained parameter, and moving them across interconnects adds communication whose cost depends on topology, the collective algorithm, and overlap. With fixed total work and ideal partitioning, compute time per step shrinks (${T_{\text{compute}}} \propto 1/{N_{\text{GPU}}}$), while communication may shrink more slowly or grow. Exposed synchronization then occupies a larger fraction of the step; traces determine how much of the observed scaling loss it explains.

| **Source**                                | **Example Contribution** | **Measurement**                     |
|:------------------------------------------|:------------------------:|:------------------------------------|
| **Gradient synchronization**              |          10–15%          | AllReduce time per step             |
| **Memory copy (CPU$\leftrightarrow$GPU)** |           3–5%           | Data transfer profiling             |
| **Load imbalance**                        |           2–5%           | Per-GPU step time variance          |
| **Batch size effects**                    |           2–5%           | Larger batches converge differently |

Table 11: **Scaling Efficiency Loss Sources**: Illustrative decomposition of the missing efficiency budget into measurable overhead categories and the corresponding measurement signal used to attribute each loss. Actual percentages depend on model, interconnect, storage, batch schedule, and framework implementation.

^tbl-benchmarking-scaling-efficiency-loss

**Step 4**: The systems insight. Scaling efficiency can decrease as ${N_{\text{GPU}}}$ grows when exposed communication and other overheads fail to shrink with per-GPU compute. In this worked example, 8 GPUs reach 75 percent efficiency; at larger scales, the same arithmetic makes clear why sophisticated communication and input-pipeline optimization become necessary.

MLPerf reports raw performance, while scaling efficiency can be calculated across matched submissions. A system that reaches 2$\times$ throughput on four accelerators (50 percent efficiency) costs more per unit of speedup than one that reaches 1.8$\times$ on two (90 percent efficiency) whenever the accelerators cost the same, and only cheaper accelerators in the larger system could reverse that ranking.

↩︎ The AllReduce collective that averages gradients across data-parallel workers is established in **[[01 Books/Volume I - Foundations/02 Development/08 Model Training/V1-08.08 Scaling Training Systems#^sec-model-training-data-parallelism-ad1c|Data parallelism]]**.

Parallelism strategies (data, model, and pipeline parallelism) and the synchronization each requires are developed in **[[01 Books/Volume I - Foundations/02 Development/08 Model Training/V1-08.08 Scaling Training Systems#^sec-model-training-data-parallelism-ad1c|Data parallelism]]**; within one machine of two to eight accelerators, data parallelism is the common case. A benchmark’s job is narrower: to report how much of the added capacity a configuration converts into speedup without degrading convergence. That conversion is **scaling efficiency**. While strong scaling evaluates whether adding processors reduces training time on a fixed total workload, **weak scaling** scales the problem size proportionally with processor count to keep the per-device workload constant. It measures whether the system can train larger models or ingest larger datasets within a fixed time budget without communication bottlenecks dominating.

#### Resource Utilization

^sec-benchmarking-resource-utilization-336b

Training efficiency depends on learning speed, cluster scalability, and how effectively the hardware resources are used. Compute utilization requires an explicit numerator. Model FLOPs utilization (MFU), the metric of definition [8.3](https://mlsysbook.ai/vol1/08_training/08_training.html#dfn-training-model-flops-utilization-mfu), compares the useful model operation rate with the matching hardware peak; activity counters instead report whether units are busy, and a busy unit may be doing work the model does not need. Low utilization may indicate bottlenecks in data movement, memory access, or inefficient workload scheduling.

For instance, when training ResNet-50 on a dataset too large to fit entirely in host memory, retrieving batches from storage and decoding and augmenting thousands of JPEG images per second on the host can limit throughput even when the accelerators have compute to spare, the crossing from compute-bound to input-bound that **[[01 Books/Volume I - Foundations/02 Development/08 Model Training/V1-08.05 Pipeline Architecture#^sec-model-training-data-pipeline-8e71|Data pipeline]]** traces. If storage retrieval or host preprocessing cannot keep up, the accelerators wait; profiling I/O and compute utilization isolates that stall so prefetching, caching, and parallel input processing can restore sustained throughput.

Memory bandwidth is another limiting factor, as deep learning models require frequent access to large volumes of data during training. If memory bandwidth saturates, increasing compute power alone will not improve training speed. Benchmarks assess how well models use available memory, ensuring that data transfer rates between storage, main memory, and processing units do not become performance bottlenecks.

#### Energy Efficiency and Cost

^sec-benchmarking-energy-efficiency-cost-15ca

Training GPT-3 was estimated to consume 1,287 MWh of electricity ([[01 Books/Volume I - Foundations/03 Optimization/12 Benchmarking/V1-12.14 Source References#^ref-patterson2021carbon|Patterson et al. 2021]]). At that scale, energy efficiency metrics quantify the power usage of training workloads so teams can identify systems that reach target accuracy without wasted energy. Fewer iterations reduce energy only when their total energy cost is lower; a more expensive step can offset the reduced count, so suites such as MLPerf Training measure total energy over the complete run to the same quality target. The same power accounting governs inference, where precision is one potential energy lever; [[01 Books/Volume I - Foundations/03 Optimization/12 Benchmarking/V1-12.08 Power and Energy Measurement#^sec-benchmarking-power-measurement-techniques-bcc2|section 12.7]] works through why INT8 quantization cuts per-inference energy by attacking both memory traffic and arithmetic cost.

Cost considerations extend beyond electricity usage to include hardware expenses, cloud computing costs, and infrastructure maintenance. Training benchmarks provide insights into the cost-effectiveness of different hardware and software configurations by measuring training time in relation to resource expenditure. Organizations can use these benchmarks to balance performance and budget constraints when selecting training infrastructure.

#### Fault Tolerance and Robustness

^sec-benchmarking-fault-tolerance-robustness-93e8

Training workloads often run for extended periods, sometimes spanning days or weeks, making fault tolerance an essential consideration. A resilient system must handle unexpected failures (hardware malfunctions, network disruptions, and memory errors) without compromising accuracy convergence.

Production systems checkpoint for fault tolerance, periodically saving progress so a failure does not restart the run. On a single host, a benchmark records the save interval, the state written, and the pause each save exposes to training. Model weights and optimizer states consume storage bandwidth, and any pause extends wall-clock time to the quality target. Asynchronous saving can overlap I/O with computation, but its staging buffers and competing storage traffic remain part of the measured workload. Cluster recovery and parallel file systems extend this problem beyond the local machine; the local benchmarking lesson is to include the intended saving behavior in sustained performance measurements. MLPerf Training primarily measures time-to-quality under standardized workloads and does not benchmark failure recovery directly.

Training runs are also where reproducibility is hardest, because stochastic processes interact with hardware-specific behavior over many hours. What a training report must preserve to make its result reproducible, the full run envelope rather than the random seed alone, is collected with the other statistical controls in [[01 Books/Volume I - Foundations/03 Optimization/12 Benchmarking/V1-12.09 Benchmarking Best Practices#^sec-benchmarking-reproducibility-standardization-7ecf|section 12.8.2]].

### Training Performance Evaluation

^sec-benchmarking-training-performance-evaluation-bdc3

A single time-to-accuracy score tells an engineering team whether a run met its deadline, but not why an 8-GPU configuration lost 25 percent of its ideal speedup or whether buying faster interconnects would fix it. Each metric category in [[01 Books/Volume I - Foundations/03 Optimization/12 Benchmarking/V1-12.06 Training Benchmarks#^tbl-training-metrics|table 12]] isolates a different way hardware investment can fail to become convergence, turning a headline runtime into an actionable diagnosis.

| **Category**                            | **Key Metrics**                                                                                                              | **Example Benchmark Use**                                   |
|:----------------------------------------|:-----------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------|:------------------------------------------------------------|
| **Training Time and Throughput**        | Time-to-accuracy (seconds, minutes, hours); Throughput (samples/sec)                                                         | Comparing training speed across different GPU architectures |
| **Scalability and Parallelism**         | Scaling efficiency (percent of ideal speedup); Communication overhead (latency, bandwidth)                                   | Analyzing distributed training performance for large models |
| **Resource Utilization**                | Compute utilization (percent GPU/TPU usage); Memory bandwidth (GB/s); I/O efficiency (data loading speed)                    | Optimizing data pipelines to improve GPU utilization        |
| **Energy Efficiency and Cost**          | Energy consumption per run (MWh, kWh); Training throughput per watt (FLOP/s/W)                                               | Evaluating energy-efficient training strategies             |
| **Fault Tolerance and Robustness**      | Checkpoint overhead (time per save); Recovery success rate (percent)                                                         | Assessing failure recovery in cloud-based training systems  |
| **Reproducibility and Standardization** | Variance across runs (percent difference in accuracy, training time); Framework consistency (TensorFlow vs. PyTorch vs. JAX) | Ensuring consistency in benchmark results across hardware   |

Table 12: **Training Benchmark Dimensions**: Key categories and metrics for evaluating machine learning training systems beyond simple speed, covering resource efficiency, reproducibility, and overall performance trade-offs across different training approaches and infrastructure configurations.

^tbl-training-metrics

The scaling notebook shows how [[01 Books/Volume I - Foundations/03 Optimization/12 Benchmarking/V1-12.06 Training Benchmarks#^tbl-training-metrics|table 12]] is used in practice. Its completion times establish a speedup and a resource-efficiency cost, so a procurement decision uses the time-to-quality result first, then the utilization and communication measurements that attribute the loss, to decide whether additional hardware or a different execution configuration would address the limiting resource.

Those measurements are comparable only when GPU boost-clock[[01 Books/Volume I - Foundations/03 Optimization/12 Benchmarking/V1-12.15 Footnotes#^fn18|¹⁸]] behavior and thermal state follow the same protocol across matched runs; otherwise a short fast run can be mistaken for a durable improvement. The repeated-run controls in [[01 Books/Volume I - Foundations/03 Optimization/12 Benchmarking/V1-12.09 Benchmarking Best Practices#^sec-benchmarking-statistical-methodological-issues-7aa5|section 12.8.1]] determine whether the difference exceeds experimental uncertainty.

In the notebook’s case, 8 GPUs reach 75 percent scaling efficiency. If the required quality target and completion deadline are met, the configuration clears the time constraint, but the remaining scaling overhead still matters for cost. If the deadline is missed, buying more accelerators is not yet justified. The next experiment must measure exposed synchronization, input stalls, and per-device work under the intended batch and checkpoint schedule before choosing an intervention. If convergence changes between configurations, the comparison uses complete time-to-quality runs rather than interpreting the difference as pure strong-scaling loss.

A training report’s acceptance criteria concern learning, so an efficiently trained model still needs separate evidence that it can serve requests under deployment constraints.

**Checkpoint 12.3: Measuring a training claim**

The first half of the chapter built the measurement and applied it to training. Before moving to deployment, you should be able to:

Restate “ResNet-50 at INT8 is faster” as a benchmark by naming its input boundary, output, quality floor, and timing statistic.

Explain why a 3× model-stage speedup inside a 50 ms request yields only about 1.2× end to end, and name the granularity that exposes it.

Use the roofline ceiling and the measured MLPerf throughput for ResNet-50 on one accelerator to compute its utilization, and name two candidate causes of the gap.

Explain what MLPerf’s closed division holds fixed and what its open division compares instead.

**Self-Check: Question**

1.  How do the primary benchmarking objectives and resource bottlenecks fundamentally differ between training systems and inference serving systems?

    1.  Training is latency-critical with millisecond deadlines, whereas inference is throughput-oriented over weeks
    2.  Training memory footprint is dominated solely by static weights, whereas inference requires large optimizer states
    3.  Training optimizes for sustained throughput (samples/sec) and time-to-quality across accelerator systems with large memory demands (weights, gradients, optimizer states, activations), whereas inference optimizes for latency percentiles (p50, p99), QPS, and energy per query under serving constraints
    4.  Training and inference have identical memory access patterns and evaluate the exact same metrics

2.  Why does MLPerf Training mandate *time-to-accuracy* (or time-to-quality) as its primary benchmark metric instead of raw throughput measured in samples per second?

    1.  Samples per second cannot be measured accurately with digital timers
    2.  Time-to-accuracy is easier to simulate without running actual GPUs
    3.  Hardware vendors do not know the batch size used during training
    4.  Raw sample throughput can be artificially inflated by using aggressive low precision or extreme batch sizes that destabilize training, whereas time-to-accuracy ensures throughput optimizations actually converge to the required model quality

What is the strong scaling efficiency, and does this timing result by itself identify the cause of the missing speedup?

    1.  $75\%$ efficiency; profiling is still required to identify the cause of the missing speedup
    2.  $100\%$ efficiency; the run achieved linear speedup because $24 / 4 = 6$
    3.  $50\%$ efficiency; GPU memory bandwidth is cut in half whenever multiple GPUs are connected
    4.  $12.5\%$ efficiency; training time only decreased by a factor of 6
