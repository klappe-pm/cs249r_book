*How can ML systems be compared fairly when hardware, models, data, and deployment all interact?*

Every optimization claim must eventually face deployment-representative conditions, and many do not survive the encounter. Each decision targets one dimension (latency, accuracy, throughput, or energy), but an ML system is the product of all these dimensions simultaneously: a pruned model may run faster on one accelerator yet slower on another, while a larger batch size improves accelerator utilization at the cost of missing a latency target. Similarly, an edge device may advertise peak throughput that thermal throttling sharply reduces within minutes of sustained load, and a speedup measured on a warm, idle machine with synthetic inputs can vanish or reverse once real requests, cold caches, and competing jobs share the hardware. The challenge is not whether a local metric improves but whether the combined system improves under conditions that actually matter. Benchmarking makes such comparisons systematic rather than anecdotal. It requires defining what to measure, at what granularity (a single kernel, a full model, an end-to-end pipeline), and under which conditions (batch size, input distribution, thermal state, concurrent load). Without this structure, teams compare numbers that were never measured on the same terms, and decisions that looked sound in a spreadsheet collapse under production workloads. Choosing hardware, a model, or a deployment configuration on such numbers can commit an organization to millions of dollars of infrastructure that delivers a fraction of the capacity it was bought to provide. Benchmarking validates each optimization, bringing claims into contact with evidence and quantifying the gap between promise and delivery. In D·A·M terms, benchmarking holds co-design to account by revealing whether Data, Algorithm, and Machine were matched or merely assembled.

**Learning Objectives**

- Explain the benchmark–production gap and benchmarking as D·A·M validation that tests whether optimization claims hold under representative conditions
- Design a benchmark that turns an optimization claim into a test by fixing its task, data, model, metrics, harness, and run rules
- Select micro, macro, or end-to-end granularity based on the engineering decision being tested, and compare a roofline prediction with a measurement
- Compare training and inference benchmarks using standardized run rules, time to quality, throughput, and latency percentiles
- Measure energy per inference and average power with matched measurement boundaries, windows, and duty cycles
- Determine whether a measured difference exceeds noise using repeated runs, paired tests, and confidence intervals, and explain why a finite evaluation is statistical evidence, never proof (the verification gap)
- Evaluate model and data quality with confidence calibration, robustness, representativeness, and slice-level metrics, and diagnose benchmark–production gaps caused by drift, thermal throttling, and dynamic load
