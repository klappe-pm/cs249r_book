# V1-12.09 Benchmarking Best Practices

[[Home|Home]] · [[01 Books/Volume I - Foundations/Volume 1 Overview|Volume Overview]] · [[01 Books/Volume I - Foundations/03 Optimization/12 Benchmarking/V1-12 Benchmarking|V1-12 Benchmarking]]

^sec-benchmarking-benchmarking-limitations-best-practices-9d65

Those sources of error, bias, and misalignment explain why an inference stack that passes a steady-state MLPerf run in the lab can still miss its latency target when production traffic arrives in correlated bursts, or why a two-point leaderboard gain can sit entirely inside test-set sampling noise. Training throughput, inference latency, and power efficiency each have established measurement protocols, yet those protocols rely on simplifying assumptions that diverge from production reality.

Training suites freeze datasets and specify randomness even though production data and retraining conditions evolve continuously. Inference suites emphasize a defined steady-state scenario even though live traffic arrives in bursts with variable sequence lengths. Power benchmarks control thermal environments where deployed accelerators instead face variable ambient temperatures and sustained thermal loads. Turning raw measurements into trustworthy engineering decisions requires guarding against four classes of failure, namely statistical and methodological noise, irreproducible run envelopes, system and deployment misalignment (including the hardware lottery), and organizational incentives to game the score.

### Statistical and Methodological Issues

^sec-benchmarking-statistical-methodological-issues-7aa5

A one-point accuracy drop on 1,000 test images or a 2 percent latency improvement between single benchmark runs can sit entirely inside sampling and system noise. Telling a genuine change from a fluke requires the same statistical apparatus for every benchmark in the chapter, from a kernel timing to an accuracy comparison.

To isolate run-to-run variability, benchmark protocols require repeated experimental trials across controlled pseudo-random seeds. The number of runs must satisfy explicit statistical-power targets, specifying the probability of detecting a stated real effect, rather than opportunistic sampling. Reporting must extend beyond single best-of-${k}$ scores or isolated arithmetic means. Reporting sample variance, standard deviations, or confidence intervals quantifies stability and establishes whether an observed speedup exceeds background system jitter. A confidence interval is a range computed from the repeated runs by a method that captures the true value in a stated share of repetitions, commonly 95 percent. A speedup whose interval excludes zero is therefore unlikely to be jitter alone. Without these controls, benchmarking yields misleading conclusions. Reported advantages of new methods for learning from trial and error have evaporated into the noise of random weight initializations and environment dynamics ([[01 Books/Volume I - Foundations/03 Optimization/12 Benchmarking/V1-12.14 Source References#^ref-henderson2018|Henderson et al. 2018]]), and unstandardized evaluations of image generators have reversed their rankings across random seeds ([[01 Books/Volume I - Foundations/03 Optimization/12 Benchmarking/V1-12.14 Source References#^ref-lucic2018gans|Lucic et al. 2018]]).

Even when run-to-run execution variance is fully controlled, benchmarking encounters a structural constraint in the evaluation dataset itself: test-set measurement capacity. A test set operates as a measurement instrument with a finite resolution limit. Because accuracy on a finite sample is a binomial proportion, empirical scores carry intrinsic sampling variance that scales inversely with sample size. When an optimization alters accuracy by one or two percentage points, the sample size and paired pattern of disagreements determine whether the test can distinguish a genuine algorithmic effect from sampling fluctuation. As analyzed in the accompanying notebook, marginal accuracy scores on a thousand-sample test set are insufficient to establish the significance of a fine-grained difference without examining the paired outcomes. That finite resolution is one half of the verification gap, the limit on what any finite evaluation can certify, and [[01 Books/Volume I - Foundations/03 Optimization/12 Benchmarking/V1-12.10 Model and Data Evaluation#^sec-benchmarking-data-benchmarking-22f9|section 12.9.2]] takes up the other half.

**Napkin Math 12.5: The statistical confidence trap**

**Problem**: A baseline classifier scores 95 percent, while a compressed version scores 94 percent on the same 1,000 images. Does this 1 percentage-point drop establish a regression?

**Math**:

1.  **Expected errors**: The scores correspond to 50 errors and 60 errors. Under the baseline binomial model, the error count has a standard deviation of about 7 errors.

2.  **Difference interval** (95 percent): The two marginal scores alone support only an unpaired interval, which assumes the models were scored on independent test sets sampled from the same population. Both models saw the same images, so that assumption is false here, and this step shows only what the unpaired view can say before step 3 applies the correct test. Let ${\hat{p}}_1$ and ${\hat{p}}_2$ denote their accuracy estimates, and let ${N}$ be the number of images evaluated for each model. Under that independent-set assumption, the standard error of their difference is

$$
\operatorname{SE}({\hat{p}}_1-{\hat{p}}_2) = \sqrt{\frac{{\hat{p}}_1(1-{\hat{p}}_1)}{{N}} + \frac{{\hat{p}}_2(1-{\hat{p}}_2)}{{N}}}.
$$

    The observed 1 percentage point difference has an approximate interval from -1 percentage point to 3 percentage points, which includes zero. Treated as independent samples, the 1 percentage-point drop is indistinguishable from sampling noise. Pairing can change that verdict, because images that both models classify the same way cancel out of the comparison.

3.  **Paired test on the same images**: The stated problem uses one test set, so the decisive evidence is where the two models disagree. Let ${n_{\text{base}}}$ count images only the baseline classifies correctly and ${n_{\text{comp}}}$ count images only the compressed model classifies correctly. Images both models get right or both get wrong carry no information about which is better, and McNemar’s test ([[01 Books/Volume I - Foundations/03 Optimization/12 Benchmarking/V1-12.14 Source References#^ref-mcnemar1947note|McNemar 1947]]) discards them. Because the compressed model makes 10 more errors overall than the baseline, the baseline-only count must exceed the compressed-only count by exactly that number. The marginal scores therefore fix the net difference ${n_{\text{base}}}- {n_{\text{comp}}}$ at 10, while leaving the total disagreement count ${n_{\text{base}}}+ {n_{\text{comp}}}$ free to vary. The test statistic, without the continuity correction (a common variant that shrinks the net difference by one before squaring), is

$$
{\chi^2}= \frac{({n_{\text{base}}}- {n_{\text{comp}}})^2}{{n_{\text{base}}}+ {n_{\text{comp}}}},
$$

    and the drop is significant at 95 percent when ${\chi^2}$ exceeds 3.84, the critical value of the chi-square distribution with one degree of freedom at that confidence level. That happens only when the models disagree on at most 26 images, for example when the compressed model makes the same 50 errors as the baseline plus 10 new ones (${n_{\text{base}}} = 10, {n_{\text{comp}}} = 0$).

    **Implication**: The marginal scores cannot answer the question; the disagreement count decides it. If the compressed model’s 10 extra errors are nearly its only disagreements with the baseline, the paired test supplies evidence of a regression at the stated significance threshold. If the models disagree on many more images, the test leaves the same 1 percentage-point gap unresolved. Nonsignificance does not establish equivalence or compliance with a permitted quality-loss tolerance. A benchmark report must therefore keep per-image outcomes, not only the two accuracies; the interval-based decision rule below separates an unresolved difference from a supported improvement.

**Systems insight**: A small test set limits what a benchmark can resolve. The test set, viewed as a measurement instrument, must be sized to match the precision of the change it is meant to detect. A single accuracy estimate alone needs about 1,825 images for ±1 percentage point precision, and that sample size does not by itself power a comparison between two models.

The same paired logic governs timing comparisons. Suppose an engineer measures baseline and candidate mean latency under the same request mix, offered load, and timing boundary. Each experimental trial pairs a complete baseline run with a complete candidate run, following the same warm-up and thermal-state protocol and randomizing their execution order so that slow changes, such as a machine warming up, do not favor whichever implementation runs second. The comparison assumes that separately repeated run pairs are independent, with persistent background load or thermal drift controlled or accounted for. Requests within one run can share queues, caches, and thermal conditions and therefore cannot automatically count as independent trials. The report states the interval method and confidence level and uses the run pairs as the repeated observations. A request-latency p99 describes the slow end of the observed request population. It is neither a confidence interval for mean latency nor a measure of uncertainty in the estimated p99.

Suppose the report supplies an uncertainty interval for the mean latency reduction, defined as baseline latency minus candidate latency. If the interval extends from a negative reduction to a positive reduction, for example from −0.3 ms to +1.1 ms, it includes no change, and the experiment cannot resolve whether the candidate is faster. That conclusion does not establish that the implementations perform identically. If a subsequent adequately controlled comparison yields an interval entirely above zero, such as +0.4 ms to +1.2 ms, it supports a latency reduction under the tested conditions. Deployment still requires a prespecified minimum useful reduction, say 0.5 ms, reflecting the cost of adopting the candidate. The interval from +0.4 ms to +1.2 ms supports a gain without establishing that the gain justifies adoption, because its lower end falls below that threshold; an interval entirely above the threshold, such as +0.6 ms to +1.2 ms, supports both claims, provided the quality and resource checks also pass.

Two further issues undermine reliability even when runs are repeated and compared correctly, because each limits what a correctly analyzed result can certify. One narrows the inputs the result speaks for, and the other adds noise from the scoring itself. Incomplete problem coverage occurs when a benchmark exercises only a narrow operating point of a system. Controlled datasets such as CIFAR-10 ([[01 Books/Volume I - Foundations/03 Optimization/12 Benchmarking/V1-12.14 Source References#^ref-cifar10-website-87d86101|Krizhevsky 2009]]) constrain inputs to uniform, low-resolution images ($32{\times}32$ pixels) with small per-image input footprints. A model that achieves high accuracy and throughput on a uniform, downscaled benchmark can stall in deployment when exposed to variable image resolutions, arbitrary aspect ratios, and sensor noise that force frequent kernel recompilations or fallback execution paths.

The evaluation medium can also add variance of its own, which raises the number of trials needed to distinguish an algorithmic improvement from measurement noise. Large language model evaluation exemplifies this vulnerability: whether scoring candidate generations against a reference via human preference ratings or an LLM-as-judge protocol, scores vary substantially based on prompt phrasing, judge model alignment, and response ordering. A reported two-point win can vanish under an alternative prompt template or judge configuration. Rigorous evaluation demands paired hypothesis tests and bootstrap confidence intervals over hundreds of independent prompt pairings. Omitting these confidence bounds obscures whether a reported margin reflects a genuine architectural advance or stochastic evaluation noise.

### Reproducibility

^sec-benchmarking-reproducibility-standardization-7ecf

Reproducibility studies have repeatedly shown that modest benchmark gains can disappear when random seeds, hardware, framework versions, or implementation details change ([[01 Books/Volume I - Foundations/03 Optimization/12 Benchmarking/V1-12.14 Source References#^ref-henderson2018|Henderson et al. 2018]]). The stochastic processes that [[01 Books/Volume I - Foundations/03 Optimization/12 Benchmarking/V1-12.05 Benchmark Suites#^sec-benchmarking-ml-measurement-challenges-60ea|section 12.4.2]] identified as measurement noise interact, over a long training run, with hardware-specific behaviors (floating-point rounding, memory layout, compiler optimizations) to produce results that can vary meaningfully across environments.

A deeper layer of non-determinism comes from the parallel hardware itself. Operations such as parallel atomic additions, used during gradient accumulation for the sparse embedding tables of recommendation models such as DLRM, execute in non-deterministic order across threads when concurrent updates target the same memory location. The resulting floating-point summation order changes across runs, and because floating-point addition is not associative (**[[01 Books/Volume I - Foundations/01 Foundations/03 ML Lifecycle/V1-03.08 Stage 3 Model Development and Training#^sec-ml-workflow-reproducibility-technical-debt-4c78|Reproducibility and system artifacts]]**), a different order produces bit-for-bit different gradients even with identical inputs and seeds. Enforcing bit-exact reproducibility in these cases requires a deterministic reduction path, which can reduce training throughput, a direct trade-off between reproducibility and performance that benchmark protocols must explicitly address. Without explicit controls for all these sources of variability, benchmark numbers reflect a specific confluence of conditions rather than a system’s genuine capability.

MLPerf Training addresses this through standardized data preprocessing, target-quality rules, and repeated accepted runs that characterize stochastic variation ([[01 Books/Volume I - Foundations/03 Optimization/12 Benchmarking/V1-12.14 Source References#^ref-mattson2020mlperf|Mattson et al. 2020]]). The aim is to show that the reported performance reflects system capability rather than a favorable combination of stochastic factors, which a single fast run cannot show.

For a training benchmark, reproducibility is therefore the full run envelope, not the random seed alone. A credible report must preserve the model commit, dataset checksum, preprocessing pipeline, seed plan, framework and compiler versions, precision policy, batch schedule, hardware topology, thermal and power limits, and checkpoint behavior. It must also report the distribution of accepted runs rather than a single best run. Only then can the benchmark separate a real system improvement from a favorable interaction among software version, hardware state, and stochastic training path.

Reproducibility also limits comparisons across software environments. A compiler or library revision can change kernel tiling, memory access, and reduction order, so an unexplained version change is a second intervention in the experiment. The run envelope above must accompany the result; matching a model name and seed alone does not make two stacks equivalent.

### System Design Challenges

^sec-benchmarking-system-design-challenges-9ed2

Even a statistically significant, bit-for-bit reproducible benchmark fails if it measures the wrong operational regime, as when a serving stack that hits high QPS in Offline mode or under smooth Poisson Server arrivals violates its latency SLO once correlated production bursts or long-context prompts fill its queues and memory. Misalignment with real-world goals persists even when a standardized benchmark is executed and reproduced cleanly, because Offline and Server scenarios answer different questions and the Server scenario specifies only one arrival distribution. The experiment must state which operating conditions its conclusion covers, and production qualification tests those boundaries before release; the summary returns to these gaps as a release problem once model and data evaluation are in place.

Beyond deployment alignment, a further category of limitations stems directly from the physical hardware executing the workload. Accelerator behavior fluctuates with thermal conditions, operating system resource contention, and underlying microarchitectural topology.

Environmental controls must match the claim being tested. Fixed clocks, CPU affinity, and isolated memory allocations can reduce noise in a comparison of implementations, while a deployment test must retain the relevant contention and thermal policy. The sustained-state mechanisms in [[01 Books/Volume I - Foundations/03 Optimization/12 Benchmarking/V1-12.07 Inference Benchmarks#^sec-benchmarking-mobile-edge-benchmarks-fd4f|section 12.6.4]] apply to servers as well as edge devices. A result obtained under tighter isolation supports an implementation comparison, but does not by itself establish production capacity.

The hardware lottery[[01 Books/Volume I - Foundations/03 Optimization/12 Benchmarking/V1-12.15 Footnotes#^fn28|²⁸]] ([[01 Books/Volume I - Foundations/03 Optimization/12 Benchmarking/V1-12.14 Source References#^ref-hooker2021|Hooker 2021]]) compounds this physical variation by coupling algorithmic viability to specialized hardware support. Modern accelerators allocate most of their silicon area to dense matrix multiplication units (such as Tensor Cores or systolic arrays) backed by wide vector units and high-bandwidth memory hierarchies. Algorithmic architectures such as dense Transformers achieve high arithmetic intensity by reusing weights across batched tokens, sustaining a large fraction of peak theoretical FLOP/s. Conversely, architectures that rely on dynamic control flow, fine-grained sparsity, or graph message passing read memory in scattered patterns and branch unpredictably, which leaves much of that compute silicon idle (**[[01 Books/Volume I - Foundations/03 Optimization/11 Hardware Acceleration/V1-11 Hardware Acceleration#^sec-hardware-acceleration|Hardware Acceleration]]**). Algorithmic performance on a benchmark often reflects how naturally a model’s computation graph maps onto dominant accelerator execution units rather than intrinsic mathematical superiority.

The hardware lottery is how the bitter lesson shows up on a leaderboard. General methods win by riding growth in computation, but the computation that grows is the dense form the dominant accelerators supply, so the winning methods are also the ones shaped to fit it.

↩︎ The bitter lesson, that general methods which scale with computation outrun hand-built structure, is established in **[[01 Books/Volume I - Foundations/00 Introduction/01 Introduction/V1-01.05 The Bitter Lesson#^sec-introduction-bitter-lesson-79c7|The Bitter Lesson]]**.

Hardware compatibility dependence introduces systematic biases when evaluating models across heterogeneous execution targets. An architecture optimized for GPU execution may underperform on an embedded CPU, DSP, or custom edge accelerator with differing vector widths, cache hierarchies, and quantization support. Across five hardware targets on a Google Pixel 4, [[01 Books/Volume I - Foundations/03 Optimization/12 Benchmarking/V1-12.09 Benchmarking Best Practices#^fig-hw-lottery|figure 12]] charts how the latency–accuracy Pareto frontier reshuffles between CPU float, CPU uint8, GPU, EdgeTPU, and DSP execution. The diagonal purple arrow links MobileNetV3 Large at roughly 75 percent top-1 accuracy, from approximately 20 ms on CPU float to 4.5 ms on the DSP. Multi-MAX, a mobile model family found by architecture search to run well across several hardware targets, reaches roughly 75 percent accuracy at 2.2 ms on EdgeTPU and 3.4 ms on DSP; its points at higher accuracy carry higher latency. Comparisons must therefore match both the model variant and the accuracy operating point. The shifted frontiers show why model selection requires measurements on the target execution path rather than a latency ranking inherited from another processor.

Without benchmarking across heterogeneous hardware configurations, system designers risk selecting architectures solely because they conform to existing accelerator memory hierarchies and instruction sets. Evaluating models across diverse execution engines ensures that architectural decisions reflect computational efficiency rather than platform-specific co-design artifacts.

### Organizational and Strategic Issues

^sec-benchmarking-organizational-strategic-issues-d25a

Statistical power, reproducible run envelopes, and target-matched hardware tests all address measurement mechanics. A distinct category of failure comes from human incentives, because when a multimillion-dollar procurement contract or a paper acceptance hinges on a single leaderboard rank, teams face strong pressure to optimize for the score itself rather than for representative deployment performance.

#### Benchmark Engineering

^sec-benchmarking-benchmark-engineering-3af4

While the hardware lottery is an unintended consequence of hardware specialization, benchmark engineering is the deliberate practice of tailoring models or systems to exploit the idiosyncrasies of specific benchmark harnesses. This practice yields inflated performance metrics that collapse under production conditions.

Benchmark engineering occurs when developers tune hyperparameters, preprocessing pipelines, or execution paths specifically to maximize benchmark scores rather than generalizable performance. Legitimate optimization respects the run rules and declared workload; misleading benchmark engineering exploits restricted test conditions while making broader claims that the evidence cannot support. For example, an inference pipeline might hardcode input tensor dimensions and pad batches specifically to match the memory stride of an accelerator’s GEMM tile configuration. Such a pipeline achieves peak throughput on fixed benchmark shapes while triggering costly kernel fallbacks and memory reallocations on arbitrary production inputs. Similarly, fine-tuning an evaluation pipeline to match the formatting biases of an automated evaluation model produces high leaderboard rankings that fail when interacting with unstructured human dialogue.

Procurement contracts and marketing claims reward exactly this tuning. It is Goodhart’s Law ([[01 Books/Volume I - Foundations/03 Optimization/12 Benchmarking/V1-12.02 The Benchmark–Production Gap#^sec-benchmarking-machine-learning-benchmarking-framework-70b8|section 12.1]]) acting on an organization rather than on a single team’s choice of metric.

To keep this benchmark gaming from corrupting procurement and engineering evaluations, standardized suites enforce strict run rules backed by reference implementations, submission audits, and division boundaries. Their rules forbid three classes of non-generalizable shortcuts:

1.  **Precision truncation without disclosure**: A compiler silently lowers numerical precision (for instance, executing intermediate activations in FP8 or INT8 instead of BF16) to inflate arithmetic throughput, without disclosing the reduced dynamic range or verifying whether the lower precision degrades numerical stability on production workloads.
2.  **Work pruning and operator elimination**: An execution engine exploits fixed benchmark test sets to skip operations required by general production inputs, such as bypassing dynamic padding removal, masking out-of-bounds tokens, or skipping input boundary validation.
3.  **Benchmark fingerprinting**: A runtime inspects input tensor dimensions, dataset hash fingerprints, or memory layout patterns to identify the benchmark suite, executing hardcoded, pre-tuned execution graphs and bespoke kernel configurations that are inaccessible to arbitrary user workloads.

MLPerf detects and deters these shortcuts through its submission divisions (defined in [[01 Books/Volume I - Foundations/03 Optimization/12 Benchmarking/V1-12.05 Benchmark Suites#^sec-benchmarking-communitydriven-standardization-5c56|section 12.4.4]]), reference-equivalence checks, and peer-reviewed code audits. The closed division permits reproducible runtime optimizations, memory layout transformations (such as reordering a tensor’s dimensions from channels-first NCHW to channels-last NHWC), hardware-generalizable kernel fusion, and approved low-precision formats with documented, verifiable output quality, while non-generalizable shortcuts remain prohibited. Broader architectural alterations belong in the open division.

#### Bias and Over-Optimization

^sec-benchmarking-bias-overoptimization-6c65

System engineers evaluating benchmark results must distinguish legitimate architectural gains from benchmark engineering, and several practices expose the difference. Submissions that publish complete compiler flags, kernel configurations, and execution traces let independent evaluators separate general efficiency gains from harness-specific tuning. Paired results from controlled benchmarks and uncurated production traces expose discrepancies in latency distributions and memory use. Evaluating across several independently maintained suites raises the barrier against overfitting, because an optimization tailored to the quirks of one harness rarely transfers to others.

Third-party verification adds accountability. Independent audits check whether reported speedups reproduce on identical hardware, as in MLPerf’s reference-versus-submission validation ([[01 Books/Volume I - Foundations/03 Optimization/12 Benchmarking/V1-12.09 Benchmarking Best Practices#^sec-benchmarking-benchmark-engineering-3af4|section 12.8.4.1]]), and application-specific stress tests probe failure modes that static benchmarks omit, such as vision models under adverse imaging conditions or inference engines under skewed request traffic.

#### Benchmark Evolution

^sec-benchmarking-benchmark-evolution-b523

Benchmarks are not static standards; they must evolve alongside model architectures and hardware capabilities. A benchmark that effectively separates system capabilities in one architectural generation becomes obsolete when models saturate its accuracy ceiling or when hardware innovations shift the primary system bottleneck. Stale benchmarks incentivize over-optimizing legacy computational kernels rather than advancing system efficiency.

Early deep learning benchmarks evaluated image classification and object detection on convolutional networks. As workloads expanded to large language models, recommendation systems, and generative AI, those vision benchmarks failed to capture long-context attention, autoregressive decoding, and sparse expert routing, and community benchmarks moved to language understanding ([[01 Books/Volume I - Foundations/03 Optimization/12 Benchmarking/V1-12.14 Source References#^ref-wang2018|Wang et al. 2018]], [[01 Books/Volume I - Foundations/03 Optimization/12 Benchmarking/V1-12.14 Source References#^ref-wang2019superglue|2019]]) and foundation model capabilities ([[01 Books/Volume I - Foundations/03 Optimization/12 Benchmarking/V1-12.14 Source References#^ref-liang2022helm|Liang et al. 2022]]). How saturation erodes a static benchmark’s discriminating power is a data question, taken up in [[01 Books/Volume I - Foundations/03 Optimization/12 Benchmarking/V1-12.10 Model and Data Evaluation#^sec-benchmarking-data-benchmarking-22f9|section 12.9.2]].

Benchmark evolution also expands the dimensions of measurement. While early benchmarks focused on throughput and accuracy, contemporary systems require multi-objective evaluation across energy efficiency, memory footprint, and tail latency under resource constraints. Scientific instruments provide a further constraint regime in [[01 Books/Volume I - Foundations/03 Optimization/12 Benchmarking/V1-12.09 Benchmarking Best Practices#^fig-sciml-graph|figure 13]]. Its shaded “Fast ML for Science” envelope places particle-physics trigger pipelines, which must decide in well under a millisecond which detector readings to keep, outside typical mobile and IoT operating points ([[01 Books/Volume I - Foundations/03 Optimization/12 Benchmarking/V1-12.14 Source References#^ref-duarte2022fastml|Duarte et al. 2022]]). The distance between these operating points is why a benchmark must match its application.

Managing benchmark evolution introduces a direct trade-off between longitudinal stability and architectural relevance. Benchmarks must remain fixed long enough to allow rigorous cross-generation hardware and software comparisons. If benchmarks change too frequently, tracking historical progress becomes impossible. Conversely, freezing a benchmark indefinitely leads to metric stagnation and over-specialization. Modern benchmarking consortia balance this trade-off by operating on fixed release cycles that introduce new representative workloads while deprecating obsolete suites.

Even the most rigorous system benchmarks validate only one dimension of deployment readiness. Achieving record throughput and energy efficiency on an MLPerf workload confirms that the hardware and runtime stack can deliver high computational efficiency. It cannot determine whether the deployed model generalizes to real-world distributions or whether the underlying dataset represents the operational population. In the D·A·M taxonomy, hardware throughput (Machine) is a necessary foundation, but algorithmic fidelity (Algorithm) and dataset integrity (Data) must hold simultaneously. Validation therefore continues with the model and the data.
