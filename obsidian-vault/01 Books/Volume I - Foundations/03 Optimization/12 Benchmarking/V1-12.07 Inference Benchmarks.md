# V1-12.07 Inference Benchmarks

[[Home|Home]] · [[01 Books/Volume I - Foundations/Volume 1 Overview|Volume Overview]] · [[01 Books/Volume I - Foundations/03 Optimization/12 Benchmarking/V1-12 Benchmarking|V1-12 Benchmarking]]

^sec-benchmarking-inference-benchmarks-2c1f

Whereas training benchmarks tolerate variable iteration times as long as gradient descent reaches its target quality, inference benchmarks test whether the trained model can serve individual requests within strict deployment deadlines. A camera trap running MobileNetV2 on an edge board must classify each frame within a 33-millisecond budget to keep up with a 30-frame-per-second video stream. If an unbenchmarked garbage collection pause or kernel launch hiccup stretches the p99 latency past that deadline, frames are dropped and an animal crossing the field of view in under a second goes unrecorded regardless of the board’s average throughput. An autonomous vehicle’s perception loop runs against the same deadline arithmetic with far higher stakes, because in serving workloads every delayed prediction directly compromises the application.

Inference is also where the optimization chapters converge, because the accelerated hardware runs the compressed models to deliver real-time predictions. Inference benchmarks reveal whether those theoretical speedups become actual latency reductions under realistic deployment conditions. Their central quantity is end-to-end request latency ${L_{\text{lat,total}}}$, the full time from a request’s arrival to its response, including queueing, preprocessing, model execution, and postprocessing. It is distinct from the iron law’s latency term ${L_{\text{lat}}}$, which counts only the fixed overhead of one execution. Queueing delay, which grows with load, belongs to ${L_{\text{lat,total}}}$ but not to ${L_{\text{lat}}}$.

**Definition 12.4: ML inference benchmarks**

***ML inference benchmarks*** are machine learning system benchmarks that quantify a system’s ability to meet latency constraints $({L_{\text{lat,total}}})$ at specified throughput levels, measuring scenario-defined latency statistics, throughput (queries per second), and power efficiency across representative serving scenarios.

1.  **Significance**: Inference benchmarks expose the workload- and system-specific gap between unconstrained throughput and throughput while meeting a service-level objective (SLO), such as a p99 latency target. Queuing delays push tail latency above the target at high load, and only a benchmark that enforces the target at each throughput level shows it.
2.  **Distinction**: Unlike training benchmarks, which measure time-to-accuracy over a fixed dataset, inference benchmarks measure per-query response time under realistic load patterns, capturing queuing effects, batching trade-offs, and cold-start overhead that determine real-world serving economics.
3.  **Common pitfall**: A frequent misconception is that average latency is a sufficient benchmark. A low mean can coexist with a long p99 tail, and at high request rates the slowest 1 percent becomes a large number of affected users. The benchmark must report the statistic the service objective names.

### Inference Benchmark Motivation

^sec-benchmarking-inference-benchmark-motivation-eee1

Large-scale benchmark training commonly runs on dedicated data center hardware, whereas inference spans a wide range of deployment scenarios, from real-time applications like autonomous driving and conversational AI to mobile devices, IoT systems, and embedded processors. This diversity extends to hardware: while GPUs and TPUs dominate large-scale training, inference workloads often use specialized accelerators like NPUs, field-programmable gate arrays, and dedicated inference chips such as Google’s Edge TPU.[[01 Books/Volume I - Foundations/03 Optimization/12 Benchmarking/V1-12.15 Footnotes#^fn19|¹⁹]] Inference benchmarks evaluate how well hardware selection, model optimization, and data pipeline design work together across these deployment environments.

When models transition from development to these serving targets, the motivating factors parallel those for training (hardware optimization, scalability, cost, fair comparison) but differ in specifics. Software optimization frameworks apply inference-specific techniques such as kernel fusion, precision calibration, and kernel tuning, whose impact on latency, throughput, and power efficiency must be measured under realistic conditions to confirm they deliver real improvements without degrading accuracy. Auto-tuning compilers add a hidden variable. The compiler itself can require hours of optimization per model-hardware pair, so benchmark results reflect the tuning budget as much as the hardware capability. A comparison across submissions should therefore state the tuning budget behind each result.

Scalability concerns also shift character. Training scales by adding GPUs to reduce time-to-accuracy on a fixed workload, whereas inference must scale dynamically in response to fluctuating user demand, handling traffic spikes without violating latency guarantees. Applications that load models on demand, such as serverless deployments, also expose the model’s loading time, its cold start, directly to users.

The cost and energy profile of inference differs sharply from training. Training concentrates cost in discrete runs that may recur as data, objectives, or models change; inference cost accumulates continuously with production traffic. Running an inefficient model at scale can multiply cloud compute expenses, and on battery-powered devices, excessive computation directly affects usability. Benchmarks that measure cost per inference request and efficiency per watt help organizations optimize for both performance and sustainability across deployment platforms.

Holding these diverse serving stacks to reproducible comparisons requires metrics that capture tail responsiveness, streaming token delivery, throughput under load, initialization delay, and energy alongside model quality ([[01 Books/Volume I - Foundations/03 Optimization/12 Benchmarking/V1-12.14 Source References#^ref-reddi2020|Reddi et al. 2019]]).

### Inference Metrics

^sec-benchmarking-inference-metrics-78d4

A camera trap must classify each frame before the next one arrives, a voice assistant must respond quickly enough that users do not perceive lag, and a recommendation engine must score enough candidates to keep pace with user scrolling. These constraints (latency and throughput) define the performance envelope within which all serving optimizations must operate. Inference metrics formalize these real-world demands into measurable quantities, and they differ from training metrics in kind rather than degree, because the optimization target shifts from convergence rate during training to latency predictability during serving. Training optimizes for throughput and time-to-accuracy; inference optimizes for latency consistency, resource efficiency, and service-level predictability, spanning cloud data centers handling millions of requests to edge devices operating under strict power constraints.

#### Latency and Tail Latency

^sec-benchmarking-latency-tail-latency-5cde

An inference service that averages 10 ms per request can still time out for thousands of users every hour if queueing bursts or garbage-collection pauses push the slowest 1 percent of requests past its deadline. Latency measures the time for an inference system to process an input and produce a prediction. Because averages conceal those high-percentile delays, benchmarks also report tail latency,[[01 Books/Volume I - Foundations/03 Optimization/12 Benchmarking/V1-12.15 Footnotes#^fn20|²⁰]] commonly the 95th percentile (p95) or 99th percentile (p99), the time within which 95 percent or 99 percent of inferences complete. Applications with hard deadlines require explicit deadline-miss or worst-case analysis beyond these percentiles. Each such measured statistic is a service-level indicator, and SLOs and SLAs formalize performance expectations by setting targets on these indicators.

**Definition 12.5: SLOs and SLAs**

***SLOs and SLAs*** are performance commitment specifications for production ML serving systems: a service-level objective (SLO) is a target value or range for a service-level indicator, while an SLA is a contract that specifies service objectives and the consequences of failing to meet them.

1.  **Significance**: A latency SLO bounds end-to-end request latency ${L_{\text{lat,total}}}$ by setting a target for a specified latency indicator, and the iron law’s ${L_{\text{lat}}}$ is only the fixed-overhead part of that budget. Request queueing and transient spikes consume part of that budget.
2.  **Distinction**: An SLO has no contractual consequence by itself, while missing an SLA objective triggers the consequences specified in the agreement, which may include credits or penalties. Teams commonly set the internal SLO tighter than the external agreement to leave operational headroom, though the definition does not require it.
3.  **Common pitfall**: A frequent misconception is that meeting average latency satisfies a tail-latency SLO. SLOs can use averages, percentiles, rates, or other indicators; when the objective is defined at p99 or p99.9, an excellent mean does not establish compliance.

Engineering teams optimize toward SLOs while the business commits to SLAs. A usable latency objective also fixes the request population, the offered load, and the measurement window. Otherwise, two teams can report the same percentile threshold over different traffic mixes and reach incompatible conclusions about compliance.

**Checkpoint 12.4: Metric selection**

The metric shapes the optimization.

Apply three rules before finalizing metric selection:

Throughput vs. latency: Name the objective to optimize and the quality, latency, and cost limits the benchmark must preserve.

Tail latency: State what p99 exposes that mean latency hides.

End-to-end: List the preprocessing, model execution, and postprocessing stages included in the reported latency.

At production scale, small p99 degradations affect many users. If 1 percent of requests take 10× longer (for example, 1000 ms instead of 100 ms), 10,000 requests per million are slow enough to risk timeouts and customer churn. Search engines and recommendation systems demonstrate this sensitivity: Google’s search-latency experiments found measurable reductions in daily searches per user after 100–400 ms server-side delays ([[01 Books/Volume I - Foundations/03 Optimization/12 Benchmarking/V1-12.14 Source References#^ref-brutlag2009speed|Brutlag 2009]]), which is why interactive services often treat sub-100 ms response times as a practical design target. Because occasional slow responses weigh heavily on user satisfaction, such services define percentile-based objectives, and large-scale systems may track deeper tails such as p99.9.

↪ Provisioning serving capacity so that p99 latency meets its SLO under peak load is developed in **[[01 Books/Volume I - Foundations/04 Deployment/13 Model Serving/V1-13.05 Queuing Theory#^sec-model-serving-tail-latency-5376|Tail latency]]**.

The challenge of meeting these tail latency targets is that the source of the tail is often architectural, not algorithmic. A garbage-collected runtime, a shared kernel driver, or a priority-inversion bug in the serving stack can inject latency spikes that no model optimization will remove.

Discord’s Read States service shows the effect outside machine learning. Periodic garbage-collection pauses in its Go implementation spiked p99 latency every two minutes until a rewrite in Rust removed the pauses ([[01 Books/Volume I - Foundations/03 Optimization/12 Benchmarking/V1-12.14 Source References#^ref-discord2020rust|Howarth 2020]]). A managed runtime in a feature store or retrieval path can impose the same floor on an ML service’s tail, which is why the complete serving stack belongs inside a production latency benchmark. **[[01 Books/Volume I - Foundations/04 Deployment/13 Model Serving/V1-13.05 Queuing Theory#^sec-model-serving-tail-latency-5376|Tail latency]]** develops the mechanisms that shape the tail under load.

#### Token Latency: TTFT and TPOT

^sec-benchmarking-token-latency-ttft-tpot-4e1a

Generative models are served one token at a time, so their latency splits into two measured quantities. **Time to first token (TTFT)** is the interval from a request’s arrival to its first output token, dominated by prefill, the pass that processes the whole prompt in parallel. **Time per output token (TPOT)** is the mean interval between subsequent tokens, set by autoregressive decode. The gap between any two successive tokens is the **inter-token latency (ITL)**, so TPOT is the average of a request’s ITLs, and the ITL distribution exposes the individual stalls that the average hides. A benchmark that reports a single aggregate throughput hides TTFT and TPOT alike. A model can achieve high throughput in tokens per second while still feeling sluggish if the first token arrives late. It can also minimize TTFT while generating subsequent text too slowly for interactive reading.

Token throughput translates these generation rates into user-perceived wall-clock time: for a response of 750 tokens, a generation rate of 25 tokens/s requires 30 seconds, whereas 100 tokens/s finishes in 7.5 seconds. TTFT and TPOT, the average inter-token latency, must therefore be reported together: one captures initial responsiveness, and the other captures sustained delivery speed. The two quantities also stress different resources, because prefill is typically compute-bound while low-batch decode often runs into memory bandwidth (**[[01 Books/Volume I - Foundations/02 Development/06 Network Architectures/V1-06.08 Transformers Parallel Sequence Processing#^sec-network-architectures-inference-memory-bandwidth-wall-98d1|Autoregressive decoding]]**). **[[01 Books/Volume I - Foundations/04 Deployment/13 Model Serving/V1-13.10 LLM Serving#^sec-model-serving-performance-metrics-ttft-tpot-b009|Performance metrics: TTFT and TPOT]]** builds serving policies on these two measurements; the benchmarking requirement is that both are reported, under the same offered load, for every generative workload.

#### Throughput and Resource Metrics

^sec-benchmarking-throughput-resource-metrics-7c2b

Latency is only half of the serving envelope. Throughput counts how many inference requests a system completes per second, typically expressed as queries per second (QPS) or frames per second (FPS), and it is meaningful only together with the latency at which it was measured. Batching raises throughput by processing several inputs at once while adding wait time to each, so a throughput figure reported without its latency percentile and offered load says nothing about whether a serving deadline is met. A benchmark therefore reports **batch throughput**, the requests completed per second at a stated batch size, and per-request latency as a pair at the same offered load. Choosing the batch size that balances them is a serving policy (**[[01 Books/Volume I - Foundations/04 Deployment/13 Model Serving/V1-13.06 Throughput Optimization#^sec-model-serving-batching-tax|Formation delay and the latency-throughput frontier]]**); the benchmark’s job is to measure both sides of that trade.

Speed alone is insufficient because inference optimizations can change model behavior. Reducing numerical precision can accelerate computation while cutting memory and energy, as the simplified MobileNetV2 energy estimate worked out in the power section shows ([[01 Books/Volume I - Foundations/03 Optimization/12 Benchmarking/V1-12.08 Power and Energy Measurement#^sec-benchmarking-power-measurement-boundaries-982c|section 12.7.1]]), but lower-precision calculations can introduce accuracy degradation. Inference benchmarks therefore evaluate how well models perform under different numerical settings, such as FP32, FP16, and INT8.[[01 Books/Volume I - Foundations/03 Optimization/12 Benchmarking/V1-12.15 Footnotes#^fn21|²¹]] Model compression techniques[[01 Books/Volume I - Foundations/03 Optimization/12 Benchmarking/V1-12.15 Footnotes#^fn22|²²]] further improve efficiency, but their impact on model accuracy varies depending on the task and dataset. Benchmarks help determine whether these optimizations are viable for deployment, ensuring that improvements in efficiency do not come at the cost of unacceptable accuracy loss.

Beyond speed and accuracy, an inference benchmark must also report several resource terms, and each exists because a system can pass the latency test and still fail to deploy. Memory footprint decides whether the model fits the device’s budget at all, since inference rarely spans accelerators the way training can. Cold-start performance, the load time plus first-request latency,[[01 Books/Volume I - Foundations/03 Optimization/12 Benchmarking/V1-12.15 Footnotes#^fn23|²³]] exposes initialization cost to the requesting user. It dominates in serverless AI environments[[01 Books/Volume I - Foundations/03 Optimization/12 Benchmarking/V1-12.15 Footnotes#^fn24|²⁴]] and on devices that evict models to free memory. When provisioning, model transfer, runtime initialization, and the first response lie on the user request path, the benchmark must include them; a warm-instance result answers a different question. Scalability records whether added accelerators or concurrent models raise throughput or collide on memory bandwidth, and energy per inference (joules per inference, or QPS/W in the cloud) sets battery life on mobile devices and operating cost in data centers.

### MLPerf Inference Benchmarks

^sec-benchmarking-mlperf-inference-benchmarks-e878

A serving score is meaningful only against the constraints of its target environment because a data-center rack, a client laptop, a smartphone, and a microcontroller cannot be judged by one test. Developed by MLCommons,[[01 Books/Volume I - Foundations/03 Optimization/12 Benchmarking/V1-12.15 Footnotes#^fn25|²⁵]] the MLPerf inference family turns the balance among latency, throughput, memory, energy, and accuracy into scored scenarios matched to each deployment tier, so a result can be interpreted against the resource limits the deployed system will face.

#### MLPerf Inference

^sec-benchmarking-mlperf-inference-4b4a

At the data-center and edge appliance tier, the baseline MLPerf Inference suite ([[01 Books/Volume I - Foundations/03 Optimization/12 Benchmarking/V1-12.14 Source References#^ref-reddi2020|Reddi et al. 2019]]) scores submissions under the closed and open divisions defined in [[01 Books/Volume I - Foundations/03 Optimization/12 Benchmarking/V1-12.05 Benchmark Suites#^sec-benchmarking-communitydriven-standardization-5c56|section 12.4.4]]. Its workloads span image classification, object detection, natural language processing, and recommendation, providing a common reference point for comparing accelerators and CPUs when the submission rules and scenario match the intended deployment. Because different workloads stress different hardware subsystems, scores on DLRM[[01 Books/Volume I - Foundations/03 Optimization/12 Benchmarking/V1-12.15 Footnotes#^fn26|²⁶]] can separate accelerator generations on memory capacity and bandwidth where compute-bound vision models cannot, although the size of the gain depends on workload, software stack, and system configuration. At the same time, the cost of comprehensive benchmarking limits who can participate and how thoroughly systems are evaluated.

**Systems Perspective 12.6: The cost of comprehensive benchmarking**

Submitting to MLPerf can require dedicated engineering effort, hardware access, tuning, validation, and careful documentation across the relevant configurations. The cost depends heavily on workload and scope, but it is high enough that submissions are dominated by major technology companies and hardware vendors, while smaller organizations rely on published results rather than conducting their own comprehensive evaluations. That barrier motivates the need for more lightweight, internal benchmarking practices that organizations can use to make informed decisions without the expense of full-scale standardized benchmarking.

The rest of the MLPerf inference family narrows that baseline as power and memory budgets tighten. MLPerf Mobile ([[01 Books/Volume I - Foundations/03 Optimization/12 Benchmarking/V1-12.14 Source References#^ref-mlperf-mobile-website-a7bdc524|MLCommons 2024a]]) evaluates whether a model stays responsive within smartphone power and memory limits ([[01 Books/Volume I - Foundations/03 Optimization/12 Benchmarking/V1-12.14 Source References#^ref-janapa2022mlperf|Janapa Reddi et al. 2022]]). MLPerf Client ([[01 Books/Volume I - Foundations/03 Optimization/12 Benchmarking/V1-12.14 Source References#^ref-mlperf-client-website-a86cc0e0|MLCommons 2026b]]) asks whether consumer devices can run local generative-AI workloads across CPUs, discrete GPUs, and integrated NPUs. MLPerf Tiny ([[01 Books/Volume I - Foundations/03 Optimization/12 Benchmarking/V1-12.14 Source References#^ref-banbury2021mlperftiny|Banbury et al. 2021]]) covers embedded and ultra-low-power systems, such as IoT devices, wearables, and microcontrollers. Moving from data-center servers to these mobile and edge targets changes the physics of the measurement itself, because passive cooling and battery limits turn thermal throttling and cross-engine transfers into primary bottlenecks.

### Mobile and Edge Benchmarks

^sec-benchmarking-mobile-edge-benchmarks-fd4f

Unlike data centers with kilowatt-scale power delivery and active cooling infrastructure, mobile and edge deployments operate within strict milliwatt- to watt-scale power envelopes and passive thermal dissipation limits. Benchmarking these systems means reporting power consumption, inference latency, and model accuracy together, because gains in one usually come at the expense of another. [[01 Books/Volume I - Foundations/03 Optimization/12 Benchmarking/V1-12.07 Inference Benchmarks#^tbl-edge-vs-cloud-constraints|Table 13]] contrasts these operational constraints against cloud environments.

| **Constraint** | **Cloud Impact**     | **Edge Impact**             |
|:---------------|:---------------------|:----------------------------|
| **Power**      | Operational cost     | Device energy budget        |
| **Latency**    | Service-level target | Local response deadline     |
| **Accuracy**   | Task-quality target  | Balanced with power/latency |

Table 13: **Edge vs. Cloud Deployment Constraints**: Typical cloud deployments treat power as an operating cost and latency as a service target, while edge deployments often face tighter energy and response-time limits that constrain achievable accuracy.

^tbl-edge-vs-cloud-constraints

Consider a smartphone camera executing real-time object detection at 30 frames per second within a 3 W to 5 W thermal envelope. In this setting, an efficient architecture such as MobileNet serves as a realistic benchmark target where a larger ResNet model would violate thermal limits, despite the larger model’s superior accuracy in cloud evaluations. An edge benchmark must therefore evaluate accuracy jointly with sustained power and thermal dissipation. The peak-versus-sustained gap ([[01 Books/Volume I - Foundations/03 Optimization/12 Benchmarking/V1-12.02 The Benchmark–Production Gap#^sec-benchmarking-machine-learning-benchmarking-framework-70b8|section 12.1]]) is especially acute at the edge, because a passively cooled device sheds heat poorly. As junction temperatures rise, the dynamic thermal management (DTM) controller scales down operating voltage and clock frequency, causing sustained throughput to drop sharply below burst-mode peaks. That thermal mechanism makes the cooling configuration part of the benchmark specification.

**Example 12.2: Benchmarking the edge**

**Scenario**: An edge engineering team benchmarks a candidate chip for the camera trap’s board with a vendor-advertised “1 W AI” power specification.

**Diagnosis**: Early burst-mode testing matches vendor claims, but continuous inference loop execution builds up junction heat, triggering thermal throttling and dropping steady-state throughput.

**Systems lesson**: Short burst benchmarks obscure long-term thermal throttling. Benchmarking edge ML workloads requires continuous, sustained execution testing to reflect true operational hardware limits.

Throttling is the most visible way a short measurement flatters edge hardware. Idle power, duty cycle, and the data movement around the accelerator can distort a headline number too, so each needs checking before a vendor claim informs a design decision.

**Systems Perspective 12.7: Edge benchmark reality check**

When evaluating edge hardware claims, four factors determine whether vendor numbers translate to real-world performance:

1.  **Peak vs. sustained**: A vendor may advertise 45 TOPS peak throughput while a sustained thermal run delivers closer to 20 TOPS. In this scenario, a valid test uses a sustained run longer than 30 s, verifies thermal stabilization, and also covers the deployment duty cycle.
2.  **Power at idle vs. active**: In this scenario, a device consuming 50 mW idle and 2 W active could report active draw for marketing, but if the application runs inference 1 percent of the time, effective power draw is ~69.5 mW, not 2 W.
3.  **Thermal envelope**: Edge devices often operate inside a narrow TDP envelope. Exceeding it triggers throttling, so benchmark reports omitting thermal conditions are incomplete.
4.  **End-to-end vs. accelerator-only**: NPU benchmarks often exclude data transfer overhead. Moving image data from camera to NPU and back can exceed inference time for small models.

A modern mobile SoC packages CPUs, GPUs, digital signal processors (DSPs), and neural processing units (NPUs) on one die that shares system memory and a common thermal budget (**[[01 Books/Volume I - Foundations/03 Optimization/11 Hardware Acceleration/V1-11.11 Heterogeneous SoC Design#^sec-hardware-acceleration-heterogeneous-soc-ai-acceleration-b1bb|Heterogeneous SoC Design]]** develops how these SoCs partition work). An edge benchmark must therefore measure the pipeline across those engines rather than one engine’s kernel throughput. Single-engine benchmarks miss three costs that fall between the engines, namely waking an accelerator from a deep sleep state, contention when several engines access shared DRAM, and converting tensor layouts between the formats different engines require. Tracing how an isolated accelerator speedup shrinks once host preprocessing and cross-engine transfers are included is the job of the camera-trap board benchmark that follows.

### Building the Edge-Board Benchmark

^sec-benchmarking-inference-performance-evaluation-6793

The camera trap’s board is the case for the inference measurements above. It pairs a low-power host CPU with an attached INT8 accelerator, and the claim inherited from the compression and acceleration chapters is that INT8 MobileNetV2 on the accelerator meets the camera’s frame deadline at a fraction of the host’s energy. The benchmark is built in the order a careful engineer would follow: precision on the host first, then the accelerator, then the criteria that decide whether the result can ship. The labeled composite values below are illustrative, not measurements of a named product.

**Example 12.3: Benchmarking a vision model for edge deployment**

**Scenario**: An edge AI team evaluates MobileNetV2 on the camera trap’s host CPU, first at FP32 and then at INT8, under strict latency and model size limits ([[01 Books/Volume I - Foundations/03 Optimization/12 Benchmarking/V1-12.14 Source References#^ref-sandler2018|Sandler et al. 2018]]).

**Diagnosis**: FP32 execution yields 50 ms latency (20 FPS) and 14 MB size. INT8 quantization speeds up inference by 3.3× to 15 ms (66.7 FPS) and reduces model size by 4×, at a cost of 0.9 percentage points Top-1 accuracy.

**Systems lesson**: Edge model benchmarking requires evaluating multi-dimensional trade-offs across latency, accuracy, and memory payload. Quantization enables high-throughput edge execution when modest accuracy drops meet product requirements.

**Lighthouse 12.2: MobileNetV2 on the edge accelerator**

Extending lighthouse [12.1](https://mlsysbook.ai/vol1/12_benchmarking/12_benchmarking.html#lhs-benchmarking-mobilenet-deployment-validation), the hardware-acceleration claim is evaluated using an illustrative SingleStream-style edge-inference scenario ([[01 Books/Volume I - Foundations/03 Optimization/12 Benchmarking/V1-12.14 Source References#^ref-reddi2020|Reddi et al. 2019]]; [[01 Books/Volume I - Foundations/03 Optimization/12 Benchmarking/V1-12.14 Source References#^ref-banbury2021mlperftiny|Banbury et al. 2021]]).

**Hardware acceleration claim**: In this illustrative edge-accelerator scenario, assume INT8 MobileNetV2 inference takes ~2 ms on the accelerator, approximately 7.5× faster than the same board’s low-power host CPU (~15 ms). The host CPU column is the INT8 host result of example [[01 Books/Volume I - Foundations/03 Optimization/12 Benchmarking/V1-12.07 Inference Benchmarks#^exmp-benchmarking-benchmarking-vision-model-edge-deployment|12.3]], so both measurements describe the same board. Actual results depend on operator coverage, clock frequency, thermal state, and implementation.

[[01 Books/Volume I - Foundations/03 Optimization/12 Benchmarking/V1-12.07 Inference Benchmarks#^tbl-benchmarking-edge-accelerator-validation|Table 14]] reports the illustrative SingleStream-style scenario assumptions across distinct measurement scopes.

| **Metric**                      | **Measurement Scope**                                                         | **Host CPU** | **Accelerator** |          **Comparative Ratio**          |
|:--------------------------------|:------------------------------------------------------------------------------|:------------:|:---------------:|:---------------------------------------:|
| **Inference latency**           | Isolated model compute                                                        |    ~15 ms    |      ~2 ms      |     7.5× faster (isolated headline)     |

^tbl-benchmarking-edge-accelerator-validation

**What this reveals**: Under these assumptions, the 7.5× inference speedup narrows to ~3× end to end because preprocessing runs on the host CPU in both cases ([[01 Books/Volume I - Foundations/03 Optimization/12 Benchmarking/V1-12.07 Inference Benchmarks#^fig-benchmarking-mobilenet-waterfall|figure 8]]). The accelerator path incurs additional latency copying each input across the host-accelerator link and retrieving the result, a transfer overhead the CPU-only path never pays.

The 1.8× energy advantage is similarly narrow. It applies specifically to the active inference interval (${P_{\text{active}}} \times {L_{\text{lat,infer}}}$), measuring component-level efficiency rather than total system consumption. This metric excludes host CPU preprocessing energy, interconnect transmission power, and baseline idle draw during non-inference phases. Because end-to-end latency improves by only 3×, host baseline power consumed during these non-accelerated phases narrows the whole-system battery efficiency gain.

Workload arrival patterns determine whether component-level energy efficiency translates to extended battery life. For intermittent duty cycles, the lower active inference energy favors the accelerator, but total battery drain depends on sleep power, wake-up latency and energy overhead, host transfer costs, and any standby leakage the accelerator draws while awaiting work. For continuous streaming workloads such as video analytics, the accelerator’s lower active energy per inference dominates cumulative energy consumption.

Matching the MLPerf scenario to the deployment matters because SingleStream scores latency, while Offline would score bulk throughput rather than latency bounds.

Against the camera’s 33-millisecond frame budget, both paths clear the deadline nominally, at about 18 ms end to end on the host and about 6 ms on the accelerator. Those are single-request figures, whereas the deadline constrains the sustained p99 of a warm board, and that measurement has not yet been made. Because the host already fits within the budget, the accelerator’s case rests on energy rather than on the deadline. The board’s INT8 results therefore support further evaluation without establishing that the camera trap’s service objective has been met. That decision requires the request population, latency statistic, quality floor, and memory and energy budgets to be specified together.

[[01 Books/Volume I - Foundations/03 Optimization/12 Benchmarking/V1-12.07 Inference Benchmarks#^tbl-inference-metrics|Table 15]] collects the metrics of [[01 Books/Volume I - Foundations/03 Optimization/12 Benchmarking/V1-12.07 Inference Benchmarks#^sec-benchmarking-inference-metrics-78d4|section 12.6.2]] and identifies the remaining evidence. A camera pipeline needs sustained completion times under its input cadence; an intermittent application also needs initialization and sleep-state costs. For either application, the candidate must retain the required model quality on representative inputs. The same model-level speedup can therefore support one deployment and fail another.

The acceptance decision can reject a candidate that fails any hard constraint. A lower model-only latency does not establish an end-to-end gain if preprocessing dominates, and a smaller weight file does not establish that runtime buffers fit the device. The latency decomposition and Amdahl calculation in [[01 Books/Volume I - Foundations/03 Optimization/12 Benchmarking/V1-12.04 Benchmarking Granularity#^sec-benchmarking-endtoend-vs-component-latency-9952|section 12.3.4]] specify which additional measurements the MobileNetV2 comparison needs.

Because physical constraints differ across environments, no single metric ordering governs all systems. Benchmarking requires establishing a priority hierarchy derived directly from the deployment specification. [[01 Books/Volume I - Foundations/03 Optimization/12 Benchmarking/V1-12.07 Inference Benchmarks#^tbl-metric-priorities|Table 16]] illustrates how metric priorities shift across five operational contexts, with training workloads included as a contrast to the four serving contexts, mapping hardware and operational constraints to optimization targets.

|:---------------------------|:---------------------|:-----------------------|:----------------------|:--------------------------------------------------|
| **Real-Time Applications** | Latency              | Reliability            | Memory Footprint      | User experience demands immediate response        |
| **Cloud-Scale Services**   | Throughput (QPS)     | Cost Efficiency        | Average Latency       | Business viability requires massive scale         |
| **Edge/Mobile Devices**    | Power Consumption    | Memory Footprint       | Latency               | Battery life and resource limits dominate         |
| **Training Workloads**     | Training Time        | GPU Utilization        | Memory Efficiency     | Research velocity enables faster experimentation  |
| **Scientific/Medical**     | Accuracy             | Reliability            | Explainability        | Correctness cannot be compromised for performance |

A metric that dictates architectural success in one deployment context may be irrelevant in another. Latency ranks first for real-time applications such as autonomous robotics, where sensor-processing pipelines must complete within strict execution deadlines to maintain vehicle stability, whereas cloud batch services accept higher queuing latency in exchange for lower operational cost per query. A 2$\times$ throughput gain represents substantial economic value in a hyperscale cloud cluster, but it provides little benefit on a battery-powered device such as the camera trap, where a 20 percent power reduction extends operational lifetime.

For the camera example, the required deadline or percentile serves as the timing threshold, the runtime peak allocation as the capacity limit, and the permitted quality loss as the model criterion. If any of the three lacks a measurement, the decision remains unresolved even when the reported inference speedup is large. A protocol should identify the missing evidence rather than treating the favorable metric as approval. An on-demand deployment adds a fourth criterion, cold-start latency, measured on the user request path as [[01 Books/Volume I - Foundations/03 Optimization/12 Benchmarking/V1-12.07 Inference Benchmarks#^sec-benchmarking-throughput-resource-metrics-7c2b|section 12.6.2.3]] specifies.

Those quality and timing criteria also explain why a vendor’s headline INT8 operation rate cannot substitute for running the model on the board. Numerical precision optimization introduces a comparability trap across accelerator platforms because accelerators advertise substantially higher INT8 throughput[[01 Books/Volume I - Foundations/03 Optimization/12 Benchmarking/V1-12.15 Footnotes#^fn27|²⁷]] than baseline FP32 rates, yet those peak figures translate into usable board performance only when the benchmark verifies accuracy retention under quantization noise, confirms hardware support across all model operators without host fallbacks, and enforces equivalent operation definitions across architectures.

Once a candidate passes the quality and capacity checks, varying offered load tests how far its timing result transfers. A runtime can sustain a favorable latency with one request in flight yet miss the target when queues and shared memory fill. The relevant outcome is the supported load under the declared latency bound, measured with the same input mix and power policy, rather than an isolated peak throughput.

The board now has a latency, a throughput, and a memory footprint, but its energy figure covers only the accelerator’s active interval. Whether a battery-powered device lasts in the field depends on the energy it spends over its whole operating cycle, idle time included, and measuring that requires its own boundaries and windows.
