# V1-12.15 Footnotes

[[Home|Home]] · [[01 Books/Volume I - Foundations/Volume 1 Overview|Volume Overview]] · [[01 Books/Volume I - Foundations/03 Optimization/12 Benchmarking/V1-12 Benchmarking|V1-12 Benchmarking]]

^footnotes

<sup>1</sup> **Goodhart’s law**: Goodhart ([[01 Books/Volume I - Foundations/03 Optimization/12 Benchmarking/V1-12.14 Source References#^ref-goodhart1984|1984]]) articulated the original 1975 Bank of England observation on monetary policy; Strathern ([[01 Books/Volume I - Foundations/03 Optimization/12 Benchmarking/V1-12.14 Source References#^ref-strathern1997improving|1997]]) generalized it into the form quoted above. The original context was macroeconomics: once a monetary aggregate became an official policy target, banks changed behavior to game the metric, destroying its predictive value. In ML, the same failure mode recurs structurally: BLEU rewards n-gram overlap ([[01 Books/Volume I - Foundations/03 Optimization/12 Benchmarking/V1-12.14 Source References#^ref-papineni2001|Papineni et al. 2002]]), ImageNet rewards performance on a fixed visual distribution ([[01 Books/Volume I - Foundations/03 Optimization/12 Benchmarking/V1-12.14 Source References#^ref-deng2009|Deng et al. 2009]]; [[01 Books/Volume I - Foundations/03 Optimization/12 Benchmarking/V1-12.14 Source References#^ref-recht2019imagenet|Recht et al. 2019]]), and benchmark leaderboards can incentivize test-set-specific tuning.

^fn1

<sup>2</sup> **Benchmark**: From surveying, where a “bench mark” was a horizontal cut in stone serving as a fixed elevation reference. The term entered computing in the 1970s to describe standardized comparison points, and the surveying metaphor carries a systems lesson. Just as an elevation measurement is meaningless without a calibrated reference, an ML throughput number is meaningless without controlled workloads, thermal state, and precision settings.

^fn2

<sup>3</sup> **SQuAD (Stanford question answering dataset)**: Introduced in 2016 with more than 100,000 question-answer pairs from Wikipedia ([[01 Books/Volume I - Foundations/03 Optimization/12 Benchmarking/V1-12.14 Source References#^ref-rajpurkar2016|Rajpurkar et al. 2016]]). AI systems exceeded the SQuAD 1.1 human baseline of 91.2 percent F1 by 2018 ([[01 Books/Volume I - Foundations/03 Optimization/12 Benchmarking/V1-12.14 Source References#^ref-devlin2019|Devlin et al. 2019]]), but the “superhuman” result reflects a benchmarking failure mode. The task’s extractive format (answers are text spans within the passage) makes it easier than open-ended question answering, inflating perceived capability relative to production NLP systems.

^fn3

<sup>4</sup> **GLUE (general language understanding evaluation)**: Introduced in 2018 as a broad language-understanding benchmark ([[01 Books/Volume I - Foundations/03 Optimization/12 Benchmarking/V1-12.14 Source References#^ref-wang2018|Wang et al. 2018]]), GLUE was quickly saturated by systems such as BERT ([[01 Books/Volume I - Foundations/03 Optimization/12 Benchmarking/V1-12.14 Source References#^ref-devlin2019|Devlin et al. 2019]]). Once GLUE became a target, leaderboard optimization reduced its discriminating power, as Goodhart’s Law predicts. The pattern motivated harder follow-on evaluations such as SuperGLUE and BIG-bench.

^fn4

<sup>5</sup> **BERT (bidirectional encoder representations from transformers)**: BERT-Large (340M parameters) is a language-processing workload in MLPerf Inference. The benchmark fixes the task, data, quality target, and applicable execution scenarios so systems can be compared on the same workload. BERT latency still depends on sequence length, batching, and implementation.

^fn5

<sup>6</sup> **Metric**: In mathematics, a metric is a distance function satisfying strict axioms including the triangle inequality. ML borrows the term loosely for quantitative measures such as BLEU and perplexity, which are scoring rules rather than mathematical metrics. Leaderboard rankings can change when the evaluation protocol, dataset slice, or metric weighting changes, making the choice of metric an engineering decision that shapes which system wins, not just how performance is measured.

^fn6

<sup>7</sup> **BLEU (bilingual evaluation understudy)**: Introduced by IBM in 2002, BLEU measures translation quality through modified n-gram precision with a brevity penalty against reference translations ([[01 Books/Volume I - Foundations/03 Optimization/12 Benchmarking/V1-12.14 Source References#^ref-papineni2001|Papineni et al. 2002]]). BLEU is a canonical example of Goodhart’s Law in ML, because optimizing for n-gram matches can reward surface-level word overlap even when meaning, fluency, or deployment usefulness diverges from the target.

^fn7

<sup>8</sup> **Poisson distribution**: Named after Siméon Denis Poisson, who formalized it in 1837 while modeling wrongful conviction rates in French courts. The distribution models independent events at a constant average rate $({\lambda_{\text{arr}}})$ and is a common baseline for server request arrivals. Real ML serving traffic often violates its assumptions through burstiness, correlation, and time-varying rates. A Poisson harness can therefore misestimate tail latency unless traces or stress cases cover those production patterns.

^fn8

<sup>9</sup> **ToyADMOS**: Developed by NTT Communications in 2019 for acoustic anomaly detection, containing audio recordings from toy car, toy conveyor, and related miniature-machine operating sounds ([[01 Books/Volume I - Foundations/03 Optimization/12 Benchmarking/V1-12.14 Source References#^ref-koizumi2019|Koizumi et al. 2019]]). The “toy” prefix is intentional: the controlled environment enables reproducible benchmarking but can create a domain gap when models are moved to noisier industrial environments with different machines, sensors, vibration, and background sound.

^fn9

<sup>10</sup> **cuDNN (CUDA deep neural network library)**: Released by NVIDIA in 2014, cuDNN provides hand-tuned kernel implementations for convolutions, pooling, and normalization. The benchmarking implication: reported inference latencies depend heavily on which cuDNN version and algorithm autotuner settings were used, making cuDNN version a mandatory element of any reproducible benchmark specification.

^fn10

<sup>11</sup> **Roofline model**: Williams et al. ([[01 Books/Volume I - Foundations/03 Optimization/12 Benchmarking/V1-12.14 Source References#^ref-williams2009|2009]]) introduced the Berkeley model, named for the visual shape of its performance ceiling. Its ridge point (peak FLOP/s divided by peak bandwidth) separates bandwidth-limited from compute-limited ceilings, suggesting which interventions to test against measured execution.

^fn11

<sup>12</sup> **FLOP/s (floating-point operations per second)**: The gap between advertised peak FLOP/s and achieved FLOP/s is the central tension in hardware benchmarking. The H100 advertises 989 TFLOP/s of dense FP16 Tensor Core arithmetic, but real workloads achieve different fractions of peak depending on arithmetic intensity, memory access patterns, precision, and runtime overhead. Reporting peak FLOP/s without utilization context is the most common benchmarking distortion.

^fn12

<sup>13</sup> **MLPerf**: Launched in 2018 by a consortium of industry and academic institutions, MLPerf takes its name from “ML” combined with “Perf” (performance), echoing SPEC’s benchmarking tradition. MLPerf’s design principles (representative workloads, full-system measurement, and open submission) directly address the gaming that plagued Whetstone and LINPACK. Vendors who could previously report peak kernel throughput on cherry-picked problem sizes must now report end-to-end system performance on standardized tasks ([[01 Books/Volume I - Foundations/03 Optimization/12 Benchmarking/V1-12.14 Source References#^ref-mattson2020mlperf|Mattson et al. 2020]]; [[01 Books/Volume I - Foundations/03 Optimization/12 Benchmarking/V1-12.14 Source References#^ref-reddi2020|Reddi et al. 2019]]).

^fn13

<sup>14</sup> **TPU (tensor processing unit)**: Google’s custom ASIC for neural network workloads. A TPU v4 pod (4,096 chips) delivers 1.1 exaFLOP/s peak BF16 ([[01 Books/Volume I - Foundations/03 Optimization/12 Benchmarking/V1-12.14 Source References#^ref-jouppi2023|Jouppi et al. 2023]]), but benchmarking TPUs requires caution: their systolic-array architecture favors regular tensor operations, so peak FLOP/s overstate performance on irregular workloads like sparse attention or dynamic control flow.

^fn14

<sup>15</sup> **ASIC (application-specific integrated circuit)**: An ASIC’s peak TOPS number applies only to the specific operators it was designed for. An unsupported layer may require a host fallback or a different implementation, adding transfer and coordination costs. This makes operator coverage an early question in any ASIC benchmark: workload compatibility can explain part of the gap between peak and achieved throughput.

^fn15

<sup>16</sup> **Mixed-precision training**: Uses lower precision for most arithmetic while preserving higher-precision accumulation where needed ([[01 Books/Volume I - Foundations/03 Optimization/12 Benchmarking/V1-12.14 Source References#^ref-micikevicius2017mixed|Micikevicius et al. 2018]]). The benchmarking consequence: mixed-precision and full-precision runs are not directly comparable because reduced memory traffic and larger feasible batch sizes can change convergence dynamics. MLPerf addresses this by fixing the accuracy target, making time-to-accuracy the comparable quantity regardless of precision strategy.

^fn16

<sup>17</sup> **Throughput**: Originating in manufacturing to measure units produced per unit time, the term entered computing in the 1960s batch-processing era. The manufacturing origin carries a systems lesson: throughput and latency are inherently opposed, because batching increases throughput (more units per hour) at the cost of individual item wait time. In ML serving, this manifests as the batch-size trade-off: larger batches improve GPU utilization but increase per-request latency.

^fn17

<sup>18</sup> **GPU boost clock**: Dynamic frequency scaling raises clocks above base when thermal and power headroom permit. Short benchmark runs can capture this burst performance, while sustained training may settle to a different operating point. The benchmark must document the measured thermal state and clock policy.

^fn18

<sup>19</sup> **Edge TPU (tensor processing unit)**: Google’s fixed-function edge AI accelerator. Its headline throughput carries the operator-coverage caveat of any ASIC, so it applies only to quantized models whose operators the chip supports.

^fn19

<sup>20</sup> **Tail latency**: A high-percentile response time, such as p95 or p99, determines production SLO or SLA compliance only when that objective or agreement specifies the percentile. Dean and Barroso ([[01 Books/Volume I - Foundations/03 Optimization/12 Benchmarking/V1-12.14 Source References#^ref-dean2013|2013]]) showed that in fan-out architectures (common in recommendation systems), even 1 percent slow responses compound: a request touching 100 backend shards has about a 63 percent chance that at least one shard hits its 1 percent tail. Benchmarks reporting only mean latency hide this failure mode.

^fn20

<sup>21</sup> **INT8 (8-bit integer)**: INT8 sits at the aggressive end of the precision hierarchy (FP32 baseline, FP16 halves raw weight storage, INT8 quarters it), and each step demands increasing care to preserve accuracy. Post-training INT8 quantization normally fits its value ranges on a representative calibration dataset (quantization-range calibration, distinct from the confidence calibration of [[01 Books/Volume I - Foundations/03 Optimization/12 Benchmarking/V1-12.10 Model and Data Evaluation#^sec-benchmarking-calibration-confidence-scores-matter-3669|section 12.9.1.2]]); accuracy depends on the model, operator coverage, quantization method, and how well the calibration data represent deployment inputs. INT8 benchmarks must specify whether they use post-training quantization or quantization-aware training and document any range-calibration procedure.

^fn21

<sup>22</sup> **Model compression benchmarking**: Compression impact must be measured across four dimensions simultaneously: accuracy degradation, inference speedup, memory reduction, and energy savings. A technique achieving 10$\times$ size reduction with 1 percent accuracy loss may still be unsuitable if latency misses the deployment target; unstructured pruning, for example, leaves dense execution unchanged unless a compatible sparse representation and kernel exploit the zeros.

^fn22

<sup>23</sup> **Cold-start latency**: The initialization time from idle state includes storage reads, host-to-accelerator transfer, and framework setup. For a 7B-parameter model in FP16 (~14 GB) already resident in host memory, PCIe 4.0 transfer at 25 GB/s effective bandwidth alone takes ~560 ms; storage and initialization add to it. This lower bound makes cold-start mitigation (model caching, speculative loading) a systems design requirement.

^fn23

<sup>24</sup> **Serverless AI**: Deployment paradigm where models scale from zero instances on demand. Cold-start time varies with the model, runtime, storage path, hardware allocation, and provider. Benchmarks for intermittent workloads must report whether initialization and model loading are included, because warm-instance latency alone can understate user-perceived latency.

^fn24

<sup>25</sup> **MLCommons**: Launched in 2020 as a nonprofit consortium evolving from the 2018 MLPerf effort, MLCommons includes members across industry and academia ([[01 Books/Volume I - Foundations/03 Optimization/12 Benchmarking/V1-12.14 Source References#^ref-mlcommons2026about|MLCommons 2026a]]). Requiring published system specifications improves result comparability, but it does not prevent submitters from selecting which systems or workloads to enter. Published results reveal large performance differences between vendors on identical workloads, making MLCommons the closest the field has to SPEC-style like-for-like hardware comparison.

^fn25

<sup>26</sup> **DLRM (deep learning recommendation model)**: Facebook’s 2019 recommendation architecture combines embedding tables for categorical features with multilayer perceptrons for continuous features ([[01 Books/Volume I - Foundations/03 Optimization/12 Benchmarking/V1-12.14 Source References#^ref-naumov2019deep|Naumov et al. 2019]]). DLRM stresses benchmarks differently than vision or language models: its embedding tables can be large enough that memory capacity and bandwidth dominate compute throughput. That makes DLRM the book’s capacity-bound workload in MLPerf-style inference evaluation, revealing hardware limitations invisible to compute-bound benchmarks ([[01 Books/Volume I - Foundations/03 Optimization/12 Benchmarking/V1-12.14 Source References#^ref-reddi2020|Reddi et al. 2019]]).

^fn26

<sup>27</sup> **TOPS (tera operations per second)**: A measure of raw computational throughput (trillions of operations/second). The H100 delivers 1979 TOPS INT8 vs. the Apple M2 Neural Engine at 15.8 TOPS and Edge TPU at 4 TOPS, but these numbers conflate different operation types (multiply-accumulate, accumulate, or activation). TOPS comparisons across vendors are meaningful only when the operation definition, precision, and sparsity assumptions are identical, conditions rarely met in vendor specifications.

^fn27

<sup>28</sup> **Hardware lottery**: Coined by Hooker ([[01 Books/Volume I - Foundations/03 Optimization/12 Benchmarking/V1-12.14 Source References#^ref-hooker2021|2021]]) to describe how algorithmic success depends on alignment with available hardware and software. Transformer workloads benefit from dense matrix operations that map efficiently to modern accelerators, while graph neural networks and sparse mixture-of-experts models can be harder to evaluate when available silicon and software stacks favor dense kernels. Hardware-specific leaderboards therefore favor architectures aligned with the measured platform and may obscure alternatives that would fare differently under other hardware assumptions.

^fn28

<sup>29</sup> **ResNet (residual network)**: Introduced by He et al. ([[01 Books/Volume I - Foundations/03 Optimization/12 Benchmarking/V1-12.14 Source References#^ref-he2016|2016]]), skip connections enabled deep residual networks and achieved 3.57 percent top-5 ImageNet error (ensemble), surpassing the estimated human error rate in the ImageNet challenge context ([[01 Books/Volume I - Foundations/03 Optimization/12 Benchmarking/V1-12.14 Source References#^ref-russakovsky2015|Russakovsky et al. 2015]]).

^fn29

<sup>30</sup> **Calibration**: From Arabic *qalib* (a mold for casting metal) via Latin *calibrare*, originally describing the adjustment of measuring instruments against known standards. In ML, calibration ensures predicted probabilities match empirical frequencies; Guo et al. ([[01 Books/Volume I - Foundations/03 Optimization/12 Benchmarking/V1-12.14 Source References#^ref-guo2017calibration|2017]]) formalize this concern for modern neural networks and show that temperature scaling is a simple effective post-hoc correction. The etymology is apt: just as an uncalibrated instrument produces precise but inaccurate measurements, an uncalibrated model produces confident but unreliable predictions, causing downstream systems that threshold on confidence scores to make systematically wrong decisions.

^fn30

<sup>31</sup> **Pareto frontier**: Named after economist Pareto ([[01 Books/Volume I - Foundations/03 Optimization/12 Benchmarking/V1-12.14 Source References#^ref-pareto1896cours|1896]]), the frontier contains all solutions where improving one objective requires degrading another. In compression benchmarking, the trade-off between adjacent candidates must be interpreted using the named axes and units. A candidate is dominated when another is at least as good on every objective and strictly better on one; frontier membership still does not establish that deployment thresholds are met.

^fn31

<sup>32</sup> **MMLU (massive multitask language understanding)**: Introduced by Hendrycks et al. ([[01 Books/Volume I - Foundations/03 Optimization/12 Benchmarking/V1-12.14 Source References#^ref-hendrycks2021mmlu|2020]]) with 15,908 multiple-choice questions across fifty-seven subjects. MMLU’s benchmarking limitation is its format: multiple-choice recognition is not the same task as open-ended generation, so an MMLU score should not be read as direct evidence that a model can produce grounded free-form answers in production.

^fn32

<sup>33</sup> **HELM (holistic evaluation of language models)**: Stanford’s 2022 evaluation framework tested a broad set of models across seven dimensions (accuracy, calibration, robustness, fairness, bias, toxicity, efficiency) ([[01 Books/Volume I - Foundations/03 Optimization/12 Benchmarking/V1-12.14 Source References#^ref-liang2022helm|Liang et al. 2022]]). HELM’s contribution is methodological: by evaluating models that score similarly on accuracy but diverge on calibration or toxicity, it demonstrates that single-metric leaderboards systematically hide failure modes that matter for production deployment.

^fn33

<sup>34</sup> **Perplexity**: From Latin *perplexus* (entangled); in information theory, $2^{{H}({p})}$ where ${H}$ is entropy. A perplexity of 10 means the model is “10-way confused” on average. The systems consequence is interpretive: perplexity measures held-out next-token prediction on a corpus and says nothing about serving memory pressure, which depends on how the system manages decoding state ([[01 Books/Volume I - Foundations/03 Optimization/12 Benchmarking/V1-12.14 Source References#^ref-kwon2023|Kwon et al. 2023]]).

^fn34

<sup>35</sup> **WILDS**: Stanford’s 2021 benchmark of ten datasets with real-world distribution shifts: hospital changes (Camelyon17), wildlife camera location shifts (iWildCam), and satellite imagery temporal drift (PovertyMap). For the reported Camelyon17-WILDS ERM baseline, average accuracy fell from 93.2 percent in-distribution to 70.3 percent out-of-distribution, demonstrating that standard held-out evaluation can overestimate performance when the i.i.d. assumption fails.

^fn35

<sup>36</sup> **DataComp**: Introduced in 2023, DataComp fixes the model family, training code, and compute budget while participants vary dataset construction. Its filtering track isolates the effect of selecting examples from a common candidate pool, making curation strategies comparable without attributing every gain to additional training compute.

^fn36

<sup>37</sup> **Dynabench**: Facebook AI Research’s 2021 platform for dynamic benchmark generation, where humans craft adversarial inputs that fool current best models. Dynabench addresses the saturation problem, where very high accuracy on static benchmarks may reflect test-set familiarity rather than robust capability, but introduces its own trade-off: dynamic benchmarks are harder to compare across time because the evaluation set changes. Static and dynamic benchmarks serve complementary diagnostic roles.

^fn37
