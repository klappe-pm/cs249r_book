# V1-12.08 Power and Energy Measurement

[[Home|Home]] · [[01 Books/Volume I - Foundations/Volume 1 Overview|Volume Overview]] · [[01 Books/Volume I - Foundations/03 Optimization/12 Benchmarking/V1-12 Benchmarking|V1-12 Benchmarking]]

^sec-benchmarking-power-measurement-techniques-bcc2

Estimating that energy from a vendor datasheet is unreliable because energy is the one benchmark quantity that cannot be read off a clock or inferred from isolated peak ratings. A chip vendor may advertise “10 TOPS at 0.5 W,” yet under sustained inference load it delivers only 3 TOPS while drawing 2 W. The datasheet watts were read at a lighter operating point than the one the workload holds, and thermal throttling lowers throughput once the chip heats. That 13.3× efficiency gap between the datasheet and reality goes undetected until deployment unless power is integrated over a stated measurement boundary and window under load. For a duty-cycled device, total energy also depends on how the hardware spends the idle intervals between inferences. KWS, the book’s power-bound lighthouse, carries the measurement of energy in this section because a wake-word detector is judged by how long it listens on a battery rather than by how fast it answers, while the edge board’s frames and the data center’s racks serve as high-duty-cycle contrasts.

The gap between datasheet and sustained efficiency matters because **[[01 Books/Volume I - Foundations/03 Optimization/11 Hardware Acceleration/V1-11.02 Anatomy of an Accelerator#^sec-hardware-acceleration-ai-hardware-acceleration-fundamentals-9b28|Anatomy of an Accelerator]]** defines acceleration by its compute-density and performance-per-watt gains, making TOPS/W a primary design objective alongside raw TOPS. Power benchmarks validate whether efficiency-optimized accelerators deliver their promised energy savings. TOPS/W is particularly susceptible to gaming because it is a ratio of two separately quotable peaks. A vendor can read the numerator (operations) at the batch size and precision that maximize throughput and the denominator (watts) at a near-idle operating point. The advertised efficiency then describes a state the chip never occupies under real load. Power benchmarks close that loophole by fixing the workload and the measurement window, forcing the numerator and denominator to be read at the same operating point.

![[90 Assets/Volume I/12 Benchmarking/Benchmarking Power Ladder 2fb5250b91.svg]]

*Two orange bars on a linear TOPS per watt scale: a long datasheet bar for 10 TOPS at 0.5 W and a short sustained bar for 3 TOPS at 2 W, joined by a dimension arrow labeled 13.3×.*

*Datasheet TOPS/W overstates sustained efficiency by 13.3×.*

Measuring power differs from measuring execution time because power is not a single timestamped interval: instantaneous power (supply voltage times current) fluctuates continuously across microsecond execution phases and drifts over minutes as silicon heats up, increasing temperature-dependent leakage current. ML deployments also span a wide physical range. The dumbbell plot in [[01 Books/Volume I - Foundations/03 Optimization/12 Benchmarking/V1-12.08 Power and Energy Measurement#^fig-power-differentials|figure 9]] isolates this disparity on a logarithmic power scale. It plots minimum (blue) and maximum (red) envelopes across four deployment tiers: TinyML systems (5.6 mW to 167 mW), edge hardware (3.9 W to 1.1 kW), data center inference nodes (267 W to 6.3 kW), and large-scale training clusters (5.5 kW to 498 kW). Spanning about eight orders of magnitude from the TinyML floor to the training-cluster ceiling, this spectrum explains why no single efficiency metric serves all deployment contexts. A figure of merit optimized for multi-rack data center throughput becomes meaningless for a battery-powered keyword spotter governed by milliwatt budgets.

Because measured power spans from milliwatts to hundreds of kilowatts, no single instrument or measurement protocol applies everywhere. Benchmarking an always-on keyword spotter requires measuring microampere currents on a low-voltage supply rail, whereas benchmarking a server rack requires measuring high-voltage power at the power distribution unit. This divergence forces benchmarking standards to establish precise physical boundaries for what hardware is included in the measured energy.

Whatever the boundary, energy accounting follows contrasting regimes across training and inference. Training energy is a capital investment amortized across the operational lifetime of the model; a large training run can consume on the order of a thousand megawatt-hours ([[01 Books/Volume I - Foundations/03 Optimization/12 Benchmarking/V1-12.06 Training Benchmarks#^sec-benchmarking-energy-efficiency-cost-15ca|section 12.5.2.4]]) ([[01 Books/Volume I - Foundations/03 Optimization/12 Benchmarking/V1-12.14 Source References#^ref-patterson2021carbon|Patterson et al. 2021]]). Inference energy accumulates continuously with request volume, dominating total lifecycle cost as query counts scale into millions.

For an isolated inference window containing one completed request, energy obeys the identity ${E_{\text{inf}}}= {P_{\text{active}}}\times {L_{\text{lat,infer}}}$, with ${P_{\text{active}}}$ the average accelerator power during that window and ${L_{\text{lat,infer}}}$ its duration. That average must be measured at runtime, because an accelerator’s TDP rating is a cooling ceiling rather than the power it actually draws. The duty-cycle form used for battery accounting appears in [[01 Books/Volume I - Foundations/03 Optimization/12 Benchmarking/V1-12.08 Power and Energy Measurement#^sec-benchmarking-battery-thermal-benchmarking-79dd|section 12.7.2]]. With batching or concurrent requests, the benchmark integrates energy over a common measurement window and divides by completed requests; multiplying total device power by each request’s overlapping serving latency would double-count energy. For example, if measured average accelerator power during the inference window is 300 W, a 10 ms inference uses $\text{300 W} \times \text{0.01 s} = \text{3 J}$, or about 0.0008 Wh; at 100 ms, that becomes about 0.0083 Wh.

### Power Measurement Boundaries

^sec-benchmarking-power-measurement-boundaries-982c

Where an engineer draws the system boundary dictates what counts as efficient. If a benchmark measures only the accelerator core, it ignores the host CPU driving the PCIe bus, the off-chip DRAM holding weights, and the cooling fans dissipating heat. [[01 Books/Volume I - Foundations/03 Optimization/12 Benchmarking/V1-12.08 Power and Energy Measurement#^fig-power-diagram|Figure 10]] partitions these measurement perimeters across three deployment tiers (Tiny, Inference, and Training), where solid olive enclosures demarcate measured components and red dashed boxes isolate excluded infrastructure such as remote storage and data-center chillers ([[01 Books/Volume I - Foundations/03 Optimization/12 Benchmarking/V1-12.14 Source References#^ref-tschand2025|Tschand et al. 2024]]).

![[90 Assets/Volume I/12 Benchmarking/Generated Figure 3348c7a6e7.svg]]

Figure 10: **Power Measurement Boundaries**: MLPerf defines system boundaries for power measurement, ranging from single-chip devices to full data center nodes, to enable fair comparisons of energy efficiency across diverse hardware platforms. These boundaries delineate which components’ power consumption is included in reported metrics, impacting the interpretation of performance results. Source: ([[01 Books/Volume I - Foundations/03 Optimization/12 Benchmarking/V1-12.14 Source References#^ref-tschand2025|Tschand et al. 2024]]).

^fig-power-diagram

The three architectural tiers in [[01 Books/Volume I - Foundations/03 Optimization/12 Benchmarking/V1-12.08 Power and Energy Measurement#^fig-power-diagram|figure 10]] (Tiny Example, Inference Example, and Training Example) reflect distinct physical instrumentation points. In TinyML deployments, the entire low-power SoC, including compute cores, internal SRAM, and power management units, falls inside the boundary, measured directly at the DC power rail. Dedicated inference nodes expand this perimeter to include host CPUs, accelerator cards, PCIe buses, local storage, and on-chassis active cooling fans, while excluding remote storage networks. Multi-rack training deployments measure compute nodes and top-of-rack interconnect switches, but exclude centralized data center chillers, facility-level power distribution units (PDUs), and disaggregated storage clusters.

The excluded components can more than double the reported energy. Google measured the energy of the median text prompt served by its Gemini assistant under two boundaries ([[01 Books/Volume I - Foundations/03 Optimization/12 Benchmarking/V1-12.14 Source References#^ref-elsworth2025measuring|Elsworth et al. 2025]]). Counting only the active accelerators, on prompts served by its most efficient data centers, gave 0.10 Wh. Counting everything the service draws to answer that prompt gave 0.24 Wh, about 2.4× more. Of that total, the active accelerators drew 58 percent and the host CPUs and DRAM that feed them 25 percent. The remaining 17 percent went to machines held idle in reserve for traffic spikes and to facility overhead such as cooling and power conversion.

The gap between the two boundaries means that an accelerator-only figure understates what a deployed service consumes, and two figures measured under different boundaries are not comparable. Utilization also moves the figure, because the narrow measurement came from the most efficiently used machines. Open benchmark suites such as ML.ENERGY measure energy under realistic serving conditions so that their results reflect deployed utilization rather than an idealized run ([[01 Books/Volume I - Foundations/03 Optimization/12 Benchmarking/V1-12.14 Source References#^ref-chung2025energy|Chung et al. 2025]]).

The measurement boundary determines which components are counted; workload composition determines where their energy is spent. On mobile devices running TensorFlow Mobile, data movement accounts for 57.3 percent of total inference energy ([[01 Books/Volume I - Foundations/03 Optimization/12 Benchmarking/V1-12.14 Source References#^ref-boroumand2018|Boroumand et al. 2018]]), exposing why operation counts alone fail to predict power consumption. Under the energy-movement principle ([[01 Books/Volume I - Foundations/03 Optimization/V1 Optimization Principles#^pri-energy-movement|7]]), total energy depends on how many events of each kind occur and what each one costs, not on arithmetic alone. The worked example in notebook [12.4](https://mlsysbook.ai/vol1/12_benchmarking/12_benchmarking.html#nbk-benchmarking-why-int8-saves-energy) decomposes MobileNetV2 inference energy to demonstrate how moving from FP32 to INT8 attacks both the memory-movement bottleneck and arithmetic switching costs.

**Napkin Math 12.4: Why INT8 saves energy**

**Problem**: Under the stated 45 nm component-energy model, how much does replacing FP32 with INT8 reduce MobileNetV2 weight-read and arithmetic energy?

Moving data costs far more energy than computing on it (principle [[01 Books/Volume I - Foundations/03 Optimization/V1 Optimization Principles#^pri-energy-movement|7]], grounded in Horowitz’s energy estimates ([[01 Books/Volume I - Foundations/03 Optimization/12 Benchmarking/V1-12.14 Source References#^ref-horowitz2014|Horowitz 2014]])), so quantization can save energy on both memory access and compute operations.

Narrower datatypes require less switching and storage energy per operation, and off-chip memory costs far more than either. In the 45 nm component model that **[[01 Books/Volume I - Foundations/03 Optimization/10 Model Compression/V1-10.04 Numerical Precision Changing How Values Are Stored#^sec-model-compression-precision-energy-cc8e|Precision and energy]]** tabulates (**[[01 Books/Volume I - Foundations/03 Optimization/10 Model Compression/V1-10.04 Numerical Precision Changing How Values Are Stored#^tbl-model-compression-energy-hierarchy|INT8 versus FP32 Energy per Operation]]**), an 8-bit multiplier uses about 18.5× less energy than a 32-bit floating-point multiplier. Reading one byte from DRAM, about 160 pJ/byte, costs over 1,600× more than a register access ([[01 Books/Volume I - Foundations/03 Optimization/12 Benchmarking/V1-12.14 Source References#^ref-horowitz2014|Horowitz 2014]]). Absolute values and ratios vary with circuit design and process technology.

**Math**: Component energy is the sum of a data-movement term and a compute term (**[[01 Books/Volume I - Foundations/00 Introduction/01 Introduction/V1-01.08 Iron Law of ML Systems#^eq-energy-cost|equation 1.5]]**), a load term ${D_{\text{vol}}} \times {E_{\text{move}}}$ (model bytes times DRAM energy per byte) and a compute term ${O}\times {E_{\text{compute}}}$ (operations times energy per operation). The FP32 terms sum as 2243 µJ + 2,220 µJ = 4,463 µJ; the INT8 terms sum as 561 µJ + 120 µJ = 681 µJ. Dividing the totals gives a 6.6× reduction under this simplified model.

[[01 Books/Volume I - Foundations/03 Optimization/12 Benchmarking/V1-12.08 Power and Energy Measurement#^tbl-benchmarking-mobilenet-int8-energy|Table 17]] combines the two effects in a deliberately simplified MobileNetV2 model. It counts one DRAM read per weight and charges every cataloged FLOP at the listed multiplier energy, while excluding activation traffic, cache behavior, additions as a distinct cost, control overhead, and static power:

| **Component**                 | **FP32 (14 MB)** | **INT8 (3.5 MB)** | **Savings** |
|:------------------------------|:----------------:|:-----------------:|:-----------:|
| **One weight read from DRAM** |     2243 µJ      |      561 µJ       |     4×      |
| **Compute (600 MFLOP)**       |     2,220 µJ     |      120 µJ       |    18.5×    |
| **Total**                     |   **4,463 µJ**   |    **681 µJ**     |  **6.6×**   |

Table 17: **MobileNetV2 INT8 Simplified Energy Estimate**: One weight read plus a multiplier-energy charge for every cataloged FLOP under a 45 nm component-energy model. This is not a whole-device inference measurement.

^tbl-benchmarking-mobilenet-int8-energy

**Systems insight**: Within this simplified model, the weight read and the arithmetic contribute comparable FP32 energy (2243 µJ and 2,220 µJ), and INT8 reduces both, taking the total from 4,463 µJ to 681 µJ. The decomposition explains the physical mechanism behind potential savings, but battery-life or per-inference claims require controlled whole-device power measurements.

Beyond individual server nodes, large-scale deployments introduce shared infrastructure that complicates boundary definition. In data centers, cooling systems and power delivery account for 20 to 30 percent of total facility electricity consumption ([[01 Books/Volume I - Foundations/03 Optimization/12 Benchmarking/V1-12.14 Source References#^ref-barroso2019|Barroso et al. 2019]]). This facility-level overhead is captured by power usage effectiveness (PUE):

$$
\text{PUE} = \frac{\text{Total Facility Energy}}{\text{IT Equipment Energy}}
$$

 PUE values range from approximately 1.1 in hyperscale facilities using direct-to-chip liquid cooling to over 2.0 in legacy facilities relying on computer room air conditioning ([[01 Books/Volume I - Foundations/03 Optimization/12 Benchmarking/V1-12.14 Source References#^ref-barroso2019|Barroso et al. 2019]]). When an evaluation reports only server-plug energy, it excludes this facility multiplier. The multiplier also moves with load, because part of the facility overhead is fixed and cooling efficiency varies as DVFS changes the heat the equipment produces ([[01 Books/Volume I - Foundations/03 Optimization/12 Benchmarking/V1-12.14 Source References#^ref-kim2008dvfs|Kim et al. 2008]]). On untethered edge and tiny devices, the boundary moves from the facility plug to the battery rail, where device lifetime depends on how active bursts, idle intervals, and rising junction temperatures accumulate over time.

### Battery and Thermal Benchmarking

^sec-benchmarking-battery-thermal-benchmarking-79dd

Battery drain depends on total energy consumed over time rather than peak instantaneous power. Computational photography bursts consume multiple watts for hundreds of milliseconds during image capture, whereas background sensor processing must remain within a milliwatt-scale budget to deliver multi-day battery life. Characterizing battery impact requires measuring the total energy consumption ${E_{\text{total}}} = \int {P_{\text{device}}}({t})\,d{t}$ across representative operational profiles, where ${P_{\text{device}}}({t})$ is the instantaneous power the device draws at time ${t}$.

The dominant operational variable is the workload duty cycle ${\alpha_{\text{duty}}}$, defined as the fraction of time the accelerator actively runs inference. For an active power draw ${P_{\text{active}}}$ and standby power draw ${P_{\text{idle}}}$, the average power is:

$$
{P_{\text{avg}}}= {\alpha_{\text{duty}}} {P_{\text{active}}}+ (1 - {\alpha_{\text{duty}}}) {P_{\text{idle}}}
$$

 For intermittent workloads such as a smart doorbell that sleeps until a motion sensor wakes it (${\alpha_{\text{duty}}}\ll 0.01$), standby power ${P_{\text{idle}}}$ and memory retention power dictate battery lifetime, making active inference efficiency secondary. For continuous pipelines like video analytics (${\alpha_{\text{duty}}}\approx 1.0$), active inference energy ${E_{\text{inf}}}= {P_{\text{active}}}\cdot {L_{\text{lat,infer}}}$ dominates. Furthermore, elevated operating temperatures increase semiconductor leakage current, causing static power dissipation to climb as the device heats up. Benchmarking battery impact therefore requires measuring both active and standby power across the operational duty cycle while monitoring junction temperature over extended multi-minute runs.

Keyword spotting makes the point concrete. DS-CNN ([[01 Books/Volume I - Foundations/03 Optimization/12 Benchmarking/V1-12.14 Source References#^ref-zhang2017hello|Zhang et al. 2017]]), the MLPerf Tiny keyword-spotting reference at 20 MFLOP per inference, runs here on an ESP32-S3-class microcontroller board with one inference per one-second audio window. At the board’s peak INT8 rate an inference takes about 8.3 ms, a duty cycle of about 0.8 percent, and costs about 3.3 mJ at the board’s 0.4 W active power. Between inferences the board idles at 50 mW. Average power is therefore about 52.9 mW, and 94 percent of it is spent waiting. A benchmark that reports only energy per inference describes the remaining 6 percent of the battery drain. Active energy would overtake the idle floor only if inference occupied more than about 11 percent of the time, and real kernels running below peak raise the active share without changing the conclusion. This is why always-on audio moves to dedicated low-power silicon whose idle draw is microwatts, and why a power benchmark for a duty-cycled device must specify the duty cycle along with the inference.

### Computational Efficiency Vs. Power Consumption

^sec-benchmarking-computational-efficiency-vs-power-consumption-d01e

Even when the workload and duty cycle are fixed, a short boost-clock burst and a warm sustained run report different efficiencies for the same chip. That shift follows from CMOS physics, since total power is dynamic switching power plus static leakage power, and over the range where supply voltage tracks clock frequency, dynamic power grows roughly with the cube of frequency (**[[01 Books/Volume I - Foundations/03 Optimization/11 Hardware Acceleration/V1-11.11 Heterogeneous SoC Design#^sec-hardware-acceleration-power-thermal-management-6c00|Power and thermal management]]** derives both). Because a few percent of clock frequency moves power far more than throughput, a measured efficiency belongs strictly to the operating point at which it was taken.

Static leakage also rises with temperature, making a warm device less efficient than a cold one at the same clock, which is why the power protocol below requires warming the device to its intended thermal state before logging begins. The hardware generation shifts efficiency as well, since computations per kilowatt-hour historically doubled approximately every 1.5 years, a long-run trend known as Koomey’s law ([[01 Books/Volume I - Foundations/03 Optimization/12 Benchmarking/V1-12.14 Source References#^ref-koomey2011|Koomey et al. 2011]]), so an efficiency comparison across devices must also state which generation each one represents.

Because frequency scaling quickly hits TDP ceilings, system designers optimize efficiency across the Data, Algorithm, and Machine axes. On the algorithm and data side, reduced-precision arithmetic (INT8 or FP8) directly reduces the capacitance switched in the ALUs and compresses memory traffic across interconnect buses, potentially cutting dynamic energy when the chosen format preserves the required model quality ([[01 Books/Volume I - Foundations/03 Optimization/12 Benchmarking/V1-12.14 Source References#^ref-jacob2018|Jacob et al. 2018]]; [[01 Books/Volume I - Foundations/03 Optimization/12 Benchmarking/V1-12.14 Source References#^ref-wu2020integer|Wu et al. 2020]]; [[01 Books/Volume I - Foundations/03 Optimization/12 Benchmarking/V1-12.14 Source References#^ref-gholami2021survey|Gholami et al. 2021]]). On the machine side, clock and voltage set the operating point, and the lowest-energy point can be a slow clock whose long completion time misses the deadline. Battery-constrained devices and power-limited data centers must choose operating points against their own latency, throughput, and energy requirements, using calibrated meters and synchronized measurement windows that capture leakage, active switching, and idle power together.

### Standardized Power Measurement

^sec-benchmarking-standardized-power-measurement-7fae

General-purpose power benchmarks such as SPECpower_ssj2008 measure server power across stepped load levels (0 to 100 percent in 10 percent increments) using steady-state transactional workloads ([[01 Books/Volume I - Foundations/03 Optimization/12 Benchmarking/V1-12.14 Source References#^ref-lange2009|Lange 2009]]). In contrast, ML workloads swing rapidly in power: instantaneous draw alternates between compute-bound matrix multiplications (where systolic arrays or Tensor Cores draw peak current) and memory-bound stalls or token transfers (where execution units stall waiting for DRAM or interconnect fetches).

Characterizing these fluctuations requires calibrated physical instrumentation. Software counters built into processors and accelerators are convenient, but their accuracy, update rates, and polling overhead vary by device. The instrument must match the measurement boundary and required uncertainty. MLPerf power protocols use external calibrated analyzers for inference systems, while training and HPC measurements can use validated node or power-distribution telemetry; Tiny measurements use instrumentation suited to low-power devices ([[01 Books/Volume I - Foundations/03 Optimization/12 Benchmarking/V1-12.14 Source References#^ref-tschand2025|Tschand et al. 2024]]).

Resolving an individual kernel transient requires instrument bandwidth and sampling fast enough to follow it, because a kilohertz log cannot trace microsecond power spikes. Total run energy instead requires integrating power over the entire measurement window:

$$
{E_{\text{total}}} = \int_{t_{\text{start}}}^{{t_{\text{end}}}} {P_{\text{device}}}({t}) \, d{t}
$$

 A calibrated power analyzer can integrate accurately even when its exported log is slow, because the instrument samples voltage and current internally at high frequency and accumulates their product in hardware between log intervals. Polling an unaccumulated instantaneous power reading at a low rate, by contrast, can alias rapid swings between compute-bound and memory-bound phases. For a steady-state energy comparison, the harness separates initialization from the timed workload through three protocol stages:

1.  A warm-up phase runs unmeasured inferences until caches, buffers, and temperature reach the intended operating state.
2.  A sustained measurement window executes repeated inferences across minutes to average over microarchitectural phase shifts and thermal drift.
3.  Hardware-to-software synchronization aligns power and workload timestamps so integrated energy and completed requests cover the same window. Cold-start or intermittent deployments require additional measurements that include initialization, sleep, and wake-up costs.

Workload structure further alters the power profile. In low-batch Transformer execution, decode often has less weight reuse than prefill and can face a lower bandwidth ceiling; their measured power profiles depend on shapes, kernels, and operating policy. DLRM embedding lookups can make memory traffic a substantial energy contributor, which must be measured alongside dense computation and static power.

Operating policy moves the power profile as well. Batch sizing creates a trade-off: larger batches can lower energy per inference, while also changing activation traffic, execution time, and instantaneous power. At the edge, the duty cycle governs the outcome through the duty-cycle average power of [[01 Books/Volume I - Foundations/03 Optimization/12 Benchmarking/V1-12.08 Power and Energy Measurement#^sec-benchmarking-battery-thermal-benchmarking-79dd|section 12.7.2]], where the keyword-spotting case shows the idle floor setting battery life.

### MLPerf Power Case Study

^sec-benchmarking-mlperf-power-case-study-a554

MLPerf Power ([[01 Books/Volume I - Foundations/03 Optimization/12 Benchmarking/V1-12.14 Source References#^ref-tschand2025|Tschand et al. 2024]]) translates raw electrical measurements into standardized efficiency metrics: completed inferences per second per watt under a rigorously defined system boundary. This protocol applies uniform measurement principles across data center, edge, and tiny inference scenarios, where the limiting constraint shifts from rack cooling and operating cost to battery runtime and microwatt energy harvesting.

Standardizing the boundary prevents misleading comparisons across architectural families. By specifying instrumentation, measurement boundaries, matched logging windows, and the applicable workload constraints, MLPerf Power makes efficiency results interpretable within the tested scenario.

Over successive benchmark submission rounds from 2021 to 2024, standardized measurements capture how architectural improvements, compiler optimizations, and lower-precision arithmetic translate into production efficiency gains. Tracking normalized energy efficiency (samples per joule) across eight submission cycles from v1.0 to v4.1, [[01 Books/Volume I - Foundations/03 Optimization/12 Benchmarking/V1-12.08 Power and Energy Measurement#^fig-power-trends|figure 11]] charts diverging efficiency trajectories across seven distinct MLPerf Inference workloads.

![[90 Assets/Volume I/12 Benchmarking/Mlperf Power Datacenter 0233393c08.png]]

Figure 11: **Data-Center Energy Efficiency Gains**: Each series is normalized to its own first plotted version, so compare improvement within a workload rather than absolute efficiency across workloads. Source: ([[01 Books/Volume I - Foundations/03 Optimization/12 Benchmarking/V1-12.14 Source References#^ref-tschand2025|Tschand et al. 2024]]).

^fig-power-trends

RetinaNet and ResNet show the largest plotted gains, each reaching 3$\times$ its initial normalized efficiency. BERT and GPT-J reach about 1.9$\times$, while DLRM-v2 reaches about 1.7$\times$, Llama 2 about 1.5$\times$, and RNN-T about 1.3$\times$. These trajectories combine changes in hardware, software, numerical formats, and submitted configurations. Static convolutional shapes can benefit from supported low-precision kernels and fusion; low-batch decoding and sparse embedding lookups often give memory improvements greater importance. The plotted ratios alone do not isolate how much each mechanism contributed.

Timing protocols and power instrumentation provide the raw data for benchmarking. Raw data alone, however, does not guarantee sound conclusions. Converting measurements into meaningful comparisons requires understanding the systematic sources of error, bias, and misalignment that can make even carefully collected benchmark numbers misleading.
