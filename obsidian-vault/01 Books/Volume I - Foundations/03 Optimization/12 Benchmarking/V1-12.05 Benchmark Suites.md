# V1-12.05 Benchmark Suites

[[Home|Home]] · [[01 Books/Volume I - Foundations/Volume 1 Overview|Volume Overview]] · [[01 Books/Volume I - Foundations/03 Optimization/12 Benchmarking/V1-12 Benchmarking|V1-12 Benchmarking]]

^sec-benchmarking-system-benchmarking-suites-e946

Without that shared measurement protocol, a team evaluating five candidate system-on-chip (SoC) designs for the camera trap’s next board faces incompatible claims. Vendor A reports 8 TOPS (trillion operations per second) at INT8; Vendor B reports 15 TOPS at INT4; Vendor C reports inference latency on a proprietary model; Vendor D cites MLPerf scores from two generations ago; Vendor E provides only peak throughput at maximum batch size. None of these figures can be directly compared because each vendor isolates a favorable metric under idiosyncratic conditions, obscuring true operating trade-offs. Standardized benchmark suites resolve this fragmentation by enforcing uniform measurement harnesses and evaluation rules across every submission.

Each generation of benchmark suites arose to close a gap of exactly this kind, when the metrics of the day stopped predicting what users experienced. Their history explains the rules modern suites enforce.

### From Whetstone to MLPerf

^sec-benchmarking-historical-context-7350

Three shifts mark that history: from isolated kernels to representative application suites, from single-metric throughput to multi-objective energy profiles, and from generic compute metrics to domain-specific execution constraints. The earliest computing benchmarks were open to benchmark gaming. Whetstone ([[01 Books/Volume I - Foundations/03 Optimization/12 Benchmarking/V1-12.14 Source References#^ref-curnow1976whetstone|Curnow and Wichmann 1976]]) used a synthetic mix of scientific operations, while LINPACK ([[01 Books/Volume I - Foundations/03 Optimization/12 Benchmarking/V1-12.14 Source References#^ref-dongarra1979linpack|Dongarra et al. 1979]]) measured dense linear-system solving. Because both relied on predictable computational loops, compiler and hardware vendors could optimize for the benchmark kernels (unrolling synthetic loops, or tailoring cache tiling to exact matrix dimensions) without improving general application performance. SPEC CPU (1989) addressed this by evaluating complete, portable software suites that stressed integer branching, irregular memory hierarchies, and floating-point pipelines ([[01 Books/Volume I - Foundations/03 Optimization/12 Benchmarking/V1-12.14 Source References#^ref-dixit1993spec|Dixit 1993]]). The same vulnerability applies to ML. Just as compilers were tuned to Whetstone’s loops, kernels and compression schemes can be tuned to one model without helping any other, so model compression claims require validation on representative tasks such as ResNet-50 and BERT across the full software runtime.

Distributed computing added a further lesson, that component-level microbenchmarks fail to predict cluster-level throughput when interconnect latency, serialization, and collective communication dominate wall-clock time, as they do in distributed ML training. To capture these interactions, DAWNBench ([[01 Books/Volume I - Foundations/03 Optimization/12 Benchmarking/V1-12.14 Source References#^ref-coleman2019|Coleman et al. 2019]]) pioneered time-to-accuracy evaluation, tying hardware throughput to algorithmic convergence. Maximizing samples per second has no engineering value if numerical instability or poor batch sizing prevents the model from reaching its target validation accuracy. MLPerf[[01 Books/Volume I - Foundations/03 Optimization/12 Benchmarking/V1-12.15 Footnotes#^fn13|¹³]] (2018) combined these lessons and institutionalized full-system measurement, multi-objective constraints, and representative workloads spanning vision, language, and recommendation ([[01 Books/Volume I - Foundations/03 Optimization/12 Benchmarking/V1-12.14 Source References#^ref-mattson2020mlperf|Mattson et al. 2020]]; [[01 Books/Volume I - Foundations/03 Optimization/12 Benchmarking/V1-12.14 Source References#^ref-reddi2020|Reddi et al. 2019]]).

Energy followed the same path. SPEC Power (2007) ([[01 Books/Volume I - Foundations/03 Optimization/12 Benchmarking/V1-12.14 Source References#^ref-lange2009|Lange 2009]]) and Green500 (2007) ([[01 Books/Volume I - Foundations/03 Optimization/12 Benchmarking/V1-12.14 Source References#^ref-feng2007green500|Feng and Cameron 2007]]) made performance per watt a ranking metric for servers and supercomputers. MLPerf Power ([[01 Books/Volume I - Foundations/03 Optimization/12 Benchmarking/V1-12.14 Source References#^ref-mlperf-power-website-81527e9b|MLCommons 2024b]]) carried it to ML workloads, whose rapid power swings and shared host components demand calibrated instruments and explicit measurement boundaries ([[01 Books/Volume I - Foundations/03 Optimization/12 Benchmarking/V1-12.08 Power and Energy Measurement#^sec-benchmarking-standardized-power-measurement-7fae|section 12.7.4]]).

Energy benchmarking also exposes algorithmic efficiency. Compression and efficient architectures such as MobileNet ([[01 Books/Volume I - Foundations/03 Optimization/12 Benchmarking/V1-12.14 Source References#^ref-howard2017mobilenets|Howard et al. 2017]]; [[01 Books/Volume I - Foundations/03 Optimization/12 Benchmarking/V1-12.14 Source References#^ref-sandler2018|Sandler et al. 2018]]) save energy by reducing operations and off-chip traffic. Traffic reduction is a primary lever, because a DRAM read costs orders of magnitude more energy than an arithmetic operation (**[[01 Books/Volume I - Foundations/03 Optimization/10 Model Compression/V1-10.04 Numerical Precision Changing How Values Are Stored#^sec-model-compression-energy-costs-d1d5|Where precision saves energy]]**).

As specialized accelerators emerged, generic benchmarks failed to capture domain-specific execution constraints. Data center training clusters optimize aggregate throughput within rack-level power envelopes, mobile AI operates within thermal limits (typically under 5 watts), and embedded microcontrollers function within milliwatt budgets. These budgets decide whether a benchmark prioritizes samples per second or joules per inference. Application requirements add functional constraints such as calibration for clinical models, hard tail-latency cutoffs for trading systems, and bounded worst-case latency for autonomous vehicles.

Because ML workloads couple tensor execution units, memory bandwidth, and communication fabrics, no single metric or workload can characterize a system. MLPerf therefore splits into distinct deployment suites. MLPerf Training evaluates multi-node scaling and high-bandwidth interconnects under time-to-quality targets ([[01 Books/Volume I - Foundations/03 Optimization/12 Benchmarking/V1-12.14 Source References#^ref-mattson2020mlperf|Mattson et al. 2020]]). MLPerf Inference measures throughput and latency percentiles under latency SLOs across server and client systems ([[01 Books/Volume I - Foundations/03 Optimization/12 Benchmarking/V1-12.14 Source References#^ref-reddi2020|Reddi et al. 2019]]). MLPerf Tiny tests ultra-low-power microcontrollers operating with tight memory constraints ([[01 Books/Volume I - Foundations/03 Optimization/12 Benchmarking/V1-12.14 Source References#^ref-banbury2021mlperftiny|Banbury et al. 2021]]). The cross-cutting MLPerf Power track measures useful work per watt across each environment. Reading the constraint column of [[01 Books/Volume I - Foundations/03 Optimization/12 Benchmarking/V1-12.05 Benchmark Suites#^tbl-mlperf-suites|table 8]] from top to bottom, cluster-scale interconnect bandwidth gives way to latency percentiles at the edge, and finally to strict memory footprints on microcontrollers.

### ML Measurement Challenges

^sec-benchmarking-ml-measurement-challenges-60ea

Three lessons from benchmark history (representative workloads, multi-objective evaluation, and integrated system measurement) converge on a requirement unique to machine learning: managing statistical approximation. Traditional computing benchmarks specify correctness checks, which may require exact outputs or permit stated numerical tolerances. In ML systems, low-level engineering choices routinely alter numerical output: aggressive weight quantization, kernel pruning, or reduced-precision accumulators trade accuracy for execution speed and lower memory bandwidth pressure. Consequently, an ML benchmark cannot evaluate execution time in isolation; output accuracy serves as an inviolable operational constraint.

Evaluating an ML system therefore means testing the coupled Data, Algorithm, and Machine (D·A·M) choices together rather than measuring raw compute throughput. A suite earns trust by solving three problems. It must produce valid measurements despite run-to-run noise, finite test sets, unrepresentative workloads, and misaligned metrics. It must then isolate what the hardware-software stack contributes, which is the job of a system benchmark and its roofline diagnostic. Finally, it must make results comparable across organizations, which is the job of community standardization.

ML systems combine several sources of measurement variability that classical software benchmarks were not designed to evaluate together. These fluctuations originate at both the machine and algorithmic layers. On the machine side, sustained execution pushes accelerators against thermal and power envelopes, triggering DVFS, which throttles processor clocks mid-run. Host operating-system activity (thread scheduling jitter, memory paging, and PCIe bus contention during batch staging) further introduces latency variance across runs. On the algorithmic side, parallel execution threads can change the order of floating-point reductions, which shifts results slightly. Mini-batch shuffling, pseudo-random augmentations, stochastic dropout masks, and weight initialization also perturb the trajectory that stochastic gradient descent takes across a non-convex loss surface.

Distinguishing a genuine systems optimization from background measurement noise requires isolating these overlapping sources of variance with repeated runs, paired comparisons, and confidence intervals. Those statistical controls apply to every benchmark in the chapter, so [[01 Books/Volume I - Foundations/03 Optimization/12 Benchmarking/V1-12.09 Benchmarking Best Practices#^sec-benchmarking-statistical-methodological-issues-7aa5|section 12.8.1]] collects them in one place, including how large a test set must be before it can resolve a one-point accuracy difference.

Beyond run-to-run variance, benchmark validity depends on workload representativeness. Synthetic microbenchmarks generate uniform, statically sized tensors directly in accelerator HBM. By executing continuous, compute-bound GEMM operations in an isolated loop, microbenchmarks measure theoretical peak arithmetic throughput. However, they conceal the memory hierarchies and scheduling bottlenecks that dominate production deployments. Synthetic workloads bypass host-to-device PCIe data transfers, input decoding pipelines, dynamic memory allocation churn, and GPU cache evictions. In contrast, **trace-based benchmarking** replays timestamped request logs recorded from live serving clusters. Production traces capture Poisson and bursty request arrival distributions, variable sequence lengths, and concurrent query streams. Replaying live traces forces the serving infrastructure to manage dynamic batching (grouping requests that arrive within a short time window into one batch), key-value (KV) cache allocation under memory pressure, and queuing delays, stress-testing the system under operational conditions that synthetic steady-state loops obscure.

↩︎ The key-value (KV) cache, the per-request attention state that grows with every generated token, is established in **[[01 Books/Volume I - Foundations/02 Development/06 Network Architectures/V1-06.08 Transformers Parallel Sequence Processing#^sec-network-architectures-system-implications-77ac|System implications]]**.

A representative workload can still mislead if the evaluation metric fails to reflect deployment constraints. When an engineering team optimizes a single algorithmic figure of merit (such as an offline BLEU score, top-1 classification accuracy, or token perplexity) in isolation from system SLOs, the metric ceases to measure genuine operational fitness. This divergence is the chapter’s second principle, Goodhart’s Law, operating inside a single team’s choice of metric, and once the proxy becomes the sole optimization target it stops reporting overall system health. In neural machine translation, an offline accuracy optimization can break the serving deadline outright.

**Example 12.1: Goodhart's Law in action**

**Scenario**: An engineering team optimizes a neural machine translation model for offline BLEU score, increasing the beam search size (the number of candidate partial translations kept at each decoding step) from 1 to 10.

**Diagnosis**: The larger beam search achieves a 0.5-point BLEU gain on paper but increases candidate evaluations by 10×, increasing inference latency from 50 ms to 200 ms (4× slowdown), exceeding the serving deadline.

**Systems lesson**: Optimizing offline accuracy without latency constraints invites Goodhart’s Law failures. Production benchmarks must enforce strict SLOs for serving latency (e.g., latency \< 100 ms).

^exmp-benchmarking-goodharts-law-action

The translation example shows an algorithmic change (a 10× wider beam) multiplying computational load and memory traffic for a marginal quality gain while violating a hardware latency deadline.

A fixed benchmark also assesses execution under an unvarying input distribution, which conceals how systems degrade under production distribution shifts, so model and data evaluation must test those distribution limits separately. System comparisons ask a different question. They hold the model and dataset fixed to isolate how efficiently the infrastructure executes the workload, and standardized suites like MLPerf make that control explicit. They establish reference workloads, datasets, quality targets, and permitted implementation changes, enabling reproducible comparisons of hardware-software execution efficiency within a specified benchmark version and division.

### System Benchmarks

^sec-benchmarking-system-benchmarks-393c

Hardware specifications advertise peak arithmetic rates that application workloads do not sustain. ResNet-50 training, for example, achieves far less model arithmetic than an H100’s dense FP16 peak ([[01 Books/Volume I - Foundations/03 Optimization/12 Benchmarking/V1-12.02 The Benchmark–Production Gap#^sec-benchmarking-machine-learning-benchmarking-framework-70b8|section 12.1]]). System benchmarks expose that gap by running standardized ML workloads across hardware architectures, memory systems, and interconnects rather than relying on spec-sheet peaks alone.

The underlying hardware, whether CPUs, GPUs, Tensor Processing Units (TPUs),[[01 Books/Volume I - Foundations/03 Optimization/12 Benchmarking/V1-12.15 Footnotes#^fn14|¹⁴]] or application-specific integrated circuits (ASICs),[[01 Books/Volume I - Foundations/03 Optimization/12 Benchmarking/V1-12.15 Footnotes#^fn15|¹⁵]] determines ML-system speed, efficiency, and scale. System benchmarks provide standardized methods for comparing hardware across AI workloads by computational throughput, memory bandwidth, power efficiency, operator coverage, and scaling ([[01 Books/Volume I - Foundations/03 Optimization/12 Benchmarking/V1-12.14 Source References#^ref-reddi2020|Reddi et al. 2019]]; [[01 Books/Volume I - Foundations/03 Optimization/12 Benchmarking/V1-12.14 Source References#^ref-mattson2020mlperf|Mattson et al. 2020]]).

System benchmarks serve two functions. For practitioners, they enable informed hardware selection by providing comparative data across configurations. For manufacturers, they quantify generational improvements and guide accelerator development. Both functions require separating what the hardware contributed from what the model and data contributed.

**Definition 12.2: Machine learning system benchmarks**

***Machine learning system benchmarks*** are standardized evaluation protocols that hold the workload and quality target constant while varying the hardware-software stack, measuring ${\eta_{\text{hw}}} = {R_{\text{sustained}}}/ {R_{\text{peak}}}$ (the measured sustained rate ${R_{\text{sustained}}}$ over the peak rate) and ${L_{\text{lat}}}$ to isolate infrastructure efficiency from algorithmic improvements.

1.  **Significance**: The same ResNet-50 model can report the same ImageNet Top-1 accuracy while delivering very different throughput across hardware stacks, precision formats, batch sizes, and compiler configurations. Only a system benchmark exposes that gap.
2.  **Distinction**: Unlike algorithmic benchmarks (which vary the Data and Algorithm factors, $\text{Samples/Task}$ and $\text{FLOPs/Sample}$, whose product is the operation count, to improve convergence or accuracy), system benchmarks hold the algorithm and quality target fixed and vary the implementation (kernel libraries, quantization formats, batch sizes, and hardware generations). They measure how efficiently the hardware-software stack executes the workload. In iron-law terms, an optimization lowers the realized Machine factor ($\text{Seconds/FLOP}$, the time each operation costs) across compute (${O}/({R_{\text{peak}}} \cdot {\eta_{\text{hw}}})$), data movement (${D_{\text{vol}}}/{\text{BW}}$), and fixed latency (${L_{\text{lat}}}$).
3.  **Common pitfall**: A frequent misconception is that a system benchmark result generalizes across workloads. An accelerator that achieves high utilization on ResNet-50 (a compute-friendly vision workload) may achieve much lower utilization on a recommendation system, whose embedding lookups are bound by memory capacity and bandwidth rather than arithmetic. System benchmarks are workload-specific; no single metric characterizes a hardware platform.

The ImageNet challenge shows why separating the hardware’s contribution from the model’s is hard to do after the fact. Between 2010 and 2014, top-5 error (the blue line in [[01 Books/Volume I - Foundations/03 Optimization/12 Benchmarking/V1-12.05 Benchmark Suites#^fig-imagenet-gpus|figure 6]]) fell from 28.2 percent to 7.3 percent, with the steepest single drop at AlexNet’s arrival in 2012. Over the same years, GPU-accelerated entries (the green bars) rose from zero to over 100 ([[01 Books/Volume I - Foundations/03 Optimization/12 Benchmarking/V1-12.14 Source References#^ref-krizhevsky2012imagenet|Krizhevsky et al. 2012]]; [[01 Books/Volume I - Foundations/03 Optimization/12 Benchmarking/V1-12.14 Source References#^ref-russakovsky2015|Russakovsky et al. 2015]]; [[01 Books/Volume I - Foundations/03 Optimization/12 Benchmarking/V1-12.14 Source References#^ref-gray2015ilsvrcgpu|Gray 2015]]). These paired series plot contemporaneous empirical trends, not a causal attribution of how much accuracy gain stemmed from hardware throughput versus algorithmic design, training schedules, or dataset expansion. A system benchmark avoids that ambiguity by construction. It fixes the model and quality target so that any change in the result belongs to the hardware-software stack.

While [[01 Books/Volume I - Foundations/03 Optimization/12 Benchmarking/V1-12.10 Model and Data Evaluation#^sec-benchmarking-model-benchmarking-4847|section 12.9.1]] evaluates model compression against task accuracy baselines, system benchmarking isolates the execution efficiency of the hardware-software stack under a fixed model. Modern accelerators rarely sustain theoretical peak throughput because physical execution depends on the coupling between tensor shapes and memory hierarchies. ResNet-50 training, which the roofline places on the compute roof, sustains about 12.4 percent of an H100’s peak (notebook [12.1](https://mlsysbook.ai/vol1/12_benchmarking/12_benchmarking.html#nbk-benchmarking-resnet50-roofline-vs-measured)). Parameter-heavy transformer decodes and activation-heavy convolutional passes stress entirely different levels of the memory subsystem, so the same accelerator sustains very different fractions of its peak on the two workloads.

Measuring sustained utilization rather than peak capability changes two further parts of a system benchmark, its energy accounting and its multi-accelerator scope. First, energy per unit of work depends on utilization, because an underutilized accelerator still draws substantial static power while producing little computation, and performance per watt varies widely across hardware platforms. Second, multi-accelerator execution introduces communication boundaries. While multi-node cluster scaling involves network-topology effects beyond this book’s single-node scope, intra-node benchmarking must account for interconnect bandwidth, memory sharing across accelerators, and gradient synchronization over NVLink or PCIe.

### Community-Driven Standardization

^sec-benchmarking-communitydriven-standardization-5c56

Without shared measurement boundaries, utilization numbers cannot be compared. If one benchmark includes host-side preprocessing and PCIe transfers while another measures only device kernel execution, or if one power meter reads only the accelerator voltage rail while another includes host processors and chassis fans, the resulting figures reflect incompatible boundaries rather than architectural efficiency. No single vendor can resolve this discrepancy, because each has a commercial incentive to draw the boundary around its own hardware strengths. Formal standards bodies move too slowly for hardware and model structures that change every year, so consortia such as MLCommons maintain living benchmark suites. These pair open-source reference implementations with compliance rules so that numbers from different laboratories describe comparable workloads under the same version, division, quality target, and scenario.

Standardizing system benchmarks requires formalizing the D·A·M taxonomy so that evaluations isolate the Machine without confounding changes to the Algorithm or Data. The MLPerf Training benchmark enforces this separation by measuring time-to-train to a specified quality target rather than raw floating-point throughput or epoch duration ([[01 Books/Volume I - Foundations/03 Optimization/12 Benchmarking/V1-12.14 Source References#^ref-mattson2020mlperf|Mattson et al. 2020]]). High TFLOP/s from aggressive low-precision scaling helps only if faster steps outweigh any extra work needed to reach the target, and a run that misses the target is invalid.

MLPerf splits submissions into two divisions. In the closed division, every submitter must run the reference model and reach the same quality target, so differences in time-to-train belong to the system. The open division permits model and algorithm changes, so its results compare whole solutions rather than machines. Within the closed division, the rules constrain the workload while permitting specified tunable hyperparameters and approved numerical formats. Quality and compliance checks establish which hardware, compiler, and training-configuration improvements constitute valid submissions.

The MLPerf Inference benchmark applies this discipline to production deployment constraints ([[01 Books/Volume I - Foundations/03 Optimization/12 Benchmarking/V1-12.14 Source References#^ref-reddi2020|Reddi et al. 2019]]). An accelerator cannot simply report peak throughput under infinite batching if interactive serving requires guaranteed responsiveness. The benchmark therefore fixes how queries arrive, through execution scenarios matched to physical operating regimes: one query at a time for a single interactive user, randomly arriving queries under a tail-latency bound for a shared service, and all inputs supplied upfront for bulk throughput. By standardizing these execution boundaries, community benchmarks account for queueing delays and host-to-device PCIe transfers, making the tested load and timing boundary explicit when interpreting a throughput claim.

#### MLPerf Execution Scenarios

^sec-benchmarking-mlperf-execution-scenarios-89f0

The same hardware can report dramatically different benchmark numbers depending on *how* requests arrive, which explains why vendor claims often fail to predict production performance. Classic MLPerf Inference defines four execution scenarios that characterize distinct traffic patterns, each requiring different optimization strategies ([[01 Books/Volume I - Foundations/03 Optimization/12 Benchmarking/V1-12.14 Source References#^ref-reddi2020|Reddi et al. 2019]]). Beyond the four classic scenarios below, current client and generative-AI benchmark variants also include interactive measurements for latency-sensitive large language model (LLM) workloads, where time to first token and time per output token, the two measured quantities defined in [[01 Books/Volume I - Foundations/03 Optimization/12 Benchmarking/V1-12.07 Inference Benchmarks#^sec-benchmarking-token-latency-ttft-tpot-4e1a|section 12.6.2.2]], become central ([[01 Books/Volume I - Foundations/03 Optimization/12 Benchmarking/V1-12.14 Source References#^ref-mlperf-client-website-a86cc0e0|MLCommons 2026b]]). Both track what a user experiences while reading a response as it streams.

##### SingleStream

^sec-benchmarking-singlestream-c709

SingleStream processes one request at a time, measuring latency for sequential inference. This scenario models mobile and embedded applications where a single user interacts with the device: a smartphone camera app classifying images, a voice assistant processing speech, or a wearable detecting gestures. The key metric is a tail percentile of per-query latency (the 90th percentile in MLPerf’s rules), and inter-request batching is unavailable because the next query arrives after the previous one completes. Optimization focuses on preprocessing efficiency and power consumption rather than throughput.

##### MultiStream

^sec-benchmarking-multistream-38d1

MultiStream processes multiple synchronized input streams simultaneously, modeling scenarios like autonomous vehicles with multiple cameras that must be processed together for spatial fusion. The benchmark measures how long the system takes to finish a group of inputs that arrive together. In current rules, each query carries several samples (one per camera, for example), the next query waits until the previous one completes, and the score is a specified latency percentile over these grouped queries. Earlier versions used fixed arrival intervals. Grouped-query latency helps assess multi-input execution; it does not establish a hard-deadline guarantee for a vehicle planning loop.

##### Server

^sec-benchmarking-server-e69f

Server generates requests following a Poisson distribution, simulating cloud API traffic where requests arrive independently and unpredictably. This scenario models web services handling millions of queries from different users. Unlike SingleStream’s guaranteed sequential arrival, Server traffic creates queuing dynamics where multiple requests compete for resources. The key metrics are throughput (queries per second) and the workload’s tail-latency bound, typically set at the 99th percentile, and dynamic batching can improve efficiency by grouping requests that arrive within a time window. Optimization balances throughput against latency SLOs.

##### Offline

^sec-benchmarking-offline-6ece

Offline provides all inputs upfront, measuring maximum throughput when latency constraints are removed. This scenario models batch processing pipelines: overnight data processing, scientific computing, or precomputing recommendations. With no per-query latency requirement, systems can tune batch size for sustained throughput within memory and execution limits. The key metric is pure throughput (samples per second), and optimization focuses entirely on hardware efficiency. The contrast with Server explains why one accelerator reports very different numbers in the two scenarios. Offline can fill every batch from inputs already waiting in memory. Server must answer each randomly arriving query within its tail-latency bound, so it cannot hold queries long enough to form full batches, and its sustained QPS typically falls well below the Offline figure on the same hardware.

| **Scenario**     | **Context**                         | **Strategy**                         | **Focus**                                  |
|:-----------------|:------------------------------------|:-------------------------------------|:-------------------------------------------|
| **SingleStream** | Mobile apps, embedded devices       | No batching (batch = 1)              | Preprocessing, power efficiency            |
| **MultiStream**  | Autonomous driving, video analytics | Synchronized sensor fusion           | Grouped-query latency percentile           |

Matching the scenario to deployment context determines which benchmark results are relevant.

**Self-Check: Question**

1.  Why did computing benchmark methodology historically transition away from synthetic instruction-mix microbenchmarks (such as Whetstone and Dhrystone) to representative application suites (such as SPEC CPU)?

    2.  Representative application suites were easier to implement and did not require source code compilation
