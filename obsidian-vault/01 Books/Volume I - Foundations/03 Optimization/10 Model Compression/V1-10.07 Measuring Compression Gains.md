# V1-10.07 Measuring Compression Gains

[[Home|Home]] · [[01 Books/Volume I - Foundations/Volume 1 Overview|Volume Overview]] · [[01 Books/Volume I - Foundations/03 Optimization/10 Model Compression/V1-10 Model Compression|V1-10 Model Compression]]

^sec-model-compression-efficiency-measurement-2424

Shrinking a model’s weights by $4\times$ with INT8 quantization rarely cuts end-to-end latency by $4\times$, because cache residency, memory bus contention, and kernel launch overhead absorb much of the theoretical gain. Every saving computed so far in this chapter is a paper bound, and only a measurement on the target device turns a bound into a result. Profiling on the deployment hardware identifies which resource limits each layer, fixes an honest baseline, and separates model execution time from runtime and pipeline overhead.

### Profiling and Opportunity Analysis

^sec-model-compression-profiling-opportunity-analysis-477f

Compressing the layers with the most parameters or FLOPs can leave wall-clock latency barely changed when low-intensity operators, such as layer normalization or elementwise activations, contribute almost no FLOPs yet consume a large share of execution time by streaming whole activation tensors across memory. The top-down profiling method of **[[01 Books/Volume I - Foundations/02 Development/08 Model Training/V1-08.06 Identifying Bottlenecks#^sec-model-training-identifying-bottlenecks-f57f|Identifying Bottlenecks]]** tells compression where to act, and two of its measurements decide the technique. Static footprint (weight tensors and persistent buffers) decides whether parameters fit in fast memory or device DRAM without spilling across PCIe, while dynamic allocation (activation tensors and KV caches) sets peak residency and so bounds batch size and serving concurrency. Alongside memory residency, each operator’s arithmetic intensity (${I}= {O}/{D_{\text{vol}}}$) shows whether removing FLOPs or removing bytes will actually shorten it.

On an edge accelerator, the FLOPs and the run time of a vision transformer (ViT) fall on different operators. Per token and layer, for hidden dimension ${d}$, the multilayer perceptron (MLP) block costs about $8{d}^2$ multiply-accumulates against $4{d}^2$ for the query, key, value, and output projections. In a ViT-Base-sized model the MLP blocks therefore carry nearly two-thirds of FLOPs and the projections just under one-third, with the attention score and weighting products adding only a few percent at standard image resolution. Layer normalization inverts the picture, contributing almost no FLOPs yet claiming a visible share of wall-clock latency, because it streams whole activation tensors with little arithmetic per byte. The camera pipeline’s MobileNetV2 splits the same way in convolutional form. Its $1{\times}1$ expansion and projection layers carry most of the multiply-accumulates ([[01 Books/Volume I - Foundations/03 Optimization/10 Model Compression/V1-10.11 Source References#^ref-sandler2018|Sandler et al. 2018]]), while its depthwise layers, like layer normalization, do little arithmetic per byte moved and take a larger share of time than of work.

Profiling isolates the governing physical bottleneck for each component, which calls for targeted optimizations rather than uniform compression. Structured pruning of MLP hidden units relieves the compute-dominant blocks. Kernel fusion eliminates intermediate global memory round-trips for memory-bound normalization layers. INT8 quantization shrinks the static footprint that the MLP and projection weights dominate.

Translating layer-level profiling metrics into end-to-end system speedup requires accounting for the complete serving pipeline. In production serving systems, model execution frequently represents only a fraction of total latency, with the remainder consumed by image decoding, tokenization, RPC deserialization, or network transfer. Optimizing the model in isolation encounters an Amdahl ceiling whenever non-model stages dominate the latency budget, a ceiling that **[[01 Books/Volume I - Foundations/03 Optimization/12 Benchmarking/V1-12.04 Benchmarking Granularity#^sec-benchmarking-endtoend-vs-component-latency-9952|End-to-end vs. component latency]]** measures end to end.

**Systems Perspective 10.4: FLOPs reduction is not proportional speedup**

Reducing a model’s FLOPs by 50 percent does not guarantee 50 percent latency reduction. Memory-bound operations see little benefit from arithmetic reduction because data movement, not multiplication, sets their time. Amdahl’s law (principle [[01 Books/Volume I - Foundations/03 Optimization/V1 Optimization Principles#^pri-amdahl|8]]) applies at the system level. If model inference accounts for only 20 percent of end-to-end latency (with the remaining 80 percent spent on data loading, preprocessing, and postprocessing), then even perfect model optimization yields at most 1.25× overall speedup. Tokenization and network transfer can occupy the non-model fraction in language-model serving; image decoding, resizing, and normalization can do the same in vision pipelines. Profile the complete path on target hardware before estimating optimization benefits.

Complementing latency and throughput profiles, layer-wise sensitivity analysis quantifies how individual layers tolerate perturbation. In deep networks, different layers tolerate different amounts of precision reduction or weight removal. Profiling accuracy degradation while systematically perturbing one layer at a time, with quantization noise or weight removal, identifies which components require high-precision representations and which can absorb aggressive 4-bit quantization or structured pruning without compromising task accuracy. Once opportunity and sensitivity profiles have guided a compression pass, the resulting candidate must be evaluated across both statistical quality and physical execution metrics.

### Measuring Optimization Effectiveness

^sec-model-compression-measuring-optimization-effectiveness-a3b2

Judging a compressed model by a single scalar metric, such as top-1 accuracy or parameter count, can approve a candidate whose batch-one latency barely budges under kernel-launch overhead or whose confidence calibration drifts past safety limits on rare subgroups. Evaluating compression requires balancing statistical quality against physical execution costs across the Data, Algorithm, and Machine (D·A·M) axes. Quality baselines must capture aggregate accuracy, confidence calibration (verifying that predicted probabilities reflect empirical correctness), and performance across underrepresented input slices. Simultaneously, efficiency baselines must record peak memory residency, latency distributions (including p99 tail latency), memory bus saturation, and per-inference energy.

ResNet-50, the camera pipeline’s teacher and the book’s compute-bound lighthouse, shows how one compression decision ripples across physical and statistical dimensions. Quantizing it from FP32 to INT8 on the reference H100 changes latency, throughput, artifact size, energy, accuracy, and confidence calibration at once, and a deployment profile must report all six rather than an isolated accuracy number ([[01 Books/Volume I - Foundations/03 Optimization/10 Model Compression/V1-10.07 Measuring Compression Gains#^tbl-resnet50-int8-profile|table 13]]).

| **Metric**                       | **FP32 baseline** | **INT8 result**     | **Deployment reading**                                                |
|:---------------------------------|:------------------|:--------------------|:----------------------------------------------------------------------|
| **Top-1 accuracy**               | 76.1%             | 75.8% (0.3 pp drop) | Aggregate accuracy mostly holds, but subgroup checks still matter.    |
| **Batch-one latency**            | 0.38 ms           | 0.30 ms (1.3×)      | Fixed per-request costs dilute the gain for one image.                |
| **Large-batch throughput**       | 15,200 img/s      | 89,470 img/s (5.9×) | INT8 Tensor Cores pay off once arithmetic dominates.                  |
| **Model size**                   | 102.2 MB          | 25.6 MB (4×)        | The artifact becomes easier to cache, transmit, and deploy on edge.   |
| **Energy/inference**             | 46.0 mJ           | 7.8 mJ (5.9×)       | Assumes full board power at large batch; measure it directly.         |
| **Confidence-calibration error** | 2.1%              | 3.4%                | Confidence calibration can drift even when accuracy looks acceptable. |

Table 13: **ResNet-50 INT8 Deployment Profile on the Reference H100**: Latency and FP32 throughput come from the modeled H100 engine profile, INT8 throughput is the MLPerf Inference measurement, and the accuracy and calibration values are illustrative. A real profile must measure all six on the target system.

^tbl-resnet50-int8-profile

Converting weights to INT8 shrinks the artifact by 4× (from 102.2 MB to 25.6 MB), but the speed gain depends on how the model is served. At batch one, a compiled INT8 engine is only 1.3× faster than its FP32 counterpart (0.30 ms against 0.38 ms), because launch overhead, per-kernel floors, and the weight read form most of a single image’s time and shrink little with precision. At large batch the arithmetic dominates, and INT8 throughput of 89,470 img/s, as MLPerf Inference measured it, is 5.9× the modeled FP32 rate, a ratio between a measured rate and a modeled one from different software stacks, so it shows the direction and rough size of the gain rather than a controlled comparison. Energy per image follows throughput only if the board draws its full 700 W in both precisions; the 5.9× energy figure is that assumption, not a separate measurement. The runtime comparison in **[[01 Books/Volume I - Foundations/04 Deployment/13 Model Serving/V1-13.07 Inference Runtime Selection#^sec-model-serving-inference-runtime-selection-5eef|Inference Runtime Selection]]** derives both latency figures from the same engine profile.

The quality side of the profile moves less visibly. While aggregate top-1 accuracy drops by only 0.3 percentage points (from 76.1 percent to 75.8 percent), confidence-calibration error (the gap between predicted probability and observed accuracy, distinct from the range calibration of quantization) increases from 2.1 percent to 3.4 percent. Post-quantization probability distributions drift, which can compromise safety-critical confidence thresholds even when top-1 classification accuracy appears preserved. Production deployment decisions require corresponding quality, latency, and energy measurements using a supported execution path on target hardware.

Suppose the application requires at least 75.5 percent top-1 accuracy and no more than 3.0 percent confidence-calibration error, measured with the same validation data and calibration metric as the profile. The INT8 candidate passes the accuracy limit but fails the calibration limit. Its modeled resource savings therefore do not justify acceptance. Retain the baseline for comparison, try confidence recalibration or a less aggressive quantization configuration, and repeat the quality checks before validating resource gains on the target runtime.

A deployment profile shows whether a compressed model meets its limits but not why it misses one. Explaining a failure such as the calibration miss above requires looking inside the model, at where clipping, rounding, and pruning masks have changed its internal values.

### Diagnostic Visualization

^sec-model-compression-diagnostic-visualization

An exported model can fail a quality or latency check, as the ResNet-50 candidate did when its confidence calibration drifted to 3.4 percent. Summary metrics cannot tell whether the culprit is a single activation channel saturating its clipping bounds, a pruning mask erasing an active feature detector, or an unsupported operator falling back to floating point. Diagnostic inspection looks inside the compressed graph to verify numerical integrity after framework APIs calibrate the model and hardware optimization libraries lower it to the execution substrate ([[01 Books/Volume I - Foundations/03 Optimization/10 Model Compression/V1-10.06 Choosing and Combining Techniques#^sec-model-compression-implementation-tools-4990|section 10.5.4]]). While system-scale validation and artifact monitoring belong to downstream deployment infrastructure, the immediate diagnostic task is verifying that quantization clipping, pruning masks, or sparsity patterns have not perturbed internal feature distributions beyond acceptable task error tolerances.

Quantization error histograms show whether error is broadly distributed or concentrated in outliers. Activation visualizations help detect clipping, overflow, and saturation. In a grid of first-layer convolutional filters, the top rows show distinct edge, color, and frequency detectors, while the bottom row shows uniform-looking filters that appear redundant at a glance but require measured activation tracing before they can be pruned safely ([[01 Books/Volume I - Foundations/03 Optimization/10 Model Compression/V1-10.07 Measuring Compression Gains#^fig-color-mapping|figure 31]]). Framework observers and debuggers can expose quantization ranges and tensor error, while compiler or runtime inspectors can show the graph and kernels that the exported model actually executes.

![[90 Assets/Volume I/10 Model Compression/Generated Figure b89d77e750.svg]]

Figure 31: **First-Layer Convolutional Filter Patterns**: An $8\times5$ grid groups stylized kernels into edge, color, frequency, mixed, and uniform-looking patterns. Appearance alone does not establish inactivity; pruning requires measured activation or importance evidence in addition to low weight variance.

^fig-color-mapping

Sparsity diagnostics identify candidate layers for sparse execution. The layer-wise schematic in [[01 Books/Volume I - Foundations/03 Optimization/10 Model Compression/V1-10.07 Measuring Compression Gains#^fig-sparse-heat-map|figure 32]] spans convolutional, fully connected, and transformer layer types and is not a measurement from one model or pruning run. Its rows illustrate how a pruning policy might distribute zeros unevenly across architectural blocks. A real diagnostic would pair each layer’s density with its storage format and target-kernel profile to determine whether those zeros reduce memory traffic or execution time. Graph viewers and sparsity inspection tools help locate the affected tensors; profiling establishes the deployment benefit.

![[90 Assets/Volume I/10 Model Compression/Generated Figure 7551f2a1df.svg]]

Figure 32: **Illustrative Layer-Wise Sparsity Distribution**: This schematic combines several layer types to illustrate non-uniform parameter removal. Darker tiles indicate more zero-valued weights; their shade establishes neither task redundancy nor a runtime benefit.

^fig-sparse-heat-map

The diagnostic value is the nonuniformity, not the exact shade of any one cell. Greater sparsity in later layers shows where the policy removed weights; it does not establish that those representations were redundant. Compare task quality after removing weights from a candidate layer and after recovery training, including performance on important input slices. Early-layer sparsity warrants the same checks because damaging feature extraction can propagate through subsequent layers. Only those comparisons can establish whether the observed removal pattern preserves required behavior. Each density also has a machine reading, because a layer whose zeros never cross the storage and execution break-even of [[01 Books/Volume I - Foundations/03 Optimization/10 Model Compression/V1-10.05 Hardware Implementation Changing How the Model Executes#^sec-model-compression-sparse-break-even-7e4a|section 10.4.2.2]] saves neither bytes nor time, however dark its tile.

**Checkpoint 10.5: From compression claim to deployment evidence**

A compressed artifact must pass the same application requirements as its baseline.

**Footprint and feasibility**: distinguish raw weight size from peak runtime memory, including activations, caches, metadata, and workspace.

**Modeled and measured gains**: identify which claims in the ResNet-50 profile require target-hardware measurements, and explain why fewer FLOPs or bytes alone cannot establish latency.

**Quality and operating limits**: specify the accuracy, confidence calibration, tail-latency, sustained-power, and energy checks needed before accepting a candidate.

Profiling, six-metric deployment scorecards, and layer-wise diagnostic plots turn compression from a paper-FLOP exercise into a verifiable engineering discipline, while the experimental design and statistical rigor that make benchmark comparisons reproducible across systems are developed in **[[01 Books/Volume I - Foundations/03 Optimization/12 Benchmarking/V1-12 Benchmarking#^sec-benchmarking|Benchmarking]]**. Even with those measurement tools in place, however, recurring traps across pruning, quantization, and hardware lowering can still turn a promising checkpoint into a latency regression or a silent quality failure, and the fallacies that follow collect the most common ones.
