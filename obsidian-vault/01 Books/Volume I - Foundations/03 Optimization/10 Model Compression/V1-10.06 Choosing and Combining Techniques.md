# V1-10.06 Choosing and Combining Techniques

[[Home|Home]] · [[01 Books/Volume I - Foundations/Volume 1 Overview|Volume Overview]] · [[01 Books/Volume I - Foundations/03 Optimization/10 Model Compression/V1-10 Model Compression|V1-10 Model Compression]]

^sec-model-compression-technique-selection-ba16

When the chapter’s phone decodes GPT-2 XL, its FP16 weights and KV cache claim 3.3 GB of the 8 GB that the operating system and other applications also share. Each generated token waits about 65 ms while every weight streams over the 51.2 GB/s bus, and the handset sustains only 3–5 W before thermal throttling. Choosing among compression techniques, and ordering them, starts from whichever of those physical limits is reached first, alongside the allowable task accuracy loss and the available training budget.

The three primary optimization levers target different levels of the system stack. Distillation alters the algorithmic architecture by training a compact dense student to emulate a larger teacher, executing efficiently on standard hardware without specialized runtime support. Pruning modifies tensor topology and operation patterns, but yields wall-clock acceleration only when the resulting sparsity aligns with hardware execution units, such as 2:4 structured sparse matrix cores or channel-aligned sub-matrices. Quantization alters data representation by reducing numerical bit width, directly lowering memory bus traffic and cache footprint while engaging higher-throughput integer datapaths. Their first-order trade-offs, with representative ranges for common choices, sit side by side in [[01 Books/Volume I - Foundations/03 Optimization/10 Model Compression/V1-10.06 Choosing and Combining Techniques#^tbl-optimization-tradeoffs|table 11]].

| **Technique**              | **Systems gain**                         | **Typical impact**                        | **Training cost**                   | **Hardware dependency**                       | **Region** |
|:---------------------------|:-----------------------------------------|:------------------------------------------|:------------------------------------|:----------------------------------------------|:----------:|
| **Kernel fusion**          | 10–30% latency reduction                 | No accuracy loss                          | None                                | The compiler must recognize the pattern       |     1      |
| **FP32 → BF16**            | 2$\times$ memory, ~2$\times$ throughput  | $<0.1\%$ accuracy drop                    | None for inference                  | A native BF16 path                            |     1      |
| **FP16 → INT8**            | 2$\times$ memory, 2–4$\times$ throughput | Quantization error, 0.5–1% accuracy drop  | Low for PTQ; higher for QAT         | INT8 kernels for a runtime speedup            |     2      |
| **50% pruning**            | ~2$\times$ smaller model                 | Capacity loss, 0.5–1% accuracy drop       | Usually includes fine-tuning        | Structured shapes or supported sparse kernels |     2      |
| **Knowledge distillation** | 2–10$\times$ smaller student             | Capability ceiling, 1–3% accuracy drop    | Teacher inference and a student run | Low; the student is an ordinary dense model   |     2      |
| **4-bit quantization**     | 4$\times$ memory reduction               | Significant error, 2–5% accuracy drop     | Low for PTQ; higher for QAT         | Low-bit kernels with fused dequantization     |    2–3     |
| **90% pruning**            | ~10$\times$ smaller model                | Severe capacity loss, 5–15% accuracy drop | Iterative fine-tuning               | Sparse kernels past the break-even point      |     3      |

Table 11: **Optimization Trade-Offs**: Systems gain, quality impact, training cost, and hardware dependency for common compression choices. Region 1 marks little or no quality loss, region 2 a moderate trade, and region 3 steep quality loss. The ranges are representative engineering values synthesized from published work on inference runtimes, quantization, pruning, low-bit LLM quantization, and distillation ([[01 Books/Volume I - Foundations/03 Optimization/10 Model Compression/V1-10.11 Source References#^ref-nvidia2024tensorrt|NVIDIA 2024]]; [[01 Books/Volume I - Foundations/03 Optimization/10 Model Compression/V1-10.11 Source References#^ref-jacob2018|Jacob et al. 2018]]; [[01 Books/Volume I - Foundations/03 Optimization/10 Model Compression/V1-10.11 Source References#^ref-han2015deep|Han et al. 2015]]; [[01 Books/Volume I - Foundations/03 Optimization/10 Model Compression/V1-10.11 Source References#^ref-lin2023awq|Lin et al. 2024]]; [[01 Books/Volume I - Foundations/03 Optimization/10 Model Compression/V1-10.11 Source References#^ref-hinton2015distilling|Hinton et al. 2015]]); actual results vary with architecture, task, range-calibration data, and implementation quality. The rows identify first-order candidates, not guaranteed outcomes, and the hardware dependency decides whether pruning or quantization changes latency at all.

### The Accuracy-Efficiency Frontier

^sec-model-compression-balancing-tradeoffs-6ae3

Pushing a single compression knob too far, such as pruning 90 percent of a network’s weights or quantizing sensitive layers to 4 bits without retraining, crosses a cliff where a modest extra byte saving costs 5 to 15 percentage points of task accuracy ([[01 Books/Volume I - Foundations/03 Optimization/10 Model Compression/V1-10.06 Choosing and Combining Techniques#^tbl-optimization-tradeoffs|table 11]]). Every lever in the table changes where the model sits on this **accuracy-efficiency trade-off**. Additional model capacity can improve predictive accuracy while demanding larger weight payloads, expanded activation working sets, and higher memory bandwidth during inference. On hardware with strict memory or power limits, engineers first eliminate avoidable implementation costs, then choose among feasible operating points on the accuracy-efficiency frontier, the Pareto frontier (principle [[01 Books/Volume I - Foundations/03 Optimization/V1 Optimization Principles#^pri-pareto-frontier|5]]) of this trade-off.

**Systems Perspective 10.2: The compression-accuracy trade-off curve**

The measured trade-off is not uniform. A dominated model, as defined earlier, is one that another design matches in accuracy at lower cost. A structure-preserving implementation improvement can move a dominated model toward the frontier with little or no accuracy loss. Once on an efficient frontier, improving one objective trades against another; aggressive structural changes can enter regions with steep quality penalties.

The engineering decision is where to stop. The “knee” of the curve marks a region where further resource savings incur a sharper quality penalty. It is a useful visual diagnostic, but accuracy points cannot be compared directly with milliseconds, bytes, or joules. Stop at an operating point that meets the deployment’s quality and resource thresholds, using explicit objective weights if a further trade must be ranked.

Structure-preserving techniques leave every layer and weight of the network in place. Kernel fusion and modest precision reduction (FP32 to BF16) are examples, and they can yield latency or memory gains with little quality loss when the model and execution path support them, so they move a dominated model toward the frontier. Structure-altering techniques, which include pruning of either kind and distillation, change what the network computes, and so they trade along the frontier. They yield larger reductions in memory and arithmetic volume but risk significant quality degradation without careful retraining. Aggressive precision reduction, such as INT4, also leaves the structure in place, but past the quantization plateau it trades along the frontier as well, because rounding error then costs accuracy. INT8 sits between these cases. It lies on the low-loss plateau, but low loss is not zero, so [[01 Books/Volume I - Foundations/03 Optimization/10 Model Compression/V1-10.06 Choosing and Combining Techniques#^tbl-optimization-tradeoffs|table 11]] records its small accuracy drop as a moderate trade (region 2).

Choosing where to stop on that frontier begins by identifying the limiting hardware constraint, such as memory capacity on mobile devices, frame latency in interactive pipelines, or energy per inference on battery-powered sensors. Each tier can then target the resource that actually limits the deployment. Structural methods cut parameter and operation counts, quantization shrinks memory traffic and raises arithmetic intensity, and compiler-level fusion eliminates intermediate memory round-trips. The regions in [[01 Books/Volume I - Foundations/03 Optimization/10 Model Compression/V1-10.06 Choosing and Combining Techniques#^tbl-optimization-tradeoffs|table 11]] mark where each choice typically lands on that curve, and a structured decision framework maps each limiting hardware constraint to the specific levers worth testing first.

**Checkpoint 10.4: The efficiency frontier**

Optimization can remove avoidable costs or trade among efficient operating points.

**Trade-offs**

**Accuracy vs. cost**: compare a structure-preserving optimization and a structure-altering one by naming which resource each saves and which quality risk it introduces.

**Dominated operating points**: explain how an implementation improvement can move a dominated operating point toward the frontier without reducing accuracy.

**The knee**: explain how a sharp change in the measured trade-off informs a decision, and state the quality and resource thresholds needed to choose an operating point.

### Decision Framework

^sec-model-compression-decision-framework-0d69

Applying a compression technique that targets the wrong hardware bottleneck wastes engineering cycles and task accuracy. Pruning FLOPs from a layer stalled on off-chip weight streaming leaves memory-bus latency intact, whereas quantizing weights to 8 bits on a compute-bound processor that lacks an integer matrix unit leaves arithmetic throughput unchanged. Identifying the limiting hardware constraint first narrows the search to the optimization tiers that can relieve it. Under the D·A·M taxonomy, compression methods redistribute resource demands across Algorithm (topology, pruning, distillation, and the numerical representation of weights) and Machine (execution units and memory hierarchies); range-calibration sets are the main Data-axis input. Each system constraint maps to the optimization tiers that can relieve it ([[01 Books/Volume I - Foundations/03 Optimization/10 Model Compression/V1-10.06 Choosing and Combining Techniques#^tbl-constraint-opt-mapping|table 12]]), where the marks identify mechanisms to investigate rather than guaranteed deployment gains.

|:---------------------------|:------------------------:|:-----------------------:|:---------------------------:|
| **Computational Cost**     |            ✓             |       $\triangle$       |              ✓              |
| **Memory and Storage**     |            ✓             |            ✓            |         $\triangle$         |
| **Latency and Throughput** |            ✓             |       $\triangle$       |              ✓              |
| **Energy Efficiency**      |            ✓             |            ✓            |              ✓              |
| **Scalability**            |            ✓             |       $\triangle$       |              ✓              |

^tbl-constraint-opt-mapping

One technique can move more than one term of the iron law. For example, quantizing weights from FP32 to INT8 directly relieves on-device storage capacity. A bandwidth-limited operator approaches proportional latency savings only when the affected traffic dominates and conversion overhead does not consume the gain. A compute-limited operator needs a faster supported execution path. Unstructured pruning likewise reduces executed arithmetic only when sparse kernels exploit the removed weights; indexing and irregular memory accesses can offset the saving. The changed representation therefore supplies a candidate improvement whose effect must be measured on the target.

When persistent storage or device memory capacity limits the deployment, as over-the-air package limits and microcontroller flash budgets do, quantization provides the most direct payload reduction. Relative to FP32 storage, INT8 post-training quantization (PTQ) cuts raw weight storage by 4$\times$ without requiring retraining cycles. INT4 cuts raw payload by 8$\times$, though aggressive bit-width reduction increases the risk of accuracy degradation. If uniform low-bit quantization causes unacceptable accuracy loss on sensitive layers, distilling knowledge into a smaller dense architecture followed by moderate quantization preserves fidelity while satisfying the storage budget.

When latency is compute-bound, the workload operates above the accelerator’s ridge point, the arithmetic intensity at which its compute and memory-bandwidth limits meet, saturating execution units. Relieving this bottleneck requires reducing executed operations or increasing arithmetic throughput. Structured pruning removes channels, attention heads, or entire layers, yielding smaller dense matrices that execute on standard libraries without sparse runtime overhead. However, pruning to dimensions that misalign with hardware warp or tile sizes (such as channel counts not divisible by 8 or 16) degrades tensor core efficiency and curtails wall-clock gains. If the target platform provides dedicated integer execution units, INT8 quantization increases the instruction throughput rate, accelerating compute-bound kernels directly. Early-exit architectures provide an alternative algorithmic path by dynamically halting inference on simpler inputs, provided that routing and pipeline synchronization overhead do not disrupt batch processing.

↪ How the ridge point shifts across accelerators and precisions is developed in **[[01 Books/Volume I - Foundations/03 Optimization/11 Hardware Acceleration/V1-11.07 Performance Bounds#^sec-hardware-acceleration-hardware-balance|Hardware ridge points]]**.

Small-batch transformer generation often exposes a weight-bandwidth bottleneck, as GPT-2 XL decoding on the phone showed, because dense decoding streams large parameter matrices for matrix-vector operations with low arithmetic intensity. When fetched weights dominate latency, weight-only quantization (such as INT4 or INT8 weights paired with FP16 activations) can approach a speedup proportional to the reduction in those bytes. KV-cache traffic and dequantization overhead limit that gain without necessarily making execution compute-bound; larger batches can increase weight reuse and shift the limiting resource.

When energy or thermal dissipation is the limit, quantization provides substantial benefits because moving data across off-chip memory buses consumes orders of magnitude more energy per bit than on-chip arithmetic or local SRAM access. Reducing operand bit widths directly lowers bus capacitance charging energy, while integer ALUs consume less switching energy than floating-point datapaths. Structured pruning compounds these savings by eliminating operations and memory transactions entirely. Full-system power audits must still measure peripheral components (memory refresh, host bus communication, and static idle leakage) to ensure that kernel-level energy savings translate into extended operating life.

Selection also depends on the engineering and computational budget available. Calibrated PTQ needs only a small representative dataset, and QAT adds training cost only when PTQ misses the accuracy threshold. Knowledge distillation costs more, requiring teacher forward passes and substantial training cycles to optimize a smaller student model. Hardware-aware NAS occupies the highest cost tier, justified only when high-volume deployment amortizes the extensive compute needed to explore the architectural search space.

Applied to GPT-2 XL decoding on the phone, this framework makes INT4 weight-only quantization the first candidate, not a completed solution. It cuts the weight bytes to a quarter of FP16, but each token also streams the unchanged KV cache, and the gain holds only when dequantization is fused into the matrix multiplication ([[01 Books/Volume I - Foundations/03 Optimization/10 Model Compression/V1-10.04 Numerical Precision Changing How Values Are Stored#^sec-model-compression-performance-gains-0656|section 10.3.5]]). Distillation to a smaller model is another candidate if the remaining gap justifies its training cost. Each candidate must pass task-quality, latency, peak-memory, and sustained-power checks against the phone’s 3–5 W envelope.

Choosing an individual technique addresses a single bottleneck, but production deployment budgets frequently require compounding gains across multiple dimensions. When memory capacity, bandwidth, and compute throughput are all constrained simultaneously, optimizing a system requires composing structural alterations, precision reductions, and custom execution kernels into an integrated transformation pipeline.

### Combining and Sequencing Techniques

^sec-model-compression-optimization-strategies-f2f6

Quantizing a network before pruning its channels leaves its integer ranges stale, and the reason sets the order of the whole pipeline. Quantization fixes those ranges to the weight and activation distributions it sees at calibration, so it has to wait until those distributions stop moving. Pruning moves them, because removing channels changes what each remaining layer receives. Distillation moves them again, because it is a training phase that adjusts the weights through many small updates. Quantizing before either step therefore leaves ranges fitted to a model that no longer exists. Distillation could not run on the INT8 weights themselves in any case, because an update smaller than one integer step rounds away to nothing. Because each tier changes the weight and activation distributions or tensor shapes that the next tier sees, the sequence governs both final accuracy and hardware utilization.

On the camera pipeline, the sequence follows directly from those dependencies between the tiers. Channel pruning comes first, with pruned channel counts kept on a multiple of the hardware’s tile width so the smaller layers still fill the phone’s matrix tiles. The modeled numbers use the width-0.75 variant of MobileNetV2 from [[01 Books/Volume I - Foundations/03 Optimization/10 Model Compression/V1-10.03 Structural Optimization Changing What the Model Computes#^sec-model-compression-scaling-optimization-6ad9|section 10.2.4.2]] as a stand-in. That variant is designed and trained narrow, whereas pruning cuts channels from a trained network, but the two end at similar layer sizes and so cost similar arithmetic. Distillation from the ResNet-50 teacher then serves as the recovery training for the pruned network, the prune-then-distill order described in [[01 Books/Volume I - Foundations/03 Optimization/10 Model Compression/V1-10.03 Structural Optimization Changing What the Model Computes#^sec-model-compression-knowledge-distillation-1842|section 10.2.3]]. INT8 quantization comes last among the model changes, because range calibration must see the weight and activation distributions of the final structure, and because INT8 is what moves the model from the phone’s CPU onto its integer accelerator. Batch-normalization folding comes just before that calibration, once the channels and weights are final, and the remaining fusions are applied when the graph is compiled for the device.

Each step is profiled on the phone before the next is taken, because each one changes the bottleneck that the next one faces. The steps also move different quantities, and most of the frame time comes off at the step that changes the processor ([[01 Books/Volume I - Foundations/03 Optimization/10 Model Compression/V1-10.06 Choosing and Combining Techniques#^fig-compression-waterfall|figure 29]]).

A modeled BERT-Base pipeline attaches numbers to the same staging. Pruning, distillation, and quantization together reduce its footprint from 440 MB to 28 MB (rounded), a 16× raw-footprint reduction. Each stage addresses a distinct bottleneck. **Example 10.2: BERT-Base mobile deployment pipeline**

**Scenario**: A modeled deployment targets executing a 110M-parameter BERT-Base language model on resource-constrained mobile hardware. The compact graph and every accuracy value below are stipulated for this example, not reported DistilBERT results.

The FP32 teacher begins at 440 MB with task accuracy 76.2 percent. Structural pruning produces a 27.5M-parameter graph, a 75 percent parameter reduction, with raw FP32 weights of 110 MB and accuracy 75.1 percent.

That pruned graph is the distillation student. Teacher supervision recovers its accuracy to 75.9 percent without another parameter-count reduction. Final INT8 quantization leaves the same parameter count, reduces raw weight storage to 27.5 MB, and leaves accuracy at 75.6 percent. The headline ratio uses this unrounded weight payload; scales, zero-points, packing, activations, and runtime workspace remain outside the calculation.

**Diagnosis**: In this modeled comparison, quantization before pruning is assigned an accuracy loss of 2.1 percent, standing for the stale-range effect in which pruning shifts the distributions that the earlier calibration fit. Pruning and distillation before quantization let calibration use the final distributions. These modeled values illustrate the mechanism and do not establish a universal ordering.

**Systems lesson**: In this modeled pipeline, pruning, then distillation, then post-training INT8 quantization produce a 16× footprint reduction (440 MB to 28 MB) while limiting task accuracy loss to 0.6 percent. Because tensor distributions and hardware kernel efficiencies vary across architectures, each intermediate artifact must be profiled on the target runtime.

Because these transformations interact through shared parameters and activation distributions, alternative schedules should be compared at matched recovery and calibration budgets. Combining techniques also changes the size-quality trade-off itself ([[01 Books/Volume I - Foundations/03 Optimization/10 Model Compression/V1-10.11 Source References#^ref-han2016deep|Han et al. 2016]]). In one reported setting ([[01 Books/Volume I - Foundations/03 Optimization/10 Model Compression/V1-10.06 Choosing and Combining Techniques#^fig-compression-methods|figure 30]]), while individual SVD and pruning curves suffer steep accuracy cliffs below about 15 percent and 6 percent model size respectively, combining pruning with quantization extends the zero-loss plateau down to roughly 3 percent of original parameter footprint. This comparison does not isolate transformation order. The magnitude of these gains remains model- and runtime-specific, requiring engineers to validate each transformation stage independently.

**Systems Perspective 10.3: The optimization composition problem**

Optimization techniques interact through shared representations and physical resources rather than composing as independent functions. Pruning changes the surviving value distribution, tensor shapes, or sparse format; quantization adds scales and changes the available kernels; fusion changes which intermediates are materialized and can alter the profile that justified earlier choices. Composing pruning with quantization, as when 2:4 structured sparsity is paired with INT8 weights, leaves an executable that must carry both the sparsity metadata and the quantization scales. A pruned shape that breaks the memory alignment a quantized kernel expects can erase the speedup of both ([[01 Books/Volume I - Foundations/03 Optimization/10 Model Compression/V1-10.11 Source References#^ref-hoefler2021sparsity|Hoefler et al. 2021]]). Effective optimization therefore treats the model-runtime-hardware combination as a coupled system, measures it after every transformation, and revisits the sequence when a new bottleneck appears. This makes compression a systems engineering problem as well as a machine learning problem.

A footprint reduction that compounds on paper therefore becomes a speedup only where the runtime supports every transformation in the sequence at once, which makes the toolchain that applies them part of the decision.

### Compression Toolchains

^sec-model-compression-implementation-tools-4990

Quantizing a large model by hand would mean editing thousands of operator call sites to insert scale factors and track activation ranges. Manual graph surgery also cannot guarantee autograd consistency during retraining or the memory alignment that accelerator kernels require. Compression therefore runs through tooling, and the tool choice follows the layer of the software stack that owns the transformation. Framework APIs operate on the high-level computation graph during training and calibration, where the execution engine maintains visibility into weights, activations, and autograd state. At this stage, quantization is typically simulated, with fake-quantization nodes that clamp and round values within floating-point registers, modeling numerical precision loss while preserving differentiability.

Simulating quantization does not itself establish a physical speedup. A compiler or runtime must lower the graph into supported kernels; frameworks may integrate that stage or export to a separate deployment engine. The runtime compiler fuses scale factor arithmetic directly into matrix-multiplication compute tiles and packs sparse weights into hardware-specific metadata layouts (such as 2:4 structured sparsity formats). This lowering eliminates round-trip memory transactions to HBM that would otherwise negate the benefits of reduced precision.

Splitting compression between the framework and the compiler makes reproducibility the first requirement of the pipeline. Compressing a model alters its numerical representations across both the algorithm and machine layers of the D·A·M taxonomy. Unrecorded calibration inputs or mismatched rounding modes can degrade accuracy silently without raising runtime errors, so every deployed artifact must remain traceable from its source training checkpoint, through the calibration data, pruning schedule, and fusions that produced it, to its compiled hardware execution graph.

#### Model Optimization APIs and Tools

^sec-model-compression-model-optimization-apis-tools-1849

At the model-development layer, compression algorithms require access to runtime state that downstream deployment compilers discard: gradient histories, optimizer state variables, and dynamic activation distributions. When post-training quantization or one-shot pruning degrades accuracy beyond acceptable operating thresholds, recovery requires retraining or iterative fine-tuning. Frameworks such as PyTorch and TensorFlow provide optimization APIs specifically to manage this graph-transformation boundary. These APIs inject quantization nodes and observers, reparameterize weight tensors with sparsity masks, and preserve calibration metadata alongside the trained parameters, which is what makes a compression recipe repeatable across many models.

For quantization-aware training, optimization APIs insert the fake-quantization operators of [[01 Books/Volume I - Foundations/03 Optimization/10 Model Compression/V1-10.04 Numerical Precision Changing How Values Are Stored#^sec-model-compression-fake-quantization-nodes-implementation-e822|section 10.3.3.2.2]] into the compute graph; these operators clamp and round in the forward pass and apply the straight-through estimator of [[01 Books/Volume I - Foundations/03 Optimization/10 Model Compression/V1-10.04 Numerical Precision Changing How Values Are Stored#^sec-model-compression-training-mathematics-699e|section 10.3.3.2.1]] in the backward pass. Producing a deployable low-precision artifact then takes a short sequence of graph transformations and metadata tracking ([[01 Books/Volume I - Foundations/03 Optimization/10 Model Compression/V1-10.06 Choosing and Combining Techniques#^lst-qat-example-ae2a1638|listing 5]]).

``` text
choose target precision and calibration policy
insert quantize/dequantize boundaries around selected tensors
train with simulated low-precision values while keeping gradient flow intact
record scales, zero points, accumulator precision, and unsupported operations
export the calibrated graph and metadata as one reproducible artifact
```

Listing 5: **Quantization-Aware Training**: Mechanism-level transformation that trains through simulated low-precision behavior and exports the calibration metadata needed for deployment.

^lst-qat-example-ae2a1638

Pruning tools implement the mask-then-export boundary of [[01 Books/Volume I - Foundations/03 Optimization/10 Model Compression/V1-10.03 Structural Optimization Changing What the Model Computes#^sec-model-compression-pruning-practice-8059|section 10.2.1.7]]. A mask-based API keeps the dense weight tensor ${\mathbf{W}}$ and a binary mask ${\mathbf{M}}$ of the same shape during recovery training, computing ${\hat{\mathbf{W}}}= {\mathbf{M}}\odot {\mathbf{W}}$ in the forward pass so the existing dense kernels and gradient updates keep working. An export pass then finalizes the transformation, either slicing away pruned channels or encoding the nonzeros in a sparse format the target’s kernels accept. What must be recorded along the way, so that the compressed model can be reproduced, is short but not optional ([[01 Books/Volume I - Foundations/03 Optimization/10 Model Compression/V1-10.06 Choosing and Combining Techniques#^lst-pytorch-pruning-fbf3d332|listing 6]]).

``` text
choose pruning granularity: individual weights, channels, blocks, or attention heads
score candidate parameters by magnitude, sensitivity, or validation loss impact
remove or mask the selected structure according to the hardware-compatible rule
fine-tune the compressed model against the original validation target
export weights, masks, sparsity pattern, and calibration evidence together
```

Listing 6: **Pruning Artifact Boundary**: Applies unstructured or structured removal rules, then records the mask and fine-tuning context needed to reproduce the compressed model.

#### Hardware-Specific Optimization Libraries

^sec-model-compression-hardwarespecific-optimization-libraries-3ab1

Exporting an optimized model from a training framework produces an intermediate graph representation, not an executable binary. Compilers and runtime engines such as TensorRT, Accelerated Linear Algebra (XLA), OpenVINO, and TVM own the final translation step: compiling a pruned, quantized, or fused graph into machine instructions tailored to the target accelerator. In D·A·M terms, algorithmic compression remains theoretical until the graph maps to kernels that physically exploit the target machine’s memory hierarchy and execution units.

The compilation pass verifies whether nominal compression yields physical speedup. Pruning that leaves zeros in dense tensors still streams and multiplies those entries with a dense kernel. Compact sparse formats need matching kernels, such as a supported 2:4 path, while structured channel removal can produce smaller tensors for ordinary dense kernels. Quantization lowers bitwidth, but a weight-only path may unpack operands into floating-point registers before arithmetic. That unpacking adds instructions; its cost must be compared with the packed weight traffic saved.

The result can be the *runtime dequantization trap*. If an accelerator provides native INT8 matrix-multiplication kernels but lacks an INT8 implementation for an adjacent operation (such as LayerNorm, GeLU, or an attention softmax reduction), the runtime encounters a type boundary. An unfused execution path may allocate a temporary buffer, launch a dequantization kernel to recover floating-point values (${x}= {s}({x_q} - {z})$, [[01 Books/Volume I - Foundations/03 Optimization/10 Model Compression/V1-10.05 Hardware Implementation Changing How the Model Executes#^eq-dequantize|equation 4]]), execute the unsupported operator, and launch another kernel to re-quantize its output. These intermediate format conversions incur extra kernel launches, introduce synchronization barriers, and add temporary floating-point traffic that can make the quantized model slower than the FP16 baseline. A fused weight-only path instead unpacks values within the consuming kernel, the fusion of [[01 Books/Volume I - Foundations/03 Optimization/10 Model Compression/V1-10.05 Hardware Implementation Changing How the Model Executes#^sec-model-compression-operator-fusion-ac1d|section 10.4.1]], and retains a net memory-traffic benefit. Which floating-point format the conversion uses, and whether it can be fused, depends on the runtime.

Low-precision kernels also require specialized tensor layouts, such as tiled or interleaved channels, so that neighboring values load together in one wide memory transaction. When an unsupported operator sequence blocks the fusions of [[01 Books/Volume I - Foundations/03 Optimization/10 Model Compression/V1-10.05 Hardware Implementation Changing How the Model Executes#^sec-model-compression-operator-fusion-ac1d|section 10.4.1]], the memory-bandwidth bottleneck remains intact. Whether integrated into a framework or supplied separately, the compiler and runtime determine which graph-level opportunities become an efficient machine execution plan. Whether that plan delivers the savings computed in the earlier sections is a question only measurement on the target hardware can answer.

    2.  Operator fusion alone, because fusing layers eliminates static weight parameter matrices
    3.  Increasing training batch size, because larger batches compress parameter representations during optimization
    4.  Model representation (pruning, distillation) and numerical precision (quantization), because both directly target the byte footprint of the deployed model

2.  A $13\text{-billion}$-parameter FP16 language model fits on the target edge server only at short context, but the required production KV cache and workspace do not fit. Profiling that feasible short-context baseline shows autoregressive decoding is dominated by weight traffic. Which optimization is the most direct initial candidate to test?

    1.  Weight-only INT4 or INT8 post-training quantization (PTQ), because it directly shrinks raw weight bytes and can reduce weight-bandwidth pressure if the remaining memory budget also fits
    2.  Unstructured magnitude pruning with 90% target sparsity, because arbitrary sparse patterns run fastest on edge memory controllers
    3.  Neural Architecture Search from scratch, because searching a new architecture is the fastest way to resolve an immediate deployment deadline
    4.  LayerNorm operator fusion alone, because LayerNorm compute dominates total parameter storage in large language models

3.  Order the stages of a practical compression decision procedure: (1) test a low-cost candidate and measure all relevant quality and resource constraints, (2) establish the limiting constraint from a decisive capacity bound or from profiling a feasible baseline, (3) map that constraint to candidate optimization levers, (4) if the candidate fails, consider recovery training or a structural candidate appropriate to the failed constraint.

4.  The chapter’s illustrative BERT compression pipeline compresses a $440\text{ MB}$ FP32 model down to $28\text{ MB}$ (roughly $16\times$) by sequencing pruning, distillation, and INT8 quantization. Why do these techniques compound multiplicatively rather than substituting for one another?

    1.  The techniques duplicate each other’s reductions, causing total compression to saturate at the performance of the single strongest method
    2.  Pruning produces the smaller student graph, distillation recovers its quality at the same parameter count, and quantization reduces numerical bit-width per parameter; because these operate on orthogonal resource axes, their compression ratios multiply ($4\times \text{ structural} \times 4\times \text{ precision} \approx 16\times \text{ total}$)
    3.  Applying quantization automatically converts the pruned network into a student model without requiring teacher supervision
    4.  Multiplicative gains occur only when the pipeline starts with operator fusion on uncompressed weights
