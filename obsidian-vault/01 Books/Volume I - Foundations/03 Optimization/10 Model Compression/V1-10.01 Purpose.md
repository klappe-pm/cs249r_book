*Why do the models that win benchmarks rarely become the models that run in production?*

A capable trained model is not automatically a deployable one. Cloud, Edge, Mobile, and TinyML environments each impose physical constraints that research benchmarks ignore, including memory budgets measured in megabytes rather than gigabytes, latency targets measured in milliseconds rather than seconds, and power envelopes measured in milliwatts rather than kilowatts. Research optimizes for accuracy on held-out test sets; production optimizes for accuracy per dollar, accuracy per watt, and accuracy per millisecond. Models that win benchmarks are frequently larger, slower, and more resource-intensive than production envelopes permit. Bridging this gap requires a systematic discipline of compression that gives up capabilities the deployment does not need so that the model meets physical constraints it cannot violate. Because many trained models carry more numerical precision, parameters, or representational capacity than the target workload requires, some of that surplus can be removed while preserving predictive behavior, though a smaller representation is not automatically a faster one. Unless the hardware and runtime software stack exploit the new precision or structure, a nominal size reduction merely moves the bottleneck while latency remains unchanged. Removing half the weights in a tensor leaves execution time and energy untouched if the underlying hardware still fetches, stores, and multiplies every zero. Applied effectively, compression reduces model size by integer factors, transforming a research artifact that runs only on data center accelerators into a production asset capable of executing on a mobile phone, sensor node, or embedded microcontroller. The engineering goal is to make the right model viable within its physical environment, which differs from making a model as small as possible. In D·A·M terms, model compression enacts algorithm-machine co-design directly on the network, reshaping its mathematical structure to satisfy the physical limits of the target hardware.

**Learning Objectives**

- Explain compression as algorithm-machine co-design that trades surplus capacity for memory, latency, and energy constraints
- Compare pruning, distillation, and quantization by the resource each relaxes and where each lands on the accuracy-efficiency frontier
- Calculate parameter memory, precision, and sparsity reductions to estimate best-case compression gains
- Apply post-training, quantization-aware, and weight-only strategies under accuracy and hardware constraints
- Select structured pruning and operator choices that map to available accelerator kernels
- Design compression pipelines that order pruning, distillation, and quantization and account for why their combined gains do not multiply
- Evaluate measured latency, energy, and accuracy on target hardware rather than relying on FLOP counts
