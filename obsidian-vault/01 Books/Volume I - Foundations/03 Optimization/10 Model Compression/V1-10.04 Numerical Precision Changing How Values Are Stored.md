# V1-10.04 Numerical Precision Changing How Values Are Stored

[[Home|Home]] · [[01 Books/Volume I - Foundations/Volume 1 Overview|Volume Overview]] · [[01 Books/Volume I - Foundations/03 Optimization/10 Model Compression/V1-10 Model Compression|V1-10 Model Compression]]

^sec-model-compression-quantization-precision-cd46

GPT-2 XL decoding on the phone shows what structural changes leave untouched. Its FP16 weights fit in the phone’s memory, yet every generated token streams all 3 GB of them across a 51.2 GB/s bus, about 59 ms per token before any arithmetic ([[01 Books/Volume I - Foundations/03 Optimization/10 Model Compression/V1-10.02 Fitting the Deployment Budget#^tbl-model-vs-device|table 1]]). Pruning or factorizing its layers would change which weights exist; the complementary optimization leaves the network topology unaltered and reduces the bit width of every surviving tensor. Quantization trades numerical precision for a smaller memory footprint, higher cache utilization, and higher arithmetic throughput on hardware with integer datapaths.

**Definition 10.4: Quantization**

***Quantization*** is a model-compression technique that reduces information fidelity by mapping high-precision continuous values to a lower-precision discrete set.

1.  **Significance**: FP32-to-INT8 conversion reduces the raw payload per quantized value by 4$\times$. Real artifact size, traffic, and latency also depend on scales, metadata, packing, operator coverage, and the target kernels.
2.  **Distinction**: Unlike pruning, which reduces the count of parameters, quantization reduces the bit depth of selected weights and/or activations.
3.  **Common pitfall**: A frequent misconception is that quantization is just “rounding.” In reality, it is a lossy mapping that requires careful range estimation and, often, quantization-aware training (QAT) to minimize its impact on accuracy.

↪ How accelerators trade precision and dynamic range for matrix-engine throughput, pairing low-precision inputs with higher-precision accumulation, is developed in **[[01 Books/Volume I - Foundations/03 Optimization/11 Hardware Acceleration/V1-11.05 Compute Units#^sec-hardware-acceleration-numerics-ai-acceleration-f7be|Numerics and sparsity on the datapath]]**.

Narrowing the bit width of weights and activations from FP32 (32 bits) or FP16 (16 bits) down to INT8 (8 bits) or lower[[01 Books/Volume I - Foundations/03 Optimization/10 Model Compression/V1-10.12 Footnotes#^fn15|¹⁵]] changes three physical terms of execution. Converting FP32 to INT8 reduces raw weight payload by 4$\times$ before scale and packing metadata, which lets models fit within constrained device memories. When those tensors stay packed as they cross the memory bus, memory traffic drops in proportion, which directly reduces token latency in bandwidth-bound autoregressive decoding. Once the values reach the processor, fixed-point integer arithmetic units execute them in smaller silicon area and with significantly less energy per operation than floating-point units, which allows higher compute density on supported hardware ([[01 Books/Volume I - Foundations/03 Optimization/10 Model Compression/V1-10.11 Source References#^ref-gupta2015deep|Gupta et al. 2015]]; [[01 Books/Volume I - Foundations/03 Optimization/10 Model Compression/V1-10.11 Source References#^ref-wang2019benchmarking|Wang et al. 2019]]).

Those gains come at an accuracy cost that varies by model, task, quantizer, calibration data, and bit width. Many convolutional neural network (CNN) inference models tolerate INT8 well; transformers, speech models, and outlier-heavy layers may need different granularity, mixed precision, or training-aware methods. The main approaches are **post-training quantization** (PTQ), **quantization-aware training** (QAT), and mixed-precision or **extreme quantization** for lower-bit deployment. They differ in training cost and control, not in a guaranteed ordering of accuracy.

The viability of each approach depends on how much precision a given model architecture can shed before quality degrades. Across precision formats from FP32 down to INT2, the illustrative CNN and transformer curves of [[01 Books/Volume I - Foundations/03 Optimization/10 Model Compression/V1-10.04 Numerical Precision Changing How Values Are Stored#^fig-quantization-free-lunch|figure 16]] both follow a low-loss plateau where INT8 quantization closely matches full-precision performance, followed by a steep accuracy cliff at lower bit widths. The plateau exists because trained networks are redundant enough to absorb small rounding errors. The cliff follows from how that error grows, since each bit removed roughly doubles the spacing between representable values, so a few bits past the plateau the error outruns what the network can absorb. Because tolerable accuracy degradation depends on end-to-end application constraints, engineering teams must evaluate these cliff boundaries for each target workload and for each calibration recipe, the procedure that chooses which range of real values the integer levels cover ([[01 Books/Volume I - Foundations/03 Optimization/10 Model Compression/V1-10.04 Numerical Precision Changing How Values Are Stored#^sec-model-compression-calibration-c650|section 10.3.3.1.1]]).

Their breakpoints are illustrative; actual boundaries depend on the model, layer distributions, quantizer, calibration or training method, and evaluation task.

Where that cliff sits depends first on how the target number format partitions its bits between dynamic range and precision.

### Numerical Formats

^sec-model-compression-numerical-format-comparison-8fa2

Cutting a tensor from 32 bits to 16 or 8 bits can still overflow on large activation outliers if the format lacks exponent range, or stall in software unpacking if the target processor lacks a native datapath for that bit width. How a format divides its bits between exponent range and fraction precision decides which values survive conversion, and whether the silicon provides a matching execution unit decides whether narrower values run any faster. Because neither property follows from bit count alone, selecting a numerical format requires balancing dynamic range and hardware execution support against memory traffic rather than minimizing bit width blindly. The formats commonly deployed across accelerator architectures differ in raw storage, peak arithmetic rate, and hardware support, each measured against standard 32-bit floating point (FP32) ([[01 Books/Volume I - Foundations/03 Optimization/10 Model Compression/V1-10.04 Numerical Precision Changing How Values Are Stored#^tbl-numerics|table 7]]).

| **Precision Format**             | **Bit-Width** | **Storage Reduction (vs. FP32)** |            **Compute Speed (vs. FP32)**             |         **Power Effect**         | **Use Cases**                                               |
|:---------------------------------|:-------------:|:--------------------------------:|:---------------------------------------------------:|:--------------------------------:|:------------------------------------------------------------|
| **FP32**                         |    32-bit     |       Baseline (1$\times$)       |                Baseline (1$\times$)                 |             Baseline             | Training & inference (general-purpose)                      |
| **FP16**                         |    16-bit     |        2$\times$ smaller         | Up to 2$\times$ peak on selected supported hardware | Hardware- and workload-dependent | Accelerated training, inference (NVIDIA Tensor Cores, TPUs) |
| **BF16 (Brain Floating Point)**  |    16-bit     |        2$\times$ smaller         |          Hardware- and workload-dependent           | Hardware- and workload-dependent | Training on TPUs, transformer-based models                  |
| **TF32 (TensorFloat-32)**        |    19-bit     |      None (stored as FP32)       | Up to 8$\times$ peak on NVIDIA Ampere Tensor Cores  | Hardware- and workload-dependent | Training on NVIDIA GPUs                                     |
| **FP8 (E4M3, E5M2)**             |     8-bit     |        4$\times$ smaller         |         2× the dense FP16 peak on the H100          | Hardware- and workload-dependent | Inference and training on recent data-center accelerators   |
| **INT8 (8-bit Integer)**         |     8-bit     |        4$\times$ smaller         |          Hardware- and workload-dependent           | Hardware- and workload-dependent | Quantized inference (Edge AI, mobile AI, NPUs)              |
| **INT4 (4-bit Integer)**         |     4-bit     |        8$\times$ smaller         |          Hardware- and workload-dependent           | Hardware- and workload-dependent | Ultra-low-power AI, experimental quantization               |
| **Binary/Ternary (1-bit/2-bit)** |    1–2-bit    |      16–32$\times$ smaller       |          Hardware- and workload-dependent           | Hardware- and workload-dependent | Extreme efficiency (binary/ternary neural networks)         |

Table 7: **Numerical Precision Formats**: Storage ratios reflect raw value width; compute speed and power effects depend on hardware support and workload behavior.

^tbl-numerics

At 16 bits, FP16 and BF16 halve memory storage relative to FP32 while targeting dedicated matrix execution units (such as NVIDIA Tensor Cores and Google TPUs) to accelerate arithmetic throughput. The range-versus-precision trade between them is established with mixed-precision training in **[[01 Books/Volume I - Foundations/02 Development/08 Model Training/V1-08.07 Pipeline Optimizations#^sec-model-training-mixedprecision-training-9218|Mixed-precision training]]**, and [[01 Books/Volume I - Foundations/03 Optimization/10 Model Compression/V1-10.04 Numerical Precision Changing How Values Are Stored#^fig-3float|figure 17]] shows the bit layouts that set it. BF16 keeps FP32’s 8-bit exponent and therefore its dynamic range, whereas FP16 spends more bits on the fraction and caps finite values at 65,504. For inference, the same range limits decide whether activations with large outliers survive the narrower format. TensorFloat-32 (TF32) adopts a hybrid compromise. It retains the 8-bit exponent of FP32 and the 10-bit fraction of FP16, executing on Tensor Cores to accelerate matrix multiplication while storing values in standard 32-bit memory.

![[90 Assets/Volume I/10 Model Compression/Generated Figure d79d51af25.svg]]

Figure 17: **Floating-Point Bit Layouts**: Bit allocation trades dynamic range against significand precision. FP32 uses 8 exponent and 23 fraction bits. BF16 retains FP32’s 8-bit exponent and therefore a similar numerical range, but reduces the fraction to 7 bits. FP16 uses 5 exponent and 10 fraction bits, providing more significand precision than BF16 but a narrower range that can require loss scaling during training.

^fig-3float

For inference workloads, INT8 precision provides a fourfold reduction in raw storage relative to FP32 when the chosen quantizer preserves acceptable prediction accuracy. Inference avoids the gradient backpropagation pass, eliminating the small-magnitude, high-dynamic-range updates that cause underflow during training. Activations and weights can still contain outliers or sensitive values, so range calibration or QAT must validate mapping them onto signed 8-bit integers ($[-128, 127]$) using a uniform symmetric or affine mapping. Each mapping has a scale, the real-valued step between adjacent integer codes, and the affine form adds a zero-point, the integer code that stands for real zero ([[01 Books/Volume I - Foundations/03 Optimization/10 Model Compression/V1-10.04 Numerical Precision Changing How Values Are Stored#^sec-model-compression-tuning-quantization-ranges-439d|section 10.3.3.1.2]]). In bandwidth-limited inference regimes, such as autoregressive language model token generation, this $4\times$ footprint reduction relieves memory bus contention by cutting DRAM and cache traffic. On compute-bound operators, dedicated INT8 vector and tensor units provide higher arithmetic operation density per clock cycle and per unit of silicon area than equivalent floating-point units.

FP8 gets its own row because it is the first 8-bit format that keeps an exponent. It comes in two layouts, each with one sign bit: E4M3, with four exponent and three fraction (mantissa) bits, for weights and activations, and E5M2, with five exponent and two fraction bits, for the wider range of gradients ([[01 Books/Volume I - Foundations/03 Optimization/10 Model Compression/V1-10.11 Source References#^ref-micikevicius2022fp8|Micikevicius et al. 2022]]). Like INT8, it halves the bytes of FP16, and on the reference H100 its peak matrix rate equals the INT8 rate, 2× the dense FP16 peak. Unlike INT8, it needs no zero-point, and its exponent absorbs the outliers that force an integer format to choose between clipping and resolution, so a model often converts to FP8 with less range-calibration effort than to INT8. FP8 as the default serving precision on such accelerators is developed in **[[01 Books/Volume I - Foundations/04 Deployment/13 Model Serving/V1-13 Model Serving#^sec-model-serving|Model Serving]]**.

Microscaling formats carry the idea of a range-absorbing exponent below eight bits ([[01 Books/Volume I - Foundations/03 Optimization/10 Model Compression/V1-10.11 Source References#^ref-rouhani2023microscaling|Rouhani et al. 2023]]). A block of values, typically 32, shares one 8-bit power-of-two scale, so the elements of an MXFP8, MXFP6, or MXFP4 block borrow their range from the shared exponent and spend their few bits on precision. They are an awareness item here, because their payoff depends on accelerators with native support.

Binary and ternary formats occupy the far end of [[01 Books/Volume I - Foundations/03 Optimization/10 Model Compression/V1-10.04 Numerical Precision Changing How Values Are Stored#^tbl-numerics|table 7]], constraining values to $\{-1, +1\}$ or $\{-1, 0, +1\}$ so that multiplication collapses into bitwise logic or a conditional add. They promise the largest storage ratio in the table, but byte-addressed memory and training stability make them a special case that [[01 Books/Volume I - Foundations/03 Optimization/10 Model Compression/V1-10.04 Numerical Precision Changing How Values Are Stored#^sec-model-compression-extreme-quantization-aba4|section 10.3.4]] develops in full. The keyword-spotting lighthouse shows why such formats are considered at all. Under the strict ~512 KB SRAM budget, INT8 is the baseline target, and INT4, binary, or ternary representations give up further precision to meet stricter memory and power bounds. Whereas [[01 Books/Volume I - Foundations/03 Optimization/10 Model Compression/V1-10.04 Numerical Precision Changing How Values Are Stored#^tbl-numerics|table 7]] counts bytes and peak rates, what each format saves in joules depends on where its bits travel, which is the subject of the energy comparison that follows.

### Precision and Energy

^sec-model-compression-precision-energy-cc8e

In silicon, every bit that changes value charges a wire, and the energy dissipated grows with that wire’s capacitance and with the square of the supply voltage (${E}\propto {C} {V}^2$). Long memory buses have far more capacitance than the short wires inside an arithmetic unit, so shuttling bits across them costs orders of magnitude more joules than toggling logic gates. Because physical data movement so often dominates a workload’s power budget, choosing a numerical precision is an energy decision governed by the number and location of memory accesses alongside the arithmetic performed. Reducing numerical precision directly shrinks the byte volume traversing the memory hierarchy (${D_{\text{vol}}}$) and lowers the switching energy per ALU operation (${E_{\text{compute}}}$), relaxing memory bandwidth and thermal bottlenecks across the hardware stack.

↪ Accelerator designs that keep operands in registers and on-chip SRAM, away from costly off-chip DRAM, are developed in **[[01 Books/Volume I - Foundations/03 Optimization/11 Hardware Acceleration/V1-11.03 Hardware Specialization#^sec-hardware-acceleration-machine-learning-hardware-specialization-09c5|Machine learning hardware specialization]]**.

#### Where Precision Saves Energy

^sec-model-compression-energy-costs-d1d5

Precision saves energy in two places, and the larger one is usually movement. The energy-movement principle ([[01 Books/Volume I - Foundations/03 Optimization/V1 Optimization Principles#^pri-energy-movement|7]]), ${E_{\text{move}}} \gg {E_{\text{compute}}}$, says that moving an operand costs far more than operating on it ([[01 Books/Volume I - Foundations/03 Optimization/10 Model Compression/V1-10.11 Source References#^ref-horowitz2014|Horowitz 2014]]). In the reference technology model, a 32-bit read from off-chip DRAM costs about 640 pJ, roughly 2,100× the energy of the INT8 multiply-accumulate that consumes it, and the cost keeps rising as data leaves the chip for storage or the network (**[[01 Books/Volume I - Foundations/01 Foundations/04 Data Engineering/V1-04.10 Physics of Data#^tbl-energy-movement-cost|Energy Cost of Moving vs. Computing on a 32-Bit Value]]** in **[[01 Books/Volume I - Foundations/01 Foundations/04 Data Engineering/V1-04.10 Physics of Data#^sec-data-engineering-feeding-problem|The feeding problem]]**). A packed INT8 stream carries one quarter of the raw value bits of an FP32 stream, so each weight or activation that crosses the memory bus costs about a quarter of the movement energy, provided transactions stay full. Caches, metadata, alignment, and transaction size set the realized saving. The reference measurements predate FP8, but an FP8 value moves the same eight bits as an INT8 value, so the movement saving applies to it unchanged.

Arithmetic supplies the second saving ([[01 Books/Volume I - Foundations/03 Optimization/10 Model Compression/V1-10.04 Numerical Precision Changing How Values Are Stored#^tbl-model-compression-energy-hierarchy|table 8]]). An FP32 addition costs 0.9 pJ/op and an INT8 addition 0.03 pJ/op, a ratio of 30×; an FP32 multiply costs 3.7 pJ/op and an INT8 multiply 0.2 pJ/op, a ratio of 18.5×. Every multiply-accumulate a matrix unit performs combines the two, so the figure that governs a format decision is their combination. Because INT8 products accumulate in INT32, a full INT8 multiply-accumulate uses roughly 15× less energy than its FP32 equivalent, on top of the 4$\times$ cut in bytes moved. **[[05 Reference/Volume I - Foundations/V1 Machine Foundations#^sec-appdx-machine-foundations-numbers-know-b531|Numbers to Know]]** collects the reference energy ratios, and **[[05 Reference/Volume I - Foundations/V1 Machine Foundations#^sec-appdx-machine-foundations-numerical-representations-c889|Numerical Representations]]** compares numerical format trade-offs.

| **Operation**           | **FP32 (pJ)** | **INT8 (pJ)** | **FP32 / INT8** |
|:------------------------|:-------------:|:-------------:|:---------------:|
| **Add**                 |     0.90      |     0.03      |       30×       |
| **Multiply**            |     3.70      |     0.20      |      18.5×      |
| **Multiply-accumulate** |     4.60      |     0.30      |       15×       |

Table 8: **INT8 versus FP32 Energy per Operation**: Reference 45 nm energy for one add, one multiply, and one multiply-accumulate at each precision. The INT8 multiply-accumulate sums its products in INT32, so its cost is an INT8 multiply plus an INT32 add, which is why its ratio is the smallest of the three. A 32-bit DRAM read, at 640 pJ, costs more than every row here combined. Total inference energy also depends on operation counts, access counts, and where operands reside ([[01 Books/Volume I - Foundations/03 Optimization/10 Model Compression/V1-10.11 Source References#^ref-horowitz2014|Horowitz 2014]]).

^tbl-model-compression-energy-hierarchy

Accelerators equipped with native low-precision units (such as NVIDIA Tensor Cores, Google TPUs, or mobile NPUs) compound these arithmetic savings with reduced memory traffic. In contrast, general-purpose processors lacking native low-precision execution pipelines lose much of this benefit to the runtime overhead of bit unpacking, format conversion, and scalar fallback. Precision is therefore a model-hardware decision, not a software configuration flag.

Which saving matters depends on where the workload sits. Under the iron law of ML systems (principle [[01 Books/Volume I - Foundations/02 Development/V1 Development Principles#^pri-iron-law|3]]), reducing bit width changes the data-movement $({D_{\text{vol}}}/{\text{BW}})$ and compute $({O}/({R_{\text{peak}}} \cdot {\eta_{\text{hw}}}))$ terms only through the execution path that uses the new representation. Workloads with little arithmetic per byte are memory bound, so shrinking data movement dominates; workloads with more arithmetic per byte are compute bound, so arithmetic savings matter more, a distinction the roofline model in **[[01 Books/Volume I - Foundations/03 Optimization/11 Hardware Acceleration/V1-11.07 Performance Bounds#^sec-hardware-acceleration-roofline-model-3e1a|The roofline model]]** formalizes. Neither ratio predicts battery life on its own, because end-to-end energy also includes control, sensors, and idle power. When memory capacity is the limit, the more immediate benefit is placement, because smaller values may let the model reside in a memory tier that the uncompressed model cannot fit.

*Three stacked boxes labeled D Data, A Algorithm, and M Machine, with the M Machine box highlighted.*

*The machine’s memory capacity, not arithmetic, now sets the constraint.*

Recommendation models show the placement benefit at its largest, at the far extreme of memory capacity, where the question is no longer how many joules each byte costs but whether the bytes fit in memory. There, quantization must shrink the dominant memory structures enough to reside in the host machine’s physical memory. When an embedding table outgrows a single accelerator’s HBM, reducing bytes per entry determines whether the table fits on the node at all.

**Lighthouse 10.1: DLRM and embedding quantization**

In DLRM, memory capacity, rather than compute throughput or memory bandwidth, is the limiting constraint ([[01 Books/Volume I - Foundations/03 Optimization/10 Model Compression/V1-10.11 Source References#^ref-naumov2019deep|Naumov et al. 2019]]). Even the reference configuration in [[01 Books/Volume I - Foundations/03 Optimization/10 Model Compression/V1-10.02 Fitting the Deployment Budget#^tbl-model-vs-device|table 1]] needs 100 GB of weights, more than one accelerator’s memory, and production embedding tables can grow to terabytes that must be sharded across many devices.

For DLRM, quantization targets storage density rather than arithmetic throughput. Reducing embedding-table precision from FP32 to INT8 (or lower) shrinks the memory footprint by 4–8$\times$, allowing larger tables to fit on fewer GPUs when accuracy and lookup kernels tolerate the lower precision. Compressing the lookup tables ensures that physical device memory can accommodate the algorithmic model.

**Lighthouse 10.2: Keyword spotting and the SRAM budget**

The KWS lighthouse, DS-CNN, operates at the opposite extreme of the iron law from DLRM ([[01 Books/Volume I - Foundations/03 Optimization/10 Model Compression/V1-10.11 Source References#^ref-zhang2017hello|Y. Zhang et al. 2017]]). Consider a deployment that keeps weights in roughly 512 KB of SRAM. Even the purpose-built DS-CNN keyword spotter overshoots that budget in FP32, where its weights occupy 800 KB. Quantized to INT8, its weights shrink to 200 KB, leaving space whose sufficiency must be checked against peak activation and runtime-buffer use. A device that reads weights directly from flash has a separate weight-storage budget; its SRAM feasibility still depends on the working set. A stricter smart-doorbell microcontroller with 256 KB SRAM leaves closer to 100 KB for model weights after runtime buffers, audio windows, and feature extraction state, motivating more aggressive INT4 or binary compression.

In FP32, even the compact DS-CNN architecture moves 4$\times$ more raw weight bytes than in INT8, and the arithmetic path can also cost more on hardware with efficient integer units. For an always-on coin-cell device, that reduction contributes to longer battery life, but sensor, wake-up, feature-extraction, and idle-power costs may dominate. Here, quantization reduces the model’s data-movement term and can improve its compute term $({O} / ({R_{\text{peak}}} \cdot {\eta_{\text{hw}}}))$ when the integer path is supported.

Together, DLRM and the TinyML lighthouse ([10.2](https://mlsysbook.ai/vol1/10_model_compression/10_model_compression.html#lhs-model-compression-tinyml-quantization-imperative)) illustrate the two operational extremes of this memory constraint. DLRM uses lower precision to fit embedding tables that can reach terabytes into host memory; TinyML uses lower precision to keep every inference within a tiny energy envelope and a few hundred kilobytes of on-chip SRAM. Across both extremes, reducing numerical precision attacks physical execution costs on two fronts, arithmetic energy and latency in the ALU and byte traffic across the memory bus. Both savings assume that the low-precision values still represent the model faithfully, and producing such values is the job of the quantization methods.

### Quantization Methods

^sec-model-compression-precision-reduction-strategies-db83

Casting trained FP32 weights and activations straight to narrow integers without fitting their range clips outlier channels and rounds small values to zero, so practical quantization methods differ in how much calibration or retraining they spend to keep that error small. Post-training quantization, quantization-aware training, and extreme low-bit formats differ chiefly in the training and validation cost they require. Representation choices, such as selecting INT8 versus INT4, mixing bit widths across sensitive layers, or choosing per-tensor versus per-channel ranges, are orthogonal options evaluated *within* these workflows. Ordered by the engineering effort each requires, the deployment sequence moves from fast post-training range calibration through quantization-aware retraining to custom low-bit pipelines ([[01 Books/Volume I - Foundations/03 Optimization/10 Model Compression/V1-10.04 Numerical Precision Changing How Values Are Stored#^fig-quantization-roadmap|figure 18]]).

![[90 Assets/Volume I/10 Model Compression/Generated Figure aacd9c8313.svg]]

Figure 18: **Quantization Engineering Workflow**: The tiers order deployment paths by engineering and validation effort, not by format or guaranteed accuracy. PTQ serves as the initial candidate because it operates over frozen weights with zero retraining cost; QAT introduces simulated quantization into the training loop when quality recovery demands backpropagation; specialized sub-4-bit and binary formats require custom kernel execution and extensive hardware-specific validation. Representation choices such as INT8 versus INT4, per-channel scaling, and mixed precision apply across these stages rather than defining separate maturity levels.

^fig-quantization-roadmap

Post-training quantization is evaluated first, because it changes representations with minimal compute and pipeline overhead. When PTQ satisfies the application’s latency, energy, and quality thresholds, the optimization process stops. When post-training calibration introduces unacceptable accuracy degradation, quantization-aware training lets the weights adapt to discretization noise during backpropagation, and extreme sub-4-bit or ternary representations are reserved for targets that INT8 and INT4 cannot meet and whose hardware has kernels for them.

#### Post-Training Quantization

^sec-model-compression-posttraining-quantization-bb5b

Retraining a large model checkpoint solely to adapt its weights to 8-bit arithmetic can cost hundreds of accelerator hours, and in many deployment pipelines the original training dataset or training cluster is not even available. **Post-training quantization (PTQ)** avoids that retraining loop altogether by converting weights and activation tensors from floating-point to fixed-point formats directly on a frozen checkpoint ([[01 Books/Volume I - Foundations/03 Optimization/10 Model Compression/V1-10.11 Source References#^ref-choukroun2019|Choukroun et al. 2019]]). Weight-only quantization stores only the weights in low precision and still computes on floating-point activations, so it needs only a single pass over the static parameter tensors. Full integer quantization also converts the activations, so it must calibrate their dynamic ranges using a small, unlabeled sample of representative inputs.

The principal constraint in PTQ is unmodeled discretization noise. Rounding introduces numerical error that cannot be compensated by gradient updates. While redundant architectures absorb 8-bit rounding noise with negligible accuracy loss, sensitive models or lower bit widths (such as INT4) experience degradation unless scaling granularities are refined or quantization-aware training is applied. Production runtimes such as TensorFlow Lite, Open Neural Network Exchange (ONNX) Runtime, and TensorRT provide automated calibration pipelines to profile tensor ranges and apply this conversion.

The core mechanism of PTQ is **uniform quantization**, which maps floating-point values to discrete integer levels using a consistent scaling factor. Because the interval between each quantized value is constant, uniform quantization simplifies implementation and enables efficient hardware execution. For the symmetric form shown here, ${s}$ is chosen from the maximum absolute value so that the resulting integers stay within the target range. The quantized value ${x_q}$ is computed as:

$$
{x_q} = \text{round} \left(\frac{{x}}{{s}} \right)
$$

 where:

- ${x_q}$ is the quantized integer representation,
- ${x}$ is the original floating-point value,
- ${s}$ is a scaling factor that maps the floating-point range to the available integer range.

Uniform quantization from FP32 to INT8 reduces the raw value payload from 32 to 8 bits per weight at the cost of a measurable quantization error, with scale metadata and packing overhead excluded ([[01 Books/Volume I - Foundations/03 Optimization/10 Model Compression/V1-10.04 Numerical Precision Changing How Values Are Stored#^lst-quantization-example-5f43a41b|listing 2]]). Once a model is quantized, the runtime can use integer arithmetic where supported ([[01 Books/Volume I - Foundations/03 Optimization/10 Model Compression/V1-10.11 Source References#^ref-gholami2022|Gholami et al. 2022]]).

``` python
import torch

# Original FP32 weights
weights_fp32 = torch.tensor(
    [0.127, -0.084, 0.392, -0.203], dtype=torch.float32
)
print(f"Original FP32: {weights_fp32}")
print(f"Memory per weight: 32 bits")

# Simple uniform quantization to INT8 (-128 to 127)
# Step 1: Find scale factor
max_val = weights_fp32.abs().max()
scale = max_val / 127  # 127 is max positive INT8 value

# Step 2: Quantize using our formula x_q = round(x/s)
weights_int8 = torch.round(weights_fp32 / scale).to(torch.int8)
print(f"Quantized INT8: {weights_int8}")
print(f"Memory per weight: 8 bits (reduced from 32)")

# Step 3: Dequantize to verify
weights_dequantized = weights_int8.float() * scale
print(f"Dequantized: {weights_dequantized}")
print(
    f"Quantization error: "
    f"{(weights_fp32 - weights_dequantized).abs().mean():.6f}"
)
```

Listing 2: **Uniform Quantization**: Converts FP32 weights to INT8, reducing raw per-weight payload from 32 to 8 bits while measuring quantization error; scale metadata and packing overhead are excluded.

^lst-quantization-example-5f43a41b

Rounding to a uniform grid introduces an error with a precise bound. A uniform quantizer maps each value to the nearest of evenly spaced levels separated by a step size ${s}$ (range calibration derives ${s}$ from the tensor’s clipping range in [equation 1](https://mlsysbook.ai/vol1/10_model_compression/10_model_compression.html#eq-quant-scale), later in this section). For any value inside the clipping range, rounding to the nearest level misses by at most half a step, so within-range error is bounded by $[-{s}/2, {s}/2]$. Aggregated across many tensors, most error concentrates inside that bound, while the tails carry the larger distortion that comes from saturation clipping and from mixing tensors that use different step sizes ([[01 Books/Volume I - Foundations/03 Optimization/10 Model Compression/V1-10.04 Numerical Precision Changing How Values Are Stored#^fig-quantization-error-dist|figure 19]]). Many CNNs tolerate INT8 well, whereas large transformers often need outlier-aware or mixed-precision schemes because a few activation channels carry extreme values. Selecting the appropriate numerical precision therefore requires balancing accuracy constraints, hardware support, and efficiency gains.

![[90 Assets/Volume I/10 Model Compression/Generated Figure b662c06aa6.svg]]

Figure 19: **Illustrative Aggregate Quantization Residuals**: Illustrative aggregate residual distribution across heterogeneous scales and clipped values. Within one unclipped uniform quantizer, rounding error is bounded by $[-{s}/2, {s}/2]$ for step size ${s}$; the displayed tails represent clipping or aggregation across different step sizes.

^fig-quantization-error-dist

An alternative, nonuniform quantization, assigns finer-grained precision to numerical ranges that are more densely populated, which can preserve accuracy for models whose weight distributions concentrate around specific values. Nonuniform schemes require more complex calibration and are less common in production, but they can be effective for models particularly sensitive to precision changes. More generally, sensitivity to PTQ varies by architecture, layer, and distribution. When a uniform configuration misses the quality target, the next options include finer granularity, mixed precision, weight-only methods, better calibration, or QAT.

##### Range Calibration

^sec-model-compression-calibration-c650

Mapping a tensor onto 8-bit or 4-bit integers with an ill-fitting range either saturates critical values through clipping or squanders available integer levels on sparse outliers. Quantization-range calibration prevents both failures by estimating, for each tensor, the real-valued clipping range $[{\alpha}, {\beta}]$, whose lower and upper bounds are the smallest and largest values the integer grid will represent. Calibration minimizes representation error over the sampled distribution, although it does not guarantee bounds on out-of-distribution deployment inputs.

Because activation tensors vary with input data, profiling requires running an unlabeled calibration dataset through the forward pass to capture dynamic ranges. In the offline conversion pipeline, the pretrained model and a representative calibration dataset pass through a profiling stage that establishes dynamic ranges, and those ranges parameterize the quantization step that yields the final quantized model ([[01 Books/Volume I - Foundations/03 Optimization/10 Model Compression/V1-10.04 Numerical Precision Changing How Values Are Stored#^fig-ptq-calibration|figure 20]]). Layer by layer, activation observers record value distributions from which the scale and zero-point metadata are computed ([algorithm 1](https://mlsysbook.ai/vol1/10_model_compression/10_model_compression.html#algo-ptq-calibration)). Range calibration in this sense is distinct from the confidence calibration that asks whether a model’s predicted probabilities match its observed accuracy, which returns in [[01 Books/Volume I - Foundations/03 Optimization/10 Model Compression/V1-10.07 Measuring Compression Gains#^sec-model-compression-efficiency-measurement-2424|section 10.6]].

$f_\theta$

![[90 Assets/Volume I/10 Model Compression/Generated Figure 9320ee1987.svg]]

Figure 20: **Post-Training Quantization Pipeline**: PTQ converts a trained FP32 model directly to INT8 without retraining. Representative calibration data passes through the model to record activation ranges $[{\alpha}, {\beta}]$, computing scale factor ${s}$ and zero-point ${z}$ parameters to quantize parameters before deployment.

^fig-ptq-calibration

Once the ranges are fixed, the quantization step converts model parameters to the lower-precision format, producing the final quantized model. The single calibration pass over ${\mathcal{C}_{\text{cal}}}$ avoids retraining, and FP32-to-INT8 weight quantization cuts raw stored weight values by 4$\times$ before scale, zero-point, and packing metadata. The price is that the static activation ranges fixed in the loop risk saturation or wasted integer levels when the calibration data misses deployment tails.

Mapping an unnecessarily wide real-valued interval to the 256 discrete bins of an 8-bit integer wastes numerical resolution on empty range, whereas clipping too aggressively truncates signal. Three calibration methods balance this clipping-versus-resolution trade-off. The **max** method sets bounds to the absolute extremum, which preserves extreme values at the cost of vulnerability to outliers. The **entropy** method selects bounds by minimizing the Kullback–Leibler (KL) divergence between original and quantized distributions. The **percentile** method retains a chosen fraction (such as 99.9 percent) and clips the remaining tail.

In one ResNet-50 layer, the dense bulk of activations clusters below 1.1, while a sparse outlier tail stretching past 2.0 motivates testing a candidate clipping boundary near 1.1 to allocate more INT8 bins to the bulk ([[01 Books/Volume I - Foundations/03 Optimization/10 Model Compression/V1-10.04 Numerical Precision Changing How Values Are Stored#^fig-resnet-activations-histogram|figure 21]]). The boundary near 1.1 is a candidate to test rather than a computed percentile, and task validation must establish whether clipping that tail preserves important rare cases.

##### Tuning Quantization Ranges

^sec-model-compression-tuning-quantization-ranges-439d

Once calibration identifies clipping boundaries $[{\alpha}, {\beta}]$, the range must be mapped onto integer endpoints, and two mapping schemes dominate ([[01 Books/Volume I - Foundations/03 Optimization/10 Model Compression/V1-10.04 Numerical Precision Changing How Values Are Stored#^fig-calibration-ranges|figure 22]]). A **symmetric** mapping uses a zero-centered range with zero-point zero, forcing real zero to integer zero by mapping $[-1, 1]$ directly onto $[-127, 127]$. An **asymmetric** mapping keeps one scale but a generally nonzero zero-point ${z}$, which accommodates skewed activation intervals such as $[-0.5, 1.5]$ across $[-128, 127]$.

![[90 Assets/Volume I/10 Model Compression/Generated Figure e04fdd3104.svg]]

Figure 22: **Calibration Range Selection**: Symmetric vs. asymmetric affine quantization mappings. Symmetric quantization (left) forces real zero to map to integer zero, using equal-magnitude negative and positive clipping bounds at the expense of wasted dynamic range for skewed activation distributions; asymmetric quantization (right) uses an explicit zero-point offset ${z}$ to map non-centered ranges $[{\alpha}, {\beta}]$ efficiently.

^fig-calibration-ranges

Symmetric quantization simplifies integer matrix multiplication kernels because the zero-point is zero (${z}= 0$), eliminating cross-term additions during hardware accumulation. However, for activations following asymmetric distributions (such as post-ReLU feature maps where all values are non-negative), symmetric quantization wastes roughly half of the integer dynamic range on unused negative values. Asymmetric quantization recovers this dynamic range by mapping $[{\alpha}, {\beta}]$ to $[0, 2^{b}- 1]$, at the cost of extra arithmetic inside the dot product. That unsigned target and the signed $[-128, 127]$ of [[01 Books/Volume I - Foundations/03 Optimization/10 Model Compression/V1-10.04 Numerical Precision Changing How Values Are Stored#^fig-calibration-ranges|figure 22]] are interchangeable, because they differ only by a constant shift of the zero-point; the notebook below uses the unsigned form.

The extra arithmetic comes from the zero-point ${z}$. Because every activation code is shifted by it, each output of a row of quantized weights ${W_{ik}}$ picks up a cross-term ${z}\sum_k {W_{ik}}$, the zero-point times the sum of that row’s weights. When the weights and the zero-point are fixed offline, a compiler precomputes that term and folds it into the layer’s 32-bit bias, so it costs nothing at run time; when the zero-point varies at runtime, hardware must compute it during inference. Either mapping is fully specified by two numbers per range, a scale and a zero-point. The uniform quantizer introduced for PTQ is the symmetric mapping written on a signed integer range, where the zero-point is zero; written on an unsigned range, the same mapping places its zero-point at the middle of the range.

**Napkin Math 10.1: Calculating scale and zero-point**

**The affine quantization formula**: An affine map sends a floating-point range $[{\alpha}, {\beta}]$ that includes zero to an integer range $[0, 2^{b}-1]$ (for example, UINT8) via the following construction.

**Analysis**: Affine quantization establishes a linear mapping ${x}\approx {s}({x_q} - {z})$, where ${s}$ is the **scale** (step size) and ${z}$ is the **zero-point** (integer value corresponding to real zero). The mapping involves three computational steps:

1.  **Calculate scale $({s})$**: Divide the real range by the integer range using [equation 1](https://mlsysbook.ai/vol1/10_model_compression/10_model_compression.html#eq-quant-scale).

$$
{s}= \frac{{\beta}- {\alpha}}{2^{b}- 1} \tag{1}
$$

    ^eq-quant-scale

2.  **Calculate zero-point $({z})$**: Shift the range so that real zero maps to an integer using [equation 2](https://mlsysbook.ai/vol1/10_model_compression/10_model_compression.html#eq-quant-zero-point).

$$
{z}= \text{round}\left(\frac{-{\alpha}}{{s}}\right) \tag{2}
$$

    ^eq-quant-zero-point

3.  **Quantize $({x}\to {x_q})$**: Divide by the scale, add the zero-point, then round and clamp to the integer range using [equation 3](https://mlsysbook.ai/vol1/10_model_compression/10_model_compression.html#eq-quantize).

$$
{x_q} = \text{clamp}\left(\text{round}\left(\frac{{x}}{{s}} + {z}\right), 0, 2^{b}- 1\right) \tag{3}
$$

    ^eq-quantize

**Scenario**: Consider quantizing a $2 \times 2$ activation matrix with values ranging from ${\alpha} = -1$ to ${\beta} = 3$, and the target precision is UINT8 $({b}=8)$:

$$
\mathbf{X} = \begin{pmatrix} -1 & 1 \\ 0 & 3 \end{pmatrix}
$$

- **Range**: ${\beta} - {\alpha} = 4$.
- **Steps**: $2^8 - 1$ = 255.
- **Scale**: ${s} = \frac{4}{255} \approx 0.0157$.
- **Zero-point**: ${z} = \text{round}(-({\alpha})/{s}) = \text{round}\!\left(-(-1)/\frac{4}{255}\right) = \text{round}(63.75) = 64$.

Applying [equation 3](https://mlsysbook.ai/vol1/10_model_compression/10_model_compression.html#eq-quantize) element-wise maps each floating-point entry to an integer bin:

$$
\mathbf{X}_q = \begin{pmatrix} 0 & 128 \\ 64 & 255 \end{pmatrix}
$$

**Systems insight**: The real value $0.0$ is represented exactly by the integer 64. This is critical because it ensures that zero-padding (common in CNNs) is represented exactly, preventing “quantization drift” where padding introduces nonzero noise.

##### Granularity

^sec-model-compression-granularity-93b3

In convolutional filters or transformer projection matrices, weight magnitudes often vary widely from one channel to the next, so sharing a single scale factor across an entire tensor can collapse small-magnitude channels to zero or clip wider channels. Quantization granularity governs how broadly scale factors and zero-points are shared across a tensor. It balances representation resolution against metadata overhead and kernel complexity. Across convolutional channels (filters 1 through ${d_{\text{out}}}$), a global layerwise threshold spans the widest distribution at the cost of resolution in narrower filters. Per-channel clipping boundaries, by contrast, adapt tightly to each filter’s dynamic range at the cost of tracking additional scale vectors during matrix multiplication ([[01 Books/Volume I - Foundations/03 Optimization/10 Model Compression/V1-10.04 Numerical Precision Changing How Values Are Stored#^fig-quantization-granularity|figure 23]]).

![[90 Assets/Volume I/10 Model Compression/Generated Figure 84d3542aa4.svg]]

Figure 23: **Quantization Range Variation**: Different convolutional filters can have different weight ranges, motivating per-filter quantization to reduce quantization error. The differing clipping scales illustrate the choice between one layerwise range and separate channelwise ranges. Source: ([[01 Books/Volume I - Foundations/03 Optimization/10 Model Compression/V1-10.11 Source References#^ref-gholami2022|Gholami et al. 2022]]).

^fig-quantization-granularity

Quantization granularity trades calibration and metadata overhead against representation error. More local ranges can preserve information when channels differ, but they do not improve accuracy automatically and may lack an efficient kernel path. Each additional scale also has to be stored, loaded, and applied at the granularity supported by the backend. Four levels are common, from one shared layer range to local ranges within a filter ([[01 Books/Volume I - Foundations/03 Optimization/10 Model Compression/V1-10.04 Numerical Precision Changing How Values Are Stored#^tbl-quantization-granularity|table 9]]).

| **Level**           | **Range sharing**                  | **Trade-off**                                                                                                                                               |
|:--------------------|:-----------------------------------|:------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------|
| **Layerwise**       | One range per layer                | Simple but suboptimal when filter ranges vary widely                                                                                                        |
| **Groupwise**       | Filters grouped with shared ranges | Used in Q-BERT ([[01 Books/Volume I - Foundations/03 Optimization/10 Model Compression/V1-10.11 Source References#^ref-shen2020\|Shen et al. 2020]]) for transformer attention layers |
| **Channelwise**     | One range per filter               | Common default; balances quantization error against scale metadata and implementation overhead                                                              |
| **Sub-channelwise** | Ranges within each filter          | More local ranges can reduce quantization error but increase metadata and implementation overhead                                                           |

Table 9: **Quantization Granularity Levels**: Granularity trades calibration and metadata overhead against representation error, from a single per-layer range to per-filter sub-ranges.

^tbl-quantization-granularity

Channelwise quantization preserves accuracy over layerwise quantization when channel dynamic ranges differ, at the cost of storing one scale per output filter. The extra rescaling is cheap, because each output channel is an independent dot product accumulated into its own 32-bit register, so its scale is applied once per output element after the sum finishes. Sub-channelwise (per-group) quantization, common in 4-bit weight compression for large language models, gives each block of 32 to 128 weights along the summed dimension its own scale, which lowers error further but forces the kernel to rescale partial sums during the sum rather than once at the end. Beyond scale granularity, execution efficiency depends on the distinction between static weights and dynamic activations, which impose different memory and latency constraints on the deployment system.

##### Weights Vs. Activations

^sec-model-compression-weights-vs-activations-5efb

Executing low-precision inference requires transforming floating-point inputs before kernel execution and requantizing intermediate accumulator outputs. During integer matrix multiplication, multiplying two 8-bit integers produces a 16-bit product. Summing these products across an inner dimension of length ${K}$ causes bit-growth that quickly overflows 8-bit registers; hardware therefore accumulates into 32-bit integer registers (`INT32`). Before feeding the result into subsequent layers, requantization rescales the 32-bit accumulated sum by the layer’s effective scale factor (${s_{\text{eff}}}= \frac{{s_W} \cdot {s_X}}{{s_Y}}$) and clamps the values back into the 8-bit integer range. The full pipeline has four stages ([[01 Books/Volume I - Foundations/03 Optimization/10 Model Compression/V1-10.04 Numerical Precision Changing How Values Are Stored#^fig-weight-activations-quantization|figure 24]]): quantizers convert floating-point activations and weights into INT8 operands, an integer core executes the matrix multiplication, a 32-bit accumulator holds the sum, and a requantization stage returns the result to INT8 before the nonlinear activation.

The ratio ${s_{\text{eff}}}$ follows from what the integers stand for. Ignoring zero-points, the INT32 sum is the real dot product divided by the weight and input scales, so multiplying by those two scales restores real units and dividing by the output scale expresses the result in the output’s integer codes.

Activation quantization maps layer outputs to a lower-precision representation during inference. It reduces intermediate payloads and can reduce arithmetic cost on hardware with an efficient integer path, but it also introduces error between layers. For example, a CNN may convert FP32 feature maps to INT8 before later convolutions. The benefit is greatest when consecutive operators remain on the integer path; isolated quantized operators may pay conversion cost without reducing enough work. Whether activation quantization improves latency therefore depends on supported kernels, scale handling, tensor lifetimes, and any quantize or dequantize boundaries that remain in the exported graph.

##### Static Vs. Dynamic Quantization

^sec-model-compression-static-vs-dynamic-quantization-4791

The clipping range also depends on *when* activation ranges are computed, which divides activation quantization into two primary execution models: static quantization and dynamic quantization. In **static quantization**, the clipping range is precalculated during offline calibration and remains fixed during inference. This approach introduces zero runtime range-estimation overhead, because inputs are scaled by fixed factors and directly fed to integer execution units. However, static ranges risk saturation or underflow whenever production inputs deviate from the calibration distribution ([[01 Books/Volume I - Foundations/03 Optimization/10 Model Compression/V1-10.11 Source References#^ref-jacob2018|Jacob et al. 2018]]; [[01 Books/Volume I - Foundations/03 Optimization/10 Model Compression/V1-10.11 Source References#^ref-yao2021hawq|Yao et al. 2021]]).

**Dynamic quantization** instead calculates scaling factors and zero-points dynamically from runtime activation tensors. Adapting the clipping window to each input reduces mismatch with a fixed calibration range. Outliers can still consume resolution, and a policy that clips tails can still saturate values. The trade-off is runtime memory and latency overhead, because the hardware must perform reduction passes over intermediate activation tensors to extract minimum and maximum values, quantize the floating-point activations into integers on the fly, and only then launch the integer matrix multiplication. On memory-bandwidth-bound operators, this reduction pass can erode the execution advantage of lower precision. Dynamic integer quantization is also distinct from *weight-only* quantization (such as 4-bit weights with 16-bit floating-point activations), which skips activation quantization altogether and instead dequantizes compressed weights into floating-point registers inside the matrix-multiplication kernel.

##### PTQ in Practice

^sec-model-compression-ptq-practice-2800

Post-training quantization is the default starting point in production because it needs only a small calibration set and minutes of compute rather than a retraining pipeline. Whether offline calibration suffices depends on the architecture’s outlier structure, the calibration data’s coverage, the scale granularity, and the target bit width.

PTQ is limited because it freezes the trained weights. Calibration can move clipping bounds and refine per-channel or per-group scales, but it cannot move parameters to compensate for rounding error. Mixed precision at inference, meaning different bit widths for different layers rather than the FP16 and FP32 training of **[[01 Books/Volume I - Foundations/02 Development/08 Model Training/V1-08.07 Pipeline Optimizations#^sec-model-training-mixedprecision-training-9218|Mixed-precision training]]**, is the override that keeps a few layers at higher precision. Quantizing one layer at a time and measuring the accuracy drop, the layer-wise sensitivity analysis of [[01 Books/Volume I - Foundations/03 Optimization/10 Model Compression/V1-10.07 Measuring Compression Gains#^sec-model-compression-profiling-opportunity-analysis-477f|section 10.6.1]], shows which layers cause most of the loss; those stay at FP16 or INT8 while the tolerant majority drops to the lower width. When recalibration, finer scale granularity, and mixed-precision overrides still leave a candidate below its quality floor, the remaining lever is to let the weights adapt during training at the cost of additional training compute (${O_{\text{train}}}$) and hyperparameter tuning.

#### Quantization-Aware Training

^sec-model-compression-quantizationaware-training-4032

When post-training quantization drops a sensitive architecture or an aggressive 4-bit target below its accuracy floor, only gradient updates can recover the lost quality. Quantization-aware training (QAT) incorporates numerical rounding noise directly into the optimization graph ([[01 Books/Volume I - Foundations/03 Optimization/10 Model Compression/V1-10.04 Numerical Precision Changing How Values Are Stored#^sec-model-compression-training-mathematics-699e|section 10.3.3.2.1]]), retraining or fine-tuning a checkpoint on the training dataset so its parameters adapt to low-precision discretization error ([[01 Books/Volume I - Foundations/03 Optimization/10 Model Compression/V1-10.04 Numerical Precision Changing How Values Are Stored#^fig-qat|figure 25]]). Throughout that training, the optimizer updates master weights, the full-precision copy of each weight, and the quantized values are recomputed from them on every forward pass.

##### Training Mathematics

^sec-model-compression-training-mathematics-699e

During forward propagation, weights and activations are quantized and dequantized to mimic reduced precision. Let ${x}$ be a full-precision value, ${s}$ the scaling factor that maps floating-point values into a lower-precision range, and ${x_{\text{fake}}}$ the simulated (quantized, then dequantized) value. Ignoring clipping and the zero-point for this simplified expression, the process is represented as:

$$
{x_{\text{fake}}}= \text{round} \left(\frac{{x}}{{s}} \right) \times {s}
$$

Although the forward pass executes with simulated quantization, computing gradients requires backpropagating through the rounding operator $\text{round}(\cdot)$. Because rounding is a piecewise-constant step function, its mathematical derivative is zero almost everywhere and undefined at step transitions, which would zero out gradients and halt gradient descent. The **straight-through estimator (STE)** resolves this bottleneck by substituting the identity function for the rounding operator during backpropagation ([[01 Books/Volume I - Foundations/03 Optimization/10 Model Compression/V1-10.11 Source References#^ref-bengio2013estimating|Bengio et al. 2013]]).[[01 Books/Volume I - Foundations/03 Optimization/10 Model Compression/V1-10.12 Footnotes#^fn16|¹⁶]]

Integrating quantization effects during training lets the model adapt its weights and activation ranges to simulated low-precision error. QAT can recover quality that a post-training route loses, particularly at aggressive bit widths, but the advantage depends on the model, quantizer, calibration, and training procedure ([[01 Books/Volume I - Foundations/03 Optimization/10 Model Compression/V1-10.11 Source References#^ref-krishnamoorthi2018quantizing|Krishnamoorthi 2018]]).

##### Fake Quantization Nodes and Implementation

^sec-model-compression-fake-quantization-nodes-implementation-e822

QAT implementation relies on fake quantization operations that simulate quantization during forward propagation while maintaining full precision for gradient computation. These operations insert quantize-dequantize pairs into the computational graph, creating a training-time simulation of inference-time behavior.

A fake quantization node must preserve the same errors the inference runtime will see, so its three operations model the deployment path during training:

1.  **Quantization**: Map floating-point value to discrete quantization level
2.  **Clipping**: Enforce range constraints based on bit width
3.  **Dequantization**: Convert back to floating-point for subsequent operations

Mathematically, for symmetric quantization with bit width ${b}$, given a floating-point input value ${x}$:

$$
\begin{aligned} {x_q} &= \text{clip}\left(\text{round}\left(\frac{{x}}{{s}}\right), -2^{{b}-1}, 2^{{b}-1} - 1\right) \\ {x_{\text{fake}}}&= {x_q} \times {s}\end{aligned}
$$

 where ${x_q}$ is the quantized integer level, ${s}= \frac{\max(|{x}|)}{2^{{b}-1} - 1}$ is the scale factor computed from the input distribution, and ${x_{\text{fake}}}$ represents the fake-quantized output that mimics INT8 values but remains in floating-point format.

For asymmetric quantization supporting unsigned integers, the node applies the scale, zero-point, and quantize steps of [equation 1](https://mlsysbook.ai/vol1/10_model_compression/10_model_compression.html#eq-quant-scale), [equation 2](https://mlsysbook.ai/vol1/10_model_compression/10_model_compression.html#eq-quant-zero-point), and [equation 3](https://mlsysbook.ai/vol1/10_model_compression/10_model_compression.html#eq-quantize) to the observed range (extended to include zero) and then dequantizes:

$$
\begin{aligned} {s}&= \frac{\max({x}) - \min({x})}{2^{b}- 1} \\ {z}&= \text{round}\left(-\frac{\min({x})}{{s}}\right) \\ {x_q} &= \text{clip}\left(\text{round}\left(\frac{{x}}{{s}} + {z}\right), 0, 2^{b}- 1\right) \\ {x_{\text{fake}}}&= ({x_q} - {z}) \times {s}\end{aligned}
$$

 where ${z}$ is the zero-point offset enabling asymmetric range representation and the observed range $[\min({x}), \max({x})]$ serves as the clipping range $[{\alpha}, {\beta}]$.

In the QAT forward path, fake quantization is applied to inputs and weights before convolution, simulating INT8 numerics while retaining floating-point tensors for training ([[01 Books/Volume I - Foundations/03 Optimization/10 Model Compression/V1-10.04 Numerical Precision Changing How Values Are Stored#^lst-qat-conv-forward|listing 3]]).

``` python
# Forward pass with fake quantization
def qat_conv_forward(x, weight):
    # Fake quantize input activations
    x_scale = compute_scale(x, bits=8, symmetric=False)
    x_zero = compute_zero_point(x, x_scale, bits=8)
    x_quant = fake_quantize(x, x_scale, x_zero, bits=8)

    # Fake quantize weights (typically symmetric)
    w_scale = compute_scale(weight, bits=8, symmetric=True)
    w_quant = fake_quantize(weight, w_scale, zero=0, bits=8)

    # Convolution with fake-quantized values
    output = conv2d(x_quant, w_quant)
    return output
```

Listing 3: **QAT Convolution Forward Pass**: Fake-quantizes activations and weights before convolution to simulate deployment numerics during training.

^lst-qat-conv-forward

During backpropagation, fake quantization nodes approximate gradients through a clipping-aware straight-through estimator:

$$
\frac{\partial {x_{\text{fake}}}}{\partial {x}} = \begin{cases} 1 & \text{if } {x}\in [{\alpha}, {\beta}] \\ 0 & \text{otherwise} \end{cases}
$$

 Within the clipping range $[{\alpha}, {\beta}]$, the estimator treats quantization as an identity mapping, passing the upstream gradient $\frac{\partial {\mathcal{L}}}{\partial {x_{\text{fake}}}}$ unchanged to the master weight ${x}$. Beyond clipping boundaries, the derivative evaluates to zero, blocking gradients from saturated outliers that would otherwise destabilize optimizer updates.

In practice, frameworks like PyTorch and TensorFlow implement fake quantization as custom autograd operators whose forward pass performs the quantize-dequantize round trip while the backward pass applies an STE. Scale handling depends on the method, since observers may track distributions and later freeze, or scales may be learned. Batch normalization also requires deployment-consistent handling. Workflows freeze batch-normalization statistics and fold them into the preceding convolution before export ([[01 Books/Volume I - Foundations/03 Optimization/10 Model Compression/V1-10.05 Hardware Implementation Changing How the Model Executes#^sec-model-compression-operator-fusion-ac1d|section 10.4.1]]). Freezing running statistics before export allows fake-quantization scales to match the static fused parameters executed during inference.

##### QAT Trade-Offs

^sec-model-compression-qat-tradeoffs-e8ef

QAT’s[[01 Books/Volume I - Foundations/03 Optimization/10 Model Compression/V1-10.12 Footnotes#^fn17|¹⁷]] primary advantage is additional control over quality at low precision. By incorporating simulated quantization noise during training or fine-tuning, the model can adapt to the deployed numerical path. Processors with dedicated integer units can then exploit INT8 arithmetic for faster or lower-energy inference when the exported operators are supported ([[01 Books/Volume I - Foundations/03 Optimization/10 Model Compression/V1-10.11 Source References#^ref-wu2020integer|Wu et al. 2020]]; [[01 Books/Volume I - Foundations/03 Optimization/10 Model Compression/V1-10.11 Source References#^ref-gholami2022|Gholami et al. 2022]]). The benefit must be measured against PTQ for the same model and target, including the extra training cost and any difference in calibration, graph coverage, or kernel selection.

The cost is additional engineering complexity. QAT inserts simulated quantization into training, so teams must validate quantization schemes, scale behavior, and accuracy recovery on the target model. For very large models whose training budgets are already constrained, that overhead can make QAT impractical, and post-training routes avoid retraining altogether ([[01 Books/Volume I - Foundations/03 Optimization/10 Model Compression/V1-10.11 Source References#^ref-choukroun2019|Choukroun et al. 2019]]). QAT is therefore warranted only when the exported PTQ model misses its quality or deployment targets and training resources permit. A hybrid approach, starting with PTQ calibration and applying QAT fine-tuning only to accuracy-critical layers, often provides a useful balance.

The choice among PTQ, QAT, and dynamic quantization comes down to engineering budget, accuracy threshold, and input variability ([[01 Books/Volume I - Foundations/03 Optimization/10 Model Compression/V1-10.04 Numerical Precision Changing How Values Are Stored#^tbl-quantization-methods-6b4e056b|table 10]]). Dynamic range computation is a timing choice orthogonal to PTQ versus QAT (dynamic PTQ is common), but it belongs in the comparison because it answers the third of those questions.

| **Method**               | **Engineering effort**                                 | **Accuracy preservation**                | **Input adaptability**  | **Reach for it when**                                                          |
|:-------------------------|:-------------------------------------------------------|:-----------------------------------------|:------------------------|:-------------------------------------------------------------------------------|
| **Static PTQ**           | Low: no retraining                                     | Model-dependent; parameters do not adapt | Fixed calibration range | a calibrated model meets the measured accuracy and performance targets         |
| **Static QAT**           | High: additional training with quantization simulation | Often stronger when PTQ falls short      | Fixed calibration range | PTQ misses the accuracy target and the training budget allows                  |
| **Dynamic quantization** | Moderate: ranges recomputed at runtime                 | Model-dependent; range fits each input   | Per-input range         | activation ranges vary widely across inputs and runtime overhead is acceptable |

PTQ serves as the low-effort baseline; QAT spends training cycles when PTQ degrades task accuracy, while dynamic quantization expends runtime memory traffic and compute to track input-dependent dynamic ranges. In practice, PTQ and QAT commonly target 8-bit or 4-bit precision, where retained accuracy depends on the model, task, and quantizer. Some deployment scenarios, however, demand even more aggressive compression, pushing precision to the absolute limits of what neural networks can tolerate.

### Extreme Quantization

^sec-model-compression-extreme-quantization-aba4

On a microcontroller whose runtime buffers and audio feature extraction leave barely 100 KB of SRAM for weights, or on an energy-harvesting sensor whose power envelope cannot afford hardware multipliers, even 8-bit and 4-bit integers exceed the deployment budget. Extreme quantization pushes numerical precision down to 1 or 2 bits per value for those targets ([[01 Books/Volume I - Foundations/03 Optimization/10 Model Compression/V1-10.11 Source References#^ref-courbariaux2015binaryconnect|Courbariaux et al. 2015]]; [[01 Books/Volume I - Foundations/03 Optimization/10 Model Compression/V1-10.11 Source References#^ref-Zhu2017|Zhu et al. 2017]]).

Binarization constrains weights and activations to two values, typically encoded as $\{-1, +1\}$. In digital logic, mapping $-1$ to binary $0$ and $+1$ to binary $1$ renders scalar multiplication isomorphic to the bitwise `XNOR` operation, replacing floating-point or integer MAC units with bitwise logic and population count (`popcount`) instructions ([[01 Books/Volume I - Foundations/03 Optimization/10 Model Compression/V1-10.11 Source References#^ref-Rastegari2016|Rastegari et al. 2016]]). Because a 64-bit register packs 64 binary values, a single 64-bit `XNOR` instruction computes 64 binary products in parallel. A hardware `popcount` instruction then counts the matching bits to accumulate the dot product ($2 \times \text{popcount}({\mathbf{w}}\text{ XNOR } {\mathbf{a}}) - 64$), which eliminates hardware multipliers altogether. That efficiency comes at a steep cost in expressiveness, since 1-bit representations degrade accuracy sharply on complex perceptual and language tasks ([[01 Books/Volume I - Foundations/03 Optimization/10 Model Compression/V1-10.11 Source References#^ref-Hubara2018|Hubara et al. 2018]]).

Ternarization mitigates this capacity loss by allowing three values: $\{-1, 0, +1\}$ ([[01 Books/Volume I - Foundations/03 Optimization/10 Model Compression/V1-10.11 Source References#^ref-Zhu2017|Zhu et al. 2017]]). The zero state introduces sparsity and simplifies arithmetic. Multiplying an activation by $+1$ requires an addition, multiplying by $-1$ requires a subtraction, and multiplying by $0$ is skipped entirely. This eliminates general-purpose multipliers, reducing the arithmetic unit to a conditional adder-subtractor accumulator. However, ternary values cannot be packed at one bit per value. They require at least two bits per weight in standard byte-aligned memory (wasting one of four possible 2-bit states) or multi-trit encoding (such as packing five trits into a single byte, since $3^5 = 243 < 256$) that introduces runtime decoding overhead. The arithmetic gains of multiplier-free execution must outweigh the instruction overhead required to decode and unpack the weights on the target architecture.

Both binary and ternary quantization introduce step functions whose derivative is zero almost everywhere ($\frac{{d}}{d{x}}\text{sign}({x}) = 0$ for ${x}\neq 0$), causing backpropagation gradients to vanish. To train these representations, quantization-aware training again relies on the straight-through estimator ([[01 Books/Volume I - Foundations/03 Optimization/10 Model Compression/V1-10.11 Source References#^ref-bengio2013estimating|Bengio et al. 2013]]; [[01 Books/Volume I - Foundations/03 Optimization/10 Model Compression/V1-10.11 Source References#^ref-Choi2019|Choi et al. 2018]]) of [[01 Books/Volume I - Foundations/03 Optimization/10 Model Compression/V1-10.04 Numerical Precision Changing How Values Are Stored#^sec-model-compression-training-mathematics-699e|section 10.3.3.2.1]], whose gradients reach full-precision latent weights that accumulate small optimizer steps until they cross the quantization thresholds.

#### Challenges and Limitations

^sec-model-compression-challenges-limitations-a99c

Translating sub-byte precision into actual wall-clock speedups is constrained by standard processor architectures. General-purpose memory hierarchies are byte-addressed and vector ALUs operate on 8-bit, 16-bit, or 32-bit lanes, so native support for binary or two-bit matrix operations is rare outside specialized engines. Moreover, when 1-bit or 2-bit weights multiply 8-bit or 16-bit activations, or when four 2-bit ternary weights are packed into a single byte, bitwise `XNOR` logic no longer applies. On a target without a matching sub-byte datapath, the kernel must load each packed byte into a register and execute bit-shifting, masking, and sign-extension instructions to unpack every 1-bit or 2-bit weight into an 8-bit or 16-bit lane before the vector multiplier can run. The instruction overhead of this bit manipulation frequently offsets memory-bandwidth savings unless workloads run on custom bit-serial accelerators, field-programmable gate arrays (FPGAs), or specialized hardware with native bitwise execution units ([[01 Books/Volume I - Foundations/03 Optimization/10 Model Compression/V1-10.11 Source References#^ref-umuroglu2017|Umuroglu et al. 2017]]).

Model quality is the second barrier. Extreme quantization sharply reduces the representational capacity of a network. While low-entropy workloads such as KWS on a microcontroller SRAM budget may tolerate binary or ternary weights, complex perceptual and generative models suffer steep accuracy degradation. Binary and ternary quantization become viable candidates only when higher-precision alternatives such as INT8 or INT4 fail to satisfy physical memory or energy budgets, and when the deployment hardware can execute bit-packed operations natively ([[01 Books/Volume I - Foundations/03 Optimization/10 Model Compression/V1-10.11 Source References#^ref-Rastegari2016|Rastegari et al. 2016]]; [[01 Books/Volume I - Foundations/03 Optimization/10 Model Compression/V1-10.11 Source References#^ref-Hubara2018|Hubara et al. 2018]]; [[01 Books/Volume I - Foundations/03 Optimization/10 Model Compression/V1-10.11 Source References#^ref-Zhu2017|Zhu et al. 2017]]; [[01 Books/Volume I - Foundations/03 Optimization/10 Model Compression/V1-10.11 Source References#^ref-umuroglu2017|Umuroglu et al. 2017]]). Production deployment requires evaluating binary and ternary baselines against INT8, INT4, structured pruning, and compact architectural redesigns on the identical workload, measuring total binary artifact size, end-to-end latency, energy per inference, and task accuracy on target hardware. Whether a narrower format actually runs faster is the last question the precision tier must answer.

### When Fewer Bits Run Faster

^sec-model-compression-performance-gains-0656

Shrinking weights from 16 bits to 8 bits fails to speed up a compute-bound matrix multiplication if the processor lacks a faster 8-bit datapath, just as faster integer ALUs fail to speed up autoregressive decoding when latency is stalled on memory-bus transfers. Every byte that quantization removes is a byte the memory system no longer moves. Whether a quantized model runs faster, however, depends on which hardware resource is the bottleneck. A compute-bound matrix multiplication speeds up only when the hardware provides a higher-throughput datapath for the narrower format, whereas a bandwidth-bound decode speeds up in proportion to the bytes removed from each token’s traffic. The same FP16-to-INT8 change therefore halves weight traffic on a bandwidth-bound decode, but on a compute-bound layer it gains the ratio of the two datapaths’ peak rates, which is nothing without a faster 8-bit path. The worked examples below bracket the payoff, first on the reference H100 for a compute-bound layer and then on the running case’s phone for GPT-2 XL decoding.

**Napkin Math 10.2: The quantization speedup (compute bound)**

**Problem**: A compute-bound matrix multiplication (for example, in a transformer multilayer perceptron block at a large batch) switches from FP16 to INT8 on the reference H100. What throughput speedup can the integer matrix units deliver?

**Math**: On hardware with dedicated INT8 units:

1.  **Integer throughput path**: The H100 rates its INT8 matrix path at 1979 TOPS against 989 TFLOP/s on the dense FP16 path ([[01 Books/Volume I - Foundations/03 Optimization/10 Model Compression/V1-10.11 Source References#^ref-nvidia2022h100|NVIDIA Corporation 2022]]; [[01 Books/Volume I - Foundations/03 Optimization/10 Model Compression/V1-10.11 Source References#^ref-choquette2023|Choquette 2023]]). The peak throughput increase is that spec ratio, about 2×.
2.  **Memory bandwidth**: Packed INT8 weights carry half the raw weight bytes of FP16. Their streaming time can approach half at the same achieved bandwidth, before metadata and conversion costs.
3.  **Combined effect**: A compute-bound operation already waits on arithmetic rather than bytes, so the halved weight traffic of step 2 adds nothing, and the arithmetic ratio of step 1 sets the ideal throughput factor at about 2×, at comparable utilization of the two arithmetic paths.

**Systems insight**: The speedup from quantization depends on the bottleneck. Compute-bound operations (large batch sizes, high arithmetic intensity ${I}$) can approach about 2× at comparable utilization of supported INT8 units, where the gain comes from the integer matrix path rather than reduced memory traffic. The bandwidth-bound case inverts this. Halving the bytes moved (FP16 to INT8) yields up to 2×, and larger bit-width reductions scale the gain further, as the decode example below traces.

Processors without dedicated matrix units see the same compute-bound effect through data-level parallelism in their registers, the path a phone’s CPU takes before a model moves to its integer accelerator. Fixed-width vector execution units (SIMD) process more operands per cycle when each operand occupies fewer bits.

**Napkin Math 10.3: The SIMD multiplier**

**Problem**: A compute-bound layer executes using INT8 rather than FP32 on the same processor. Determine the architectural source of arithmetic speedup under fixed register width.

**Mechanism**: SIMD execution. A CPU or GPU core processes data in fixed-width vector registers (for example, AVX-512 is 512 bits wide).

**Math**:

1.  **Register width**: 512 bits.
2.  **FP32 capacity**: 512/32 = 16 elements per vector instruction.
3.  **INT8 capacity**: 512/8 = 64 elements per vector instruction.

**Result**: Switching to INT8 packs 4× more elements into the same register. $\text{Throughput Gain} = \text{INT8 elements/inst} / \text{FP32 elements/inst}$ = 64/16 = 4×

**Systems insight**: The packing ratio gives an ideal 4× throughput factor, assuming comparable vector-instruction throughput, occupied lanes, and supported operators. It is a candidate bound for compute-bound layers, not a measured layer speedup; memory traffic and conversion overhead still affect execution.

When an operation is bandwidth bound, speedup tracks the reduction in bytes transferred per token rather than peak arithmetic throughput. In autoregressive token generation with small batch sizes, memory bus traffic dominates latency.

**Napkin Math 10.4: The quantization speedup (bandwidth bound)**

**Problem**: The running case’s phone decodes GPT-2 XL (1.5B parameters) at batch size one with a full 1,024-token context. Its memory bus delivers 51.2 GB/s, and each token must read every weight and the whole KV cache. How much faster does decoding run with INT4 weights than with FP16 weights?

**Math (FP16 weights)**:

1.  **Weights**: 3 GB at two bytes per parameter.
2.  **KV cache**: Keys and values across 48 layers, 25 heads, and 64 dimensions per head, stored in FP16 for 1,024 tokens, occupy 0.31 GB.
3.  **Total per token**: 3.3 GB, which fits the phone’s 8 GB but takes 65 ms to stream, or about 15 tokens/s.

**Fix (INT4 weights)**:

1.  **Weights**: 0.75 GB at half a byte per parameter, for 1.06 GB per token with the unchanged FP16 KV cache.
2.  **Speed**: Streaming that total takes 21 ms, or about 48 tokens/s.

**Systems insight**: INT4 weights yield a 3.1× speedup in this traffic model, below the 4$\times$ weight reduction because the FP16 KV-cache traffic is unchanged. The model already fit in memory at FP16; quantization bought speed, not feasibility. The realized speedup still depends on a kernel that unpacks INT4 weights inside the matrix multiplication rather than in a separate pass.

Vision networks show the same split between bytes and time. Across three of them (Inception v3, MobileNet v1, and ResNet v2), INT8 conversion contracts weight storage by exactly $4\times$ (135 MB to 33.75 MB for the illustrative Inception bar), while the latency change varies with the architecture and its kernels ([[01 Books/Volume I - Foundations/03 Optimization/10 Model Compression/V1-10.04 Numerical Precision Changing How Values Are Stored#^fig-quantization-impact|figure 27]]).

*Quantization speedup depends on whether memory bandwidth or compute throughput is the limit.*

Activation-aware methods such as **activation-aware weight quantization (AWQ)**[[01 Books/Volume I - Foundations/03 Optimization/10 Model Compression/V1-10.12 Footnotes#^fn18|¹⁸]] target weight traffic in large language model (LLM) inference. This approach serves exactly the regime of GPT-2 XL decoding on the phone, where loading model weights limits the token rate. Storing the most sensitive 1 percent of weights in FP16 while quantizing the remaining 99 percent to INT4 would preserve accuracy, but mixing bit widths inside a weight matrix breaks contiguous memory access and slows down vector kernels.

Instead of using mixed precision, AWQ multiplies each salient weight channel, identified by the average magnitude of the input activation channel it multiplies, by a scale factor greater than one before quantizing *all* weights uniformly to INT4, and divides the corresponding input activation channel by the same factor so the mathematical product is unchanged. The scaled channels lose a smaller fraction of their value to rounding, and every weight stays in a uniform, hardware-friendly INT4 layout. Packed low-bit weights can then reduce the memory traffic required during token generation when the runtime provides a matching kernel ([[01 Books/Volume I - Foundations/03 Optimization/10 Model Compression/V1-10.11 Source References#^ref-lin2023awq|Lin et al. 2024]]).

**Checkpoint 10.3: Quantization and precision checkpoint**

Quantization design review criteria:

**INT8 memory vs. energy**: Explain why INT8’s per-operation energy saving can exceed 4$\times$ while its end-to-end energy saving is usually smaller.

**QAT vs. PTQ**: Evaluate the architectural advantages of quantization-aware training over post-training quantization, identifying the accuracy and retraining cost trade-offs that justify auxiliary optimization.

**Bit-width speedup**: For GPT-2 XL decoding on the phone, estimate FP16 versus INT4 weight bytes and the ideal weight-traffic speedup bound. Identify the conditions under which weight-traffic reduction approaches a proportional latency gain, and explain how KV traffic or a separate dequantization pass can limit that gain.

**Compute versus bandwidth**: Explain why the same FP16-to-INT8 change can roughly double throughput on a compute-bound layer and halve time on a bandwidth-bound one for different reasons.

These speedups are bounds computed from bytes and peak rates. Whether a deployed model reaches them depends on how its compressed operations are lowered onto kernels, which is the third tier of the stack.
