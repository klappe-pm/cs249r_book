# V1-01.16 Footnotes

[[Home|Home]] · [[01 Books/Volume I - Foundations/Volume 1 Overview|Volume Overview]] · [[01 Books/Volume I - Foundations/00 Introduction/01 Introduction/V1-01 Introduction|V1-01 Introduction]]

^footnotes

<sup>1</sup> **GPU (graphics processing unit)**: Originally designed to render graphics by performing many calculations in parallel. Neural networks also require large numbers of similar arithmetic operations, especially matrix multiplications, making this hardware useful for training. Keeping those operations supplied with data remains a separate engineering challenge.

^fn1

<sup>2</sup> **Model weights**: The learned numerical parameters of a neural network. They must be stored and accessed during execution, so their number and numerical representation affect memory use, data movement, and cost.

^fn2

<sup>3</sup> **Andrej Karpathy**: A founding member of OpenAI and former director of AI at Tesla. His “Software 2.0” essay described neural network weights as a new form of “source code” ([[01 Books/Volume I - Foundations/00 Introduction/01 Introduction/V1-01.15 Source References#^ref-karpathy2017software|Karpathy 2017]]). The analogy shifts debugging attention toward the examples that shaped those weights and the conditions under which the model is evaluated.

^fn3

<sup>4</sup> **Inference**: Applying a trained model to inputs, whether during evaluation or deployment. Interactive serving often imposes a response-time limit, whereas offline inference may prioritize the total number of examples processed per second. These workloads can require different execution choices even for the same model (see **[[01 Books/Volume I - Foundations/04 Deployment/13 Model Serving/V1-13 Model Serving#^sec-model-serving|Model Serving]]**).

^fn4

<sup>5</sup> **Stochastic gradient descent (SGD)**: A training algorithm, descended from the stochastic approximation method of Robbins and Monro ([[01 Books/Volume I - Foundations/00 Introduction/01 Introduction/V1-01.15 Source References#^ref-robbins1951stochastic|1951]]), that repeatedly adjusts model parameters using small, randomly sampled groups of examples, called batches, rather than processing the entire dataset for each update.

^fn5

<sup>6</sup> **AlexNet**: Alex Krizhevsky, Ilya Sutskever, and Geoffrey Hinton trained this image-recognition network across two GPUs. At ILSVRC 2012, its 15.3 percent top-5 error, meaning the correct class was absent from its five highest-scoring predictions, substantially beat the second-place system’s 26.2 percent ([[01 Books/Volume I - Foundations/00 Introduction/01 Introduction/V1-01.15 Source References#^ref-krizhevsky2012imagenet|Krizhevsky et al. 2012]]). [[01 Books/Volume I - Foundations/00 Introduction/01 Introduction/V1-01.04 The Evolution of AI Bottlenecks#^sec-introduction-deep-learning-era-infrastructure-bottleneck-2f02|Section 1.3.3]] returns to its architecture and systems co-design.

^fn6

<sup>7</sup> **Alan Turing**: His “Imitation Game” approached machine intelligence through observable responses rather than a definition of its internal nature ([[01 Books/Volume I - Foundations/00 Introduction/01 Introduction/V1-01.15 Source References#^ref-turing1950|Turing 1950]]). It provides an early example of evaluating a system through what it does.

^fn7

<sup>8</sup> **ELIZA**: A natural-language program whose scripts could store selected inputs and reuse them in later responses ([[01 Books/Volume I - Foundations/00 Introduction/01 Introduction/V1-01.15 Source References#^ref-weizenbaum1966|Weizenbaum 1966]]). This limited memory was programmed explicitly rather than learned from conversations. Extending its coverage required changing the scripts, even when the existing program ran correctly.

^fn8

<sup>9</sup> **AI winters as systems failures**: The first AI winter (1974–1980) unfolded amid funding cuts; the 1973 Lighthill Report criticized the gap between AI promises and delivered results ([[01 Books/Volume I - Foundations/00 Introduction/01 Introduction/V1-01.15 Source References#^ref-lighthill1973artificial|Lighthill 1973]]). The second winter (1987–1993) involved a market and funding collapse around expert systems and specialized Lisp machines as general-purpose workstations undercut their economics ([[01 Books/Volume I - Foundations/00 Introduction/01 Introduction/V1-01.15 Source References#^ref-hendler2008avoiding|Hendler 2008]]). These episodes involved technical limitations, unmet expectations, and economics; hardware capacity alone does not explain them.

^fn9

<sup>10</sup> **STUDENT**: An early program that translated algebra word problems into equations using hand-written language rules ([[01 Books/Volume I - Foundations/00 Introduction/01 Introduction/V1-01.15 Source References#^ref-bobrow1964student|Bobrow 1964]]). Its success depended on the wording fitting those rules; an algebra solver could solve the resulting equations without establishing that the program had interpreted the original problem correctly.

^fn10

<sup>11</sup> **Dartmouth Conference (1956)**: The workshop organized around the term “artificial intelligence,” already used in its 1955 proposal ([[01 Books/Volume I - Foundations/00 Introduction/01 Introduction/V1-01.15 Source References#^ref-mccarthy1955dartmouth|McCarthy et al. 1955]]). The proposal discussed limits in computer speed and memory while treating programming as the larger obstacle. Physical capacity was part of the problem from the outset, even as researchers focused on language, abstraction, problem solving, and self-improvement.

^fn11

<sup>12</sup> **Moravec’s paradox**: Hans Moravec observed that skills people acquire with little conscious effort, such as perception and movement, can be harder to reproduce computationally than formal reasoning tasks ([[01 Books/Volume I - Foundations/00 Introduction/01 Introduction/V1-01.15 Source References#^ref-moravec1988mind|Moravec 1988]]). Human familiarity with a task is therefore a poor guide to its engineering difficulty or resource requirements.

^fn12

<sup>13</sup> **Knowledge acquisition bottleneck**: Feigenbaum ([[01 Books/Volume I - Foundations/00 Introduction/01 Introduction/V1-01.15 Source References#^ref-feigenbaum1984knowledge|1984]]) treated extracting and representing expert knowledge as a central challenge in building applied AI systems. Experts could exercise judgment without being able to state every rule behind it, so acquiring knowledge required more than transcribing a ready-made list.

^fn13

<sup>14</sup> **Unreasonable effectiveness of data**: The observation that a simple statistical model with abundant data can outperform a more sophisticated model with less data ([[01 Books/Volume I - Foundations/00 Introduction/01 Introduction/V1-01.15 Source References#^ref-halevy2009|Halevy et al. 2009]]). The benefit depends on the task, model, and data quality. This makes collecting and preparing useful examples part of the engineering work, rather than merely a preliminary step.

^fn14

<sup>15</sup> **Viola-Jones algorithm**: The detector applied a sequence of classifiers, rejecting likely nonface regions early and spending more computation on promising regions. On the reported test set, it evaluated an average of ten features per candidate image region, although the complete cascade contained over 6,000 features ([[01 Books/Volume I - Foundations/00 Introduction/01 Introduction/V1-01.15 Source References#^ref-viola2001|Viola and Jones 2001]]). Its speed depended on how little work most inputs required, not just on how fast each operation ran.

^fn15

<sup>16</sup> **Richard Sutton**: A reinforcement learning pioneer whose essay emphasizes search and learning as methods that scale with computation ([[01 Books/Volume I - Foundations/00 Introduction/01 Introduction/V1-01.15 Source References#^ref-sutton2019bitter|Sutton 2019]]). The bitter lesson is about long-term progress, not a claim that domain knowledge has no engineering value.

^fn16

<sup>17</sup> **Deep Blue**: Its 480 custom chess chips worked alongside general-purpose processors, searching about 100 million positions per second in tactical positions and approaching 200 million in quieter positions ([[01 Books/Volume I - Foundations/00 Introduction/01 Introduction/V1-01.15 Source References#^ref-campbell2002|Campbell et al. 2002]]). The hardware accelerated chess-specific search and evaluation; it did not remove the system’s reliance on encoded chess knowledge.

^fn17

<sup>18</sup> **AlphaGo Zero**: After three days of self-play training, it defeated AlphaGo Lee, the version that had beaten Lee Sedol, by 100 games to 0 ([[01 Books/Volume I - Foundations/00 Introduction/01 Introduction/V1-01.15 Source References#^ref-silver2017|Silver et al. 2017]]). Self-play generated the training examples, allowing search and learning to improve play without a dataset of human games.

^fn18

<sup>19</sup> **GPT-3 training energy**: Patterson et al. ([[01 Books/Volume I - Foundations/00 Introduction/01 Introduction/V1-01.15 Source References#^ref-patterson2021carbon|2021]]) estimated that GPT-3’s training consumed approximately 1,287 MWh and produced 552 metric tons of CO<sub>2</sub>-equivalent emissions. The energy estimate corresponds to the annual electricity use of about 120 US households at a baseline of 10.7 MWh/household-year. Emissions also depend on how that electricity is generated.

^fn19

<sup>20</sup> **Memory bandwidth**: The rate at which a memory interface transfers data, measured in bytes per second. A processor must receive the values an operation needs before it can perform the arithmetic. When moving those values takes longer than computing with them, faster arithmetic alone cannot remove the delay; reducing transfers or increasing the available bandwidth addresses the limiting resource.

^fn20

<sup>21</sup> **Microcontrollers**: Single-chip computers integrating a processor, memory, and interfaces to sensors or other devices. The keyword-spotting lighthouse has a kilobyte-scale memory budget and a milliwatt-scale power budget. A candidate model must fit those limits as well as meet its prediction-quality requirement.

^fn21

<sup>22</sup> **ImageNet**: The full image collection grew from 3.2 million images reported in the initial paper to about 14.2 million across thousands of labeled categories ([[01 Books/Volume I - Foundations/00 Introduction/01 Introduction/V1-01.15 Source References#^ref-deng2009|Deng et al. 2009]]; [[01 Books/Volume I - Foundations/00 Introduction/01 Introduction/V1-01.15 Source References#^ref-russakovsky2015|Russakovsky et al. 2015]]). The 2012 challenge used a subset of this collection; AlexNet’s training split contained about 1.3M labeled images. Distinguishing the full collection from a benchmark split matters when comparing dataset sizes and training costs.

^fn22

<sup>23</sup> **Model compression**: A family of techniques that reduces model storage or computation, including quantization (storing values in fewer bits), pruning (removing parameters that contribute little), and distillation (training a smaller model to imitate a larger one). Its size and accuracy effects depend on the model, method, workload, and target hardware.

^fn23

<sup>24</sup> **Moore’s law**: Gordon Moore’s 1965 observation described rapid growth in the number of components that could be economically integrated on a chip ([[01 Books/Volume I - Foundations/00 Introduction/01 Introduction/V1-01.15 Source References#^ref-moore1998|Moore 1998]]); later industry summaries often expressed the cadence as roughly a two-year doubling.

^fn24

<sup>25</sup> **Computer engineering**: A discipline spanning digital circuits, processor design, and the software that runs on them. Moving a function from software into specialized hardware can change its speed, power use, and flexibility. ML systems engineering extends this shared design problem to include learned behavior and the data that shapes it.

^fn25

<sup>26</sup> **Latency at the edge**: Every millisecond of delay is distance traveled before a decision takes effect. A vehicle traveling at 30 m/s moves 3 cm during each millisecond of delay. Sensing, communication, computation, and actuation all contribute to the time before braking can begin, so reducing network delay addresses only part of the response budget.

^fn26

<sup>27</sup> **Inference attack**: An attempt to deduce sensitive information about training data from a model’s outputs. Memorization can expose information, but the risk depends on the data, training procedure, and access available to the attacker.

^fn27
