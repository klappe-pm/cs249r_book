# V1-01.10 AI Engineering as a Discipline

[[Home|Home]] · [[01 Books/Volume I - Foundations/Volume 1 Overview|Volume Overview]] · [[01 Books/Volume I - Foundations/00 Introduction/01 Introduction/V1-01 Introduction|V1-01 Introduction]]

^sec-introduction-defining-ai-engineering-19ce

A cloud service may optimize throughput across racks of accelerators, while an edge device must remain within a strict battery and thermal envelope, so the same model can be efficient in one setting and unusable in another. An engineer who must hold predictive quality and resource cost to one specification across such different operating budgets needs to combine three quantitative tools. The degradation equation judges predictive quality in both settings, the iron law and its energy counterpart ([[01 Books/Volume I - Foundations/00 Introduction/01 Introduction/V1-01.08 Iron Law of ML Systems#^eq-energy-cost|equation 5]]) judge execution time and energy, and return on compute judges whether the cost of improving either is worth paying.

Applying these three tools draws on computer science, electrical engineering, statistics, and established software and systems engineering practice. Combining these fields resembles the earlier convergence of computing and electrical engineering in computer engineering.[[01 Books/Volume I - Foundations/00 Introduction/01 Introduction/V1-01.16 Footnotes#^fn25|²⁵]] In this book’s usage, AI engineering names the discipline and its shared principles, while ML systems engineering names the work of applying them to build and operate a specific system under its deployment constraints.

**Definition 1.3: AI engineering**

***AI engineering*** is the discipline of designing, deploying, and maintaining ML systems whose predictive quality and execution meet stated deployment requirements. It integrates decisions about data, algorithms, and machines, using statistical evidence to assess learned behavior and measurements to assess resource use.

1.  **Significance**: A model improvement must still satisfy the other system requirements. AI engineering jointly considers data quality and governance, prediction quality, execution time, memory, energy, and cost. A model that achieves 95 percent accuracy in research but violates a 100 ms deployment latency requirement does not meet the system specification.
2.  **Distinction**: A model-level evaluation establishes evidence under a stated dataset and protocol. AI engineering must carry that evidence into a complete system whose latency, throughput, accuracy, cost, fairness, and robustness meet deployment requirements, and reassess it when operating conditions change.
3.  **Common pitfall**: A frequent misconception is that AI engineering is just “software engineering for ML.” Software correctness remains necessary, but the system specification also includes statistical claims about learned behavior over an operating population. Code tests cannot establish those claims by themselves, making continuing evaluation and monitoring part of the system design.

Because the definition rests on statistical evidence, a requirement can be fixed while the evidence for meeting it remains uncertain. Pedestrian recall of at least 95 percent over a stated population is a fixed target; evaluation estimates recall from sampled outcomes, with uncertainty that must be reported and reassessed as conditions change. Execution requirements also need a defined workload and measurement protocol. Sampled response times support a statistical latency claim; a hard bound requires assumptions and analysis covering the specified execution conditions.

### Engineering Across the ML Lifecycle

^sec-introduction-ml-system-lifecycle-849f

Shipping a trained model to production does not end the engineering work, because the evidence behind it must be reassessed as conditions change. The first shift in user behavior or upstream data can force a deployed system back into data collection and training. Putting an algorithmic advance into service requires data ingestion and preprocessing, computation within the available machine budget, reliable serving, and ongoing evidence of real-world performance. These obligations form a recurring lifecycle rather than a linear sequence that ends at deployment. The engineering object is no longer only code but also data, model behavior, deployment context, and monitoring evidence evolving together.

The difference from conventional software shows up first in tooling. Version control, continuous integration, and static analysis have long supported code-defined behavior. For learned behavior, these practices must also track dataset and model versions and test predictive quality, because source-code changes alone do not describe changes to the deployed system.

↪ The workflow controls that learned behavior adds to established software practice are developed in **[[01 Books/Volume I - Foundations/01 Foundations/03 ML Lifecycle/V1-03.02 The Lifecycle as a Loop#^sec-ml-workflow-ml-vs-traditional-software-development-c5f5|ML vs. traditional software]]**.

At the project level, the work begins with problem definition, which sets the task and acceptance requirements before any data is collected. [[01 Books/Volume I - Foundations/00 Introduction/01 Introduction/V1-01.10 AI Engineering as a Discipline#^fig-ml-lifecycle-overview|Figure 7]] follows the work that comes after it through six stages: data collection, data preparation, model training, evaluation, deployment, and monitoring.

Feedback in this loop must assess learned behavior as well as software execution. In [[01 Books/Volume I - Foundations/00 Introduction/01 Introduction/V1-01.10 AI Engineering as a Discipline#^fig-ml-lifecycle-overview|figure 7]], a failed evaluation sends work back to data preparation, while degraded production performance sends it back to data collection. The six stages and the feedback paths that join them into a loop are developed in **[[01 Books/Volume I - Foundations/01 Foundations/03 ML Lifecycle/V1-03.02 The Lifecycle as a Loop#^sec-ml-workflow-understanding-ml-lifecycle-ca87|The Lifecycle as a Loop]]**.

Each stage of this cycle carries resource demands of its own. Those demands are not uniform, because the limiting physical constraints depend on where the system is deployed.

### Deployment Context Shapes the Lifecycle

^sec-introduction-deployment-spectrum-a38c

Deployment context determines which lifecycle pressures dominate. The same stages apply across ML systems, but a megawatt-scale data center and a milliwatt-scale embedded device impose different bottlenecks on data collection, model updates, monitoring, and serving. Four **deployment paradigms** span that range (cloud, edge, mobile, and TinyML), and their envelopes sit orders of magnitude apart.

From cloud to TinyML, the endpoints differ by roughly $10^{5}$ in memory and $10^{6}$ in compute. Because a cloud model cannot fit directly on a microcontroller, moving down that scale requires compression, a smaller architecture, or a narrower task. At the upper end, cloud ML systems offer large computing budgets alongside capacity limits, operational complexity, and substantial infrastructure bills. At the lower end, TinyML systems on microcontrollers make memory and energy feasibility an immediate concern, as a sensor may need to detect anomalies on a single battery for years. Between these poles, placement determines where work and resource demands fall. Edge ML systems, such as a gateway in a factory or a camera that analyzes its own video, move computation toward data sources to reduce latency[[01 Books/Volume I - Foundations/00 Introduction/01 Introduction/V1-01.16 Footnotes#^fn26|²⁶]] and network traffic. Mobile ML systems share memory, thermal headroom, and battery power with foreground applications. Hybrid systems split work across tiers to balance latency, privacy, bandwidth, and update control.

↪ The light barrier, power wall, and memory wall that shape these operating envelopes are developed in **[[01 Books/Volume I - Foundations/01 Foundations/02 System Paradigms/V1-02.03 Physical Constraints on Deployment#^sec-ml-systems-deployment-spectrum-71be|Physical Constraints on Deployment]]**.

Tight response deadlines or unreliable connectivity can require local execution, as in vehicle control. Fraud detection, in contrast, can run on cloud infrastructure whenever network and service latency fit the decision budget. Services built on large language models rely on centralized clusters to accommodate their memory and compute footprint. The tier a system runs on then sets limits that reach back into the lifecycle. Cloud capacity and cost govern how often models are retrained, what historical data is retained, and how inference load is distributed, while edge and mobile limits constrain model size and update frequency.

As computation spreads across tiers, operational complexity grows alongside it. Edge and hybrid deployments must keep data collection, model updates, and monitoring consistent across many distant devices whose connectivity comes and goes. Data pressures pull in opposite directions at the same time: privacy rules and data sovereignty push computation toward the edge, whereas training high-capacity models pulls data back toward centralized clusters. A common production pattern resolves this tension by running inference locally at the edge while routing sampled telemetry to cloud clusters for retraining.

In every deployment context, the model eventually meets inputs its evaluation never sampled. In autonomous driving, perception runs on the vehicle while cloud clusters train updated weights on logged fleet data. The Waymo Open Dataset shows pronounced domain gaps among San Francisco, Phoenix, and Mountain View ([[01 Books/Volume I - Foundations/00 Introduction/01 Introduction/V1-01.15 Source References#^ref-sun2020waymo|Sun et al. 2020]]), and models that score well on benchmarks often lose accuracy when deployment inputs shift ([[01 Books/Volume I - Foundations/00 Introduction/01 Introduction/V1-01.15 Source References#^ref-koh2021wilds|Koh et al. 2021]]). Every remote update must therefore be revalidated against the population it will actually serve.

Deployment also places predictions in front of the people they affect, so beyond predictive accuracy and latency, deployed systems must satisfy fairness, interpretability, and privacy requirements across data, models, and governance. Training sets can encode societal biases. Regulated fields such as healthcare and finance require decisions that auditors and clinicians can interpret. Inference attacks[[01 Books/Volume I - Foundations/00 Introduction/01 Introduction/V1-01.16 Footnotes#^fn27|²⁷]] can leak sensitive information from trained weights. Meeting these technical, operational, and governance obligations across all four deployment regimes sets the structure of the book that follows.
