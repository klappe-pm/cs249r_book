# V1-01.09 Three Dimensions of ML Efficiency

[[Home|Home]] · [[01 Books/Volume I - Foundations/Volume 1 Overview|Volume Overview]] · [[01 Books/Volume I - Foundations/00 Introduction/01 Introduction/V1-01 Introduction|V1-01 Introduction]]

^sec-introduction-efficiency-framework-8dd4

Because training compute grew faster than hardware ([[01 Books/Volume I - Foundations/00 Introduction/01 Introduction/V1-01.09 Three Dimensions of ML Efficiency#^fig-ai-training-compute-growth|figure 6]]), reaching a given capability had to cost less on every factor of [[01 Books/Volume I - Foundations/00 Introduction/01 Introduction/V1-01.08 Iron Law of ML Systems#^eq-intro-dam-iron-law|equation 2]]. AlexNet’s 2012 run ([[01 Books/Volume I - Foundations/00 Introduction/01 Introduction/V1-01.04 The Evolution of AI Bottlenecks#^sec-introduction-deep-learning-era-infrastructure-bottleneck-2f02|section 1.3.3]]) is where that pressure began. Its two consumer GPUs, running for about six days on the 1.3M labeled images of the 2012 ImageNet challenge training split[[01 Books/Volume I - Foundations/00 Introduction/01 Introduction/V1-01.16 Footnotes#^fn22|²²]] ([[01 Books/Volume I - Foundations/00 Introduction/01 Introduction/V1-01.15 Source References#^ref-deng2009|Deng et al. 2009]]), made up a budget a university laboratory could afford. Modern foundation models, large models trained on broad data and then adapted to many tasks, demand training compute roughly 8 orders of magnitude larger.

Scale drives AI progress, as the bitter lesson ([[01 Books/Volume I - Foundations/00 Introduction/01 Introduction/V1-01.05 The Bitter Lesson#^sec-introduction-bitter-lesson-79c7|section 1.4]]) argued, but ever-larger datasets and compute budgets narrow participation to the most resource-rich organizations. Even those organizations eventually meet physical limits in data center power, memory bandwidth, and the diminishing returns of adding more parameters. Using existing compute more efficiently is therefore a complementary path to progress.

Efficiency offers three complementary choices that align with the three factors of [[01 Books/Volume I - Foundations/00 Introduction/01 Introduction/V1-01.08 Iron Law of ML Systems#^eq-intro-dam-iron-law|equation 2]]: perform less computation per sample, execute that computation in fewer seconds per FLOP, or choose more informative examples so the task needs fewer samples. **Algorithmic efficiency** reduces computational requirements ($\text{FLOPs/Sample}$) through model design and training procedures. **Compute efficiency** lowers realized machine time per operation ($\text{Seconds/FLOP}$) by raising hardware utilization ${\eta_{\text{hw}}}$ and cutting memory and latency stalls, turning more of the machine’s theoretical capacity into useful work. **Data selection** identifies examples that contribute useful learning signal, reducing $\text{Samples/Task}$ (and therefore ${O}$) when training processes fewer examples; under a fixed computation budget, it can instead redirect work toward more informative examples.

Choosing among them calls for a bottleneck diagnosis across the interfaces in [[01 Books/Volume I - Foundations/00 Introduction/01 Introduction/V1-01.06 Defining ML Systems#^fig-ai-triad|figure 4]]. All three must account for information movement: reducing $\text{FLOPs/Sample}$ alone has little effect on wall-clock time when the memory term ${D_{\text{vol}}}/{\text{BW}}$ dominates the sum, because the same bytes still move and each remaining FLOP then costs more realized seconds.

The optimization chapters (**[[01 Books/Volume I - Foundations/03 Optimization/09 Data Selection/V1-09 Data Selection#^sec-data-selection|Data Selection]]**, **[[01 Books/Volume I - Foundations/03 Optimization/10 Model Compression/V1-10 Model Compression#^sec-model-compression|Model Compression]]**, and **[[01 Books/Volume I - Foundations/03 Optimization/11 Hardware Acceleration/V1-11 Hardware Acceleration#^sec-hardware-acceleration|Hardware Acceleration]]**) develop these dimensions in the order work flows through a system. Data selection asks which examples merit training computation. Model compression[[01 Books/Volume I - Foundations/00 Introduction/01 Introduction/V1-01.16 Footnotes#^fn23|²³]] asks how much storage and arithmetic a trained model requires. Hardware acceleration explains how the remaining operations map onto memory and compute units. This teaching order does not prescribe an optimization sequence. The measured limiting constraint and deployment budget determine where to intervene first, and hardware limits inform data and model choices from the outset.

Algorithmic efficiency has the clearest measured history. Hernandez and Brown ([[01 Books/Volume I - Foundations/00 Introduction/01 Introduction/V1-01.15 Source References#^ref-hernandez2020measuring|2020]]) held accuracy fixed at the AlexNet level and asked how much training computation successive architectures needed to reach it. Because they counted operations rather than seconds, hardware speed does not enter the measurement. Between AlexNet in 2012 and EfficientNet in 2019, the required compute fell by 44×, a halving roughly every 15 months between those two endpoints ([[01 Books/Volume I - Foundations/00 Introduction/01 Introduction/V1-01.09 Three Dimensions of ML Efficiency#^fig-algo-efficiency|figure 5]]). The dashed line in the figure is an exponential fit to those measured points. The reduction measures algorithmic progress at fixed task quality. Comparing it with hardware progress would require a matching measure of useful throughput or energy efficiency; Moore’s law describes transistor integration,[[01 Books/Volume I - Foundations/00 Introduction/01 Introduction/V1-01.16 Footnotes#^fn24|²⁴]] which alone does not determine either quantity.

![[90 Assets/Volume I/01 Introduction/Cell 17 Output 1 58a4e39622.png]]

Figure 5: **Algorithmic Efficiency Trajectory**: Each point shows how much less training compute an ImageNet architecture needed than AlexNet to reach AlexNet-level accuracy, so height measures algorithmic progress with hardware speed held out. Most later architectures sit well above AlexNet, although individual points vary; VGG-11 needed more compute than AlexNet. The dashed line is a least-squares exponential fit to the measured points. Architecture-by-architecture gains tell only half the story, because over the same years aggregate training compute in published frontier runs climbed far faster than algorithms could offset. [[01 Books/Volume I - Foundations/00 Introduction/01 Introduction/V1-01.09 Three Dimensions of ML Efficiency#^fig-ai-training-compute-growth|Figure 6]] charts that climb on a logarithmic scale, from early deep learning models near $10^{18}$ FLOPs to frontier systems beyond $10^{25}$ FLOPs. Sevilla et al. ([[01 Books/Volume I - Foundations/00 Introduction/01 Introduction/V1-01.15 Source References#^ref-sevilla2022|2022]]) measured a doubling time of roughly six months across the deep learning era, and a least-squares fit to the runs in the figure gives a similar cadence. An earlier study over 2012 through 2018 found a 3.4-month doubling ([[01 Books/Volume I - Foundations/00 Introduction/01 Introduction/V1-01.15 Source References#^ref-amodei2018aicompute|Amodei and Hernandez 2018]]); different windows and sets of runs yield different estimates. Both studies found training compute growing faster than the roughly two-year doubling of transistor counts associated with Moore’s law. This divergence motivates efficiency improvements across algorithms, software runtimes, and hardware architectures, helping keep useful systems within reach as workload demands grow.

The two plots show how efficiency gains can coexist with growth in training scale. [[01 Books/Volume I - Foundations/00 Introduction/01 Introduction/V1-01.09 Three Dimensions of ML Efficiency#^fig-algo-efficiency|Figure 5]] reports a 44× reduction in compute for a fixed accuracy target, while [[01 Books/Volume I - Foundations/00 Introduction/01 Introduction/V1-01.09 Three Dimensions of ML Efficiency#^fig-ai-training-compute-growth|figure 6]] illustrates roughly 8 orders of magnitude of growth in selected training runs. They measure different quantities and include contextual estimates, so they do not by themselves establish an economic cause.

**Systems Perspective 1.2: The efficiency paradox**

**Jevons paradox** ([[01 Books/Volume I - Foundations/00 Introduction/01 Introduction/V1-01.15 Source References#^ref-jevons1865coal|Jevons 1865]]) describes cases in which more efficient resource use leads to greater total consumption because demand expands. A similar response is possible in AI. If EfficientNet needs 44× less compute than AlexNet to reach the same accuracy, an organization can reinvest the savings in larger models or more experiments. It can also choose a smaller budget or serve more users at the same cost. Reinvestment is a possible consequence of efficiency, not an inevitable one, and the plotted trends alone do not establish it.

Whether reinvestment pays is an economic question. The same physical terms that determine time and energy also determine cost: every byte moved, operation executed, and millisecond of latency consumes infrastructure budget. The test is whether added compute buys enough model improvement to justify the expenditure.

### Return on Compute: Weighing Accuracy Against Cost

^sec-introduction-roc-invariant

A model upgrade that raises accuracy by 1 percentage point can still be the wrong choice if it requires a 10$\times$ increase in ${O}$ (total operations) whose cost exceeds the application’s budget or the gain’s value. **Return on compute (RoC)** measures the incremental accuracy gain per added dollar of infrastructure investment, in accuracy points per dollar:

$$
\text{RoC} = \frac{{\Delta}\text{Accuracy}}{{\Delta}\text{Compute Cost}} \tag{6}
$$

^eq-intro-roc

The RoC test compares that ratio against what each point of accuracy is worth to the application. Each alternative is compared against the same baseline, and the ratio is defined only when the added cost is positive.

**Napkin Math 1.3: Comparing two upgrades**

**Problem**: A team can add training data, which raises accuracy by 2 points for \$20,000 of added compute, or train a larger model, which raises it by 3 points for \$120,000. Each added accuracy point is worth \$15,000 to the application. Which upgrade passes the RoC test?

**Math**: Dividing each gain by its cost gives an RoC of 1 point per \$10,000 for added data and 0.25 points per \$10,000 for the larger model. A point worth \$15,000 sets the break-even RoC at 0.67 points per \$10,000.

**Result**: Added data clears the break-even rate and the larger model does not, even though the larger model gains more accuracy.

**Systems insight**: Measured against the added-data option instead of the baseline, the larger model buys 1 more point for \$100,000, or 0.1 points per \$10,000. The largest accuracy gain is not the best investment; the decision turns on accuracy bought per dollar compared with what accuracy is worth.

A cost saving is not itself a return on compute, but it can pay for work that RoC then evaluates. The GPT-3 estimate in notebook [1.2](https://mlsysbook.ai/vol1/01_introduction/01_introduction.html#nbk-introduction-training-gpt-3) shows such a saving. Assuming scheduling changes preserve model quality, raising ${\eta_{\text{hw}}}$ from 45 percent to 60 percent saves 6 days of cluster time for the same training work. The freed budget can fund another experiment, whose incremental accuracy and cost must then be measured. The iron law helps estimate what computation costs; RoC asks whether an additional investment is worthwhile. Efficiency choices must therefore be evaluated against the system’s goals and deployment constraints.
