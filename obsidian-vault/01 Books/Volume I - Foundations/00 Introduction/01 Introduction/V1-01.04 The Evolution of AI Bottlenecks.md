# V1-01.04 The Evolution of AI Bottlenecks

[[Home|Home]] · [[01 Books/Volume I - Foundations/Volume 1 Overview|Volume Overview]] · [[01 Books/Volume I - Foundations/00 Introduction/01 Introduction/V1-01 Introduction|V1-01 Introduction]]

^sec-introduction-ai-paradigm-evolution-ae2b

When Alan Turing[[01 Books/Volume I - Foundations/00 Introduction/01 Introduction/V1-01.16 Footnotes#^fn7|⁷]] proposed evaluating machine intelligence through observable responses ([[01 Books/Volume I - Foundations/00 Introduction/01 Introduction/V1-01.15 Source References#^ref-turing1950|Turing 1950]]), computing machinery had little memory or processing capacity, and every early attempt to build intelligent behavior ran into a structural ceiling. Early researchers pursued two contrasting paths: Frank Rosenblatt’s Perceptron (1958) ([[01 Books/Volume I - Foundations/00 Introduction/01 Introduction/V1-01.15 Source References#^ref-rosenblatt1958|Rosenblatt 1958]]) attempted to learn weights directly from sensory inputs, whereas Joseph Weizenbaum’s ELIZA[[01 Books/Volume I - Foundations/00 Introduction/01 Introduction/V1-01.16 Footnotes#^fn8|⁸]] ([[01 Books/Volume I - Foundations/00 Introduction/01 Introduction/V1-01.15 Source References#^ref-weizenbaum1966|Weizenbaum 1966]]) executed hand-coded pattern substitutions. Both soon met hard limits. A single-layer perceptron could separate categories of input, such as two kinds of image, only by drawing a straight line (or flat plane) between them ([[01 Books/Volume I - Foundations/00 Introduction/01 Introduction/V1-01.15 Source References#^ref-minsky1969perceptrons|Minsky and Papert 1969]]), and practical methods for training deeper networks remained out of reach. ELIZA’s scripts broke whenever inputs strayed from their hand-written templates. Across the decades that followed, four overlapping eras (symbolic AI, expert systems, statistical learning, and deep learning) each relaxed one limiting constraint only to expose the next.

[[01 Books/Volume I - Foundations/00 Introduction/01 Introduction/V1-01.04 The Evolution of AI Bottlenecks#^fig-ai-timeline|Figure 1]] traces attention to AI across seven decades of published literature and shades two “AI winter” intervals (1974–1980 and 1987–1993) marked by funding cuts and unmet expectations.[[01 Books/Volume I - Foundations/00 Introduction/01 Introduction/V1-01.16 Footnotes#^fn9|⁹]] These setbacks reflected recurring gaps between promised generality and what contemporary algorithms, datasets, and hardware could actually sustain.

Figure 1: **AI Development Timeline**: The red curve tracks how often published books mention artificial intelligence, a measure of attention rather than capability, and the light-brown bands mark the two AI winters. ### Rule-Based Approaches: Logic and Knowledge Bottlenecks

^sec-introduction-prelearning-era-logic-knowledge-bottlenecks-f312

Before data-driven training became practical, engineers sought to construct intelligent behavior the same way they built conventional software: by writing explicit rules. That effort unfolded in two waves, moving from general-purpose formal reasoning to narrowly scoped domain expertise.

#### The Symbolic AI Era and the Logic Bottleneck

^sec-introduction-symbolic-ai-era-logic-bottleneck-a250

In 1964, Daniel Bobrow’s STUDENT[[01 Books/Volume I - Foundations/00 Introduction/01 Introduction/V1-01.16 Footnotes#^fn10|¹⁰]] program ([[01 Books/Volume I - Foundations/00 Introduction/01 Introduction/V1-01.15 Source References#^ref-bobrow1964student|Bobrow 1964]]) demonstrated that a computer could solve high-school algebra word problems by translating English sentences into algebraic equations. STUDENT exemplified **symbolic AI** (1950s–1970s), which represented knowledge and reasoning through explicit symbols and transformation rules. That approach traced back to the 1956 Dartmouth Conference[[01 Books/Volume I - Foundations/00 Introduction/01 Introduction/V1-01.16 Footnotes#^fn11|¹¹]] ([[01 Books/Volume I - Foundations/00 Introduction/01 Introduction/V1-01.15 Source References#^ref-mccarthy1955dartmouth|McCarthy et al. 1955]]), whose organizers hypothesized that intelligence could be described precisely enough for a machine to simulate it.

A competing idea appeared in the same years. In 1959, Arthur Samuel at IBM showed that a checkers program could improve through self-play and coined the term **machine learning** for programs that improve with experience rather than by being explicitly programmed ([[01 Books/Volume I - Foundations/00 Introduction/01 Introduction/V1-01.15 Source References#^ref-samuel1959|Samuel 1959]]). Symbolic manipulation nevertheless remained the dominant approach.

Symbolic systems could produce impressive demonstrations, yet they were operationally brittle. Their success depended on manually coded rules that mapped each acceptable input form to a symbolic representation. STUDENT could solve an algebra problem once it translated English into equations, but a minor variation in phrasing could prevent that translation. That brittleness is the symbolic era’s logic bottleneck, because general-purpose rules could not anticipate the open-ended variety of real inputs. Perception and movement proved still harder to capture in rules than language.[[01 Books/Volume I - Foundations/00 Introduction/01 Introduction/V1-01.16 Footnotes#^fn12|¹²]]

**Example 1.1: STUDENT (1964)**

STUDENT separated language interpretation from algebra:

``` text
language  "Two numbers sum to 30; one is twice the other."
parse     x + y = 30, x = 2y
solve     x = 20, y = 10
```

Once the equations were written, solving was routine. The bottleneck was the translation. Each unfamiliar phrasing needed another hand-written rule, so linguistic coverage grew only as fast as the rule base.

^exmp-introduction-student-1964

#### The Expert Systems Era and the Knowledge Bottleneck

^sec-introduction-expert-systems-era-knowledge-bottleneck-f928

When general-purpose symbolic rules proved too brittle for open-ended worlds, engineers in the **expert systems** era narrowed the scope to specialized professional domains. MYCIN, built at Stanford in the 1970s to diagnose bacterial blood infections and recommend antibiotics, encoded clinical expertise directly as if-then production rules ([[01 Books/Volume I - Foundations/00 Introduction/01 Introduction/V1-01.15 Source References#^ref-shortliffe1975|Shortliffe et al. 1975]]).

**Example 1.2: MYCIN (1975)**

MYCIN made its reasoning explicit. Patient facts triggered readable production rules:

``` text
facts  stain=gram-positive, shape=coccus, arrangement=clumps
rule   IF facts match THEN organism=staphylococcus (certainty=0.7)
cycle  match facts -> fire rule -> update certainty -> repeat
```

An inference engine is a program that applies rules to known facts, an older, logic-based sense of “inference” than applying a trained model. MYCIN’s engine repeated this match-and-fire cycle across hundreds of rules and propagated certainty factors (numerical confidence weights) toward a diagnosis. A physician could inspect every step, but coverage still grew one rule at a time: each exception had to be elicited, encoded, and reconciled with the existing rule base.

^exmp-introduction-mycin-1976

MYCIN performed well in specific tests, but its success exposed the **knowledge acquisition bottleneck**.[[01 Books/Volume I - Foundations/00 Introduction/01 Introduction/V1-01.16 Footnotes#^fn13|¹³]] The problem was no longer whether a rule could express expertise. It was how tacit judgment entered the system and remained consistent as the rule base grew.

Faster machines could fire rules more quickly, but processor speed could not speed up the extraction or upkeep of human expertise. Scaling instead required learning the decision boundary, the line a classifier draws between categories such as spam and legitimate mail, from examples rather than asking specialists to enumerate every case. This transition did not eliminate human design; it relocated engineering effort. Instead of hand-coding decision logic, engineers now curated datasets, chose how to represent the inputs, and chose what measure of error training should reduce. The next era exchanged the knowledge acquisition bottleneck for a new one, the manual design of features extracted from raw data.

### The Statistical Learning Era and the Feature Engineering Bottleneck

^sec-introduction-statistical-learning-era-feature-engineering-bottleneck-8cf5

In the 1990s, **statistical learning** replaced hand-elicited rule bases with parameters estimated from labeled examples. Instead of following hard-coded logic, a system estimated probabilities from data, such as the probability of a label ${y}$ given an input ${x}$, written ${p(y \mid x)}$. The shift capitalized on growing digital text collections and the “unreasonable effectiveness”[[01 Books/Volume I - Foundations/00 Introduction/01 Introduction/V1-01.16 Footnotes#^fn14|¹⁴]] of large datasets. The learned spam filter introduced in [[01 Books/Volume I - Foundations/00 Introduction/01 Introduction/V1-01.03 From Code-Defined Logic to Data-Defined Behavior#^sec-introduction-datacentric-paradigm-shift-4eca|section 1.2]] originated in this transition: rather than maintaining lists of forbidden keywords, early statistical filters estimated the probability that each word indicated spam across millions of labeled messages.

**Example 1.3: Early spam detection systems**

Statistical filters shifted the decision from a hand-written rule to a probability estimated from labeled messages:

``` text
rules: if "free" or "winner" appears -> spam
learn: labeled email -> word likelihoods -> Pr(spam | email)
```

Maintenance did not disappear. It moved from editing keyword lists to curating representative examples and checking whether the learned probabilities still tracked current email.

^exmp-introduction-early-spam-detection-systems

Learning the decision boundary from data reduced one source of manual work but exposed another. Many statistical classifiers relied on engineers to convert raw inputs into features, measurable properties such as word counts or image edges. In computer vision, engineers designed feature extractors such as SIFT (Scale-Invariant Feature Transform) ([[01 Books/Volume I - Foundations/00 Introduction/01 Introduction/V1-01.15 Source References#^ref-lowe1999|Lowe 1999]]) and HOG (Histogram of Oriented Gradients) ([[01 Books/Volume I - Foundations/00 Introduction/01 Introduction/V1-01.15 Source References#^ref-dalal2005hog|Dalal and Triggs 2005]]) to summarize local image patterns. Classifiers such as support vector machines (SVMs) ([[01 Books/Volume I - Foundations/00 Introduction/01 Introduction/V1-01.15 Source References#^ref-cortes1995support|Cortes and Vapnik 1995]]) then learned decision boundaries from those features. The learner adjusted the boundary; engineers decided which evidence it could see. Extending the system to a new problem often meant rebuilding the preprocessing stack, creating the **feature engineering bottleneck**.

This hybrid approach combined human-engineered features with statistical learning. The Viola-Jones algorithm[[01 Books/Volume I - Foundations/00 Introduction/01 Introduction/V1-01.16 Footnotes#^fn15|¹⁵]] ([[01 Books/Volume I - Foundations/00 Introduction/01 Introduction/V1-01.15 Source References#^ref-viola2001|Viola and Jones 2001]]), typical of this era, achieved real-time frontal-face detection using simple rectangular features and cascaded classifiers. It showed that well-engineered features could enable practical low-latency applications, but only within narrow domains where experts could hand-craft the right representations. A conventional vision pipeline makes that manual effort visible, because several hand-crafted stages preceded any learning at all.

**Example 1.4: Traditional computer vision pipeline**

A conventional vision system separated representation from classification:

``` text
image -> resize -> HOG/SIFT features -> SVM -> label
```

Moving from faces to pedestrians or another domain meant redesigning the feature extractor and often the entire preprocessing stack.

^exmp-introduction-traditional-computer-vision-pipeline

### The Deep Learning Era and the Infrastructure Bottleneck

^sec-introduction-deep-learning-era-infrastructure-bottleneck-2f02

Redesigning hand-crafted feature extractors for every new domain could not scale, so **deep learning** ([[01 Books/Volume I - Foundations/00 Introduction/01 Introduction/V1-01.15 Source References#^ref-lecun2015|LeCun et al. 2015]]) moved feature extraction inside the model itself. A neural network computes in layers, stages of arithmetic on learned weights in which each layer transforms the output of the one before. Instead of receiving features designed by humans, deep networks with many such layers learned representations directly from raw inputs such as pixels and audio waveforms. The training process could now shape both the representation and the decision boundary, enabling “end-to-end” learning.

The breakthrough was not algorithmic alone. Convolutional neural networks (CNNs), whose convolutional layers slide small learned weight patterns called filters across an image to detect the same feature at every position, existed earlier ([[01 Books/Volume I - Foundations/00 Introduction/01 Introduction/V1-01.15 Source References#^ref-lecun1989|LeCun et al. 1989]], [[01 Books/Volume I - Foundations/00 Introduction/01 Introduction/V1-01.15 Source References#^ref-lecun1998|1998]]). What AlexNet added was to pair architecture and training with **systems co-design**, choosing the model, training procedure, and hardware mapping together ([[01 Books/Volume I - Foundations/00 Introduction/01 Introduction/V1-01.15 Source References#^ref-krizhevsky2012imagenet|Krizhevsky et al. 2012]]). Its parallel matrix operations matched GPU capabilities. With 60 million parameters distributed across two GTX 580 GPUs ([[01 Books/Volume I - Foundations/00 Introduction/01 Introduction/V1-01.04 The Evolution of AI Bottlenecks#^fig-alexnet|figure 2]]), AlexNet achieved 15.3 percent top-5 error, a 41.6 percent relative improvement over the next-best entry that year.

![[90 Assets/Volume I/01 Introduction/Generated Figure 8195baf2b8.svg]]

Figure 2: **AlexNet Architecture**: Two parallel GPU streams process $224{\times}224$ input images through convolutional layers (green blocks) that extract spatial features at decreasing resolutions, converging through three fully connected layers to 1,000 output classes. Because each stream holds half of the network, the figure also shows how the model was partitioned across two GPUs. Adapted from Krizhevsky et al. ([[01 Books/Volume I - Foundations/00 Introduction/01 Introduction/V1-01.15 Source References#^ref-krizhevsky2012imagenet|2012]]).

^fig-alexnet

Hardware still shaped what could be learned. In 2012, storing AlexNet’s 60 million 32-bit parameters required roughly 240 MB, well within a single card’s capacity. Training needs more memory than the stored weights alone, because it also holds the intermediate results of every layer and the bookkeeping used to adjust each parameter, both developed in **[[01 Books/Volume I - Foundations/02 Development/05 Neural Computation/V1-05 Neural Computation#^sec-neural-computation|Neural Computation]]**. That working memory grows with the number of images processed together. Each GTX 580 offered only 3 GB of video random-access memory (VRAM), and the original paper reports that this limit made the network too large to train on one card ([[01 Books/Volume I - Foundations/00 Introduction/01 Introduction/V1-01.15 Source References#^ref-krizhevsky2012imagenet|Krizhevsky et al. 2012]]). AlexNet therefore divided its layers between two GPU streams that exchanged intermediate results only at selected layers, limiting traffic over the PCIe bus, the host’s general-purpose peripheral interconnect. Model structure and machine organization were designed together. This early model-parallel design foreshadowed modern multi-GPU training. Deep learning reduced the need for hand-crafted features, but it moved the limiting constraint to the infrastructure required to store data, move parameters, and coordinate computation.

↪ Model parallelism, which partitions a model across accelerators when it outgrows one device’s memory, as AlexNet’s two-GPU split did, is developed in **[[01 Books/Volume I - Foundations/02 Development/08 Model Training/V1-08.08 Scaling Training Systems#^sec-model-training-model-parallelism-c97e|Model parallelism]]**.

This shift produced the **infrastructure bottleneck**, in which physical memory capacity, interconnect bandwidth, and hardware orchestration dictate the boundaries of what models can learn. Eight years after AlexNet, GPT-3 (Generative Pre-trained Transformer 3), with 175 billion parameters, was trained on roughly 300 billion tokens (the word fragments a language model reads and predicts) curated from filtered web text, books, and Wikipedia ([[01 Books/Volume I - Foundations/00 Introduction/01 Introduction/V1-01.15 Source References#^ref-brown2020language|Brown et al. 2020]]).

Training at that scale requires a predictable amount of arithmetic. GPT-3 is a dense model, one in which every parameter takes part in processing every token. For such models, the dense-training approximation ([[01 Books/Volume I - Foundations/00 Introduction/01 Introduction/V1-01.15 Source References#^ref-kaplan2020scaling|Kaplan et al. 2020]]) estimates ${O} \approx 6 \cdot {P} \cdot {D}$ operations for a model with ${P}$ parameters trained on ${D}$ tokens, counting both the forward pass that computes a prediction and the backward pass that works out how to adjust each parameter. This estimate agrees with the roughly 314 zettaFLOPs of total computation that the GPT-3 authors report, where one zettaFLOP is $10^{21}$ floating-point operations (FLOPs). A retrospective audit by Patterson et al. ([[01 Books/Volume I - Foundations/00 Introduction/01 Introduction/V1-01.15 Source References#^ref-patterson2021carbon|2021]]) estimated that this single training run consumed roughly 405 V100 GPU-years, where one GPU-year is one graphics processor running for a full year.

↪ The dense-training approximation used for this operation count is developed in **[[01 Books/Volume I - Foundations/02 Development/08 Model Training/V1-08.02 From One Step to a Training Run#^sec-model-training-running-example-training-gpt2-19cd|Running cases: GPT-2 XL and ResNet-50]]**.

The hard part had moved from designing edge detectors to keeping some 10,000 accelerators running in parallel across high-speed networks without failures that halt training midway. Viewed through this systems lens, each transition traded one limiting constraint for the next, first in human effort (rules, knowledge, features) and then in physical infrastructure ([[01 Books/Volume I - Foundations/00 Introduction/01 Introduction/V1-01.04 The Evolution of AI Bottlenecks#^tbl-ai-evolution-strengths|table 2]]). Relieving one limit exposed the next, a pattern this book calls bottleneck migration.

| **Aspect**        | **Symbolic AI**           | **Expert Systems**                         | **Statistical Learning**                   | **Deep Learning**                       |
|:------------------|:--------------------------|:-------------------------------------------|:-------------------------------------------|:----------------------------------------|
| **Key Strength**  | Logical reasoning         | Domain expertise                           | Versatility                                | Pattern recognition                     |
| **Bottleneck**    | Brittleness (Rules break) | Knowledge Acquisition (Experts are scarce) | Feature Engineering (Manual preprocessing) | Infrastructure (Compute and data scale) |
| **Data Handling** | Minimal data needed       | Domain knowledge-based                     | Moderate data required                     | Massive data processing                 |

Table 2: **AI Paradigm Evolution**: Each era is viewed through a recurring systems bottleneck. Deep learning (far right) reduced the need for manual feature engineering while increasing demands on computing infrastructure.

^tbl-ai-evolution-strengths

Across the four eras, engineering effort migrated from handcrafting domain heuristics toward building scalable compute and data pipelines. The bitter lesson explains why this shift recurred.
