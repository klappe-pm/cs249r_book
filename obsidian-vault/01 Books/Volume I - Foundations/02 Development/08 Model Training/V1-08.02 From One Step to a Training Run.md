# V1-08.02 From One Step to a Training Run

[[Home|Home]] · [[01 Books/Volume I - Foundations/Volume 1 Overview|Volume Overview]] · [[01 Books/Volume I - Foundations/02 Development/08 Model Training/V1-08 Model Training|V1-08 Model Training]]

^sec-model-training-training-systems-fundamentals-05d2

A training run is one training step repeated millions of times, so every byte a step holds and every millisecond it spends is multiplied by the length of the run. Each step reads a batch of examples, computes a forward pass and a loss, propagates gradients backward, and updates every parameter, and the run repeats that sequence until the model reaches its quality target. The anatomy of a training step in **[[01 Books/Volume I - Foundations/02 Development/07 ML Frameworks/V1-07.08 Anatomy of a Training Step#^sec-ml-frameworks-putting-together-anatomy-training-step-c7f1|Anatomy of a Training Step]]** traced one such step for a two-layer MNIST classifier, a smaller sibling of the three-layer network of **[[01 Books/Volume I - Foundations/02 Development/05 Neural Computation/V1-05 Neural Computation#^sec-neural-computation|Neural Computation]]**. The classifier has 101,770 parameters and costs about 19.5 MFLOP per batch of 32 images. That estimate uses the dense-training rule of about six FLOPs per parameter per example, which the GPT-2 lighthouse below derives; the exact trace counts slightly less because it skips the gradient of the raw input. The classifier’s weights, gradients, and optimizer state occupy 1.6 MB.

A language model turns the same step into a different engineering problem. One step of GPT-2 XL over 32 sequences of 1,024 tokens costs about 295 TFLOP, roughly 15 million times the classifier’s arithmetic, and its weights, gradients, and optimizer state occupy 24 GB before a single activation is stored. The framework mechanisms that made the small step possible leave every decision about the large run open: which intermediate activations to keep when high-bandwidth memory (HBM) runs out, how large a batch the memory permits, how to keep the accelerator supplied with data, and when one accelerator stops being enough.

Repeating that step across an entire training corpus creates the **training cost asymmetry**, the gap between answering one query with a trained model and running the forward-backward-update loop over billions of tokens. By the standard transformer count, one forward pass of GPT-2 over a single token costs about 3.00 × 10⁹ floating-point operations ([[01 Books/Volume I - Foundations/02 Development/08 Model Training/V1-08.12 Source References#^ref-radford2019language|Radford et al. 2019]]). Pretraining the full model on its WebText corpus took 1.50 × 10²¹ FLOPs in total, the work of about 167 billion tokens, each carried through a forward and a backward pass. In 2019 that budget cost about \$50,000 in compute, while pretraining a frontier model such as GPT-4 cost an estimated \$100M.[[01 Books/Volume I - Foundations/02 Development/08 Model Training/V1-08.13 Footnotes#^fn1|¹]] Neither figure is an audited disclosure, but the jump of more than three orders of magnitude between them shows how quickly parameter counts, token budgets, and fleet sizes compound.

**Definition 8.1: Training systems**

***Machine learning training systems*** are software-hardware systems that execute the iterative optimization loop (forward pass, loss computation, backward pass, and parameter update) to minimize a loss function over a training dataset.

1.  **Significance**: Training holds far more state per parameter than inference. In the common mixed-precision recipe with the Adaptive Moment Estimation (Adam) optimizer, gradients and optimizer state raise the per-parameter footprint to 8× the FP16 weights an inference server stores, before any activation is counted ([[01 Books/Volume I - Foundations/02 Development/08 Model Training/V1-08.04 Computation and Memory Costs of Training#^sec-model-training-optimization-algorithm-system-implications-f9f2|section 8.3.2.3]] derives the multiplier). This multiplier is a major reason a model that serves on one accelerator needs several for training.
2.  **Distinction**: Unlike inference systems, which execute a single forward pass and discard intermediate activations, standard backpropagation stores the tensors needed by the backward pass, creating a footprint that grows with model depth and batch size.
3.  **Common pitfall**: A frequent misconception is that training failures are compute problems. A common failure is an out-of-memory (OOM) error, a memory-management problem: tensors saved for backpropagation accumulate during the forward pass and can exhaust memory before the first gradient is computed.

The asymmetry comes from three properties of the training workload, and each one shapes a part of this chapter. Training needs an enormous amount of arithmetic. Spreading the 1.50 × 10²¹ FLOPs budget over days of wall-clock time requires sustained PFLOP/s-scale throughput from hardware whose realized large-model throughput often falls far below its theoretical peak ([[01 Books/Volume I - Foundations/02 Development/08 Model Training/V1-08.12 Source References#^ref-narayanan2021|Narayanan et al. 2021]]; [[01 Books/Volume I - Foundations/02 Development/08 Model Training/V1-08.12 Source References#^ref-chowdhery2022palm|Chowdhery et al. 2022]]), which pushes training toward higher utilization and lower-precision number formats.

Training also needs far more memory than inference. GPT-2’s 1.5B weights occupy 6 GB in FP32, their gradients occupy as much again, and the Adam optimizer keeps two more values per parameter that add another 12 GB. Activations come on top, in an amount set by the architecture, sequence length, batch size, precision, and checkpointing. When this total exceeds one accelerator’s memory, training relies on gradient checkpointing,[[01 Books/Volume I - Foundations/02 Development/08 Model Training/V1-08.13 Footnotes#^fn2|²]] which recomputes some activations instead of storing them, and on mixed-precision training,[[01 Books/Volume I - Foundations/02 Development/08 Model Training/V1-08.13 Footnotes#^fn3|³]] which halves activation storage by keeping activations in 16-bit formats while FP32 master weights (a full-precision copy of the weights that the optimizer updates) and optimizer moments preserve numerical stability.

The third property is the order of the steps. Each update depends on the result of the previous one, so steps cannot run in parallel with one another, and any speedup must come from inside each step. Pipelines that overlap data loading and communication with computation keep the execution units from stalling while they wait for input. This chapter covers training on one machine with one to eight accelerators. Training across many networked machines adds communication fabrics and fault-tolerance protocols beyond this scope, which the chapter’s final section outlines ([[01 Books/Volume I - Foundations/02 Development/08 Model Training/V1-08.08 Scaling Training Systems#^sec-model-training-scaling-beyond-single-node-a671|section 8.7.2]]).

↩︎ The feeding problem, in which data delivery must keep pace with accelerator consumption, is established in **[[01 Books/Volume I - Foundations/01 Foundations/04 Data Engineering/V1-04.10 Physics of Data#^sec-data-engineering-feeding-problem|The feeding problem]]**.

### Running Cases: GPT-2 XL and ResNet-50

^sec-model-training-running-example-training-gpt2-19cd

Two of the book’s lighthouse models, the recurring workloads each chapter tests its ideas against, carry this chapter. GPT-2 is the bandwidth-bound lighthouse. Generating one token at a time reads every weight for little arithmetic, so in inference its home regime is the bandwidth-bound one. Training changes that profile. A batch of long sequences reuses each weight across thousands of token positions, so the same model now presses on arithmetic throughput and, through activations and optimizer state, on memory capacity. The chapter uses it to test each training cost and optimization.

↩︎ GPT-2’s home regime in inference, bandwidth-bound because each generated token rereads every weight, is established in **[[01 Books/Volume I - Foundations/01 Foundations/02 System Paradigms/V1-02.04 Analyzing Workloads#^sec-ml-systems-workload-archetypes-fd10|Workload archetypes]]**.

**Lighthouse 8.1: Lighthouse example: Training GPT-2**

**Context**: GPT-2 (1.5 billion parameters) serves as the primary case study because it is large enough to expose meaningful compute, activation, and optimizer-state constraints while remaining easier to analyze than frontier-scale models. Whether it requires distributed training depends on precision, optimizer state, activation memory, hardware capacity, and the target training time. [[01 Books/Volume I - Foundations/02 Development/08 Model Training/V1-08.02 From One Step to a Training Run#^tbl-training-gpt2-lighthouse-specs|Table 1]] maps each model property to the systems constraint it creates:

| **Property**     |  **Specification**  | **Systems Implication**                                                                  |
|:-----------------|:-------------------:|:-----------------------------------------------------------------------------------------|
| **Parameters**   |      1.5B (XL)      | Requires ~3 GB (FP16) or ~6 GB (FP32) for weights alone.                                 |
| **Architecture** | 48 Layers, 1600 Dim | Deep pipeline creates heavy activation memory pressure.                                  |
| **Dataset**      |   WebText (40 GB)   | Preprocessing, shuffling, and delivery must sustain the selected training configuration. |
| **Compute**      | ~ 1.50 × 10²¹ FLOPs | Training duration depends on achieved throughput and accelerator count.                  |

Table 1: **GPT-2 (1.5 Billion Parameters) Lighthouse Model Specifications**: Parameter count, architecture depth, dataset size, and total training compute mapped to their systems implications. Each row pairs a model property with the engineering constraint it creates: memory footprint for weights, activation pressure from pipeline depth, I/O throughput requirements, and parallelization demand.

^tbl-training-gpt2-lighthouse-specs

**Mechanism**: In dense transformer models, the arithmetic work per token is directly proportional to parameter count ${P}$. In the forward pass, each parameter participates in one multiply-accumulate operation ($1\text{ MAC} = 2\text{ FLOPs}$), consuming $2{P}\text{ FLOPs/token}$. Backpropagation computes two matrix products per parameter: one to propagate gradients backward with respect to activations ($2{P}\text{ FLOPs}$), and another to calculate weight gradients for the optimizer ($2{P}\text{ FLOPs}$). The backward pass therefore requires twice the compute of the forward pass ($4{P}\text{ FLOPs/token}$), yielding a total work factor of $2{P} + 4{P} = 6{P}\text{ FLOPs/token}$. Across a dataset of ${D}$ tokens, total training work is ${O} \approx 6 \cdot {P} \cdot {D}$, or 1.50 × 10²¹ FLOPs.

The run must also fit the four-term training-memory budget of **[[01 Books/Volume I - Foundations/02 Development/05 Neural Computation/V1-05.05 Learning Process#^eq-training-memory|equation 5.29]]**, ${M_{\text{total}}} ={M_{\text{weights}}} + {M_{\text{gradients}}} + {M_{\text{optimizer}}} + {M_{\text{activations}}}$. Its execution time is ${T_{\text{train}}}= \frac{{O}}{{R_{\text{peak}}} \times {\eta_{\text{hw}}}}$, where ${R_{\text{peak}}}$ is the accelerator’s peak arithmetic rate and ${\eta_{\text{hw}}}$ is the fraction of that peak the run actually achieves ([[01 Books/Volume I - Foundations/02 Development/08 Model Training/V1-08.03 Training-Time Law#^sec-model-training-iron-law-training-performance-a53f|section 8.2]] develops this law). A fast run therefore keeps the accelerator supplied with data and busy with arithmetic while weights, gradients, optimizer state, and activations all fit in HBM.

ResNet-50, the book’s compute-bound lighthouse, carries the chapter’s input pipeline. Its home regime is compute-bound because a convolution reuses each weight at every spatial position, so one $224{\times}224$ image costs about 8.2 GFLOP in the forward pass and about 24.6 GFLOP once the backward pass is added, for only 25.6 million parameters. Training it on a current data-center accelerator moves the limiting term upstream. Each image must be read, decoded, and augmented on the host before the accelerator sees it, and the data pipeline ([[01 Books/Volume I - Foundations/02 Development/08 Model Training/V1-08.05 Pipeline Architecture#^sec-model-training-data-pipeline-8e71|section 8.4.2]]) shows the accelerator consuming images faster than the host processors can prepare them. The two cases therefore cross regimes in opposite directions. GPT-2 leaves its bandwidth-bound home for arithmetic and memory capacity, while ResNet-50 leaves its compute-bound home for an input-bound one.

Every number in the chapter uses one reference machine, an NVIDIA H100 with 80 GB of HBM delivering 3.35 TB/s and a dense 16-bit arithmetic peak of 989 TFLOP/s. The H100 is a point-in-time instance of the data-center accelerator class whose design **[[01 Books/Volume I - Foundations/03 Optimization/11 Hardware Acceleration/V1-11 Hardware Acceleration#^sec-hardware-acceleration|Hardware Acceleration]]** develops; its successors change the constants, not the reasoning.

The chapter follows GPT-2 XL’s run from estimate to execution ([[01 Books/Volume I - Foundations/02 Development/08 Model Training/V1-08.02 From One Step to a Training Run#^tbl-training-chapter-spine|table 2]]). The first half converts the run’s operation count into time and energy, accounts for the arithmetic and bytes of one step, and assembles the pipeline that executes it, including the checkpoints that let a long run survive failure. The second half diagnoses a measured step, removes its limiting term one technique at a time until GPT-2 XL trains on a single accelerator, and closes with when and how to add accelerators.

**Self-Check: Question**

1.  A 7-billion-parameter model uses 14 GB of FP16 weights for inference and fits comfortably on a single 40 GB accelerator. Switching the same model to standard mixed-precision Adam training causes immediate out-of-memory failures even before activations are accounted for. Using the mixed-precision Adam accounting that the section previews, which factor is most directly responsible?

    1.  Adam internally re-runs the forward and backward passes, so the same weights must be stored several extra times during each step.
    2.  Adam adds 14 GB of FP16 gradients, 28 GB of FP32 master weights, and 56 GB of FP32 first- and second-moment state, pushing the static training state to about 112 GB before activations.
    3.  Adam requires the full training corpus to be cached in accelerator memory so batches can be reused across optimizer steps.
    4.  Adam forces all model tensors into FP64 during backpropagation, which multiplies the weight footprint beyond device capacity.

2.  True or False: Sudden NaN loss spikes during mixed-precision training are usually the same failure mode as an out-of-memory error, because both come from the GPU running out of memory during backpropagation.

3.  In machine learning frameworks, the technique of discarding selected intermediate forward activations and recomputing them on demand during the backward pass to reduce peak memory is known as activation \_\_\_\_.
