# V1-08.03 Training-Time Law

[[Home|Home]] · [[01 Books/Volume I - Foundations/Volume 1 Overview|Volume Overview]] · [[01 Books/Volume I - Foundations/02 Development/08 Model Training/V1-08 Model Training|V1-08 Model Training]]

^sec-model-training-iron-law-training-performance-a53f

Every later estimate in the chapter starts from how long the run takes, and the training-time law fixes that first. GPT-2 XL’s run needs about 1.50 × 10²¹ FLOPs. Converting that operation count into days on an accelerator, and every loss of time into a factor engineering can recover, requires splitting training time into physical factors, with every wasted second showing up as a lower utilization factor.

The iron law of ML systems (principle [[01 Books/Volume I - Foundations/02 Development/V1 Development Principles#^pri-iron-law|3]]) supplies that decomposition directly once specialized to training. In its multiplicative D·A·M form, a training run processes ${D}$ tokens (Data). Each token costs roughly $6{P}$ forward-and-backward FLOPs (Algorithm), for ${O} = 6 \cdot {P} \cdot {D}$ total operations. The machine executes those operations at a realized pace of $1/({R_{\text{peak}}} \times {\eta_{\text{hw}}})$ seconds per FLOP (Machine). This training-time form of the iron law, which this chapter shortens to the training-time law, keeps that macroscopic product as the organizing equation so that every optimization technique maps to one of its factors.

**Definition 8.2: The training-time law**

***The training-time law*** expresses training time through the workload’s operation count and its effective achieved throughput:

$$
{T_{\text{train}}}= \frac{{O}}{{R_{\text{peak}}} \times {\eta_{\text{hw}}}} \tag{1}
$$

^eq-training-iron-law

Here ${O}$ counts useful model forward-and-backward FLOPs, excluding rematerialization, the recomputation of activations that were discarded to save memory. Additional execution work is reflected in effective throughput. The factor ${\eta_{\text{hw}}}$ is an effective end-to-end utilization factor, the achieved model-operation rate divided by the relevant hardware peak. Data stalls, communication, synchronization, launch overhead, and inefficient kernels all reduce this factor. Prefetching and overlap can hide part of those costs but do not guarantee their removal.

1.  **Significance**: In the D·A·M taxonomy, these terms map directly to the three multiplicative factors. The numerator ${O} = (\text{Samples/Task}) \times (\text{FLOPs/Sample})$ is governed by Data (training token count ${D}$) and Algorithm (model architecture or compression methods such as pruning and distillation, which **[[01 Books/Volume I - Foundations/03 Optimization/10 Model Compression/V1-10 Model Compression#^sec-model-compression|Model Compression]]** defines). The task here is the whole training run, so samples per task is the number of samples the run processes. The Machine factor $\text{Seconds/FLOP} = 1/({R_{\text{peak}}} \times {\eta_{\text{hw}}})$ combines hardware execution units at the chosen numerical precision (${R_{\text{peak}}}$) with effective systems utilization (${\eta_{\text{hw}}}$), the primary engineering target.
2.  **Distinction**: The training-time law ([[01 Books/Volume I - Foundations/02 Development/08 Model Training/V1-08.03 Training-Time Law#^eq-training-iron-law|equation 1]]) is the macroscopic D·A·M product form of the iron law, folding every non-arithmetic delay into ${\eta_{\text{hw}}}$. When ${\eta_{\text{hw}}}$ is low, the unpacked three-term iron law exposes data movement (${D_{\text{vol}}}/{\text{BW}}$), kernel computation, and fixed latency (${L_{\text{lat}}}$) separately to diagnose which underlying bottleneck is responsible.
3.  **Common pitfall**: A frequent misconception is that ${\eta_{\text{hw}}}$ is fixed by hardware. It is a pipeline property, so diagnosing which factor dominates requires profiling rather than reading hardware specs.

Three levers for improvement follow from [[01 Books/Volume I - Foundations/02 Development/08 Model Training/V1-08.03 Training-Time Law#^eq-training-iron-law|equation 1]]: reduce total operations through data curation or algorithmic innovation, increase peak throughput through hardware selection and precision formats, or improve utilization through pipeline orchestration. Measured runs show how far below peak the utilization factor sits. Counting only useful model arithmetic, Megatron-Turing NLG trained at 30.2 percent of peak and PaLM at 46.2 percent ([[01 Books/Volume I - Foundations/02 Development/08 Model Training/V1-08.12 Source References#^ref-chowdhery2022palm|Chowdhery et al. 2022]]). The 52 percent of peak that Narayanan et al. ([[01 Books/Volume I - Foundations/02 Development/08 Model Training/V1-08.12 Source References#^ref-narayanan2021|2021]]) report for Megatron-LM is higher because their count includes rematerialization work, which ${O}$ excludes. Memory does not appear in the law at all. It acts as a feasibility constraint, because a configuration whose state does not fit runs at no speed. The chapter map ([[01 Books/Volume I - Foundations/02 Development/08 Model Training/V1-08.02 From One Step to a Training Run#^tbl-training-chapter-spine|table 2]]) shows which section works on which factor, and [[01 Books/Volume I - Foundations/02 Development/08 Model Training/V1-08.07 Pipeline Optimizations#^tbl-optimization|table 8]] places each optimization technique against the factor it moves, with its memory effect listed separately.

The training-time law focuses on execution efficiency, how fast the hardware processes a given workload. It does not predict how data quality, dataset size, or curriculum design determines the total operations ${O}$ needed to reach a target accuracy; that workload must be supplied or estimated separately. A cleaner dataset or a better data mix can reduce the number of epochs required, shrinking ${O}$ without touching hardware at all. Holding the workload fixed, the question is how to execute it as fast as possible.

↪ Data selection, which shrinks total operations (${O}$) by keeping the examples that return the most learning per unit of compute, is developed in **[[01 Books/Volume I - Foundations/03 Optimization/09 Data Selection/V1-09.02 The Data Wall#^sec-data-selection-data-selection-fundamentals-e839|The Data Wall]]**.

Actual training throughput remains below the arithmetic peak whenever kernels, data delivery, or communication leave resources idle. Scaling to multiple accelerators introduces additional communication overhead, a trade-off quantified in [[01 Books/Volume I - Foundations/02 Development/08 Model Training/V1-08.08 Scaling Training Systems#^sec-model-training-scaling-training-systems-adfd|section 8.7]].

For GPT-2 XL on the reference H100, the law becomes a schedule. At the accelerator’s 989 TFLOP/s dense 16-bit peak and a utilization ${\eta_{\text{hw}}}$ of 50 percent, a round figure at the high end of what large training runs sustain ([[01 Books/Volume I - Foundations/02 Development/08 Model Training/V1-08.12 Source References#^ref-chowdhery2022palm|Chowdhery et al. 2022]]), the run takes about 35 days on one accelerator. That is about 5.1 million steps of 0.60 s each over 32 sequences of 1,024 tokens. Time converts directly into energy. Energy is power integrated over time, ${E_{\text{total}}} = {P_{\text{device}}} \times {T_{\text{train}}}$ at constant power, so at the H100’s 700 W thermal design power the run draws about 590 kWh at the accelerator alone, about 417 J per step. Every technique that shortens the run at constant power removes energy in the same proportion. On rented accelerators it also removes billed accelerator-hours, and with them dollars, in that proportion. The utilization factor therefore matters to an energy budget and a compute bill as much as to a schedule.

↩︎ The iron law’s first estimate of a training run, the same operation-count-to-days conversion applied to a GPT-3-class model on an accelerator cluster, is established in **[[01 Books/Volume I - Foundations/00 Introduction/01 Introduction/V1-01.08 Iron Law of ML Systems#^sec-introduction-iron-law-ml-systems-c32a|Iron Law of ML Systems]]**.

The same conversion scales to runs far larger than GPT-2. A 70B-parameter Llama 2 model trained on 2T tokens across a 1,024-accelerator H100 cluster at the same utilization takes about 19.2 days and \$1.89M of rented accelerator time. Buying that cluster costs as much as about 16.3 such runs before networking, facilities, power, and staff are counted (**[[01 Books/Volume I - Foundations/04 Deployment/15 Responsible Engineering/V1-15.05 Environmental and Cost Awareness#^sec-responsible-engineering-total-cost-ownership-35c1|Total cost of ownership]]** develops the full cost model).

Not every training workload is bound by arithmetic. DLRM, the book’s capacity-bound lighthouse, holds 100B to 10T parameters, mostly in embedding tables, so its first training problem is fitting those tables in memory at all, whereas dense models such as GPT-2 meet arithmetic and activation-memory limits first.

**Checkpoint 8.1: The physics of training**

Training speed is governed by the utilization of hardware peaks.

**Utilization gap**

**Peak vs. real**: why does sustained end-to-end utilization remain below the arithmetic peak?

**Utilization losses**: which sources of lost time does the definition of ${\eta_{\text{hw}}}$ fold in, and which of them can overlap hide rather than remove?

**Order of magnitude**: a frontier run needs on the order of $10^{23}$ FLOPs at a utilization ${\eta_{\text{hw}}}$ well below 1. Estimate which lever shortens wall-clock time more, doubling ${R_{\text{peak}}}$ or doubling ${\eta_{\text{hw}}}$, and why, in practice, the two levers differ in cost and reachability even though the training-time law treats them symmetrically.

Each milestone in the history of deep learning moved the limiting constraint from one factor of this law to another. Backpropagation, popularized for multilayer networks in 1986 ([[01 Books/Volume I - Foundations/02 Development/08 Model Training/V1-08.12 Source References#^ref-rumelhart1986|Rumelhart et al. 1986]]), made the operation count ${O}$ of a deep network computable at all. AlexNet’s five-to-six-day ImageNet run on two GPUs in 2012 ([[01 Books/Volume I - Foundations/02 Development/08 Model Training/V1-08.12 Source References#^ref-krizhevsky2012imagenet|Krizhevsky et al. 2012]]) showed that this arithmetic mapped onto the far higher ${R_{\text{peak}}}$ of graphics hardware. Transformers from 2017 onward ([[01 Books/Volume I - Foundations/02 Development/08 Model Training/V1-08.12 Source References#^ref-vaswani2017|Vaswani et al. 2017]]) concentrated the work into large dense matrix multiplications with large activation footprints. GPT-3’s roughly $3.14 \times 10^{23}$ FLOPs in 2020 ([[01 Books/Volume I - Foundations/02 Development/08 Model Training/V1-08.12 Source References#^ref-brown2020language|Brown et al. 2020]]) made utilization $({\eta_{\text{hw}}})$ the difference between an affordable run and an unaffordable one, and IO-aware kernels such as FlashAttention later raised ${\eta_{\text{hw}}}$ by attacking attention’s memory traffic.

If capability tracks the computation a general method can absorb, as the bitter lesson holds, then every point of ${\eta_{\text{hw}}}$ lost to stalls, and every gigabyte of memory that forces a smaller model or batch, is capability forgone at a fixed budget. The first obligation of a training system is therefore to convert its compute budget into useful operations as completely as the hardware allows.

↩︎ The bitter lesson, that general methods which absorb more computation outperform encoded human expertise, is established in **[[01 Books/Volume I - Foundations/00 Introduction/01 Introduction/V1-01.05 The Bitter Lesson#^sec-introduction-bitter-lesson-79c7|The Bitter Lesson]]**.

The law takes a run’s operation count and its achieved rate as given, and both come from the costs of individual operations. Every bottleneck traces back to specific mathematical operations: compute-intensive matrix multiplications governed by peak throughput ([[01 Books/Volume I - Foundations/02 Development/08 Model Training/V1-08.12 Source References#^ref-goto2008|Goto and Geijn 2008]]), low-intensity normalization layers governed by memory bandwidth, and optimizer state tensors that inflate the static memory footprint. On modern accelerators the ridge point, the operations per byte at which arithmetic rather than memory becomes the limit (**[[01 Books/Volume I - Foundations/01 Foundations/02 System Paradigms/V1-02.05 System Balance and Hardware#^sec-ml-systems-matching-workloads-hardware-bf91|Matching workloads to hardware]]**), far exceeds the arithmetic intensity of many neural network layers, leaving memory-bound kernels starved for operands ([[01 Books/Volume I - Foundations/02 Development/08 Model Training/V1-08.12 Source References#^ref-patterson2017hardware|Hennessy and Patterson 2017]]). Software frameworks expose these physical boundaries as configurable switches, such as mixed-precision autocasting contexts or activation checkpointing flags, and each switch must be set against the machine’s measured limits rather than tuned blindly. Optimizing a training system therefore begins with what each operation costs in FLOPs and bytes.

**Self-Check: Question**

1.  A training run has confirmed that input delivery and communication are off the critical path. The model FLOP count and the peak-throughput convention are being held fixed, yet profiling still reports only 38 percent effective end-to-end utilization relative to the stated peak. Under the training-time law $T_{\text{train}} = \frac{O}{R_{\text{peak}} \times \eta_{\text{hw}}}$, which lever is the most direct target for the next round of profiling?

    1.  Hardware utilization $\eta_{\text{hw}}$, because with $O$ and the relevant $R_{\text{peak}}$ convention fixed, the remaining gap points to how efficiently kernels are being executed end to end.
    2.  Peak throughput $R_{\text{peak}}$, because low effective end-to-end utilization relative to the stated peak automatically means the hardware peak specification is too small for the workload.
    3.  Total operations $O$, because once data and communication are hidden, utilization is no longer relevant to training time.
    4.  Dataset size, because shrinking the number of tokens directly raises effective end-to-end utilization relative to the stated peak even when execution efficiency is unchanged.

2.  When a team transitions a model from FP32 training to mixed-precision (FP16/BF16) on Tensor Core accelerators without changing the model architecture, batch size, or dataset, which term of the training-time law ($T_{\text{train}} = \frac{O}{R_{\text{peak}} \times \eta_{\text{hw}}}$) is most directly improved?

    1.  Total operations $O$, because lower numerical precision halves the number of multiplications required in each matrix multiplication.
    2.  Hardware utilization $\eta_{\text{hw}}$, because mixed precision automatically eliminates all GPU pipeline bubbles and CPU data stalls.
    3.  Peak throughput $R_{\text{peak}}$, because Tensor Cores provide a substantially higher theoretical FLOP/s ceiling for reduced-precision matrix operations compared to standard FP32 execution units.
    4.  Data volume $D_{\text{tokens}}$, because lower precision requires fewer training tokens for the loss to converge.

3.  Explain the scope conditions under which the simplified training-time law $T_{\text{train}} = \frac{O}{R_{\text{peak}} \times \eta_{\text{hw}}}$ is useful, and identify why the formula alone cannot diagnose a slow small-batch debugging run.

4.  True or False: All large deep learning models are primarily compute-bound, so training speed is always governed mainly by Tensor Core TFLOP/s.

5.  Place the following milestones in chronological order, reflecting how training-system constraints evolved: (1) Transformers shift sequence modeling toward high-throughput dense matrix multiplication and large activation footprints. (2) Backpropagation is popularized for multilayer neural networks. (3) Large-scale foundation models such as GPT-3 make utilization and multi-accelerator scaling central engineering concerns. (4) AlexNet demonstrates that deep neural network training maps effectively to GPUs. (5) IO-aware FlashAttention appears as a response to attention memory-bandwidth limits.
