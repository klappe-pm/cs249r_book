# V1-05.13 Footnotes

[[Home|Home]] · [[01 Books/Volume I - Foundations/Volume 1 Overview|Volume Overview]] · [[01 Books/Volume I - Foundations/02 Development/05 Neural Computation/V1-05 Neural Computation|V1-05 Neural Computation]]

^footnotes

<sup>1</sup> **Gradient instabilities**: In a simplified 20-layer path through sigmoid (S-shaped) activations, the activation-derivative factors alone are bounded above by $0.25^{20}$, or $9.1 \times 10^{-13}$. The complete gradient also includes weights and other operations, but repeated saturated derivatives can still make early-layer updates negligible. Plateaus or not-a-number (NaN) values can reveal the symptom without identifying the cause; rectified linear unit (ReLU) activations and careful initialization (both taught later in this chapter), together with normalization, better optimizers, and residual connections, all helped make deep networks trainable, and **[[01 Books/Volume I - Foundations/02 Development/06 Network Architectures/V1-06.05 Skip Connections and Normalization#^sec-network-architectures-evolution-dense-spatial-processing-2761|Skip connections and gradient flow]]** treats residual connections in detail.

^fn1

<sup>2</sup> **Histogram of oriented gradients (HOG)**: An influential handcrafted descriptor for pedestrian detection before deep learning ([[01 Books/Volume I - Foundations/02 Development/05 Neural Computation/V1-05.12 Source References#^ref-dalal2005hog|Dalal and Triggs 2005]]). One common configuration computes gradient orientations in $8{\times}8$ pixel cells, with cell size and other choices tuned for the task. HOG’s fixed computation graph can run efficiently on CPUs with predictable latency, while learned features can reduce descriptor redesign but do not automatically transfer across domains and often benefit from accelerators only as model and batch scale grow.

^fn2

<sup>3</sup> **Backpropagation**: Short for “backward propagation of errors,” the algorithm uses the chain rule to compute how the loss is locally sensitive to millions of weights in one backward traversal. Its systems cost is memory rather than arithmetic, because the backward pass needs the forward-pass activations, which training must store alongside gradients and optimizer state, the several-times-larger footprint quantified in [[01 Books/Volume I - Foundations/02 Development/05 Neural Computation/V1-05.05 Learning Process#^sec-neural-computation-memory-training-vs-inference|section 5.4.5.5]].

^fn3

<sup>4</sup> **Neuron**: McCulloch and Pitts ([[01 Books/Volume I - Foundations/02 Development/05 Neural Computation/V1-05.12 Source References#^ref-mcculloch1943|1943]]) introduced a mathematical threshold model of nervous activity: inputs combine and an all-or-none output fires when a threshold is met. That logical neuron is an origin point for the artificial-neuron abstraction and for networks built from many simple units. Learned weights, deep hierarchies, and modern fused multiply-add (FMA) accelerator datapaths are later developments that turn this abstraction into the matrix-heavy workloads studied in this chapter.

^fn4

<sup>5</sup> **MAC (multiply-accumulate)**: Hardware data sheets report fused multiply-add throughput in FLOP/s, so a quoted FLOP/s rate is twice the corresponding MAC/s rate. Layer and batch choices also create activation, communication, and data-movement costs, so MAC count is necessary but not sufficient for a systems budget.

^fn5

<sup>6</sup> **Perceptron**: A machine built to execute a learning algorithm, directly linking hardware and software from the start. A single linear-threshold layer cannot represent nonlinearly separable functions such as XOR, regardless of additional input hardware ([[01 Books/Volume I - Foundations/02 Development/05 Neural Computation/V1-05.12 Source References#^ref-minsky1969perceptrons|Minsky and Papert 1969]]). Multilayer nonlinear networks expand the representable function class, but they also introduce the training and systems costs developed in this chapter.

^fn6

<sup>7</sup> **Universal approximation theorem**: Under conditions on the activation, target function, compact domain, and approximation tolerance, a sufficiently wide single-hidden-layer network can approximate the target arbitrarily well ([[01 Books/Volume I - Foundations/02 Development/05 Neural Computation/V1-05.12 Source References#^ref-cybenko1989|Cybenko 1989]]; [[01 Books/Volume I - Foundations/02 Development/05 Neural Computation/V1-05.12 Source References#^ref-hornik1989|Hornik et al. 1989]]). This existence result is nonconstructive: it does not specify how to find the weights or how many neurons practical training will require. Why depth can nonetheless beat width is the subject of [[01 Books/Volume I - Foundations/02 Development/05 Neural Computation/V1-05.04 Neural Network Fundamentals#^sec-neural-computation-depth-matters-power-hierarchical-representations-f83c|section 5.3.5]].

^fn7

<sup>8</sup> **ReLU (rectified linear unit)**: ReLU’s cheap comparison-and-select evaluation and its non-saturating positive branch made deep networks markedly faster to train; in the AlexNet paper, a four-layer convolutional network reached 25 percent training error on CIFAR-10 six times sooner with ReLU than with tanh ([[01 Books/Volume I - Foundations/02 Development/05 Neural Computation/V1-05.12 Source References#^ref-krizhevsky2012imagenet|Krizhevsky et al. 2012]]).

^fn8

<sup>9</sup> **Sigmoid**: From Greek *sigma* + *eidos* (“sigma-shaped”), referring to the S-curve that maps inputs to the bounded (0, 1) range. The mapping requires an exponential approximation, commonly implemented through specialized instructions, lookup tables, or polynomial methods. It is generally more expensive than ReLU’s comparison-and-selection operation, although the measured gap depends on precision, vectorization, and hardware. ReLU’s adoption reflected both easier gradient flow on its positive branch and cheaper execution.

^fn9

<sup>10</sup> **Tanh (hyperbolic tangent)**: By centering its output range on zero, `tanh` can reduce the correlated-update behavior associated with all-positive activations. Its computational cost is similar to sigmoid because it also depends on exponential approximations, while its saturation behavior still permits vanishing gradients.

^fn10

<sup>11</sup> **Batch normalization**: Normalization layers ([[01 Books/Volume I - Foundations/02 Development/05 Neural Computation/V1-05.12 Source References#^ref-ioffe2015batch|Ioffe and Szegedy 2015]]) stabilize intermediate activations across training batches, permitting higher learning rates and reducing the risk of dying ReLUs. **[[01 Books/Volume I - Foundations/02 Development/06 Network Architectures/V1-06.05 Skip Connections and Normalization#^sec-network-architectures-normalization-layers-mathematical-foundations-0fb7|Normalization and activation stability]]** examines the trade-offs among normalization variants, and **[[01 Books/Volume I - Foundations/03 Optimization/10 Model Compression/V1-10.05 Hardware Implementation Changing How the Model Executes#^sec-model-compression-operator-fusion-ac1d|Compression-specific fusion]]** shows how batch normalization folds into adjacent weights for inference.

^fn11

<sup>12</sup> **Softmax**: The name, coined by Bridle ([[01 Books/Volume I - Foundations/02 Development/05 Neural Computation/V1-05.12 Source References#^ref-bridle1990probabilistic|1990]]), reflects its role as a “soft” or differentiable version of `argmax`, a function that must evaluate an entire vector to find its maximum value. This vector-wise operation is not used as a drop-in replacement for element-wise nonlinearities such as ReLU, but it is central whenever a model must normalize a vector of scores, including classification heads and attention layers. Direct exponentiation can overflow in 32-bit floating point, so stable implementations subtract the largest logit first ([[01 Books/Volume I - Foundations/02 Development/05 Neural Computation/V1-05.05 Learning Process#^sec-neural-computation-batch-loss-calculation-methods-f02b|section 5.4.3.3]]).

^fn12

<sup>13</sup> **Logits**: In multiclass neural classifiers, logits are unnormalized output scores. Score differences correspond to log probability ratios after softmax, while an individual multiclass logit is not itself a calibrated log-odds quantity. Because `argmax` over logits and `argmax` over softmax probabilities select the same class, inference can skip softmax when only the top class is needed.

^fn13

<sup>14</sup> **Parameter memory cost**: Parameter count is a misleading proxy for memory importance. The normalization layers that **[[01 Books/Volume I - Foundations/02 Development/06 Network Architectures/V1-06.05 Skip Connections and Normalization#^sec-network-architectures-normalization-layers-mathematical-foundations-0fb7|Normalization and activation stability]]** introduces, for example, add a few learned scale and shift parameters per layer that barely affect byte budgets but can strongly affect model behavior when training choices change. Conversely, the bulk of parameters in dense weight matrices require extra state during training: gradients plus optimizer bookkeeping beyond the stored weights themselves. A model that fits in memory for inference may therefore require several times more memory for training, a cost quantified later in the training memory budget.

^fn14

<sup>15</sup> **Bias terms**: Biases add one parameter per neuron, compared with ${d_{\text{in}}}$ weights for a neuron with ${d_{\text{in}}}$ inputs, so they account for $1/({d_{\text{in}}}+1)$ of that layer’s parameters. Their model-wide share and effect on accuracy depend on layer widths, architecture, and normalization. Some modern architectures omit biases in layers followed by batch normalization because the normalization transform includes a learned shift.

^fn15

<sup>16</sup> **Loss function**: Formalized by Abraham Wald in statistical decision theory ([[01 Books/Volume I - Foundations/02 Development/05 Neural Computation/V1-05.12 Source References#^ref-wald1949statistical|Wald 1949]]) as the “cost” of an incorrect decision, ${\mathcal{L}}$ quantifies the gap between prediction ${\hat{\mathbf{y}}}$ and ground truth ${\mathbf{y}}$. The choice of loss function shapes the optimization geometry: it determines the gradient landscape that backpropagation must navigate. A loss with flat regions near incorrect predictions produces weak gradients that stall learning, while a loss with steep gradients near the decision boundary accelerates convergence where it matters most, a systems consequence explored in [[01 Books/Volume I - Foundations/02 Development/05 Neural Computation/V1-05.05 Learning Process#^sec-neural-computation-loss-functions-6fc2|section 5.4.3]].

^fn16

<sup>17</sup> **One-hot encoding**: Representing each class as a binary vector with one entry per class, exactly one of which is 1. The encoding is sparse by construction, and for MNIST’s 10 classes, 90 percent of each label vector is zeros. Implementations commonly store an integer class index instead of materializing that vector. For very large output spaces, methods such as sampled softmax can also reduce the output computation.

^fn17

<sup>18</sup> **Chain rule**: The calculus identity $\frac{\partial {\mathcal{L}}}{\partial {w}} = \frac{\partial {\mathcal{L}}}{\partial {a}^{({N_L})}} \cdot \frac{\partial {a}^{({N_L})}}{\partial {a}^{({N_L}-1)}} \cdots \frac{\partial {a}^{(1)}}{\partial {w}}$ becomes a product of ${N_L}$ terms for an ${N_L}$-layer network. If each partial derivative is slightly less than one, the product vanishes exponentially; if slightly greater, it explodes. This multiplicative structure makes depth a systems constraint rather than an arbitrary design choice: it dictates the numerical precision requirements and initialization strategies ([[01 Books/Volume I - Foundations/02 Development/05 Neural Computation/V1-05.12 Source References#^ref-glorot2010xavier|Glorot and Bengio 2010]]; [[01 Books/Volume I - Foundations/02 Development/05 Neural Computation/V1-05.12 Source References#^ref-he2015|He et al. 2015]]) needed to keep training stable.

^fn18

<sup>19</sup> **Xavier/Glorot initialization**: For a layer with ${d_{\text{in}}}$ inputs and ${d_{\text{out}}}$ outputs, a common Glorot choice uses weight variance $2/({d_{\text{in}}}+{d_{\text{out}}})$ to balance forward activations and backward gradients ([[01 Books/Volume I - Foundations/02 Development/05 Neural Computation/V1-05.12 Source References#^ref-glorot2010xavier|Glorot and Bengio 2010]]). The fix costs zero additional FLOPs; it is purely a matter of setting the random distribution at startup.

^fn19

<sup>20</sup> **FP32 and reduced-precision formats**: The IEEE 754 binary32 format uses 32 bits (1 sign bit, 8 exponent bits, 23 fraction bits), whereas 16-bit alternatives split exponent and fraction differently: IEEE 754 FP16 allocates 5 exponent and 10 fraction bits, providing high precision but a narrow dynamic range ($6.1 \times 10^{-5}$ to $65{,}504$), so small gradients in FP16 training need the loss scaling of **[[01 Books/Volume I - Foundations/02 Development/08 Model Training/V1-08.07 Pipeline Optimizations#^sec-model-training-mixedprecision-training-9218|Mixed-precision training]]** to stay representable. Brain Floating Point (BF16) allocates 8 exponent bits matching FP32’s normal exponent range to reduce underflow risk relative to FP16; sufficiently small values can still underflow. The formats are collected for reference in **[[05 Reference/Volume I - Foundations/V1 Machine Foundations#^sec-appdx-machine-foundations-numerical-representations-c889|Numerical Representations]]**.

^fn20

<sup>21</sup> **Adam (adaptive moment estimation)**: Maintains per-parameter first- and second-moment estimates of the gradient, requiring 2$\times$ additional FP32 memory beyond the parameters themselves ([[01 Books/Volume I - Foundations/02 Development/05 Neural Computation/V1-05.12 Source References#^ref-kingma2014adam|Kingma and Ba 2015]]). For a 100K-parameter MNIST model this overhead is negligible, but for a 7-billion parameter model it adds ~56 GB, often the difference between fitting on one GPU or requiring partitioning. Adam is widely used because its adaptive updates work robustly across many tasks, but learning rate, schedule, regularization, and memory strategy still require validation.

^fn21

<sup>22</sup> **Epoch**: The number of passes is a direct multiplier on total work, so a 100-epoch MNIST run executes 100$\times$ more forward and backward passes than a single epoch. Large language-model training is often specified by a token budget rather than a simple epoch count because data sources may be mixed and sampled at different rates. The same accounting still applies: every additional training token adds another forward and backward computation.

^fn22

<sup>23</sup> **Inference**: From Latin *inferre* (“to bring in, to conclude”), borrowed from logic where it means deriving conclusions from premises. The ML usage marks a systems boundary: training executes forward and backward passes and maintains optimizer state, while ordinary inference executes the forward graph with frozen parameters. Removing gradients, optimizer state, and most saved activations can reduce memory and computation substantially, but the ratio depends on architecture, batch size, precision, runtime workspace, and serving strategy.

^fn23

<sup>24</sup> **Training GPU power budget**: The “high-memory” requirement is driven by the need to hold parameters, gradients, optimizer state, and activations simultaneously. The corresponding power draw dictates the “dedicated data-center cooling infrastructure,” as a single high-end training GPU consumes 400 W–700 W. Even compared with a 4 W mobile inference chip, that is at least 100× the power budget.

^fn24

<sup>25</sup> **Edge inference accelerators**: Google’s edge Tensor Processing Unit (TPU), the Edge TPU (Google Coral), operates in the mobile/embedded tier at about 2 W, delivering 4 TOPS through an INT8 datapath. Edge servers sit in a different tier: Jetson AGX Orin reaches 275 TOPS at 15 W–60 W, about 68.8× more throughput but with wired-power assumptions.

^fn25
