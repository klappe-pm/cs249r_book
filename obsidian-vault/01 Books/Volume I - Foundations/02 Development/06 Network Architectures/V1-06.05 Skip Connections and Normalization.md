# V1-06.05 Skip Connections and Normalization

[[Home|Home]] · [[01 Books/Volume I - Foundations/Volume 1 Overview|Volume Overview]] · [[01 Books/Volume I - Foundations/02 Development/06 Network Architectures/V1-06 Network Architectures|V1-06 Network Architectures]]

^sec-network-architectures-making-depth-trainable

When plain convolutional networks are stacked beyond roughly 20 layers, adding more layers can make *training* error worse rather than better. Gradients vanish or explode on the way back through the stack, and the scale of intermediate activations drifts on the way forward. Convolution made deep vision networks affordable in parameter count, and two building blocks made them trainable. Skip connections keep gradients alive through dozens of layers, and normalization keeps the activations those layers produce within a stable numerical range. Both arrived with deep CNNs, both carry their own memory and bandwidth costs, and both reappear in the deep families that follow, most visibly the transformer.

### Skip Connections and Gradient Flow

^sec-network-architectures-evolution-dense-spatial-processing-2761

The chain rule, whose layer-by-layer Jacobian form appears in **[[01 Books/Volume I - Foundations/02 Development/05 Neural Computation/V1-05.05 Learning Process#^sec-neural-computation-error-signal-propagation-9eeb|Error signal propagation]]**, explains both why gradients vanish or explode across depth and why additive identity shortcuts (skip connections) resolve that barrier. For a deep network with layers ${f}_1, {f}_2, \ldots, {f_{N_L}}$, the gradient of the loss ${\mathcal{L}}$ with respect to the weights in layer 1 is:

$$
\frac{\partial {\mathcal{L}}}{\partial {\mathbf{W}}^{(1)}} = \frac{\partial {\mathcal{L}}}{\partial {\mathbf{a}}^{({N_L})}} \cdot \frac{\partial {\mathbf{a}}^{({N_L})}}{\partial {\mathbf{z}}^{({N_L})}} \cdot \frac{\partial {\mathbf{z}}^{({N_L})}}{\partial {\mathbf{a}}^{({N_L}-1)}} \cdot \ldots \cdot \frac{\partial {\mathbf{z}}^{(2)}}{\partial {\mathbf{a}}^{(1)}} \cdot \frac{\partial {\mathbf{a}}^{(1)}}{\partial {\mathbf{z}}^{(1)}} \cdot \frac{\partial {\mathbf{z}}^{(1)}}{\partial {\mathbf{W}}^{(1)}}
$$

 where ${\mathbf{z}}^{({\ell})}$ represents the preactivation and ${\mathbf{a}}^{({\ell})} = {\sigma}({\mathbf{z}}^{({\ell})})$ the postactivation output of layer ${\ell}$, written here with the sigmoid activation ${\sigma}$. Each layer contributes both a weight transformation and an activation derivative ${\sigma}'({\mathbf{z}}^{({\ell})})$. With sigmoid activations, whose derivative peaks at 0.25 (**[[01 Books/Volume I - Foundations/02 Development/05 Neural Computation/V1-05.04 Neural Network Fundamentals#^sec-neural-computation-sigmoid-a98f|Sigmoid]]**), the activation-derivative factors alone contribute at most $(0.25)^{{N_L}}$ along a path. The full gradient also depends on the intervening weight matrices; when their transformations do not offset this attenuation, early layers receive negligible updates. Exploding gradients are the destabilizing counterpart, in which per-layer gains above one compound exponentially with depth and training diverges rather than stalls (**[[01 Books/Volume I - Foundations/02 Development/05 Neural Computation/V1-05.05 Learning Process#^sec-neural-computation-parameter-initialization-5d1a|Initialization and numerical range]]**).

ReLU removes the activation’s share of this decay, since its derivative is one on active paths (**[[01 Books/Volume I - Foundations/02 Development/05 Neural Computation/V1-05.04 Neural Network Fundamentals#^sec-neural-computation-relu-7184|ReLU]]**), and that change made networks of 10–20 layers trainable. It does not remove the problem. Units can still die, the dying ReLU problem of that section, and ReLU does nothing to control how much each weight matrix shrinks or stretches the gradient vector passing through it, so the weights continue to attenuate or amplify gradients across depth. The original ResNet study showed the consequence on CIFAR-10. A 56-layer plain network had higher training and test error than a 20-layer plain network throughout training ([[01 Books/Volume I - Foundations/02 Development/06 Network Architectures/V1-06.14 Source References#^ref-he2016|He et al. 2016a]]). Because the deeper network was worse on the *training* set, the failure was an optimization barrier, not overfitting.

A residual block gives the gradient a direct path back through its input. [[01 Books/Volume I - Foundations/02 Development/06 Network Architectures/V1-06.05 Skip Connections and Normalization#^eq-residual|Equation 6]] adds the identity path ${\mathbf{x}}$ to the residual mapping ${\mathcal{F}}({\mathbf{x}})$:

$$
{\mathbf{y}}= {\mathcal{F}}({\mathbf{x}}) + {\mathbf{x}} \tag{6}
$$

 where ${\mathcal{F}}({\mathbf{x}})$ represents the residual function and ${\mathbf{x}}$ is the identity skip connection. In ResNet the residual function is typically two convolutional layers with ReLU and batch normalization, a rescaling step that [[01 Books/Volume I - Foundations/02 Development/06 Network Architectures/V1-06.05 Skip Connections and Normalization#^sec-network-architectures-normalization-layers-mathematical-foundations-0fb7|section 6.4.2]] develops. The original post-activation ResNet block ([[01 Books/Volume I - Foundations/02 Development/06 Network Architectures/V1-06.05 Skip Connections and Normalization#^fig-example-skip-connection|figure 7]]) routes an identity shortcut around two weight layers and the ReLU between them, adds it back at a junction, and then applies the final activation.

^eq-residual

![[90 Assets/Volume I/06 Nn Architectures/Generated Figure 4d68e247f7.svg]]

Figure 7: **Residual Connection Block (Original Post-Activation Form)**: The original ResNet block ([[01 Books/Volume I - Foundations/02 Development/06 Network Architectures/V1-06.14 Source References#^ref-he2016|He et al. 2016a]]) implements ${\mathbf{y}} = \text{ReLU}({\mathcal{F}}({\mathbf{x}}) + {\mathbf{x}})$, where an identity shortcut bypasses the weight layers before a final ReLU activation. In later pre-activation variants ([[01 Books/Volume I - Foundations/02 Development/06 Network Architectures/V1-06.14 Source References#^ref-he2016identity|He et al. 2016b]]) and modern transformer blocks, the nonlinearity moves inside ${\mathcal{F}}({\mathbf{x}})$, making the addition a completely clean identity shortcut ${\mathbf{y}} = {\mathcal{F}}({\mathbf{x}}) + {\mathbf{x}}$ that matches the unobstructed gradient flow in [[01 Books/Volume I - Foundations/02 Development/06 Network Architectures/V1-06.05 Skip Connections and Normalization#^eq-residual|equation 6]].

^fig-example-skip-connection

During backpropagation, the gradient flows through this addition, and applying the chain rule gives:

$$
\frac{\partial {\mathcal{L}}}{\partial {\mathbf{x}}} = \frac{\partial {\mathcal{L}}}{\partial {\mathbf{y}}} \cdot \left(\frac{\partial {\mathcal{F}}({\mathbf{x}})}{\partial {\mathbf{x}}} + {\mathbf{I}}\right) = \frac{\partial {\mathcal{L}}}{\partial {\mathbf{y}}} \cdot {\mathcal{F}}'({\mathbf{x}}) + \frac{\partial {\mathcal{L}}}{\partial {\mathbf{y}}}
$$

Here ${\mathbf{I}}$ is the identity matrix, and the gradient decomposes into two additive terms. Even if the residual term $\frac{\partial {\mathcal{L}}}{\partial {\mathbf{y}}}{\mathcal{F}}'({\mathbf{x}})$ attenuates, the identity term passes $\frac{\partial {\mathcal{L}}}{\partial {\mathbf{y}}}$ directly to the earlier layer. While the residual branch can partially interfere with the identity signal, keeping $\|{\mathcal{F}}'({\mathbf{x}})\|$ small keeps the block Jacobian close to the identity, so the block neither shrinks nor stretches the gradient much in any direction. Through ${N_L}$ residual blocks, the gradient becomes:

$$
\frac{\partial {\mathcal{L}}}{\partial {\mathbf{x}}^{(0)}} = \frac{\partial {\mathcal{L}}}{\partial {\mathbf{x}}^{({N_L})}} \cdot \prod_{{\ell}=1}^{{N_L}} \left({\mathcal{F}}'_{\ell}({\mathbf{x}}^{({\ell}-1)}) + {\mathbf{I}}\right)
$$

Each factor $({\mathcal{F}}'_\ell + {\mathbf{I}})$ contains the identity term, keeping the factor near identity when the residual-branch Jacobian is small. A plain network instead multiplies arbitrary layer Jacobians, and if their average gain is even slightly below one the product vanishes exponentially ($0.9^{50} \approx 0.005$). ResNets multiply near-identity factors. The deviations can still compound, and the additive path does not guarantee exact unit gain across arbitrary depth, but this structure keeps the per-block gain close enough to one to make networks with 100+ layers trainable. The CIFAR-10 experiments confirm it. A 56-layer residual network reached about 7.0 percent test error against 8.8 percent for its 20-layer residual counterpart, reversing the degradation seen in plain networks ([[01 Books/Volume I - Foundations/02 Development/06 Network Architectures/V1-06.14 Source References#^ref-he2016|He et al. 2016a]]). There is no single depth at which skip connections suddenly become mandatory; trainability also depends on initialization, normalization, optimizer dynamics, and block architecture ([[01 Books/Volume I - Foundations/02 Development/06 Network Architectures/V1-06.14 Source References#^ref-he2016identity|He et al. 2016b]]).

The shortcuts carry a concrete hardware cost that every deep network pays. Evaluating ${\mathbf{y}}= {\mathcal{F}}({\mathbf{x}}) + {\mathbf{x}}$ requires the input tensor ${\mathbf{x}}$ to remain resident in memory throughout the execution of every operation in the branch ${\mathcal{F}}({\mathbf{x}})$. This extended liveness window increases peak activation memory in accelerator HBM during the forward pass and preserves ${\mathbf{x}}$ for backpropagation unless activation checkpointing, which recomputes such values during the backward pass instead of storing them, is used (**[[01 Books/Volume I - Foundations/02 Development/08 Model Training/V1-08.07 Pipeline Optimizations#^sec-model-training-activation-checkpointing-2ee1|Activation checkpointing]]**). The elementwise addition is also memory-bandwidth bound. It performs one addition per two loaded operands and one stored result, about 0.08 FLOP/byte in FP32 (one FLOP per 12 bytes), so unfused residual merges become memory stalls.

Residual connections stabilize the backward gradient path, but they do not constrain the forward scale of intermediate activations. Unbounded variance across depth can still destabilize layer inputs, which calls for a complementary block: normalization.

### Normalization and Activation Stability

^sec-network-architectures-normalization-layers-mathematical-foundations-0fb7

Without normalization, small weight updates in early layers compound across depth, shifting and amplifying intermediate activations until later nonlinearities saturate or overflow and training requires tiny learning rates. Skip connections protect the backward pass, and normalization steadies the forward pass by re-centering and re-scaling those intermediate activations at each block. Batch normalization in CNNs[[01 Books/Volume I - Foundations/02 Development/06 Network Architectures/V1-06.15 Footnotes#^fn17|¹⁷]] ([[01 Books/Volume I - Foundations/02 Development/06 Network Architectures/V1-06.14 Source References#^ref-ioffe2015batch|Ioffe and Szegedy 2015]]), layer normalization for sequence models, and RMSNorm[[01 Books/Volume I - Foundations/02 Development/06 Network Architectures/V1-06.15 Footnotes#^fn18|¹⁸]] for large language models illustrate different choices of reduction axes and statistics.

Batch normalization standardizes activations using statistics computed across the mini-batch for each feature or channel during training. For fully connected activations, the reduction axis is the batch dimension. For convolutional activations, implementations compute per-channel statistics over both the batch and spatial positions (${B} \times {H_{\text{img}}}\times {W_{\text{img}}}$). For a mini-batch ${\mathcal{B}}= \{{a}_1, \ldots, {a_{B}}\}$ of activations at a particular layer, the transformation proceeds in two stages, starting with the mini-batch mean and variance:

$$
{\mu_{\mathcal{B}}}= \frac{1}{{B}}\sum_{i=1}^{{B}} {a}_i \qquad {\sigma_{\mathcal{B}}}^2 = \frac{1}{{B}}\sum_{i=1}^{{B}} ({a}_i - {\mu_{\mathcal{B}}})^2
$$

The second stage normalizes each activation over the batch and applies a learned scale and shift. The normalization step in [[01 Books/Volume I - Foundations/02 Development/06 Network Architectures/V1-06.05 Skip Connections and Normalization#^eq-batchnorm-normalize|equation 7]] centers and scales activations, while [[01 Books/Volume I - Foundations/02 Development/06 Network Architectures/V1-06.05 Skip Connections and Normalization#^eq-batchnorm-transform|equation 8]] lets the network learn a useful output scale and offset:

$$
{\hat{a}}_i = \frac{{a}_i - {\mu_{\mathcal{B}}}}{\sqrt{{\sigma_{\mathcal{B}}}^2 + {\epsilon}}} \tag{7}
$$

$$
{y}_i = {\gamma}{\hat{a}}_i + {\beta} \tag{8}
$$

^eq-batchnorm-transform

^eq-batchnorm-normalize

The parameters ${\gamma}$ (scale) and ${\beta}$ (shift) are learned during training, while ${\epsilon}$ (typically $10^{-5}$) prevents division by zero. The affine transform restores learned scale and shift flexibility, but fixed ${\gamma}$ and ${\beta}$ cannot exactly invert varying statistics for every batch. Normalization permits larger learning rates by preventing intermediate activations from exploding into saturating non-linear regimes, accelerating convergence relative to unnormalized networks.

While batch normalization enabled training of much deeper CNNs, it needs batch-wide statistics, which constrains distributed and sequence workloads. When a mini-batch is split across several accelerators, computing true mini-batch statistics requires an all-reduce, a collective that sums every accelerator’s partial statistics and returns the total to all of them, adding communication latency to every normalized layer. If instead each accelerator computes statistics on its own small batch, the noisy estimates destabilize optimization. Batch normalization also fits poorly with the sequence models later in this chapter. Their inputs vary in length, so batch statistics must mask out the padding that fills shorter sequences. A text generator also produces one token (a word or word piece) at a time, so at serving time there is no batch to average over, and the statistics it uses differ from those seen in training, a training-serving skew. Layer normalization resolves these limitations by normalizing across the feature dimension rather than across the batch ([[01 Books/Volume I - Foundations/02 Development/06 Network Architectures/V1-06.14 Source References#^ref-ba2016layer|Ba et al. 2016]]).

↪ The AllReduce collective and its communication cost are developed in **[[01 Books/Volume I - Foundations/02 Development/08 Model Training/V1-08.08 Scaling Training Systems#^sec-model-training-data-parallelism-ad1c|Data parallelism]]**.

For an input vector ${\mathbf{x}}\in \mathbb{R}^{{d}}$ with ${d}$ features, layer normalization first computes the mean and variance across those ${d}$ components:

$$
{\mu_{\text{LN}}}= \frac{1}{{d}}\sum_{i=1}^{{d}} {x}_i \qquad {\sigma_{\text{LN}}}^2 = \frac{1}{{d}}\sum_{i=1}^{{d}} ({x}_i - {\mu_{\text{LN}}})^2
$$

With those per-vector statistics in hand, [[01 Books/Volume I - Foundations/02 Development/06 Network Architectures/V1-06.05 Skip Connections and Normalization#^eq-layernorm|equation 9]] defines the complete layer normalization operation, where $\odot$ denotes element-wise multiplication:

$$
\text{LayerNorm}({\mathbf{x}}) = \frac{{\mathbf{x}}- {\mu_{\text{LN}}}}{\sqrt{{\sigma_{\text{LN}}}^2 + {\epsilon}}} \odot {\boldsymbol{\gamma}}+ {\boldsymbol{\beta}} \tag{9}
$$

^eq-layernorm

Because each token or sample is normalized independently, the computation stays within an accelerator’s on-chip SRAM and registers, with no cross-sample or cross-device communication. That independence makes layer normalization the standard choice for transformers, where sequences vary in length and text generation evaluates a single token at a time. [[01 Books/Volume I - Foundations/02 Development/06 Network Architectures/V1-06.05 Skip Connections and Normalization#^tbl-normalization-comparison|Table 4]] contrasts the three variants along the machine and operational dimensions that decide between them.

| **Characteristic**        | **BatchNorm**                          | **LayerNorm**            | **RMSNorm**                   |
|:--------------------------|:---------------------------------------|:-------------------------|:------------------------------|
| **Normalization Axis**    | Batch and, for CNNs, spatial positions | Feature dimension        | Feature dimension             |
| **Batch Size Dependency** | High (noisy for small batches)         | None                     | None                          |
| **Typical Use Case**      | CNNs, vision models                    | Transformers, RNNs       | LLaMA, efficient transformers |
| **Computation Cost**      | Higher (mean + variance)               | Higher (mean + variance) | Lower (RMS only)              |
| **Training/Inference**    | Different (running stats)              | Identical                | Identical                     |

Table 4: **Normalization Variant Comparison**: Different normalization techniques trade off between computational efficiency, batch size sensitivity, and architectural compatibility. RMSNorm ([[01 Books/Volume I - Foundations/02 Development/06 Network Architectures/V1-06.14 Source References#^ref-zhang2019root|Zhang and Sennrich 2019]]), used in LLaMA-family and other efficient transformer architectures ([[01 Books/Volume I - Foundations/02 Development/06 Network Architectures/V1-06.14 Source References#^ref-touvron2023llama|Touvron, Lavril, et al. 2023]]; [[01 Books/Volume I - Foundations/02 Development/06 Network Architectures/V1-06.14 Source References#^ref-touvron2023llama2|Touvron, Martin, et al. 2023]]), omits mean centering: $\text{RMSNorm}({\mathbf{x}}) = {\mathbf{x}}/ \sqrt{\frac{1}{{d}}\sum_i {x}_i^2 + {\epsilon}} \odot {\boldsymbol{\gamma}}$.

^tbl-normalization-comparison

The computational profile of normalization is defined by its arithmetic intensity. Because normalization applies elementwise operations to large activation tensors, execution is memory-bandwidth bound rather than compute bound. A straightforward LayerNorm kernel (a compiled accelerator routine, a different sense of the word from a convolution kernel) makes two reductions across the hidden dimension ${d}$, one for the mean ${\mu_{\text{LN}}}$ and one for the variance ${\sigma_{\text{LN}}}^2$, although an implementation can compute both in a single pass. RMSNorm drops the mean-centering reduction, leaving a single sum of squares, which simplifies the kernel and eases fusion with neighboring operations.

↪ Kernel fusion, which collapses memory-bound normalization and activation chains into single kernels whose intermediates stay on chip, is developed in **[[01 Books/Volume I - Foundations/02 Development/07 ML Frameworks/V1-07.04 Execution from Graphs to Kernels#^sec-ml-frameworks-execution-strategy-matters-memory-wall-1ce8|The memory wall and kernel fusion]]**.

The table’s last row is an operational cost. BatchNorm keeps nonlearned running mean and variance buffers and switches from mini-batch statistics in training to those running statistics at inference, so a mismatch between the buffers and deployment data is a frequent source of training-serving skew. LayerNorm and RMSNorm compute identically in both modes and carry no running state. With skip connections and normalization in place, depth stops being an optimization barrier.

**Checkpoint 6.2: Making depth trainable**

**Gradient path**: can you explain why the identity term in a residual block keeps gradients from vanishing across many layers?

**Liveness cost**: why does a skip connection raise peak activation memory, and why is an unfused residual addition memory-bandwidth bound?

**Normalization choice**: why does layer normalization suit sequence models and token-by-token generation better than batch normalization?

Depth extends how far a network’s view reaches across its input, but a convolutional stack still sees only its receptive field, whether that field spans pixels or the time bins of a spectrogram. A streaming system that must carry information across variable time spans within a fixed state budget motivates a different trade-off: updating a compact recurrent state as each observation arrives.

**Self-Check: Question**

1.  In a residual block implementing $\mathbf{y} = \mathcal{F}(\mathbf{x}) + \mathbf{x}$, how does the additive identity shortcut mathematically improve gradient conditioning through the layer Jacobian $\mathbf{J} = \frac{\partial \mathbf{y}}{\partial \mathbf{x}}$ during backpropagation in very deep networks?

    1.  The shortcut forces the residual function $\mathcal{F}(\mathbf{x})$ to have zero weights, turning the network into an immutable linear identity operator.
    2.  The Jacobian takes the form $\mathbf{J} = \mathbf{I} + \frac{\partial \mathcal{F}}{\partial \mathbf{x}}$, so when residual-branch derivatives are small the block stays near identity and gradients have a direct additive path, improving conditioning without guaranteeing exact unit gain at arbitrary depth.
    3.  The shortcut doubles the singular values of the weight matrix at every layer, ensuring gradients explode exponentially rather than vanish.
    4.  The shortcut eliminates the backpropagation chain rule by replacing gradient updates with forward-only finite differences.

2.  Compare Batch Normalization (BatchNorm) and Layer Normalization (LayerNorm) along two critical systems dimensions: (a) sensitivity to mini-batch size during training, and (b) operational differences between training and inference (including training-serving skew).
