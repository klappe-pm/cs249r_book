# V3-13.05 Grading Trajectories

[[Home|Home]] · [[01 Books/Volume III - Agentic Systems/Volume 3 Overview|Volume Overview]] · [[01 Books/Volume III - Agentic Systems/04 The Agent Runtime/13 Agent Evaluation/V3-13 Agent Evaluation|V3-13 Agent Evaluation]]

^sec-vol3-evaluation-grading

Two trials of `pool_repair_01` can submit byte-identical source files and still differ in acceptance status. For `ws_r19`, only the targeted check has passed, while another trial of the same patch may already have established the regression and accounting evidence that `ws_r19` still awaits. Comparing the patches alone cannot tell the two apart. Grading connects each declared criterion to evidence, records what that evidence establishes, and preserves what remains unknown.

### Outcome and Process Criteria

^sec-vol3-evaluation-grading-criteria

Inspecting only the final workspace snapshot `ws_r19` cannot tell whether the agent reached a passing patch cleanly or bypassed required approvals and leaked credentials along the way. An evaluation contract therefore separates outcome criteria from process criteria. An outcome criterion concerns the requested result, such as the regression checks against `ws_r19`; it may still inspect history when history determines the result, because a final resource count cannot establish that no duplicate allocation occurred. Process criteria concern how the trial reached its result, and the evidence that process deserves separate grading comes from reasoning tasks. On math word problems, Uesato et al. ([[01 Books/Volume III - Agentic Systems/04 The Agent Runtime/13 Agent Evaluation/V3-13.11 Source References#^ref-uesato2022solving|2022]]) found that outcome and process supervision reached similar final-answer accuracy while process supervision sharply reduced correct answers reached through flawed reasoning. Lightman et al. ([[01 Books/Volume III - Agentic Systems/04 The Agent Runtime/13 Agent Evaluation/V3-13.11 Source References#^ref-lightman2023let|2024]]) found that process-supervised reward models selected correct solutions more reliably. For an agent, the process also includes actions. Current authority, forbidden disclosures, required approvals, and recovery obligations can be mandatory conditions of acceptance, while other process measurements only diagnose. Tool-call validity, for example, measures the fraction of proposed calls that satisfy their schemas and is silent on whether the chosen calls were useful.

↩︎ Process-supervised reward models and step-level verifiers are established in **[[01 Books/Volume III - Agentic Systems/01 The Model/03 Test-Time Compute/V3-03.05 Verifying Candidates#^sec-vol3-test-time-compute-outcome-step-evidence|Checking whole outputs vs. intermediate steps]]**.

Diagnostic metrics need their own denominators and endpoints. A recovery rate counts episodes that met a stated recovery condition among the eligible episodes observed, reporting unresolved episodes separately; a different next action alone is not a recovery. Partial-credit scores show which checked subgoals were met and leave a failed mandatory condition failed, and turns, tokens, and costs are reported with the task outcomes so that a shorter failed trial never reads as an efficiency gain.

### Grader Error Rates

^sec-vol3-evaluation-grading-errors

Rerunning an automated grader on the same submission measures only whether the grader agrees with itself. Auditing only the submissions it accepted misses every bad patch it rejected, which leaves the team unable to predict how often the grader will approve a broken submission when a new candidate changes the failure mix. Because the verification asymmetry (principle [[01 Books/Volume III - Agentic Systems/01 The Model/V3 Model Principles#^pri-vol3-verification-asymmetry|3]]) separates checking a stated property from establishing that the property captures the task, calibrating a grader requires an independent reference assessment of whether the criterion actually held. False acceptance is especially consequential when accepted trajectories support release or later training, but the two conditional error rates answer different questions. Let ${F}$ denote failure of the specified criterion under an independent reference assessment, and let $\text{Acc}$ denote acceptance by the grader under study. Then distinguish

$$
{\epsilon_{\text{FA}}}=\Pr(\text{Acc}\mid {F}), \qquad \text{Failure among accepted}=\Pr({F}\mid \text{Acc}).
$$

The **false-acceptance rate** conditions on reference-assessed failures and measures how often the grader accepted them. Failure among accepted conditions on grader acceptances and measures how often independent assessment found failure among them. A random audit of accepted outputs estimates the second quantity; the first requires reference-assessed failures, including those the grader rejected. Both need stated sampling, enough assessed cases, and an uncertainty estimate. Failure among accepted is the complement of the passing-verdict precision of **[[01 Books/Volume III - Agentic Systems/01 The Model/03 Test-Time Compute/V3-03.05 Verifying Candidates#^eq-vol3-verifier-precision|equation 3.3]]**, $1-{\Pi_{V}}$, with the grader’s acceptance as the passing verdict, so the base-rate argument developed there for candidate checks applies to graders unchanged.

↩︎ The precision of a passing verdict, and its collapse at a low valid rate, is established in **[[01 Books/Volume III - Agentic Systems/01 The Model/03 Test-Time Compute/V3-03.05 Verifying Candidates#^sec-vol3-test-time-compute-check-precision|How base rates limit check precision]]**.

The distinction matters when the system under test changes, for two reasons. First, failure among accepted cases ($\Pr({F}\mid \text{Acc})$) depends on the overall prevalence of failures in the submissions, so a candidate that fails more often increases the share of bad outputs among accepted runs even if the grader’s false-acceptance rate stays fixed. Second, the false-acceptance rate ($\Pr(\text{Acc}\mid {F})$) itself can shift when a new candidate fails in ways that were rare in the original calibration set, exposing blind spots in the grader. Each candidate’s audit therefore rechecks both rates rather than borrowing a historical false-acceptance figure. Calibration cases come from the intended task population, with deliberate coverage of important failure modes, assessed independently against the same versioned rubric. Reference assessors also err, so the set keeps its adjudication procedure and unresolved labels. A set that oversamples failures reports that design, since its unweighted error fraction describes the calibration mix rather than deployment, and cases used to tune the grader stay out of it.

### Calibration of Qualitative Judgment

^sec-vol3-evaluation-grading-calibration

An uncalibrated model judge asked whether a repair report is “clear and complete” will reward fluent, lengthy prose even when the report omits a failed regression check or echoes an adversarial instruction embedded in the candidate text. When a report’s adequacy or a repair explanation’s clarity requires judgment rather than an exact mechanical test, a **large language model (LLM) judge** can score the criterion under an explicit rubric. The evaluation contract must state whether its verdict is a required acceptance condition, a trigger for human review, or a diagnostic score. Even then, a favorable judgment cannot override another criterion’s failure or turn unavailable evidence into a pass.

Ground the judge in trusted result records and discrete rubric items. For the repair explanation, those items can distinguish describing the patch accurately, identifying which checks ran, and acknowledging unresolved work. Candidate text and artifacts remain untrusted inputs to the grader, not instructions that may revise its rubric, and a tool-assisted judge inherits the same authority boundaries as any other tool user. Zheng et al. ([[01 Books/Volume III - Agentic Systems/04 The Agent Runtime/13 Agent Evaluation/V3-13.11 Source References#^ref-zheng2023judging|2023]]) measured position and verbosity biases in model judges and found the evidence for self-enhancement bias inconclusive, so each judge is tested on its own workload. Pairwise judging declares order swapping, ties, and abstentions before scoring and keeps them in the denominator, and a judge from a different model family is not independent by that fact alone.

For categorical ratings of the same cases, Cohen’s kappa ([[01 Books/Volume III - Agentic Systems/04 The Agent Runtime/13 Agent Evaluation/V3-13.11 Source References#^ref-cohen1960|Cohen 1960]]) summarizes agreement relative to the raters’ marginal label frequencies:

$$
{\kappa} = \frac{{p_o} - {p_e}}{1 - {p_e}}
$$

Here ${p_o}$ is the observed agreement fraction, and ${p_e}=\sum_{y} {p_{y,1}} {p_{y,2}}$ uses each rater’s fraction of labels in category ${y}$. When ${p_e}=1$, the expression is undefined. High raw agreement can arise from heavily concentrated label frequencies.

If two raters each label 90 percent of cases as passing, independently of what the case contains, they agree on 82 percent of cases by chance alone and ${\kappa}$ is 0. Report those frequencies, disagreements, and assessment coverage with kappa; the coefficient measures agreement, not whether either rater is correct. Because shared mistakes survive high agreement, a grader’s operating policy is set by the errors that matter for its role, and it is recalibrated whenever the rubric, judge, evidence interface, or evaluated population changes.

**Checkpoint 13.2: Defend a grading verdict**

**Evidence scope**: The trial’s targeted check passes on `ws_r19`, but required regression and accounting evidence remain pending under `repair_accept_v1`. Which criteria are supported, and why does an acknowledged residual-duty handoff not fill those gaps?

**Conditional denominator**: An audit samples only grader-accepted reports. Does its failure fraction estimate $\Pr(\text{Acc}\mid {F})$ or $\Pr({F}\mid \text{Acc})$? What population is missing for the other estimate?

**Process requirement**: When can a recovered malformed proposal remain a diagnostic event, and when must a process violation reject the trial despite a correct final artifact?

**Agreement vs. truth**: Two judges each pass 80 percent of reports, independently of what the reports contain. Estimate their raw agreement and ${\kappa}$, and decide whether that agreement supports using either one as an acceptance grader.

^callout-checkpoint-13-2-5cbd5f05

Grading yields calibrated criterion-level verdicts and diagnostic measurements for individual trials, but those verdicts evaluate only the specific executions observed in the test harness. Even when graders are isolated and judges are calibrated against reference assessments, observing that a candidate passes more trials than a baseline does not establish that it is genuinely superior. Generalizing beyond those observed runs requires decomposing variance across stochastic attempts and diverse tasks, establishing confidence intervals that do not collapse at extreme probabilities, and bounding comparative uncertainty through paired trials.
