# V3-12.14 Exercises

[[Home|Home]] · [[01 Books/Volume III - Agentic Systems/Volume 3 Overview|Volume Overview]] · [[01 Books/Volume III - Agentic Systems/04 The Agent Runtime/12 Failure Recovery/V3-12 Failure Recovery|V3-12 Failure Recovery]]

^sec-vol3-failure-recovery-exercises

Each problem states its constraints and ends in a decision the runtime or its designer must make. Givens are hypothetical unless a lighthouse profile supplies them, and dollar figures cover only the charges each problem names.

**1. Cleanup graph versus reverse stack (Basic).** A branching repair workflow has five registered cleanup operations. Once admitted, ending the integration test takes 30 s, and preserving result artifacts outside the environment takes 20 s. Releasing the temporary environment takes 40 s and requires both of the first two to be settled. Revoking the environment’s temporary credential takes 5 s and requires the release. Deleting an unrelated private scratch directory takes 10 s and has no dependencies. Compute the elapsed cleanup time when a coordinator runs the five operations one at a time as a reverse stack, and when it runs the cleanup graph with unlimited parallelism. Then suppose the cancellation reply for the test is lost, so ending the test stays unresolved. Determine which operations can still settle, which are blocked, and what must be recorded for each blocked node.

**2. Repair first on two axes (Basic).** After a locally rejected test configuration, compensating and rebuilding to the same readiness endpoint takes 120 s and \$0.40 in model charges. One bounded repair attempt takes 6 s and \$0.03, and a failed attempt leaves the original fallback unchanged. Recorded episodes for this failure class succeed with probability 0.06. Compute ${p}^*$ and the expected cost of repair first on each axis, and state which axis favors repair. Decide what the runtime should do when the task’s binding limit is its deadline and when it is its dollar allowance, and find the smallest measured success rate at which repair first pays on both axes.

**3. Where a loop spends its budget (Basic).** An append-only repair loop starts with a 2,000-token input. Each turn appends 400 output tokens and a 1,100-token error observation to the next input, with no compaction or prefix caching. The assumed combined input and output capacity is 64,000 tokens, and a cumulative ceiling stops the loop after 40 turns. A watchdog could instead intervene after completed turn 6. Compute the total re-sent input at the ceiling and at the intervention, and their ratio. Find the first turn by which the loop has sent half of its ceiling input, and check whether the context capacity would stop the loop before the ceiling does. Use these results to decide where engineering effort on loop cost belongs: the ceiling, the context limit, or detection latency.

**4. Classify before tripping (Intermediate).** A test-service breaker applies [[01 Books/Volume III - Agentic Systems/04 The Agent Runtime/12 Failure Recovery/V3-12.08 Tool Circuit Breakers#^eq-vol3-breaker-trip|equation 7]] with ${n_{\min}} = 20$ and ${r_{\text{trip}}} = 0.5$ over the last 50 classified outcomes. The current window holds 14 transport timeouts, 6 server errors, 12 validation rejections of one trajectory’s malformed requests, 3 authorization denials, and 15 successful runs. The validation rejections and authorization denials were returned by the test-service endpoint, rather than by local pre-dispatch gates; this policy includes them as nonfailure observations in the window. (a) Compute ${r}$ under the chapter’s classification rule and decide whether the breaker opens. (b) Recompute ${r}$ for a breaker that counts every outcome other than a successful run as a failure, and identify who pays for that design. (c) The breaker has opened during a sustained outage. Each half-open probe resolves by a 2 s timeout, the next cooldown starts when the probe resolves, the first cooldown is 5 s, and each failed probe doubles the cooldown up to an 80 s cap. Count the probes sent in the first 600 s after the trip, compare the count with a fixed 5 s cooldown, and explain what the cap trades against.

**5. Size the bulkhead (Intermediate).** Logical test operations are offered at a steady 5 per second to a shared pool of 400 workers. During a sustained stall every admitted attempt holds a worker for its full 20 s timeout. All three retry layers exhaust their allowances, one try plus one retry per layer, wherever admission permits. Ignore backoff gaps for this worker-demand estimate. Use a smooth-load, work-conserving approximation, and distinguish the unconstrained attempt demand from the rate the finite pool can actually admit; waiting or rejected attempts do not occupy workers. A client library, a gateway, and trajectory-level recovery supply the three retry layers. (a) Compute the unconstrained attempt rate and worker demand. Estimate saturated occupancy when a persistent dependency backlog keeps the pool busy, and determine the maximum admitted attempt rate at that timeout. Explain why the entire offered retry stream cannot be admitted in steady state. (b) Repeat the demand and occupancy comparison with a single retry owner that allows one try plus one retry in total, under the same smooth-load assumptions. (c) Local tool work needs at least 150 workers at all times. Choose a dependency cap, and determine which of three levers (the cap, coordinated retries, or a shorter 10 s timeout) guarantees that reservation whatever the stall’s timeout turns out to be. (d) Under the coordinated policy, suppose all its offered attempts are admitted and each produces a distinct remote test, with no remote deduplication or cancellation and enough remote capacity for every test to run for 120 s. Estimate the mean outstanding remote jobs, and explain why the local cap does not bound them.

**6. Price an approval gate at the pivot (Intermediate).** Take the remediation agent’s profile from notebook [12.2](https://mlsysbook.ai/vol3/12_failure_recovery/12_failure_recovery.html#nbk-12-pivot-expected-loss). A proposed fleet change is wrong with probability 4 percent, and a wrong change that commits costs \$12,000. Canary checks catch 80 percent of wrong changes before the pivot, and reverting a caught change avoids 90 percent of its impact. An additional human approval placed before the fleet rollout costs \$20 of reviewer time per attempt and catches a fraction ${q}$ of the wrong changes the canary misses; a change caught at approval is reverted on the canary like any other caught change. (a) Write ${L_A}$ with the gate as a function of ${q}$, and derive the break-even ${q}^*$ at which the gate pays for its reviewer cost. (b) Evaluate the net saving per task at ${q} = 0.5$. (c) The same reviewer placed after the rollout, auditing committed changes, catches the same fraction. Compute its effect on ${L_A}$ and state the design rule it illustrates. (d) Approval adds elapsed time to every mitigation. Propose a policy that keeps most of the saving while gating fewer changes, and name the evidence that policy needs.

**7. Second lighthouse: the support agent’s deadline (Intermediate).** The support agent must answer each customer turn within 8 s. In one turn it has used 5 s when the runtime rejects its refund request before dispatch because the amount field fails schema validation. The runtime keeps a 0.4 s finalization reserve for delivering the reply or the handoff notice. One bounded repair call takes 2 s of generation plus 0.5 s of local validation, costs \$0.006, and succeeds for this failure class with measured probability 0.6. The fallback is a human handoff at \$4 per session. (a) Compute the dollar threshold ${p}^*$ and the expected dollar cost of repair first. (b) Apply the admission check to the turn’s deadline and find the largest repair allowance ${K_{\text{repair}}}$ this turn can grant. (c) In a different session the refund request is dispatched and times out. In 30 percent of such timeouts the refund actually executed, and the mean refund is \$60. Compute the expected duplicate payout of blindly reissuing the refund, compare it with the handoff cost, and state what the endpoint’s contract must provide before any repeat is safe. (d) Using the results of (a) through (c), contrast the constraint that binds this agent’s recovery with the one that binds the repair agent’s.

**8. Contain a suspect remediation (Intermediate).** A remediation agent trajectory R is flagged at ${t} = 0$: the fleet change it dispatched derives from a runbook summary that an injected instruction shaped, and the summary sits in shared memory where other trajectories retrieve it. Four restrictions are available, and the incident owner issues and confirms them one at a time. The control hold at the supervisor takes 0.5 min to confirm; until it does, R admits new changes to 4 hosts per minute. Canceling the fleet rollout R already dispatched takes 3 min to confirm with the rollout service, which meanwhile applies the change to 20 hosts per minute. Revoking the old grant version that a dispatch adapter still honors takes 2 min, and until then the adapter forwards R’s queued requests to 2 hosts per minute. Quarantining the summary at memory retrieval takes 1 min, and until then 6 trajectories per minute retrieve it. Each affected host costs \$30 to verify and restore, and each trajectory that retrieves the summary costs \$50 to review; these are hypothetical givens. (a) Compute the containment cost, the sum over restrictions of each source’s cost rate times the time its restriction is confirmed, for the order hold, revocation, cancellation, quarantine. Then find the order that minimizes it, state the rule that ranks the restrictions, and count the hosts and trajectories each order lets through. (b) Find the cancellation confirmation time below which cancellation should be issued first. (c) An engineer proposes replacing the first step with one 0.5 min action that reverts every configuration file R touched and revokes all of R’s grants, including the recovery service’s grant to undrain the 3 canary hosts R drained. Identify what this action destroys or strands, and state which restrictions the owner should issue instead. (d) The summary entered shared memory 40 min before detection and was retrieved at the same rate throughout. Compute the review cost of the trajectories that retrieved it before ${t} = 0$, compare it with the gap between the two orders in (a), explain why the quarantine does not contain those trajectories, and decide where the next engineering investment in containment belongs.

**9. Critique a watchdog policy (Advanced).** A team proposes detector A, which hashes the entire workspace after each turn and ends model-directed repair on the first recurrence. In a cohort of 1,000 repair trajectories, 120 enter a period-2 loop that would never be accepted. Of the remaining trajectories, 200 are useful trajectories that deliberately backtrack once and would all be accepted, and the other 680 are useful trajectories that never repeat a state and yield 500 acceptances. Detector A intervenes on every loop after completed loop turn 4 and also ends all 200 backtracking trajectories. Detector B canonicalizes a declared candidate projection and intervenes only after two recurrences with no Pareto improvement in the declared checks; it intervenes on loops after loop turn 8 and ends 20 of the backtracking trajectories. Without a detector, loops run to the 40-turn ceiling. Price each looping trajectory by its loop turns alone, using the token givens of Problem 3 at \$3 per million input tokens and \$15 per million output tokens. A useful trajectory that runs to completion costs \$1.20, and one ended early costs \$0.60. (a) Compute the cost per accepted task, total cohort cost divided by accepted tasks, for no detector, detector A, and detector B. (b) Find how many backtracking trajectories detector A could end falsely and still match detector B’s cost per accepted task. (c) Critique detector A’s projection and its response beyond these numbers, then propose a defensible redesign that states which parts the runtime enforces and which remain fallible evidence.

**10. Settle a lost provisioning reply (Advanced).** A repair fleet provisions 5,000 temporary environments per day at \$2.40 per environment-hour, and the provisioning reply is lost for 2 percent of calls. For 25 of each day’s lost replies, the creation is still pending when the runtime first queries the endpoint’s result-recovery interface, which returns no environment; the creation then completes at a time distributed uniformly between 2 and 10 minutes later. For the other lost replies, result recovery returns the created environment at once. Policy X closes the obligation on the empty lookup and provisions a replacement, relying on a sweep that runs every ${T}$ hours to delete environments with no owner; an orphan’s creation time is uniform within the sweep interval. Policy Y keeps the obligation open under the settlement rule, polls result recovery for up to 15 minutes while the task waits, and binds the late environment when it appears. Value task waiting time at \$0.50 per task-minute. (a) Compute policy X’s daily orphan charge for a weekly sweep (${T} = 168$ h) and policy Y’s daily waiting cost. (b) Find the sweep interval at which the two costs are equal. (c) Identify two defects of policy X that no sweep interval fixes, and specify what policy Y must do when a creation has not appeared after 15 minutes.

**What’s Next: From recovery behavior to evaluation evidence**

Once recovery has kept every effect owned and accounted for, the runtime’s own evidence cannot verify that the repair actually succeeded. Settlement, compensation, and containment establish that execution stayed within its bounds, while a targeted pass on `ws_r18`, a released temporary environment, and a well-formed final report each answer a narrower question than task success. One successful run is also a single draw from a stochastic process, and replacing one agent configuration with another needs evidence across many tasks. **[[01 Books/Volume III - Agentic Systems/04 The Agent Runtime/13 Agent Evaluation/V3-13 Agent Evaluation#^sec-vol3-evaluation|Agent Evaluation]]** develops the acceptance criteria, isolated graders, and paired comparisons that turn individual trials into evidence about one configuration of the single-agent system. Every later release and learning decision rests on that evidence.
