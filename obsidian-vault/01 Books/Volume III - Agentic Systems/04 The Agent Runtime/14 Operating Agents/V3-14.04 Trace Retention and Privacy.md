# V3-14.04 Trace Retention and Privacy

[[Home|Home]] · [[01 Books/Volume III - Agentic Systems/Volume 3 Overview|Volume Overview]] · [[01 Books/Volume III - Agentic Systems/04 The Agent Runtime/14 Operating Agents/V3-14 Operating Agents|V3-14 Operating Agents]]

^sec-vol3-evaluation-budgets

If a delayed regression suite for the trial that produced `ws_r19` fails hours after execution concludes, that trial suddenly becomes diagnostically indispensable. By that time, however, its runtime process has terminated and its model and tool spans have closed. The forensic value of the run depends entirely on what the telemetry system chose to keep. Trace retention therefore requires integration with final task verdicts and their arrival timelines, rather than relying purely on process exit codes or span completion markers. Because retaining exhaustive execution histories rapidly escalates storage expenditures and expands sensitive data exposure, systems designers must determine which evidence each evaluation goal demands, and which diagnostic guarantees remain possible when capture budgets are constrained.

### Accounting Records and Head Sampling

^sec-vol3-evaluation-budgets-accounting

If a telemetry pipeline samples trial registrations at the same rate as diagnostic span bodies, any trial that times out, crashes a worker, or drops its spans disappears from the evaluation denominator, biasing pass rates toward fast, easy runs. Beyond the recovery history and diagnostic traces separated in perspective [14.1](https://mlsysbook.ai/vol3/14_agent_operations/14_agent_operations.html#psp-13-telemetry-duality), evaluation requires a third artifact: the *accounting record*. This record catalogs every admitted trial, its exact environment configuration, evaluation criteria, current verdict status, measured resource expenditures, and missing telemetry markers. While these three records can reside on shared physical storage, sampling policies must apply exclusively to diagnostic detail. Telemetry systems must never sample recovery history or the evaluation denominator.

Diagnostic detail also expands substantially faster than the underlying trajectory when spans mirror raw message history. If each model-call span replicates the entire conversation context sent to the provider, diagnostic trace volume inherits the quadratic resend pattern formalized in **[[01 Books/Volume III - Agentic Systems/01 The Model/02 The Foundation Model/V3-02.09 The Cost of a Model Call#^eq-vol3-trajectory-resend|equation 2.9]]**. Telemetry systems avoid this runaway expansion by referencing immutable conversation prefixes via cryptographic content digests and storing each new tool observation exactly once. This content-addressable deduplication keeps storage growth linear in the number of turns, provided the underlying blob store retains the referenced content for the lifetime of the trace.

**Head sampling** decides whether to retain detailed diagnostic traces at the initial entry point of execution. Drawing a random sample with recorded inclusion probabilities allows engineers to compute unbiased population estimates for metrics such as mean latency or aggregate token consumption. However, uniform head sampling struggles with rare failure modes, as an intermittent defect can easily slip through without leaving a single retained trace.

Because head sampling decides retention before execution outcomes are known, each occurrence of a failure mode is retained independently with sampling probability ${\theta}$. Consequently, a failure mode that occurs ${n_{f}}$ times leaves no detailed diagnostic trace with probability $(1-{\theta})^{{n_{f}}}$. At ${\theta} =$ 10 percent, a failure mode that occurs 5 times carries roughly a 59 percent probability of leaving zero detailed examples in storage. Only when that mode recurs 50 times does the probability of a complete miss fall to 0.5 percent. By its mathematical nature, uniform sampling is least likely to preserve the rare, anomalous executions that engineers most urgently need to inspect.

To capture these elusive events without retaining every routine execution, **tail sampling** defers the retention decision until operational evidence accumulates downstream, such as a failed acceptance assertion, an unhandled tool exception, an unexpected human intervention, or an anomalous latency spike. Tail selection deliberately concentrates diagnostic storage on high-value, problematic runs, producing a trace collection that is intentionally unrepresentative of normal production traffic. Engineers must therefore compute population failure rates from the complete accounting record rather than from the tail-sampled trace collection. Whenever teams derive statistical estimates from sampled records, the telemetry infrastructure must record the exact inclusion probabilities alongside the active selection policy version.[[01 Books/Volume III - Agentic Systems/04 The Agent Runtime/14 Operating Agents/V3-14.13 Footnotes#^fn3|³]] Diagnostic workflows also require a baseline for comparison. The pipeline must retain a known random sample of ordinary, accepted trials as a control group, because an isolated failure trace cannot reveal which intermediate steps were anomalous and which were routine.

### Tail Sampling and Verdict Windows

^sec-vol3-evaluation-budgets-tail-sampling

If a tail-sampling buffer evicts trace bodies before a slow regression suite finishes grading `ws_r19`, the accounting record logs the late failure while the spans needed to explain it are already gone. Because tail selection spans multiple operational stages, an execution trace is held provisionally in a staging buffer while acceptance checks run, receiving its final retention classification only when conclusive evaluation evidence arrives within a declared verdict window. When the staging buffer expires before an asynchronous failure is reported, the accounting record must explicitly flag the missing diagnostic detail so audit integrity holds. [[01 Books/Volume III - Agentic Systems/04 The Agent Runtime/14 Operating Agents/V3-14.04 Trace Retention and Privacy#^tbl-vol3-tail-sampling-policies|Table 3]] outlines this tiered retention policy, establishing a strict precedence order so that overlapping classification rules do not count or persist a single trace multiple times.

| Evidence at the decision boundary                                | Possible detailed-trace policy                                           | Limit to record                                                                                              |
|:-----------------------------------------------------------------|:-------------------------------------------------------------------------|:-------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------|
| Failed task criterion or enforced-policy violation               | Give high retention priority, with the criterion and supporting evidence | A process error does not inherently prove whole-task failure, and buffer saturation may still force eviction |
| Pending or unknown task verdict                                  | Retain provisionally or under an explicit unresolved-case sample         | State the deadline, missing evidence, and what happens when the window expires                               |
| Accepted but unusually slow, costly, or intervention-heavy trial | Retain under declared diagnostic thresholds                              | Selection enriches those behaviors and does not establish their population frequency                         |
| Ordinary accepted trial                                          | Retain a known random control sample                                     | Preserve its inclusion probability and distinguish unsampled from lost                                       |

Table 3: **Verdict-aware trace selection**: Selection uses declared task criteria and available evidence, including delayed evaluation outcomes. Successful process completion does not imply task acceptance.

^tbl-vol3-tail-sampling-policies

*Eviction priority*: When ring buffers experience memory pressure, eviction policies must shed routine successes and unclassified traces before dropping runs flagged for policy violations or failed assertions. While differential eviction priority substantially improves diagnostic survival rates, extreme burst traffic can still cause trace loss.

Physical buffer limits inevitably govern this architecture. Buffer managers must enforce four concurrent bounds: active trace count, aggregate byte volume, individual trace size limits, and maximum staging age. Sizing from averages, such as mean concurrent traces multiplied by mean trace size, is a frequent engineering trap, because cascading failures typically produce bursts of unusually long, tool-heavy trajectories that quickly exhaust buffer memory. Telemetry systems must expose real-time metrics for head-dropped records, buffer evictions, and span truncations broken down by cohort and selection class. Without this visibility, correlated loss during incidents will silently purge the exact failure traces engineers need to diagnose system outages. Furthermore, while deferred tail selection curtails long-term downstream storage, it does not reduce the network ingress and memory needed to buffer traces prior to selection, and strict privacy restrictions apply to these staging buffers as well. The accounting record therefore sits outside the buffer ([[01 Books/Volume III - Agentic Systems/04 The Agent Runtime/14 Operating Agents/V3-14.04 Trace Retention and Privacy#^fig-vol3-tail-sampling-pipeline|figure 2]]). Every admitted trial enters it directly, together with every recorded drop, eviction, and truncation, so the denominator survives whatever the buffer loses.

Figure 2: **Verdict-Aware Trace Selection**: Trace bodies pass a fallible minimization boundary into a bounded buffer that holds them until task verdicts, which can arrive late, reach the selector. Selection applies a declared precedence among the classes of [[01 Books/Volume III - Agentic Systems/04 The Agent Runtime/14 Operating Agents/V3-14.04 Trace Retention and Privacy#^tbl-vol3-tail-sampling-policies|table 3]], keeps inclusion probabilities with retained traces, and discards unselected bodies. Every admitted trial also enters the accounting record directly, together with recorded drops, evictions, and truncation, so the denominator does not depend on which bodies survived. Assigning high retention priority protects failure traces under moderate load, though severe buffer saturation can still force evictions before verdicts arrive.

**Napkin Math 14.2: What verdict-aware selection saves**

**Problem**: How much downstream diagnostic storage do uniform head sampling and verdict-aware tail selection use for the same hypothetical workload?

**Assumptions**: Each hour brings 10,000 trials with equal 1.4 MB permitted trace bodies. At the selection boundary, 400 have failed criteria, 100 remain pending or unknown, and 9,500 are ordinary accepted trials. These classes are disjoint, repeat for 24 hours, and all survive collection. Head selection samples each trial with probability 5 percent. Tail selection retains the failed and unresolved classes and independently samples 2 percent of ordinary accepted trials.

**Math**: Keeping every body requires 336 GB over the stated period. Head sampling retains an expected 500 traces per hour, or 16.8 GB over the stated period. It includes an expected 20 failed and 5 unresolved trials per hour, not guaranteed counts. Tail selection retains 400 failed, 100 unresolved, and an expected 190 ordinary accepted trials: 690 traces per hour and 23.184 GB over the stated period.

**Result**: Full retention uses about 14.5× the downstream bytes of the tail policy. Tail selection consumes more capacity than uniform head sampling in this scenario, but it guarantees diagnostic visibility for every failed and unresolved execution rather than retaining only an unpredictable handful of anomalous runs.

**Systems insight**: Retained volume is governed by two terms. The first is an inelastic storage floor determined by the failure-plus-unresolved rate (16.8 GB per day in this workload). The second is an adjustable overhead determined by the control sampling fraction on routine successes, where each percentage point of control sampling adds 3.2 GB per day. Capacity planning must size the baseline floor to accommodate elevated failure rates during service disruptions while dialing the control rate to fit available long-term storage budgets. These calculations reflect decimal gigabytes for raw trace payloads; indexing metadata, replication factors, accounting records, and upstream ingestion buffers impose additional overhead.

### Disclosure Minimization

^sec-vol3-evaluation-budgets-disclosure

Dumping raw model prompts and unredacted tool responses directly into a shared tracing backend turns the observability pipeline into a secondary data leak, spreading customer records, database keys, and proprietary source snippets across search indexes and crash logs where retroactive scrubbing cannot reach them. Diagnostic investigations rarely require wholesale ingestion of raw prompts and complete tool outputs; structured metadata (typed return codes, schema versions, sub-span latencies, policy decisions, and hashed artifact pointers) often provides complete debugging context without exposing sensitive user data. Even when credentials and API tokens are cleanly scrubbed, tool arguments and responses frequently contain private records or personal identifiers. Authorization policies must therefore govern specific data attributes and restrict access by investigator role, rather than granting blanket read access to an entire telemetry tier. Data minimization belongs at the trusted capture boundary inside the execution runtime, before unscrubbed buffers enter network queues, ingestion daemons, or alerting systems.

Automated redaction mitigates disclosure risks, but systems designers must treat every scrubbing mechanism as inherently fallible. Regular expressions miss non-standard or obfuscated secret patterns. Shannon entropy detectors flag UUIDs and cryptographic nonces while missing predictable credentials. Named-entity recognition models err, skipping private names or stripping valid operational syntax required to diagnose a tool error. Because heuristic sanitizers cannot guarantee zero disclosure across arbitrary payloads, architectural schema design must prioritize structural field exclusion over text redaction, so that unnecessary fields are never captured in the first place. For retained fields that undergo transformation, pipelines must validate sanitization rules against curated benchmark suites containing real-world sensitive patterns. When downstream diagnosis requires correlating multiple actions by the same entity without revealing real-world identities, telemetry systems should replace identifiers with keyed cryptographic pseudonyms. Generating these pseudonyms via a hash-based message authentication code (HMAC) requires a securely managed secret key rather than a public salt ([[01 Books/Volume III - Agentic Systems/04 The Agent Runtime/14 Operating Agents/V3-14.12 Source References#^ref-krawczyk1997hmac|Krawczyk et al. 1997, sec. 2]]). However, engineers must recognize that persistent pseudonyms preserve relational linkability across traces. Regular key rotation limits longitudinal correlation across evaluation epochs, at the cost of diagnoses that must follow one entity across several weeks. Pseudonymization is not complete anonymization, nor does it justify indefinite trace retention.

### Replay Fidelity After Redaction

^sec-vol3-evaluation-budgets-replay-fidelity

Stripping a sensitive database column from a tool response can conceal the exact formatting defect that triggered a model’s parsing error, and replacing an address string with a placeholder or pseudonym can shift sub-word token boundaries and steer the model down an entirely different decoding path during re-execution. Because scrubbing or masking input tokens alters the precise byte sequences that drove agent behavior, sanitization creates an inherent tension between privacy enforcement and replay fidelity: a redacted trace cannot serve as a bit-exact substitute for the environment interactions that actually occurred. When forensic investigations require exact replay and compliance policies permit it, systems must isolate raw execution payloads in a dedicated, access-restricted artifact repository, completely separate from general diagnostic telemetry. The diagnostic trace then stores only cryptographic hashes pointing to these secured blobs, alongside metadata recording the exact transformation pipeline used to generate the sanitized view. When raw payloads expire under data-retention schedules or cannot be kept due to privacy constraints, teams must formally narrow the scope of their diagnostic claims. Replaying an agent against synthetic mock fixtures validates whether the model handles a class of problems in a fresh run, but it does not prove what occurred during the original production execution.

**Checkpoint 14.2: Retained evidence and supported conclusions**

**Buffer expiration**: A broader acceptance check fails after the trace buffer expires. What can the accounting record establish, and what can it no longer provide for diagnosis?

**Tail-sampling bias**: Why does the failure fraction in a tail-selected collection not estimate the population failure rate without an appropriate inclusion design?

**Head-sampling estimate**: Uniform head sampling keeps 5 percent of traces, and a failure mode has occurred 20 times. Estimate the chance that no detailed trace of it survives, and decide whether verdict-aware selection is worth its extra storage for this mode.

**Sanitized replay**: A sanitized observation is available but its original protected artifact has expired. Which replay claim must be narrowed, and why is its digest insufficient?

Storage budgets determine which runtime observations survive, while privacy policies govern which preserved artifacts an investigator may legally inspect. Every postmortem analysis and regression benchmark operates within the boundary those two constraints set.
