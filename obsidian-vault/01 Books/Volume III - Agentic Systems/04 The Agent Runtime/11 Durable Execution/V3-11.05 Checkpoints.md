# V3-11.05 Checkpoints

[[Home|Home]] · [[01 Books/Volume III - Agentic Systems/Volume 3 Overview|Volume Overview]] · [[01 Books/Volume III - Agentic Systems/04 The Agent Runtime/11 Durable Execution/V3-11 Durable Execution|V3-11 Durable Execution]]

^sec-vol3-persistence-checkpointing

A durable intent informs a replacement worker that an operation such as the `r18` test may have begun, but it does not store the files that the test actually touched. An execution log stating that an edit succeeded cannot restore modified bytes unless those bytes, or reconstructible deltas against a known base, remain preserved in storage. Durable execution therefore requires the control record and the mutable workspace to share a common recovery boundary.

A **checkpoint** binds a recoverable record snapshot, its associated workspace files, and all required external artifacts to an exact, committed log position. Checkpointing accelerates recovery by replacing an unbounded prefix of historical events with a single compatible snapshot. This architectural checkpoint differs from the compact progress summary used to assemble prompt context: an LLM prompt summary cannot reconstruct raw filesystem bytes, prove resource liabilities, or track uncommitted external operations.

### Consistent Recovery Boundaries

^sec-vol3-durable-execution-recovery-boundaries

If a runtime snapshots the control record at one instant and copies the workspace directory a few seconds later while a tool is writing files, restoring the pair produces a torn state. In a torn state, the record refers to file bytes that the snapshot lacks, or the reverse.[[01 Books/Volume III - Agentic Systems/04 The Agent Runtime/11 Durable Execution/V3-11.15 Footnotes#^fn5|⁵]] A checkpoint must therefore freeze both halves at an authoritative log position ${j_{\text{ckpt}}}$, mirroring the fold boundary ${j}$, rather than at a loose model turn. A checkpoint manifest serves as the formal contract for this boundary. It binds ${j_{\text{ckpt}}}$ to the record snapshot, the workspace snapshot, references to external artifacts, cryptographic hashes for integrity, and the reducer schema versions required to interpret them. The control record snapshot captures cumulative resource consumption, active budget reservations, approval states, and in-flight operations with their identities and settlement owners. These obligations persist in the record even while a crashed agent awaits replacement.

Two capture designs keep the record and the filesystem consistent at ${j_{\text{ckpt}}}$. One design pauses workspace mutations, drains in-flight disk writes, and captures the record and filesystem together at a quiet boundary. An alternative design stores workspace states as content-addressed, immutable trees, each named by a hash of its bytes ([[01 Books/Volume III - Agentic Systems/04 The Agent Runtime/11 Durable Execution/V3-11.09 Log Retention and State Compaction#^sec-vol3-durable-execution-payload-storage|section 11.8.2]]), and maps each versioned root directly to a log boundary. External tasks may remain in flight during capture under either design, provided the checkpoint explicitly records their unresolved status and idempotency tokens.

Snapshot publication must strictly follow data availability. The runtime first captures the consistent state and flushes all underlying bytes to durable storage within the failure domain. Only after those bytes are durable does the runtime commit the manifest or append a `Checkpoint` event to the authoritative log, marking the snapshot eligible for recovery. If a host crashes during capture, the recovery supervisor ignores the incomplete snapshot and falls back to the previous published checkpoint and its subsequent log suffix. Background cleanup can safely reclaim unreferenced staging files, provided garbage collection protects any artifacts still referenced by active recovery paths. Operating systems and storage engines frequently offer copy-on-write snapshots (file-system subvolume snapshots or process-level memory forks) to freeze point-in-time state with near-zero latency; however, serializing and offloading those blocks to independent durable storage remains a necessary second phase.

The publication order in [[01 Books/Volume III - Agentic Systems/04 The Agent Runtime/11 Durable Execution/V3-11.05 Checkpoints#^fig-vol3-checkpoint-consistent-cut|figure 3]] makes the recovery boundary explicit. A compatible pair becomes eligible only after its payload bytes are durable and its manifest has entered the authoritative history.

![[90 Assets/Volume III/11 Durable Execution/Checkpoint Consistent Cut e505b5c776.svg]]

Figure 3: **A Recoverable Checkpoint Cut**: The record and workspace on the left refer to the same committed boundary. Payload durability precedes manifest publication; restoration then folds the recorded suffix while preserving unresolved operations and their reservations. The right-hand pair is invalid because the newer record refers to bytes absent from the older workspace. Recovery must reject that pair and use a compatible published checkpoint with an available suffix.

^fig-vol3-checkpoint-consistent-cut

An unpublished capture does not advance the recovery boundary, even if some of its files survived the crash. Likewise, a published control position cannot substitute for a missing workspace payload. Recovery needs both the committed reference and the bytes it identifies.

Restoration proceeds deterministically. The replacement supervisor first verifies the integrity hashes in the manifest, ensures all referenced artifacts are accessible, and fetches the authoritative log suffix spanning from ${j_{\text{ckpt}}}$ to the desired recovery target ${j_{\text{target}}}$. It unpacks the record and workspace snapshots at ${j_{\text{ckpt}}}$, replays the intervening control events through the state reducer, and applies recorded workspace patches up to ${j_{\text{target}}}$. This reconstruction step invokes neither the foundation model nor the original tool implementations. Instead, it writes preserved byte streams into the worker’s isolated workspace, reinstating identical state without re-executing nondeterministic actions.

Consider the crash worksheet scenario: the checkpoint at ${j_{\text{ckpt}}}$ binds the record snapshot `record_R_ckpt` to workspace snapshot `ws_r18`. The subsequent log suffix captures the model prompt and response, token budget reservations, and the durable intent for operation `test_r18` under `attempt1`. Suppose the host crashes while the test executes, before any execution result reaches disk. Because the test executed against an immutable snapshot using isolated scratch storage, the main candidate workspace experienced zero mutations during this suffix. To recover, the replacement worker restores `ws_r18` and folds the suffix events. This reconstructs the pending test obligation and its outstanding budget reservation, recognizing that the operation remains in doubt. Any intermediate scratch data or uncommitted output artifacts must either possess durable references or be marked unavailable.

When an execution suffix modifies workspace files, durable recovery requires comprehensive delta tracking: the base snapshot identifier, ordered byte replacements or unified diffs, file deletions, metadata flags, and SHA-256 integrity checksums. A success return code or a dangling content hash whose backing payload was lost to a crash cannot reconstruct state. If the control log points to a workspace revision whose delta payload is missing from storage, the runtime must fail fast and flag the state as unrecoverable, rather than erroneously pairing a forward log position with an obsolete directory tree. Recomputing missing workspace artifacts constitutes new nondeterministic execution, which falls under admission and repair policy rather than reconstruction.

↪ Whether to repair a failed step or rebuild its state, and the admission check every recovery action must pass first, are developed in **[[01 Books/Volume III - Agentic Systems/04 The Agent Runtime/12 Failure Recovery/V3-12.05 Forward Recovery#^sec-vol3-sagas-forward-vs-backward|Forward Recovery]]**.

### Full and Incremental Snapshots

^sec-vol3-durable-execution-snapshot-representation

As an agent writes workspace files, adds operation-table entries, and retains observations turn by turn, copying the entire state at every checkpoint rewrites every previously saved byte. Architects can avoid that repeated copying by storing incremental delta chains instead of full snapshots, satisfying the same recovery contract while trading storage write bandwidth against reconstruction latency. Under full snapshots, restoration latency scales with total state size; under incremental deltas, it scales with the length of the delta chain.

Write volume separates the two representations when checkpointed state accumulates monotonically. What grows across turns is not the log itself, which already appends each turn’s events once, but the state those events produced. A full snapshot re-serializes all of that accumulated state at every checkpoint. A delta, in contrast, holds only the workspace bytes and record fields that changed since the previous checkpoint, which is a different object from the event suffix the reducer folds. Suppose state grows by ${s_{\text{turn}}}$ bytes per turn and the runtime writes a checkpoint every ${k}$ turns. The ${m}$th full snapshot must re-serialize all ${m} \cdot {s_{\text{turn}}} \cdot {k}$ accumulated bytes. Over a trajectory requiring ${N_{\text{ckpt}}}$ checkpoints, the cumulative write volumes are:

$$
{D_{\text{vol,full}}} = \sum_{{m}=1}^{{N_{\text{ckpt}}}} {m}\,{s_{\text{turn}}}\,{k} = {s_{\text{turn}}}\,{k}\,\frac{{N_{\text{ckpt}}}({N_{\text{ckpt}}}+1)}{2}, \qquad {D_{\text{vol,delta}}} = {s_{\text{turn}}}\,{k}\,{N_{\text{ckpt}}}. \tag{3}
$$

^eq-vol3-snapshot-bytes

As [[01 Books/Volume III - Agentic Systems/04 The Agent Runtime/11 Durable Execution/V3-11.05 Checkpoints#^eq-vol3-snapshot-bytes|equation 3]] illustrates, full snapshots cause write volume to scale quadratically with trajectory length, whereas incremental deltas scale linearly. The ratio between them, $({N_{\text{ckpt}}}+1)/2$, represents the **write amplification** penalty incurred by full snapshots, which spend storage I/O by re-serializing previously checkpointed state at every boundary.

![[90 Assets/Volume III/11 Durable Execution/Vol3 Durable Margin 002 ac9c8d9aa7.svg]]

*Two curves of bytes written against the number of checkpoints. A purple curve labeled full bends upward steeply. A gray line labeled delta rises slowly and stays near the horizontal axis.*

*Full snapshots rewrite old state, so their bytes grow quadratically.*

For a 500-turn trajectory whose state expands by 128 KB per turn, checkpointing every 5 turns produces 100 checkpoints. Full snapshots write 3.2 GB over the run, whereas incremental deltas write only 64 MB, a 50.5-fold reduction in write volume. The saving moves cost onto the recovery path. Instead of loading a single flat image, restoration must fetch, validate, and replay the sequential delta chain, so the choice turns on what each representation needs to survive and which capture and reconstruction costs must be measured ([[01 Books/Volume III - Agentic Systems/04 The Agent Runtime/11 Durable Execution/V3-11.05 Checkpoints#^tbl-vol3-snapshot-models|table 4]]).

| Choice               | What must remain available                                                     | Cost to measure                                                                   |
|:---------------------|:-------------------------------------------------------------------------------|:----------------------------------------------------------------------------------|
| Full snapshot        | The complete selected record/workspace state and required referenced artifacts | Capture pause, bytes written, transfer and restore time for the actual state size |
| Incremental snapshot | A compatible base, every required ordered delta, and referenced artifacts      | Capture and write savings vs. chain retrieval, validation, and application cost   |

Table 4: **Checkpoint Representation Trade-offs**: Compare measured capture and reconstruction costs under the same failure domain and retained-state promise; storage sharing alone establishes neither durability nor isolation.

^tbl-vol3-snapshot-models

Periodic consolidation can merge an extended delta chain back into a new base snapshot, but the garbage collector must verify that the new base is durably published before retiring earlier deltas. Snapshot retention policies also hinge on audit requirements: while restoring an active agent only requires the latest base and its subsequent deltas, compliance or post-hoc debugging may mandate retaining older historical snapshots across the entire trajectory.

### Checkpoint Cadence

^sec-vol3-durable-execution-checkpoint-cadence

Checkpointing after every turn pauses the trajectory on every step to copy state that the log already recorded, whereas never checkpointing forces a recovering worker to replay an entire run’s suffix or recompute unlogged work from scratch. Selecting a checkpoint cadence balances that snapshot pause against the time required to reconstruct the suffix after a crash. When logged events and workspace deltas survive host failure, replaying that suffix incurs only the modest cost of reading, validating, folding, and applying recorded mutations, with no foundation-model inference. The agent resumes dispatching new model calls only after state reconstruction and guarded resume succeed.

Classical checkpointing models address a harsher failure regime: one where a crash obliterates all in-flight work, forcing the system to recompute from the last checkpoint.[[01 Books/Volume III - Agentic Systems/04 The Agent Runtime/11 Durable Execution/V3-11.15 Footnotes#^fn6|⁶]] In agentic execution, this regime governs state the log does not capture. A long local analysis step that keeps intermediate results in memory, or workspace writes that bypass the write-ahead log, must be recomputed from the last checkpoint after a crash. An in-flight tool or model call is a different case that no cadence can help, because recovery treats it as in doubt rather than as lost work. Under Poisson failure assumptions, each crash discards an average of half a checkpoint interval of execution. Balancing that expected loss against the pause overhead of writing snapshots determines the classical checkpoint interval.

**Theorem 11.1: Optimal checkpoint interval (Young's approximation)**

*A checkpoint taken too often wastes pauses; one taken too rarely wastes re-execution after each failure.* Assume that failures are rare, Poisson, and independent of progress, that each failure loses on average half an interval of useful work, and that the pause is small relative to the interval. Under these assumptions, **Young’s approximation** ([[01 Books/Volume III - Agentic Systems/04 The Agent Runtime/11 Durable Execution/V3-11.14 Source References#^ref-young1974|Young 1974]]) gives the useful-work interval between checkpoints that minimizes expected overhead:

$$
{T^\ast_{\text{ckpt}}} \approx \sqrt{2\,{T_{\text{write}}}\,{\text{MTBF}}} \tag{4}
$$

^eq-vol3-young-interval

- **Checkpoint write time** (${T_{\text{write}}}$): Time execution stops to capture and durably publish one checkpoint.
- **Mean time between failures** (${\text{MTBF}}$): Expected time between crashes that lose unrecorded work.
- **Overhead at the optimum**: The cadence-dependent overhead is $\sqrt{2\,{T_{\text{write}}}/{\text{MTBF}}}$ of useful time. **[[05 Reference/Volume III - Agentic Systems/V3 Mathematical Foundations#^sec-vol3-appendix-math-daly|Checkpoint lost-work balance]]** derives the interval, from which this overhead follows, and Daly ([[01 Books/Volume III - Agentic Systems/04 The Agent Runtime/11 Durable Execution/V3-11.14 Source References#^ref-daly2006|2006]]) refines both for failures during saving and recovery.
- **Sensitivity**: For a 2 s pause and a mean time between failures of 10 minutes, ${T^\ast_{\text{ckpt}}} \approx$ 49 s and the overhead is 8.2 percent. The minimum is flat: an interval off by a factor of 2 in either direction raises overhead by only 25 percent, while a factor-of-10 miss raises it to 41 percent.

Appending events to a persistent trajectory log alters this trade-off by reducing the lost-work penalty without changing the snapshot pause. In distributed systems literature, pairing periodic snapshots with an append-only log of nondeterministic inputs is termed log-based rollback recovery ([[01 Books/Volume III - Agentic Systems/04 The Agent Runtime/11 Durable Execution/V3-11.14 Source References#^ref-elnozahy2002survey|Elnozahy et al. 2002]]).[[01 Books/Volume III - Agentic Systems/04 The Agent Runtime/11 Durable Execution/V3-11.15 Footnotes#^fn7|⁷]] When an agent durably records model outputs and workspace diffs turn by turn, recovery costs only the time to replay and validate those entries rather than re-executing inference. Substituting this smaller reconstruction cost into the same balance lengthens the best interval, as the derivation below shows. Only work whose state the log does not capture, such as unlogged workspace writes or in-memory analysis, still pays the full re-execution penalty that [[01 Books/Volume III - Agentic Systems/04 The Agent Runtime/11 Durable Execution/V3-11.05 Checkpoints#^eq-vol3-young-interval|equation 4]] prices.

![[90 Assets/Volume III/11 Durable Execution/Vol3 Durable Margin 003 83b1d6218b.svg]]

*A U-shaped purple curve of overhead against checkpoint interval on a logarithmic axis, with a dot at its minimum labeled T star, a shaded band around the minimum labeled flat, and the axis ends labeled too often and too rarely.*

*Overhead is flat near the optimum interval and steep far from it.*

Counted in turns rather than seconds, the logged balance has the same shape as Young’s. Let a checkpoint every ${k}$ turns pause execution for ${T_{\text{write}}}$, as in [[01 Books/Volume III - Agentic Systems/04 The Agent Runtime/11 Durable Execution/V3-11.05 Checkpoints#^eq-vol3-young-interval|equation 4]]. Let crashes arrive at failure rate ${\lambda_{\text{fail}}}$ per completed turn. Let ${T_{\text{recon}}}$ be the time to reconstruct one logged turn of the suffix (reading, validating, folding, and applying its records). Per completed turn, checkpointing costs ${T_{\text{write}}}/{k}$. A crash finds on average ${k}/2$ turns past the last checkpoint, so expected reconstruction costs ${\lambda_{\text{fail}}}\,{T_{\text{recon}}}\,{k}/2$ per turn. Setting the derivative of the sum to zero gives the logged stride

$$
{k}^* = \sqrt{\frac{2\,{T_{\text{write}}}}{{\lambda_{\text{fail}}}\,{T_{\text{recon}}}}}. \tag{5}
$$

^eq-vol3-logged-stride

Repeating the derivation with ${T_{\text{exec}}}$, the cost of re-executing an unlogged turn’s model calls and tools, in place of ${T_{\text{recon}}}$ recovers Young’s regime. Logging therefore lengthens the best stride by $\sqrt{{T_{\text{exec}}}/{T_{\text{recon}}}}$. The cost ratio itself is large, because a re-executed turn samples the model again while a reconstructed one only reads records, but the stride grows only with its square root.

An averaged balance hides how a particular failure trace spends its time, so a design is checked against traces as well. Let ${N}_k$ denote the number of checkpoints taken at stride ${k}$, ${N_{\text{crash}}}$ the number of crashes in the trace, and ${T_{\text{restore}}}$ the fixed restore latency per crash. If crash ${i}$ leaves ${H_{\text{suffix},i}}({k})$ completed turns beyond the most recent checkpoint, the total time lost to snapshotting and recovery is

$$
{T_{\text{overhead}}}({k}) = {N}_k {T_{\text{write}}} + {N_{\text{crash}}} {T_{\text{restore}}} + {T_{\text{recon}}}\sum_i {H_{\text{suffix},i}}({k}), \tag{6}
$$

^eq-vol3-checkpoint-overhead

with every term in seconds. Charging reconstruction per turn simplifies the model, though in production one turn’s suffix may span several fine-grained log records.

**Napkin Math 11.1: Checkpoint cadence on a fixed crash trace**

**Problem**: A hypothetical trajectory completes 100 turns, with crashes after turns 37 and 83. Each published checkpoint pauses execution for 0.5 s. Each crash costs 1 s to restore the checkpoint plus 0.1 s per turn of recorded events and workspace changes since it. An initial checkpoint exists, every required suffix survives, and each policy also checkpoints at the final turn; only capture and reconstruction time count. Which stride, 10, 25, or 50 turns, spends the least time on this trace?

**Math**: The 10-turn policy publishes 10 checkpoints and reconstructs suffixes of 7 and 3 turns. The 25-turn policy publishes 4 checkpoints and reconstructs suffixes of 12 and 8 turns. The 50-turn policy publishes 2 checkpoints and reconstructs suffixes of 37 and 33 turns. Reconstruction totals are 3 s, 4 s, and 9 s; adding capture pauses gives ${T_{\text{overhead}}}({k})$ of 8 s, 6 s, and 10 s ([[01 Books/Volume III - Agentic Systems/04 The Agent Runtime/11 Durable Execution/V3-11.05 Checkpoints#^eq-vol3-checkpoint-overhead|equation 6]]). The trace’s failure rate is ${\lambda_{\text{fail}}} =$ 0.02 crashes per turn, so the logged balance ([[01 Books/Volume III - Agentic Systems/04 The Agent Runtime/11 Durable Execution/V3-11.05 Checkpoints#^eq-vol3-logged-stride|equation 5]]) gives ${k}^* = \sqrt{2{T_{\text{write}}}/({\lambda_{\text{fail}}} {T_{\text{recon}}})} \approx$ 22 turns. If each lost turn instead had to be re-executed at ${T_{\text{exec}}}$ = 20 s, the same balance gives ${k}^* \approx$ 1.6 turns.

**Result**: The 25-turn stride wins on this trace, at 6 s, next to the balance point of about 22 turns.

**Systems insight**: Logging each turn’s events and workspace changes lengthens the best stride by $\sqrt{{T_{\text{exec}}}/{T_{\text{recon}}}}$, about 14.1× here, because a crash then costs reconstruction instead of repeated model calls. The stride moves with the square root of each knob: quadrupling the capture pause doubles it, and so does quartering the crash rate. Because the optimum is flat (theorem [11.1](https://mlsysbook.ai/vol3/11_durable_execution/11_durable_execution.html#thrm-11-young-checkpoint-interval)), a stride within a factor of 2 of the balance point costs at most 25 percent extra overhead. Measure ${T_{\text{write}}}$, ${T_{\text{recon}}}$, and the crash rate from fault-injection traces before tuning more finely.

Operational transitions such as waiting for human approval, compacting execution context, or performing a zero-downtime worker handoff justify taking an opportunistic, out-of-cadence checkpoint if doing so truncates pending recovery chains or enables log pruning. If the record and workspace artifacts are already durable and recoverable, these boundary events require no extra snapshot. Checkpoint cadence remains an efficiency trade-off, whereas intent ordering is an absolute correctness invariant that no cadence optimization can relax.

**Checkpoint 11.2: A checkpoint must match its suffix**

**Boundary**: A record reconstructed through ${j_{\text{target}}}$ is paired with workspace bytes from ${j_{\text{ckpt}}}$, and the suffix changed a file. Decide whether the runtime may resume, and name the data that would make the pair consistent.

**Publication**: If capture fails before the manifest is durable, which checkpoint and suffix must remain recoverable, and which new artifacts may cleanup delete?

**Cadence estimate**: A faster reducer cuts per-turn reconstruction cost fourfold while the capture pause and crash rate stay fixed. By what factor should the stride change, and roughly how much extra overhead does keeping the old stride cost?

A checkpoint and its suffix bring the worker’s internal record and workspace state up to date, but recovery does not automatically grant authority to resume external execution. The replacement worker must first establish exclusive lease ownership, reconcile pending liabilities, and verify fencing tokens before dispatching new external side effects.
