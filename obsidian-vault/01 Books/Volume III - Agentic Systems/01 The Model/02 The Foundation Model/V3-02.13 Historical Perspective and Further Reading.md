# V3-02.13 Historical Perspective and Further Reading

[[Home|Home]] · [[01 Books/Volume III - Agentic Systems/Volume 3 Overview|Volume Overview]] · [[01 Books/Volume III - Agentic Systems/01 The Model/02 The Foundation Model/V3-02 The Foundation Model|V3-02 The Foundation Model]]

^sec-vol3-foundation-model-history

The foundation model call joins two intellectual lineages that developed independently for decades. The first is statistical language modeling. Shannon ([[01 Books/Volume III - Agentic Systems/01 The Model/02 The Foundation Model/V3-02.15 Source References#^ref-shannon1948|1948]]) framed natural language as a discrete stochastic process, treating English as a sequence of symbols drawn from conditional probability distributions and generating text from successively longer context windows. Bengio et al. ([[01 Books/Volume III - Agentic Systems/01 The Model/02 The Foundation Model/V3-02.15 Source References#^ref-bengio2003neural|2003]]) replaced high-dimensional count tables with a continuous neural function over distributed word representations, establishing the autoregressive next-token factorization that modern architectures still use. The Transformer made that formulation parallelizable across token positions during training ([[01 Books/Volume III - Agentic Systems/01 The Model/02 The Foundation Model/V3-02.15 Source References#^ref-vaswani2017|Vaswani et al. 2017]]). In production serving, that same architectural choice creates an operational split: an initial prompt prefill phase often limited by arithmetic throughput, followed by a small-batch autoregressive generation loop often limited by memory bandwidth ([[01 Books/Volume III - Agentic Systems/01 The Model/02 The Foundation Model/V3-02.15 Source References#^ref-pope2023efficiently|Pope et al. 2023]]). Subword tokenization algorithms borrowed from data compression solved the out-of-vocabulary dilemma ([[01 Books/Volume III - Agentic Systems/01 The Model/02 The Foundation Model/V3-02.15 Source References#^ref-gage1994bpe|Gage 1994]]; [[01 Books/Volume III - Agentic Systems/01 The Model/02 The Foundation Model/V3-02.15 Source References#^ref-sennrich2016subword|Sennrich et al. 2016]]), and scaling laws subsequently produced models capable of task execution directly from natural language prompts ([[01 Books/Volume III - Agentic Systems/01 The Model/02 The Foundation Model/V3-02.15 Source References#^ref-brown2020language|Brown et al. 2020]]). Bommasani et al. ([[01 Books/Volume III - Agentic Systems/01 The Model/02 The Foundation Model/V3-02.15 Source References#^ref-bommasani2021opportunities|2021]]) formalized the concept of foundation models while warning of their central systems risk: when hundreds of applications build on a single upstream base model, any flaw, bias, or failure mode in that model propagates throughout the entire software stack.

Decoding strategies evolved alongside model scale to mitigate systematic failure modes in generation. Early inference relied on greedy search or beam search to maximize likelihood, but these objectives frequently trapped models in bland, repetitive loops. Truncated sampling addressed this degeneration by restricting candidates to the head of the output distribution, first through a fixed token count in top-${k}$ sampling ([[01 Books/Volume III - Agentic Systems/01 The Model/02 The Foundation Model/V3-02.15 Source References#^ref-fan2018hierarchical|Fan et al. 2018]]) and subsequently through top-${p}$ (nucleus) sampling, which adapts the truncation boundary dynamically to the distribution’s cumulative probability mass ([[01 Books/Volume III - Agentic Systems/01 The Model/02 The Foundation Model/V3-02.15 Source References#^ref-holtzman2020curious|Holtzman et al. 2020]]). Speculative decoding decoupled generation latency from model parameter scale by using a lightweight draft model to generate token candidates that the larger target model verifies in parallel ([[01 Books/Volume III - Agentic Systems/01 The Model/02 The Foundation Model/V3-02.15 Source References#^ref-leviathan2023fast|Leviathan et al. 2023]]; [[01 Books/Volume III - Agentic Systems/01 The Model/02 The Foundation Model/V3-02.15 Source References#^ref-chen2023accelerating|Chen et al. 2023]]). At the infrastructure level, serving engines moved beyond rigid static batching: Orca introduced iteration-level scheduling to interleave prefill and decode phases dynamically ([[01 Books/Volume III - Agentic Systems/01 The Model/02 The Foundation Model/V3-02.15 Source References#^ref-yu2022orca|Yu et al. 2022]]), while SGLang demonstrated how prefix caching across requests amortizes repetitive prompt computations ([[01 Books/Volume III - Agentic Systems/01 The Model/02 The Foundation Model/V3-02.15 Source References#^ref-zheng2024sglang|Zheng et al. 2024]]). Together, these runtime innovations transformed the per-token computational costs analyzed in this chapter into shared, schedulable execution pipelines.

The second lineage stems from distributed systems engineering and access mediation across untrusted boundaries. When Birrell and Nelson introduced remote procedure calls, their goal was to make remote execution feel like a local function call, yet they showed that network boundaries expose failure semantics that local calls never experience, most notably states in which the caller cannot determine whether an operation ran ([[01 Books/Volume III - Agentic Systems/01 The Model/02 The Foundation Model/V3-02.15 Source References#^ref-birrell1984rpc|Birrell and Nelson 1984]]). Decades of systems architecture reinforce that lower-level abstractions cannot reliably guarantee end-to-end semantics; as the end-to-end argument demonstrates, true correctness must ultimately be validated by the participating endpoints ([[01 Books/Volume III - Agentic Systems/01 The Model/02 The Foundation Model/V3-02.15 Source References#^ref-saltzer1984endtoend|Saltzer et al. 1984]]). In an agent runtime, an external service returning a `COMPLETED` status serves as an execution hint rather than a definitive guarantee of success. Similarly, classical security engineering established the principle of complete mediation ([[01 Books/Volume III - Agentic Systems/01 The Model/02 The Foundation Model/V3-02.15 Source References#^ref-saltzer1975|Saltzer and Schroeder 1975]]), which requires validating every access request against current authority rather than trusting past authorization. An agent runtime must enforce the same discipline when dispatching model-proposed tool calls. Finally, parsing theory provides the formal prefix property ([[01 Books/Volume III - Agentic Systems/01 The Model/02 The Foundation Model/V3-02.15 Source References#^ref-knuth1965translation|Knuth 1965]]) that underpins modern constrained decoding, ensuring token-by-token alignment with structured schema grammars ([[01 Books/Volume III - Agentic Systems/01 The Model/02 The Foundation Model/V3-02.15 Source References#^ref-scholak2021picard|Scholak et al. 2021]]; [[01 Books/Volume III - Agentic Systems/01 The Model/02 The Foundation Model/V3-02.15 Source References#^ref-willard2023outlines|Willard and Louf 2023]]). While sampling payloads dynamically across an open vocabulary is a modern capability, the defensive engineering required to govern those calls safely rests on these older systems foundations.

The following six papers provide essential reading on the conceptual and systems foundations of foundation model runtimes:

- Birrell and Nelson ([[01 Books/Volume III - Agentic Systems/01 The Model/02 The Foundation Model/V3-02.15 Source References#^ref-birrell1984rpc|1984]]) introduces the mechanics of remote procedure calls and catalogs their failure semantics. It remains the classic reference for why invocation contracts across process boundaries must handle network partitions, cancellations, and undecidable execution states.
- Saltzer et al. ([[01 Books/Volume III - Agentic Systems/01 The Model/02 The Foundation Model/V3-02.15 Source References#^ref-saltzer1984endtoend|1984]]) presents the end-to-end argument in system design, articulating why lower architectural layers cannot reliably guarantee higher-level application correctness. It underpins why an agent runtime cannot treat external service status codes (such as `COMPLETED`) as substitutes for independent outcome validation.
- Holtzman et al. ([[01 Books/Volume III - Agentic Systems/01 The Model/02 The Foundation Model/V3-02.15 Source References#^ref-holtzman2020curious|2020]]) analyzes the degeneration of likelihood-maximizing decoding in open-ended text generation and introduces nucleus (top-${p}$) sampling. It demonstrates that decoding algorithms represent deliberate engineering trade-offs that govern output entropy, coherence, and candidate diversity.
- Pope et al. ([[01 Books/Volume III - Agentic Systems/01 The Model/02 The Foundation Model/V3-02.15 Source References#^ref-pope2023efficiently|2023]]) develops an analytical roofline model of Transformer serving that separates compute-bound prompt prefill from memory-bandwidth-bound autoregressive decoding. It establishes the quantitative foundation for per-token latency floors, KV cache memory footprints, and batching trade-offs.
- Willard and Louf ([[01 Books/Volume III - Agentic Systems/01 The Model/02 The Foundation Model/V3-02.15 Source References#^ref-willard2023outlines|2023]]) introduces efficient constrained decoding by compiling regular expressions and context-free grammars into finite-state machines indexed over the model’s token vocabulary. It demonstrates how to enforce strict structural schemas at runtime with minimal per-token computational overhead.
- Bommasani et al. ([[01 Books/Volume III - Agentic Systems/01 The Model/02 The Foundation Model/V3-02.15 Source References#^ref-bommasani2021opportunities|2021]]) surveys the technical capabilities and architectural risks of foundation models. Its analysis of model homogenization clarifies why centralized upstream dependencies require strict isolation boundaries, independent validation, and runtime guardrails across downstream applications.
