# V3-03.07 Bounded Search

[[Home|Home]] · [[01 Books/Volume III - Agentic Systems/Volume 3 Overview|Volume Overview]] · [[01 Books/Volume III - Agentic Systems/01 The Model/03 Test-Time Compute/V3-03 Test-Time Compute|V3-03 Test-Time Compute]]

^sec-vol3-test-time-compute-bounded-search

The repair agent now holds several partial candidates at once, some freshly revised and some untouched. At each step it can sample a fresh patch from the root, extend an existing partial candidate by one segment, or run a diagnostic probe to separate competing explanations. Every choice draws from the same task budget while creating different scheduling dependencies. A probe that gates the next code extension sits on the critical path. Independent candidate extensions can run in parallel across worker slots. Dropping a dead-end branch still forfeits the tokens and sandbox time already spent on it. A search policy therefore needs a pruning structure, workspace isolation between branches, separate accounts for tokens, time, and memory, reservations for in-flight work, and an ordered stopping rule.

### Where Each Search Structure Prunes

^sec-vol3-test-time-compute-search-structures

Decoding a candidate patch to full length after its first line already rests on a broken premise wastes every remaining token on that branch, yet checking after every short segment stalls generation behind a scorer. Search structures trade those two costs by choosing when to check and discard partial candidates. Flat sampling waits until the end: in best-of-${N}$, the runtime dispatches up to ${N}$ independent generation calls and runs its selector only after all of them finish. Because the calls do not depend on one another, free serving capacity turns directly into lower latency through batching, provided the cluster can run all ${N}$ streams without slowing per-token decoding, at the price of decoding every flawed branch to completion.

**Beam search**[[01 Books/Volume III - Agentic Systems/01 The Model/03 Test-Time Compute/V3-03.14 Footnotes#^fn6|⁶]] moves that check inside the generation loop. The runtime keeps a frontier of at most ${W}$ partial candidates (the beam width). At each step it generates a fixed number of extensions per retained candidate, scores the extended prefixes, and keeps only the top ${W}$ survivors for the next step. If fewer than ${W}$ survive a check, only those survivors advance, and the root starts as a single prompt. Step scores can combine model log-probabilities, a learned process scorer, or a deterministic check. While ${W}$ caps the survivors carried between stages, peak memory during expansion must hold ${W}$ times the branching factor in active extensions along with their KV caches, scores, and parent pointers.

Pruning at each step trades generation tokens for scoring work and bets on the accuracy of the step scorer. If a noisy step score drops a prefix that would have led to a working patch, a fixed-width beam search never visits that branch again unless backtracking is added. Conversely, killing a broken prefix early saves all the tokens and final test runs its completion would have cost. Step pruning pays off when scoring costs less than the avoided downstream generation and the step check rarely rejects valid prefixes.

Rather than expanding every surviving candidate equally at every depth, tree search over intermediate reasoning steps ([[01 Books/Volume III - Agentic Systems/01 The Model/03 Test-Time Compute/V3-03.13 Source References#^ref-yao2023tree|Yao et al. 2023]]) directs the next extension to whichever partial candidate looks most promising or most uncertain. **Monte Carlo tree search** ([[01 Books/Volume III - Agentic Systems/01 The Model/03 Test-Time Compute/V3-03.13 Source References#^ref-coulom2006efficient|Coulom 2006]]; [[01 Books/Volume III - Agentic Systems/01 The Model/03 Test-Time Compute/V3-03.13 Source References#^ref-kocsis2006bandit|Kocsis and Szepesv’ari 2006]]) tracks visit counts and average leaf scores along the edges of the search tree. Its PUCT selection rule[[01 Books/Volume III - Agentic Systems/01 The Model/03 Test-Time Compute/V3-03.14 Footnotes#^fn7|⁷]] from AlphaGo ([[01 Books/Volume III - Agentic Systems/01 The Model/03 Test-Time Compute/V3-03.13 Source References#^ref-silver2016mastering|Silver et al. 2016]]) walks down from the root by picking at each partial state ${s}$ the extension ${a}$ that maximizes

$$
{Q}({s},{a}) + {c_{\text{explore}}}\,{p_{\text{prior}}}({a} \mid {s})\,\frac{\sqrt{\sum_{{a'}} {N_{\text{visit}}}({s},{a'})}}{1 + {N_{\text{visit}}}({s},{a})}, \tag{5}
$$

^eq-vol3-puct

where ${Q}({s},{a})$ is the mean leaf score seen on visits through extension ${a}$, and ${N_{\text{visit}}}({s},{a})$ is its visit count. The term ${p_{\text{prior}}}({a} \mid {s})$ is a prior distribution over extensions (such as the generator’s probability for that step), and ${c_{\text{explore}}}$ is a positive weight that balances high-scoring branches against plausible, less-visited ones. The exploration ratio grows with the square root of total visits across all sibling extensions at ${s}$ while shrinking linearly with $1 + {N_{\text{visit}}}({s},{a})$, so repeatedly visiting one branch diminishes its own bonus while raising the bonus of plausible siblings that have not yet been tried. The **leaf value** ${V}$ is the score assigned to a newly expanded partial candidate by a process scorer or step check, and ${Q}$ averages those leaf values across visits. When a new leaf returns value ${V}$, every edge along the path back to the root increments its visit count (${N_{\text{visit}}} \leftarrow {N_{\text{visit}}} + 1$) and updates its running mean (${Q} \leftarrow {Q} + ({V} - {Q})/{N_{\text{visit}}}$). If ${V}$ comes from a noisy learned scorer, ${Q}$ inherits the selection optimism of [[01 Books/Volume III - Agentic Systems/01 The Model/03 Test-Time Compute/V3-03.05 Verifying Candidates#^eq-vol3-argmax-noise|equation 4]] and pulls future visits toward branches whose early scores were inflated by noise. Tree search therefore helps only when its smarter allocation saves more work than leaf scoring and tree bookkeeping add.

The three structures differ in where they prune ([[01 Books/Volume III - Agentic Systems/01 The Model/03 Test-Time Compute/V3-03.07 Bounded Search#^fig-vol3-search-topologies|figure 7]]). Best-of-${N}$ waits until all candidates finish, beam search prunes at every step boundary, and tree search expands one favored leaf at a time while updating visit counts and mean values along its path. Those pruning points set each structure’s concurrency, live state, and prefix sharing ([[01 Books/Volume III - Agentic Systems/01 The Model/03 Test-Time Compute/V3-03.07 Bounded Search#^tbl-vol3-search-topologies|table 6]]).

↪ Radix prefix caching, which lets later requests for a branch reuse the KV state of its shared ancestors, is developed in **[[01 Books/Volume III - Agentic Systems/02 Agent Memory/05 KV Cache Management/V3-05.06 Prefix Caching Across Turns#^sec-vol3-kv-cache-radix-lookup|Radix prefix lookup]]**.

### How Step Pruning Bounds Tree Growth

^sec-vol3-test-time-compute-tree-growth

A search tree that expands every node into 4 branches across 10 steps without pruning produces 1,048,576 leaves and quickly exhausts memory and token budgets. Step pruning bounds that growth. Suppose each retained node proposes ${b}$ child extensions and a step check independently rejects each child with probability ${p_{\text{prune}}}$. The expected number of surviving children per expanded node, the **effective branching factor**, is

$$
{b_{\text{eff}}} = {b}\,(1 - {p_{\text{prune}}}). \tag{6}
$$

^eq-vol3-effective-branching

Under [[01 Books/Volume III - Agentic Systems/01 The Model/03 Test-Time Compute/V3-03.07 Bounded Search#^eq-vol3-effective-branching|equation 6]], the expected frontier width at depth ${d}$ is ${b_{\text{eff}}}^{\,{d}}$. Because each surviving node at depth ${d}-1$ generates and checks ${b}$ children, the expected total number of generated nodes through depth ${d_{\max}}$ is $\sum_{{d}=1}^{{d_{\max}}} {b}\,{b_{\text{eff}}}^{\,{d}-1}$. This is a Galton–Watson branching process.[[01 Books/Volume III - Agentic Systems/01 The Model/03 Test-Time Compute/V3-03.14 Footnotes#^fn8|⁸]] When ${b_{\text{eff}}} < 1$, which requires a rejection rate above $1 - 1/{b}$, the frontier shrinks geometrically with depth and dies out on its own. When ${b_{\text{eff}}} > 1$, the expected frontier still grows exponentially, so hard ceilings on depth, tokens, or time must stop the search.

Suppose each node proposes 5 extensions, the step check rejects 68 percent of them, and the search runs to depth 3. The effective branching factor is 1.6. Because the 68 percent rejection rate sits below the critical rate of 80 percent for ${b} =$ 5, the tree remains supercritical (${b_{\text{eff}}} > 1$). Even so, an unpruned tree across three steps generates 155 nodes and ends with 125 leaves. The pruned tree generates an expected 25.8 nodes and ends with 4.1 leaves, cutting node generations by 83.4 percent. The total saving beats the 68 percent single-step rejection rate because rejecting a parent also removes the entire subtree beneath it. With equal-sized steps, generated tokens drop by the same fraction, though each rejected node still pays for its own generation and step check.

![[90 Assets/Volume III/03 Test Time Compute/Vol3 Ttc Margin Pruned Nodes 922e0cc50c.svg]]

*Three horizontal orange bars of nodes generated. Unpruned: 155. A dashed, lightly filled reference bar for a flat 68 percent cut is about one third as long. Pruned: 25.8, about half the length of the reference bar.*

*Each rejected child also removes the subtree it would have grown.*

Two practical limits qualify this calculation. First, a step check that falsely rejects valid steps kills good branches permanently, so aggressive pruning helps only when false rejections are rare. Second, when a step needs just one surviving child to move forward, proposing children one at a time takes $1/(1 - {p_{\text{prune}}})$ tries in expectation, whereas proposing a batch of ${n_{\text{batch}}}$ children in parallel takes $1/(1 - {p_{\text{prune}}}^{\,{n_{\text{batch}}}})$ rounds of ${n_{\text{batch}}}$ children each. Batching cuts wall-clock rounds at the price of generating more total children, the same latency-versus-work trade that notebook [3.3](https://mlsysbook.ai/vol3/03_test_time_compute/03_test_time_compute.html#nbk-03-best-of-n-pool-price) priced for best-of-${N}$ sampling.

### Isolating Workspaces Across Search Branches

^sec-vol3-test-time-compute-mutable-sandbox-search

If one search branch edits files in a shared workspace and a sibling branch then runs its tests against that modified working tree, the sibling’s test results no longer reflect its own proposed patch. Although a partial candidate is just data in memory while being decoded, testing it requires running code against a real filesystem and process state. Every branch that writes files or mutates state therefore needs its own isolated container, copy-on-write filesystem snapshot, or deterministic rollback before another branch runs, so that each observation belongs strictly to the candidate and workspace snapshot that produced it.

↪ Copy-on-write task workspaces that keep each run’s file modifications private are developed in **[[01 Books/Volume III - Agentic Systems/03 Tool Use/08 Agent Sandboxes/V3-08.07 The Agent Workspace#^sec-vol3-sandboxes-cow-provisioning|Copy-on-write provisioning]]**.

Isolating branches costs real system resources even when most branches are later pruned. Concurrent sandboxes consume host RAM, disk volumes, process slots, and network bandwidth, while their test logs accumulate in the runtime’s state. Even read-only diagnostic commands need sandboxed permissions so they cannot leak host credentials or environment variables into the search trace. Pruning a branch drops it from the search frontier, but backtracking in the search tree cannot unsend an outbound network call or undo a write to an external database. Sandbox boundaries block those external writes during search, and any external action the runtime does permit has to be budgeted as irreversible.

### Accounting for the Whole Search

^sec-vol3-test-time-compute-search-accounting

Collapsing tokens, test runtimes, and memory into a single composite “search cost” hides whichever physical limit the search will hit first, because a beam search that cuts generated output tokens nearly in half can still cost more in dollars once repeated prompt reads are counted. A search runtime therefore keeps four separate accounts: money spent, physical work by unit, elapsed wall-clock time, and peak live state. Money is the one common unit that adds up across generator tokens, scorer calls, and tool runs. Physical work, by contrast, comes in non-interchangeable kinds: prompt tokens, output tokens, scorer tokens, test executions, and sandbox startups each load different hardware resources, and adding model FLOPs to test seconds produces a meaningless number.

Wall-clock time depends on how the scheduler maps those operations onto hardware: sequential stages add along the critical path, concurrent branches overlap up to the available worker slots, and requests beyond those slots wait in waves. Peak live state is a fourth, separate limit, set by what must sit in memory and storage at the same instant (active KV caches, candidate trees, test logs, and live sandboxes) rather than the cumulative work over the whole run. Saving output tokens by pruning early often adds prompt re-reads, scoring barriers, or concurrent state, so keeping the four accounts separate shows which resource actually constrains the policy.

**Napkin Math 3.6: Flat sampling vs. bounded beam expansion**

**Problem**: A coding agent generates candidate patches in 4 segments of 128 tokens. Compare 16 complete candidates with beam width 4 and 3 extensions per retained prefix. How do early pruning and repeated scoring change the resource totals?

**Setup**: Every generator request supplies the same 4,096-token task prompt plus its retained generated prefix. The illustrative per-token generation time from **[[01 Books/Volume III - Agentic Systems/01 The Model/02 The Foundation Model/V3-02.09 The Cost of a Model Call#^sec-vol3-foundation-model-per-call-work-estimate|Estimating per-call hardware work]]** is 21.075 ms. Assume 8 generation streams can maintain that rate concurrently. The scorer reads the entire generated prefix, excluding the common task prompt, and processes requests serially at a fixed 15 ms per request regardless of prefix length. The growth with prefix length that [[01 Books/Volume III - Agentic Systems/01 The Model/03 Test-Time Compute/V3-03.05 Verifying Candidates#^eq-vol3-prm-rescoring|equation 2]] describes therefore appears in the scorer’s token bill rather than in this stipulated time. Each final test takes 0.5 s, with 8 isolated runs available concurrently. Generation, scoring, and final testing do not overlap in this schedule.

Hypothetical prices per million tokens are \$3 for generator input, \$15 for output, and \$0.50 for scorer input. Input charges include every supplied token, without a prefix-reuse discount. The timing subtotal omits prefill, queueing, request transfer, workspace preparation, and cleanup. It is not a prediction of complete service latency, and the token-charge subtotal excludes execution and infrastructure charges.

**Math**: Multiplying 16 candidates by 512 output tokens per candidate gives 8,192 output tokens. Multiplying the same candidate count by the common prompt length gives 65,536 input tokens. Generation occupies 2 waves of complete candidates, followed by 2 test waves. Multiplying the generation waves by candidate length and the per-token allowance, then adding the test waves times test duration, gives 22.58 s. Multiplying each token count by its corresponding price gives a token-charge subtotal of \$0.3195.

The beam expands frontiers of 3, 9, 12, 12 extensions in successive stages, because the root expands 3 ways in stage 1, those 3 prefixes expand to 9 extensions in stage 2, and pruning retains at most the beam width (4 prefixes) from stage 2 onward so each later stage expands 4 $\times$ 3 = 12 extensions. Adding these counts gives 36 newly generated segments, and multiplying by the segment length gives 4,608 output tokens. For each request, generator input is the common prompt plus the preceding generated segments, and summing across requests gives 156,288 input tokens. The scorer instead reads each complete generated prefix, including its new segment. Summing each stage’s extension count times its prefix length gives 13,440 scorer-input tokens. Because stages whose frontier exceeds the 8 concurrent generation slots split into two waves, generation requires 7 waves of one-segment requests, serial scoring adds 0.54 s, and the final survivors fit in 1 test wave. Applying the same duration and price arithmetic gives 19.92 s and \$0.5447.

**Systems insight**: The beam generates 43.75 percent fewer output tokens, worth \$0.054, but resupplying the task prompt and retained prefixes to every extension costs \$0.272 more in input, so its token bill is the higher one. The knob is the price of repeated input. The beam’s bill falls below flat sampling’s only when input tokens cost less than 17.3 percent of list price, the regime a prefix cache creates. Its shorter generation waves already give it the lower timing subtotal under this schedule, before omitted prefill is added back. Quality does not appear in these totals; the choice between the two rests on the check results each produces.

### Reserving Budget Before Admitting Work

^sec-vol3-test-time-compute-admission-limits

Checking how many tokens a search has spent only after concurrent model calls return is too late to prevent a budget overrun, because eight in-flight calls can all finish and bill their full output allowance before the budget account updates. Before dispatching any candidate extension, the runtime must reserve the call’s maximum allowed output tokens along with its scorer and sandbox allowances, keep settled spend plus open reservations below the search ceilings, and release unused capacity only after the call settles. A post-call usage receipt tells the runtime what to settle, but only a pre-dispatch reservation can bound concurrent spend in advance.

↪ Charging every pending operation its worst-case ceiling until settlement is the bounded-liability admission developed in **[[01 Books/Volume III - Agentic Systems/04 The Agent Runtime/10 The Agent Harness/V3-10.10 Budgets and Ceilings#^sec-vol3-agent-harness-reservation-settlement|Reservation and settlement]]**.

Search depth and wall-clock time also need separate limits. A depth cap limits the chain of sequential extensions or tool turns, while a deadline caps user-perceived latency. When the deadline expires, the runtime stops admitting new work, drops late responses from the expired phase, and sends cancellation signals to in-flight calls. As **[[01 Books/Volume III - Agentic Systems/01 The Model/02 The Foundation Model/V3-02.06 The Invocation Contract#^sec-vol3-foundation-model-streaming-cancellation|Streaming and cancellation]]** showed, however, sending a cancel request does not guarantee that the remote server stops immediately; a server that ignores cancellation can keep decoding and billing up to the call’s full output ceiling. Until the remote service settles the request, the runtime must keep the full reservation charged against the budget.

Running out of budget ends the search without lowering the acceptance bar. A finished candidate can still fail regression tests, and a high learned score cannot stand in for a required check pass. If no candidate meets the acceptance contract when a ceiling is reached, the runtime reports an incomplete result, abstains, or escalates to a human operator. That separation is invariant closure (principle [[01 Books/Volume III - Agentic Systems/00 Introduction/01 Introduction/V3-01.06 Invariant Closure#^pri-invariant-closure|1]]) applied to deliberation: mechanical limits sit below the model to stop spending, and acceptance evidence sits above the model to authorize returning an answer.

### When to Stop Searching Early

^sec-vol3-test-time-compute-early-exit

Hard ceilings stop a search when its allowance runs out, whereas early-stopping rules end the search inside the ceilings as soon as a candidate passes or the next round is no longer worth its cost. Many repair workloads need only the first candidate that meets their acceptance criteria, a stopping rule known as **satisficing**.[[01 Books/Volume III - Agentic Systems/01 The Model/03 Test-Time Compute/V3-03.14 Footnotes#^fn9|⁹]] Once a candidate passes the required checks and policy rules, the search stops and returns it. If the search stage checks only compilation, stopping at the first compiling patch hands testing and deployment authorization to the next stage of the pipeline.

An explicit secondary objective can justify searching further after finding an acceptable candidate, such as looking for a smaller diff or a faster query plan within the remaining budget. A rising learned score by itself does not justify continuing if held-out benchmarks show that higher scores past the pass threshold rarely improve real outcomes. Continuing therefore requires an explicit estimate of what the next unit of work is worth.

**Marginal stopping**[[01 Books/Volume III - Agentic Systems/01 The Model/03 Test-Time Compute/V3-03.14 Footnotes#^fn10|¹⁰]] asks whether the expected acceptance gain from the next round is worth its added cost. During a live repair, the runtime does not know the exact probability that the next attempt will pass, so it estimates that gain from calibration curves measured on held-out tasks or from progress signals such as fewer failing tests. Because any single diagnostic is only a partial signal, those estimates hold only for workloads and test suites similar to the calibration set.

For a nested sequence of round limits under the same workload and acceptance criterion, let ${\hat{p}}_{{m}}$ estimate the probability of finding an acceptable result by round ${m}$. Let ${C}_{{m}}$ denote the expected cumulative expenditure of the policy that stops at its first acceptable result and otherwise runs up to that round limit. Before admitting round ${m}$, with no earlier acceptance, the marginal policy stops when

$$
\frac{{\hat{p}}_{{m}} - {\hat{p}}_{{m}-1}}{{C}_{{m}} - {C}_{{m}-1}} < {\epsilon_{\text{stop}}}, \tag{7}
$$

^eq-vol3-marginal-stopping

where ${\epsilon_{\text{stop}}}$ is the minimum required gain in acceptance probability per unit of spend. Because round ${m}$ executes only when the first ${m} - 1$ rounds fail to produce an acceptable result (an event with probability $1 - {\hat{p}}_{{m}-1}$), the denominator ${C}_{{m}} - {C}_{{m}-1}$ equals the full cost of running round ${m}$ multiplied by $1 - {\hat{p}}_{{m}-1}$. Dividing both numerator and denominator by that same reach probability $1 - {\hat{p}}_{{m}-1}$ converts the numerator into the conditional probability of passing in round ${m}$ given earlier failure and cancels the reach probability from the denominator. The ratio in [[01 Books/Volume III - Agentic Systems/01 The Model/03 Test-Time Compute/V3-03.07 Bounded Search#^eq-vol3-marginal-stopping|equation 7]] therefore equals the conditional next-round pass probability divided by the full cost of running that round. Dividing the unconditional probability gain by the full round cost would mix two different denominators. In an adaptive search, these conditional estimates should be updated from the current search state rather than read blindly from an unconditional table.

The numerator measures the gain in the estimated chance of a verified pass, not the number of new candidates or a rise in a heuristic score. The verification asymmetry (principle [[01 Books/Volume III - Agentic Systems/01 The Model/V3 Model Principles#^pri-vol3-verification-asymmetry|3]]) explains why, since generating more candidates and obtaining a verified solution are distinct events. A search whose calibrated chance of passing stays flat is burning budget without making progress, no matter how many tokens it emits. The threshold ${\epsilon_{\text{stop}}}$ is an economic choice set by the value of an automated fix relative to human escalation, net of checker error and delay; high failure costs never justify searching longer against an untrustworthy check.

For satisficing workloads, the runtime applies these three stopping checks in a fixed order before admitting any new work ([[01 Books/Volume III - Agentic Systems/01 The Model/03 Test-Time Compute/V3-03.07 Bounded Search#^fig-vol3-stopping-boundaries|figure 8]]). First, hard ceilings on tokens, calls, depth, or wall-clock time stop new admissions unconditionally; if an acceptable candidate is already in hand it goes to the acceptance gate, and otherwise the runtime abstains or escalates. Second, within the ceilings, any candidate that meets the acceptance criteria stops the search and goes to the acceptance gate. Third, when the ceilings still have room and no candidate has passed yet, the marginal stopping rule ([[01 Books/Volume III - Agentic Systems/01 The Model/03 Test-Time Compute/V3-03.07 Bounded Search#^eq-vol3-marginal-stopping|equation 7]]) stops the search early if the next round’s expected gain per dollar falls below ${\epsilon_{\text{stop}}}$. Reversing this order would either let an optimistic gain estimate breach a hard ceiling or let a diminishing-returns check discard a candidate that already passed. Whichever condition ends the search, the runtime settles all open reservations and separates verified passes from unverified attempts.

![[90 Assets/Volume III/03 Test Time Compute/Search Stopping Boundaries 009ddea931.svg]]

Figure 8: **Stopping Precedence**: Before admitting more work, the runtime tests three conditions in a fixed order. A hard ceiling on tokens, calls, depth, or time stops new admissions, preserving any already acceptable candidate for the acceptance decision. Within the ceilings, a candidate that meets the acceptance criteria ends search and goes to the acceptance decision, which stopping does not itself make. Only then does the marginal rule compare the gain per unit cost with ${\epsilon_{\text{stop}}}$. A stop without an acceptable candidate is reported as incomplete or escalated, never returned as accepted.

**Checkpoint 3.2: Accounting for a bounded search**

The resource accounts and the stopping policy must count work even when it produces no accepted result.

**Input budget**: Using the notebook’s prompt length and expansion counts, estimate the input tokens spent on common prompts alone and explain why they exceed flat sampling’s input count.

**Pending work**: Which requests must remain reserved when the runtime reaches its deadline but remote cancellation is unconfirmed?

**Stopping precedence**: A search has nearly exhausted its token ceiling, holds a candidate that passes the sealed suite, and still estimates a marginal gain above ${\epsilon_{\text{stop}}}$. Which rule decides, and what does the runtime do next?

**Acceptance evidence**: What evidence would justify stopping with an accepted repair, and what should the runtime return if only the highest learned score is available?

Search structures, resource accounts, and stopping rules define how a single deliberation policy runs inside its bounds. Choosing among competing policies requires comparing their end-to-end pass rates, actual spend, and deadline completions on the same workload, charging every failed and timed-out attempt to the accepted results.
