# V3-03.08 Evaluating Deliberation Policies

[[Home|Home]] · [[01 Books/Volume III - Agentic Systems/Volume 3 Overview|Volume Overview]] · [[01 Books/Volume III - Agentic Systems/01 The Model/03 Test-Time Compute/V3-03 Test-Time Compute|V3-03 Test-Time Compute]]

^sec-vol3-test-time-compute-strategy-design

Suppose an iterative search policy resolves more connection-pool failures than a single-call baseline. That gain might come from the search algorithm itself, or it might come from a larger token budget, a stronger base model, or silently dropping timed-out tasks from the denominator. Judging any policy that combines candidate allocation, selection, verification checks, and early stopping therefore requires recording three things: the exact resource ceilings and permissions granted to each policy, the tokens, checks, and wall-clock time actually spent, and the fate of every task that failed to produce an accepted result.

### Controlled and System-Level Comparisons

^sec-vol3-test-time-compute-comparison-design

Comparing a multi-round search on a newer model against a single call on an older model, or counting only the tasks that finished before the deadline, mixes model capability and survivor bias with the effect of the search policy. A controlled policy comparison isolates the search policy by holding the base model, serving setup, benchmark tasks, tool interfaces, and acceptance criteria fixed while varying only the deliberation rule under the same resource ceilings and deadline. Prompt tokens, output tokens, check runs, and concurrency slots should each be stated as separate ceilings rather than folded into one vague “compute budget.” Because a good policy stops early when a candidate passes, unspent budget reflects efficiency rather than an unfair test.

↪ Complete agent policies are compared at matched budgets, with every initiated trial charged, by the method developed in **[[01 Books/Volume III - Agentic Systems/04 The Agent Runtime/13 Agent Evaluation/V3-13.06 Statistical Evaluation Rigor#^sec-vol3-evaluation-matched-budgets|Matched-budget comparison]]**.

A system-design comparison answers a broader engineering question: how two complete stacks trade cost, latency, and accuracy against one another. For instance, an engineer might compare one call to a large model against repeated calls to a smaller, faster model under the same dollar and latency caps. That comparison tells the team which stack to deploy, but it cannot separate the effect of search from the difference between the two models. Keeping controlled policy tests separate from stack-level comparisons prevents teams from crediting a model upgrade to their search algorithm.

Evaluation logs must record every started task, including explicit failures, abstentions, and deadline timeouts. To avoid crediting a search for exploiting its own scorer (the Goodhart failure of [[01 Books/Volume III - Agentic Systems/01 The Model/03 Test-Time Compute/V3-03.05 Verifying Candidates#^sec-vol3-test-time-compute-selection-pressure|section 3.4.3]]), final output quality must be graded by an independent check rather than the internal score that guided the search. Logs should also separate three failure modes: false acceptance (returning a broken candidate), abstention or escalation, and deadline timeout. When reporting checker error rates, state the denominator explicitly.[[01 Books/Volume III - Agentic Systems/01 The Model/03 Test-Time Compute/V3-03.14 Footnotes#^fn11|¹¹]] The share of broken candidates that pass a check (${\epsilon_{\text{FA}}}$) differs from the share of passing candidates that are broken ($1-{\Pi_{V}}$). Furthermore, because sampling and branching are stochastic, benchmarks need repeated runs across the task set rather than a single point number.

Total spend includes every resource used along the way: pruned branches, repeated prompt prefill across turns, verifier runs, and tokens decoded in flight after a cancellation signal. Dividing the total spend across the whole task cohort by the number of tasks that produced an accepted result gives the effective cost per accepted task, the cohort measure of ${C_{\text{eff}}}$ (**[[01 Books/Volume III - Agentic Systems/00 Introduction/01 Introduction/V3-01.07 Reliability and Cost Over a Horizon#^eq-vol3-cost-per-accepted-task|equation 1.2]]**). Because a slow repair that misses the service deadline is unusable, the share of tasks that finish before the deadline belongs right beside ${C_{\text{eff}}}$; a low average latency can hide a long tail where sequential revisions time out.

### Policies Under a Deadline

^sec-vol3-test-time-compute-deadline-policies

A deliberation policy that allows three feedback rounds on paper can run at most two rounds in practice when each round takes 30 seconds and the service deadline is 75 seconds. To see how concurrency limits and wall-clock deadlines constrain a policy in production, the notebook below compares three policies evaluated by a scoped test suite rather than an omniscient oracle. All probabilities are stipulated parameters chosen to show how concurrency, stopping rules, and token budgets interact under one deadline.

**Napkin Math 3.7: Three policies, one deadline**

**Scenario**: Consider an automated software repair workload checked by a sealed regression suite. An initial candidate passes the suite with hypothetical probability ${p}$ = 40 percent, and repeated attempts on the same task draw candidates independently. Passing the suite is the acceptance rule for this stage, though passing tests does not guarantee that the patch is defect-free. Each policy runs under the same permissions and resource ceilings: at most 32,768 input tokens, 8,192 generated tokens, and 8 test runs, with up to 8 parallel workers and a hard 75 s deadline.

Each initial request sends 2,048 prompt tokens, each candidate generates 1,000 tokens, and running the test suite takes 10 s. Using the illustrative 21.075 ms per-token decode time from **[[01 Books/Volume III - Agentic Systems/01 The Model/02 The Foundation Model/V3-02.09 The Cost of a Model Call#^sec-vol3-foundation-model-per-call-work-estimate|Estimating per-call hardware work]]**, assume that concurrent streams keep the same per-token rate and that tests run on dedicated workers without contention (omitting prefill, queueing, network transfer, and filtering overhead). Token prices are \$3 per million prompt tokens and \$15 per million output tokens, with no prefix-cache discount.

**Option A (single candidate)**: Generate and test one candidate. It passes the suite with probability 40 percent, spending 2,048 prompt tokens, 1,000 generated tokens, and one test run. The attempt finishes in 31.07 s at a model bill of \$0.02114, and if the test fails the runtime abstains.

**Option B (parallel breadth)**: Dispatch all 8 candidates at once without early stopping, test each as it finishes, and return the first passing patch in index order. At least one of the ${N}$ = 8 candidates passes with probability pass@${N} = 1-(1-{p})^{{N}}$ = 98.32 percent, which is also the chance of returning an accepted repair. Because all eight streams and tests fit within the concurrency limit, wall-clock time stays 31.07 s, while the fan-out spends 16,384 prompt tokens, 8,000 generated tokens, and 8 test runs for a model bill of \$0.16915.

**Option C (sequential feedback)**: Generate one candidate and stop right away if it passes. If it fails, append the failed patch and a 340-token failure trace to the prompt and request a revision, which passes with conditional probability ${p_{\text{rev}}}$ = 35 percent. Although the policy nominally allows 3 rounds, three sequential rounds would take 93.22 s and miss the 75 s deadline. Only 2 complete rounds fit inside 75 s, so the deadline caps the policy at one revision and forces an abstention if the second attempt also fails.

**Math**: With the third round cut off by the deadline, sequential feedback passes with probability ${p}+(1-{p})\,{p_{\text{rev}}}$ = 61 percent and runs an expected $1+(1-{p})$ = 1.6 rounds. Expected spend is 4,080.8 prompt tokens, 1,600 generated tokens, and 1.6 test runs, for an expected model bill of \$0.03624 and an expected latency of 49.72 s (at most 62.15 s). The remaining 39 percent of tasks end without an accepted patch and must be counted as failed attempts. Across all started tasks, expected generated tokens per accepted repair are about 2,500 for Option A, 8,137 for Option B, and 2,623 for Option C.

**Systems insight**: Per accepted repair, the model bill is \$0.0529 for Option A, \$0.0594 for Option C, and \$0.1720 for Option B (3.3× the single-candidate baseline). Sequential feedback lifts the pass rate from 40 percent to 61 percent at a small cost premium per accepted task, whereas parallel breadth reaches 98.32 percent at a much steeper bill per accepted repair.

The deadline decides which policy can run. When the service has room for only one 31.07 s round, parallel breadth is the only option here that improves on the 40 percent single-call pass rate. When 75 s allows two rounds, sequential feedback raises the pass rate at a much smaller token bill than breadth, while a third round at 93.22 s would never finish in time. In production, test-runner cost and false acceptance rates belong alongside the token bill so that passing the suite tracks real task fixes.

### Choosing an Operating Point

^sec-vol3-test-time-compute-operating-point

No single policy in the comparison above wins on every axis: the single call is cheapest, parallel breadth finishes in one round at the highest pass rate, and two-round feedback reaches an intermediate pass rate at a much lower token bill than breadth. Each of the three sits on the Pareto frontier. A policy is strictly dominated only when another feasible policy matches or beats it on every metric (latency, token bill, checking load, and pass rate) and strictly beats it on at least one. Among non-dominated policies, the service’s deadline, budget cap, and error tolerance pick the operating point.

Picking that operating point requires testing under the same limits the service will enforce in production. Analytical formulas cannot plug an ad-hoc “effective pool size” into pass@${N}$ when worker queues, prefix caching, and check runtimes reshape the actual cost and latency. Likewise, the gain from a feedback round depends on how informative the test failure trace actually is, and hard deadlines cut off multi-round searches before their planned final round. Benchmarks must therefore enforce the real worker timeouts, early-exit rules, and admission ceilings rather than extrapolating uncensored curves into a deadline-bound service.
