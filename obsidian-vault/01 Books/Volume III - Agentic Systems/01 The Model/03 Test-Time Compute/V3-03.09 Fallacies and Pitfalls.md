# V3-03.09 Fallacies and Pitfalls

[[Home|Home]] · [[01 Books/Volume III - Agentic Systems/Volume 3 Overview|Volume Overview]] · [[01 Books/Volume III - Agentic Systems/01 The Model/03 Test-Time Compute/V3-03 Test-Time Compute|V3-03 Test-Time Compute]]

^sec-vol3-test-time-compute-fallacies

The most common mistakes in test-time compute come from treating extra work as proof of progress. Extra tokens, wider pools, and longer searches pay off only when the pool holds diverse hypotheses, the check separates valid repairs from plausible mistakes, and the budget account counts every discarded branch. When any of those pieces is missing, spending more compute only burns budget faster or selects more reliably for checker blind spots.

**Fallacy**: *More generated tokens necessarily produce a better answer.*

Extended reasoning can help a model trace constraints already present in the prompt, but it can just as easily elaborate and defend a wrong initial guess. When the open question concerns runtime behavior, generating tokens cannot supply the missing observation. In [[01 Books/Volume III - Agentic Systems/01 The Model/03 Test-Time Compute/V3-03.02 Limits of a Single Response#^sec-vol3-test-time-compute-insufficient-response|section 3.1]], generating a 1,024-token explanation takes 21.58 s, 539.5× the 40 ms test run that directly separates the two hypotheses. Token count measures spend, not progress; extra reasoning tokens earn their keep only when benchmarks show they raise the accepted pass rate enough to justify their latency and cost.

**Pitfall**: *Treating independent samples as independent capabilities.*

Samples drawn independently for a given prompt still share the same model weights and blind spots, so plugging a workload’s average pass rate into pass@${k}$ overstates what breadth can deliver ([[01 Books/Volume III - Agentic Systems/01 The Model/03 Test-Time Compute/V3-03.04 Selecting from a Candidate Pool#^sec-vol3-test-time-compute-pass-at-k|section 3.3.3]]). Suppose half the tasks in a workload pass at 60 percent per candidate and the other half never pass (${p} = 0$). The average single-sample pass rate across the workload is 30 percent, the same rate that notebook [3.3](https://mlsysbook.ai/vol3/03_test_time_compute/03_test_time_compute.html#nbk-03-best-of-n-pool-price) used for a single task. Plugging that average into $1-(1-{p})^{{N}}$ predicts 94.2 percent success for a pool of 8, whereas the true success rate across the workload is only 50.0 percent because sampling never solves the half where ${p} = 0$. Always compute pass@${k}$ per task first and average afterward.

**Fallacy**: *A check with a low false acceptance rate makes almost every pass trustworthy.*

How trustworthy a passing verdict is depends as much on the generator’s base rate of valid candidates as on the check’s error rates ([[01 Books/Volume III - Agentic Systems/01 The Model/03 Test-Time Compute/V3-03.05 Verifying Candidates#^eq-vol3-verifier-precision|equation 3]]). When valid candidates are scarce, even a small false acceptance rate lets broken candidates fill a large share of the passing pool. With a 2 percent false acceptance rate, a 10 percent false rejection rate, and a 5 percent base rate of valid candidates, only 70.31 percent of passing candidates are valid, and a first-passing selector returns a broken repair on 29.26 percent of task attempts (notebook [3.4](https://mlsysbook.ai/vol3/03_test_time_compute/03_test_time_compute.html#nbk-03-compute-overhead-and-yield-under)). On hard tasks, raising the generator’s base rate matters just as much for precision as tightening the check.

**Pitfall**: *Charging only for the final answer.*

Pruned branches, repeated prompt prefill, test runs, and scorer calls all consume resources, and in-flight requests canceled at a deadline often keep billing until the remote server settles them. Pricing a deliberation policy by the tokens in its final returned answer hides most of its cost. In notebook [3.7](https://mlsysbook.ai/vol3/03_test_time_compute/03_test_time_compute.html#nbk-03-strategy-comparison), parallel breadth returns a single 1,000-token patch, but it bills 8,000 output tokens per task and spends 8,137 output tokens per passing task. Likewise, the beam search in notebook [3.6](https://mlsysbook.ai/vol3/03_test_time_compute/03_test_time_compute.html#nbk-03-compute-allocation-and-latency-trade-offs) reads 156,288 input tokens to deliver 4 final survivors. Production cost accounts must charge every discarded branch and failed attempt to the accepted tasks.

**Fallacy**: *The highest-scoring candidate has completed the task.*

A scorer orders candidates on whatever proxy it was built to measure, and picking the maximum score concentrates positive scoring errors into the winner. With equal true utilities and independent Gaussian noise, the winner among 64 candidates has an expected scoring error of about 2.3 standard deviations ([[01 Books/Volume III - Agentic Systems/01 The Model/03 Test-Time Compute/V3-03.05 Verifying Candidates#^eq-vol3-argmax-noise|equation 4]]), and a checker loophole present in only 0.5 percent of single candidates shows up in 27.4 percent of 64-candidate pools. A high relative score is neither a proof nor an authorization for properties the verifier did not test. When the budget runs out without a candidate passing the acceptance check, the top-scoring candidate is still an unverified failure.
