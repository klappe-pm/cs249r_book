# V3-20.10 Open Questions Within the Digital Boundary

[[Home|Home]] · [[01 Books/Volume III - Agentic Systems/Volume 3 Overview|Volume Overview]] · [[01 Books/Volume III - Agentic Systems/07 Synthesis/20 Conclusion/V3-20 Conclusion|V3-20 Conclusion]]

^sec-vol3-conclusion-frontiers

The safety case for this incident establishes a bounded claim, that an approved configuration change produced acceptable service behavior under a declared contract. Even with every policy boundary enforced, every tool call sandboxed, and every transition logged, three systems questions remain open: how completely acceptance criteria capture service health, how to stay consistent when production state changes during deliberation, and how far the audit trail supports causal attribution.

Acceptance criteria are incomplete. The telemetry from the health window satisfies the latency and error thresholds of the incident contract, yet a service can meet those two metrics while exhausting background worker pools, corrupting downstream records, or leaking memory. Rubric-guided reviews can catch some of these regressions, but human and model judges have blind spots of their own. Tamper-proofing a check stops an agent from modifying its own evaluation without making the specification complete. Coverage grows only as unmodeled failure modes are found and written into the contract, and searching many candidate actions against an incomplete check overfits the search to the proxy.

↩︎ Calibrating rubric-based human and model judges, whose shared mistakes survive high agreement, is established in **[[01 Books/Volume III - Agentic Systems/04 The Agent Runtime/13 Agent Evaluation/V3-13.05 Grading Trajectories#^sec-vol3-evaluation-grading-calibration|Calibration of qualitative judgment]]**.

Concurrent state drift imposes a second limit, because production services do not pause while an agent deliberates. While the agent fetches telemetry, runs model calls, or waits for approval, traffic shifts, autoscalers adjust capacity, and peer services deploy schema updates. An expected-version check at the write keeps an approved change off a mutated baseline, but a rejected mutation only restarts the loop, since the agent must re-read state, revise its diagnosis, and seek a fresh approval. When churn is fast or deliberation is slow, the agent can livelock, spending its step and token budgets on retries that never land. Designing protocols that balance evidence freshness, lock granularity, and deliberation latency within bounded incident budgets remains an open systems challenge.

Causal attribution is narrower than the execution record, because an audit log establishes temporal order, not cause. That the rollback applied the new configuration just before the health window recorded healthy telemetry does not prove the change resolved the incident; a transient network bottleneck, an upstream dependency, or a client retry storm may have cleared on its own. Controlled comparisons, fault-injection tests, and dependency analysis can support a causal claim where operations allow them.

A second attribution problem arises inside long trajectories, which this incident is too short to show. When a trajectory fails only at its final acceptance check, the postmortem faces two distinct questions. The first is locating the earliest observable mismatch, which is a replay problem: where retained inputs and boundary records support reconstruction, comparing the run step by step against a reference execution finds that divergence under a declared equivalence relation, even though divergence alone does not prove which action caused the failure. Showing which step was *responsible*, meaning that a different choice there would have passed, is a counterfactual problem whose search space is every untaken branch. That counterfactual search space grows as the branching factor raised to the task horizon ${H}$, the same exponent that drives the reliability decay of **[[01 Books/Volume III - Agentic Systems/00 Introduction/01 Introduction/V3-01.07 Reliability and Cost Over a Horizon#^eq-vol3-horizon-compounding|equation 1.1]]**. It is the credit-assignment problem of **[[01 Books/Volume III - Agentic Systems/05 Learning from Trajectories/17 Reinforcement Learning from Verifiable Rewards/V3-17.03 Foundations of Verifiable Rewards#^sec-vol3-rlvr-baselines-credit|Baselines and credit assignment]]** seen from the debugging side, with one terminal verdict and every turn a candidate for blame. With an illustrative four candidate actions per step over a fifty-step task, that space spans $4^{50} \approx 10^{30}$ paths, far beyond any practical replay budget.

Passing checks every five steps can change this arithmetic. If check coverage establishes that the defect occurred after the last passing check, and the window’s state is isolated and safely restorable, the search covers a single five-step window of $4^{5} = 1,024$ alternatives, about 20 minutes of replay at 1.2 s per check. Without those two conditions, the investigation must keep earlier steps and external changes as possible causes.

↩︎ Replaying a recorded run to its first divergence point is established in **[[01 Books/Volume III - Agentic Systems/04 The Agent Runtime/11 Durable Execution/V3-11.08 Locating a Divergence#^sec-vol3-persistence-non-determinism|Locating a Divergence]]**.

Digital systems, the scope of this book, routinely produce effects that cannot be restored: spent compute and API budgets, third-party financial transactions, messages sent to people, and credentials leaked to external logs. Physical actuation adds sensing, kinematics, continuous dynamics, and hard real-time deadlines, which lie beyond this book’s scope. Software agents are nonetheless not confined to reversible simulation. Both kinds of system act across the causal boundary into a world whose state cannot be rolled back by terminating a process, and in both the engineer must catalog what state can be observed, what actions can be controlled, what effects can be restored, and which residual risks escalate to an accountable human principal.

These boundaries define the scope of an automated acceptance decision and the residual duties it leaves behind, and setting that scope is an engineering responsibility. Models and runtimes can propose plans, run diagnostic checks, and optimize candidate actions, but they cannot assume organizational accountability. Deciding which failure modes are acceptable, which invariants must hold, and which operational risks require human oversight remains the non-delegable duty of authorized system architects.
