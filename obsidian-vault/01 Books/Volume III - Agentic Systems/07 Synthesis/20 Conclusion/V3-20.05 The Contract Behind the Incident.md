# V3-20.05 The Contract Behind the Incident

[[Home|Home]] · [[01 Books/Volume III - Agentic Systems/Volume 3 Overview|Volume Overview]] · [[01 Books/Volume III - Agentic Systems/07 Synthesis/20 Conclusion/V3-20 Conclusion|V3-20 Conclusion]]

^sec-vol3-conclusion-envelope

When the latency alert fires, the runtime starts a diagnostic workflow, not an open-ended authorization to modify the production database. An unconstrained loop could restore query throughput by disabling secondary index updates or relaxing integrity constraints, trading durability for speed. The deployment therefore grounds the workflow in an adopted task contract that separates the desired service outcome from the mechanisms an agent may use to reach it.

### The Incident’s Five-Part Contract

^sec-vol3-envelope-contract-formalism

Before dispatching any diagnostic query or rollback on the database, the runtime must know which settings may change, which endpoints may be read, and which metric window closes the ticket. The five contract components bind the incident to an identified service, explicit permission boundaries, and named decision authorities ([[01 Books/Volume III - Agentic Systems/07 Synthesis/20 Conclusion/V3-20.05 The Contract Behind the Incident#^tbl-vol3-contract-decomposition|table 3]]).

↩︎ The five-part task contract governing an agentic workload is established in **[[01 Books/Volume III - Agentic Systems/00 Introduction/01 Introduction/V3-01.06 Invariant Closure#^sec-vol3-intro-five-part-contract|The five-part task contract]]**.

| Contract field                                         | Incident-specific commitment                                                                                                                                    | Owner or supporting record                                               |
|:-------------------------------------------------------|:----------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------|:-------------------------------------------------------------------------|
| Goal, ${G}$                                            | Restore acceptable query latency for `db_primary_01` without violating the required error-rate and change-policy conditions; leave unrelated settings unchanged | `incident_contract_v1`, adopted by the authorized task owner             |
| Environment, ${\mathcal{E}_{\text{env}}}$              | Operate on the identified live service; treat `config_v7` as the expected baseline, not as a promise that the service remains unchanged                         | Configuration source and its versioned operation interface               |
| Permitted actions, ${\mathcal{A}_{\text{perm}}}$       | Initially perform diagnostics under `read_scope_42`; apply the exact proposed settings only after the required current approval and dispatch checks             | `change_request_42`, `approval_42`, and the runtime’s action gate        |
| Available observations, ${\mathcal{O}_{\text{avail}}}$ | Return authorized, bounded diagnostic results with source, version, capture time, and omission information                                                      | Tool result records and `context_manifest_42`                            |
| Completion criteria, ${\mathcal{K}_{\text{comp}}}$     | Confirm the authorized operation’s outcome, the required service metrics over the stated window, and the applicable change-policy checks                        | `op_42`, `health_window_42`, and the adopted decision in `acceptance_42` |

Table 3: **The Incident Contract**: The same five components connect this delegated task directly to its operational permissions, evidence records, and accountable owners. Merely identifying a record name does not establish that its underlying validation checks have passed.

^tbl-vol3-contract-decomposition

The goal states process constraints as well as target metrics, because whether an outcome is acceptable depends on how it was reached. Here the constraints are an approval binding and a version precondition, and the contract places the precondition in the endpoint. An endpoint without an atomic expected-version check cannot host this workflow safely, however often the runtime re-reads the baseline configuration, because a concurrent write can still land between the last read and the update. The observation bounds are equally strict. Under the read grant the agent may query telemetry from designated endpoints but not customer rows, payload logs, or external destinations, and tool output arrives truncated and flagged, with the full output kept in a separate log. A filter that strips known keys cannot prove an output free of sensitive data or injected instructions, so protection comes from limiting what the agent reads, where its context flows, and which endpoints receive its output.

↩︎ Head-and-tail byte budgets and explicit truncation notices for tool output are established in **[[01 Books/Volume III - Agentic Systems/03 Tool Use/07 Tool Calling/V3-07.07 Observation Stream Truncation#^sec-vol3-tool-calling-bounded-capture|Bounded output capture]]**.

### Exposures and Envelope Bounds

^sec-vol3-envelope-four-questions

The introduction placed the remediation agent at a horizon of tens of turns, mutable shared production state (${S}_3$), and changes that cannot always be compensated (${A}_3$), and this incident sits exactly there. Each exposure sets an obligation. Diagnosis needs finite allowances for model and tool calls, live service state needs source-aware tracking, and the step from diagnostic reads to a production change needs an explicit escalation of authority. The envelope attached to the incident contract applies the harness’s trajectory budgets: caps on turns, wall-clock time, observation size, disk, and spend, with the liability of each admitted call reserved in advance and a reserve kept for the health check and recovery.

↩︎ Multi-dimensional budget vectors and the advance reservation of each admitted call’s liability are established in **[[01 Books/Volume III - Agentic Systems/04 The Agent Runtime/10 The Agent Harness/V3-10.10 Budgets and Ceilings#^sec-vol3-controlplane-accounting|Budgets and Ceilings]]**.

Priced on the remediation workload profile used for this lighthouse in **[[01 Books/Volume III - Agentic Systems/06 Agents at Scale/19 Agent Economics/V3-19.10 Cost per Accepted Task by Archetype#^sec-vol3-agent-economics-synthesis|Cost per Accepted Task by Archetype]]**, one attempt on this incident runs about 10 turns and costs \$20.45: \$0.15 of model calls, \$0.10 of telemetry queries and change tools, \$0.20 for the health-window check, and \$20 of on-call review for the approval. At 70 percent acceptance, each accepted incident costs \$29.21 (**[[01 Books/Volume III - Agentic Systems/00 Introduction/01 Introduction/V3-01.07 Reliability and Cost Over a Horizon#^eq-vol3-cost-per-accepted-task|equation 1.2]]**). The model accounts for 0.7 percent of the attempt, while operator approval takes 97.8 percent of the money and 50 percent of the 30-minute time to mitigate. This profile sets a threshold for additional diagnosis rather than establishing superiority over a fixed workflow. If all other expected attempt costs remain unchanged, doubling the model-call charges raises attempt cost by its existing model share, 0.7 percent. The added calls lower cost per accepted task only if acceptance rises by a greater relative fraction under the same contract and latency limits; that gain must be measured.

The same profile shows where the danger lies. A wrong change, proposed in 4 percent of attempts and costing \$12,000 when it fully commits, carries an expected irreversible loss of \$480 per attempt if no check stands ahead of the write (**[[01 Books/Volume III - Agentic Systems/04 The Agent Runtime/12 Failure Recovery/V3-12.06 Irreversible Commitments#^eq-vol3-expected-irreversible-loss|equation 12.5]]**), about 23× the cost of the attempt itself. For this workload, then, the envelope’s caps on turns and spend matter less than its gates: the approval binding, the expected-version check, and the health window that bounds how long a wrong change runs before anyone reverses it. The contract also makes idempotency, request deduplication, and an operation-status lookup prerequisites for admitting any remediation workload, so that a timeout leads the runtime to ask the endpoint for the operation’s state rather than issue a duplicate mutation ([[01 Books/Volume III - Agentic Systems/07 Synthesis/20 Conclusion/V3-20.07 Execution, Settlement, and Recovery#^sec-vol3-conclusion-execution|section 20.6]]).

### Operating-Envelope Decisions

^sec-vol3-envelope-boundaries

Suppose the run reaches its deadline while the status of the rollback is still being reconciled. Extending the deadline because the model predicts imminent success violates the envelope, and marking the operation failed fabricates an unverified outcome. The runtime instead halts further admission, logs the expiration, and preserves the pending operation identifier with its reconciliation quota, while the operations owner remains responsible for the orphaned operation. Staying within the turn and token limits proves nothing about recovery either, because resource accounting and acceptance are separate boundaries: mutating the service, verifying its health, and closing the ticket are distinct milestones that each require their own evidence. Sizing those limits is therefore a trade-off between blast radius and completion rate, since tighter ceilings bound exposure at the risk of halting a viable repair, whereas looser ones grant latitude that requires broader human oversight. Even within a well-sized envelope, however, those checks hold only if the state and evidence feeding each step stay aligned as the service changes underneath the run.
