# V3-20.12 Fallacies and Pitfalls

[[Home|Home]] · [[01 Books/Volume III - Agentic Systems/Volume 3 Overview|Volume Overview]] · [[01 Books/Volume III - Agentic Systems/07 Synthesis/20 Conclusion/V3-20 Conclusion|V3-20 Conclusion]]

^sec-vol3-conclusion-fallacies

Accepting an automated remediation safely depends on keeping four concerns apart: capability, enforcement, evidence, and accountability. Even when the model proposes an accurate diagnosis and a valid fix, each answers a different question, and conflating them lets a locally successful component close the task before the environment has verified the result.

**Fallacy**: *A sufficiently capable model removes the need for explicit runtime boundaries.*

Deploying a more capable model improves diagnostic accuracy and produces fewer malformed actions, but training suppresses the rate of violating proposals without driving it to zero. Over multi-step operational horizons, compound probability amplifies whatever residual risk remains, as captured by **[[01 Books/Volume III - Agentic Systems/00 Introduction/01 Introduction/V3-01.07 Reliability and Cost Over a Horizon#^eq-vol3-horizon-compounding|equation 1.1]]**. Even with a residual violation rate of only 0.1 percent per step, 4.9 percent of 50-turn trajectories generate at least one violating proposal. Across 10,000 daily trajectories in production, that tiny fraction yields roughly 488 hazardous proposals each day. Explicit runtime boundaries determine whether each proposal is safely intercepted or permitted to produce side effects on live infrastructure. In the incident, even an accurate restoration proposal still requires strict approval bindings and version checks; model confidence can never authorize the rollback.

**Pitfall**: *Treating passing checks as proof of the entire completion claim.*

![[90 Assets/Volume III/20 Conclusion/Vol3 Conclusion Passing Set b367c807d5.svg]]

*Two stacked vertical bars on the same 0 to 100 percent height, joined by an arrow labeled check. The left bar, Candidates, is 70 percent red (invalid) over 30 percent green (valid). The right bar, Passing set, has a thin red top segment labeled 11.5 percent invalid over a large green valid remainder.*

*An accurate check shrinks the invalid share from 70 percent to about one in nine, not to zero.*

Observing acceptable service latency does not prove that an approved operation occurred, because external traffic might have simply subsided. Conversely, confirming that a configuration change took effect does not guarantee that the service has returned to healthy operation. A valid completion certificate, such as the acceptance record, must assemble evidence of both correct execution and restored service behavior, alongside all mandatory policy checks. Passing a check is also weaker assurance than the check’s accuracy suggests, because under verifier precision (**[[01 Books/Volume III - Agentic Systems/01 The Model/03 Test-Time Compute/V3-03.05 Verifying Candidates#^eq-vol3-verifier-precision|equation 3.3]]**) the base rate dominates. If only 30 percent of proposed candidate actions are valid, a verifier that misses 10 percent of valid proposals while mistakenly passing 5 percent of invalid ones still leaves 11.5 percent of its passing set invalid. This is the verification asymmetry (principle [[01 Books/Volume III - Agentic Systems/01 The Model/V3 Model Principles#^pri-vol3-verification-asymmetry|3]]) in numbers; high nominal accuracy does not ensure correctness when candidate quality is low. A defensible safety case therefore documents the scope and blind spots of each check and assigns an owner to any residual monitoring.

**Fallacy**: *An intervention is justified because it is the next rung of the intervention ladder.*

The intervention ladder organizes mitigation strategies by ascending operational cost and decreasing reversibility, but stepping to the next rung does not ensure that the action addresses the underlying defect. A stale context entry, an unconstrained retry loop, and an inaccurate model diagnosis call for different remedies, and escalating to a heavier mechanism adds cost whether or not it fixes the problem. For example, adapting a smaller model (priced in **[[01 Books/Volume III - Agentic Systems/06 Agents at Scale/19 Agent Economics/V3-19.09 Pricing the Choice of Architecture#^sec-vol3-selection-adaptation-payback|Payback of an adaptation investment]]**) demands a one-time investment in data, training, and evaluation that is recovered only over a large volume of reviews, and the payback period lengthens in proportion when review volume falls short of plan. That investment yields zero return if the operational failure actually resided in prompt context or the retry policy. Interventions should follow directly from attributed cause, comparative outcome evidence, and total lifecycle costs; model fine-tuning and operational delegation remain specialized tools rather than automatic defaults.

**Pitfall**: *Allowing the component producing a result to redefine the conditions for accepting it.*

A model can identify an unstated requirement, draft a new integration test, or propose revising an impractical service-level objective, but it never holds the authority to shorten the health window, expand its own permissions, or certify its own completion claim. Redefining acceptance to declare success early also carries a statistical penalty, because halving the observation window halves the metric samples. For approximately independent measurements, this reduction widens the confidence interval on the service latency estimate by a factor of 1.41. The revised claim then rests on weaker evidence at the moment the incident is hardest to verify. Modifying acceptance criteria requires explicit sign-off from the designated system authority, backed by an audit record detailing which contract version governs the work. Delegating automated reasoning or execution to an agent never transfers the ultimate responsibility for accepting the result.
