# V3-20.03 The Principles of the Book

[[Home|Home]] · [[01 Books/Volume III - Agentic Systems/Volume 3 Overview|Volume Overview]] · [[01 Books/Volume III - Agentic Systems/07 Synthesis/20 Conclusion/V3-20 Conclusion|V3-20 Conclusion]]

^sec-vol3-conclusion-principle-table

Across Parts I through VI, each invariant was derived in the subsystem that owns it, and [[01 Books/Volume III - Agentic Systems/07 Synthesis/20 Conclusion/V3-20.03 The Principles of the Book#^tbl-vol3-principle-table|table 1]] collects all fifteen in one reference organized by the three H·S·A exposures, pairing each principle with its governing equation or structural contract and the design consequence it predicts. Part I prices an isolated model call and states the statistical limits of verification. Parts II, III, and IV build closure for the three exposures a single call cannot manage on its own: state carried across turns, authority over external systems, and a horizon over which errors compound. Part V changes the policy weights without expanding authority, since fine-tuning alters which trajectories the model proposes but grants no new access. Part VI multiplies all three exposures across sub-agents. Invariant closure anchors the table, because it marks the one thing computation cannot certify for itself, the task contract that defines acceptance. The exposures also interact; persisting state over a long horizon requires durable write-ahead logging, and delegation carries state and authority across agent boundaries.

These principles bound every performance optimization. KV cache prefix reuse saves the same compute whether or not the cached text still describes the live service, so a reused prefix holding a stale diagnostic snapshot is cheap and wrong at once; freshness has to come from the source’s read or version contract, never from the cache. A smaller, faster model has economic value only if its acceptance rate and tail latency still meet the service level objective. Post-training and delegation remain elective interventions, justified only by matched comparison and cost-per-accepted-task accounting.

↩︎ Cost per accepted task, measured under a declared contract, checker, and environment, as the unit for comparing configurations is established in **[[01 Books/Volume III - Agentic Systems/06 Agents at Scale/19 Agent Economics/V3-19.03 Cost per Accepted Task#^sec-vol3-agent-economics-accounting|Cost per Accepted Task]]**.

**Checkpoint 20.1: Reading the principle table as a design tool**

The equations in the principle table give numbers for sizing and evaluating real architectures.

**Horizon budget**: Using **[[01 Books/Volume III - Agentic Systems/00 Introduction/01 Introduction/V3-01.07 Reliability and Cost Over a Horizon#^eq-vol3-horizon-compounding|equation 1.1]]**, what per-step violation rate keeps the chance of any unchecked violation over ${H}$ turns below a target violation probability, and how must that rate change when ${H}$ doubles?

**Acceptance vs. price**: By **[[01 Books/Volume III - Agentic Systems/00 Introduction/01 Introduction/V3-01.07 Reliability and Cost Over a Horizon#^eq-vol3-cost-per-accepted-task|equation 1.2]]**, compare cutting the cost per attempt by a fraction ${\delta}$ with raising acceptance by the same relative fraction. Which lowers cost per accepted task more, and why does a token discount that touches only part of the attempt cost lose ground to the acceptance gain?

**Pass rate vs. acceptance**: A change raises the verifier’s pass rate but leaves acceptance under protected checks unchanged. Which two rows of the principle table explain why cost per accepted task does not fall?

The table states what each mechanism guarantees in general. A single incident shows what it takes for all of them to hold at once.
