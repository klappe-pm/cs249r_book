# V3-19.06 Model Cascades and Routing

[[Home|Home]] · [[01 Books/Volume III - Agentic Systems/Volume 3 Overview|Volume Overview]] · [[01 Books/Volume III - Agentic Systems/06 Agents at Scale/19 Agent Economics/V3-19 Agent Economics|V3-19 Agent Economics]]

^sec-vol3-agent-economics-cascades

Critical-path analysis shows where faster execution shortens a task, while routing decides which model configuration should attempt the work in the first place. Sending every ticket to a frontier model overpays on routine code transformations that a small, specialized model can complete at negligible cost, whereas sending every ticket to the small model fails on complex, ambiguous system bugs and multiplies retry and triage costs. A **model cascade**[[01 Books/Volume III - Agentic Systems/06 Agents at Scale/19 Agent Economics/V3-19.16 Footnotes#^fn4|⁴]] evaluates a task through a sequence of configurations, beginning with an inexpensive tier and escalating only when necessary; FrugalGPT formalized this architecture for language model APIs ([[01 Books/Volume III - Agentic Systems/06 Agents at Scale/19 Agent Economics/V3-19.15 Source References#^ref-chen2023frugalgpt|L. Chen et al. 2023]]). The economic value of a cascade depends directly on three operational quantities: the fraction of requests resolved by cheaper tiers, the overhead of their failed attempts, and the difficulty distribution of tasks that escalate.

Every exit from a cascade must satisfy the same declared acceptance contract. A schema check suffices for a purely syntactic transformation, but it cannot establish that a source patch preserves authentication behavior. An inexpensive tier never earns the right to dilute verification; a tier’s price neither strengthens nor weakens the contract. Passing an assessment gate yields an accepted candidate record, while publication and tool execution remain separate runtime decisions governed by effect authorization.

The price spread a router can exploit is wide, and it does not track capability closely. A long-horizon software benchmark runs each configuration five times on every task under a 20-hour budget per trial and reports the mean score, the best and worst of the five, and the average cost and duration of a trial ([[01 Books/Volume III - Agentic Systems/06 Agents at Scale/19 Agent Economics/V3-19.15 Source References#^ref-frontierswe2026|FrontierSWE 2026]]). [[01 Books/Volume III - Agentic Systems/06 Agents at Scale/19 Agent Economics/V3-19.06 Model Cascades and Routing#^fig-vol3-cost-vs-score-long-horizon|Figure 3]] plots those results against cost.

![[90 Assets/Volume III/19 Agent Economics/Cell 10 Output 1 258257e366.png]]

Figure 3: **Cost per Trial Barely Tracks Score**: Mean score over five trials against the average cost of one trial, on a logarithmic cost axis, for 19 configurations on long-horizon software tasks. Bars run from the worst to the best of the five trials, and parentheses give average hours per trial. Points at similar cost differ by tens of points, and the highest score sits an order of magnitude to the right of nearly equal ones. Data from FrontierSWE ([[01 Books/Volume III - Agentic Systems/06 Agents at Scale/19 Agent Economics/V3-19.15 Source References#^ref-frontierswe2026|2026]]) via Epoch AI ([[01 Books/Volume III - Agentic Systems/06 Agents at Scale/19 Agent Economics/V3-19.15 Source References#^ref-epochai2026benchmarks|2026]]).

^fig-vol3-cost-vs-score-long-horizon

Cost per trial spans \$9 to \$1,030 across the 19 configurations, and a single trial runs from 1 to 19 hours. The highest-scoring configuration costs 10× as much per trial as the runner-up for 3 points, while the median gap between a configuration’s best and worst trial is 21 points. Price is therefore a poor proxy for score, and the variation between repeated trials is larger than the difference between the top configurations. That is the opening a router or cascade exploits, and it is also why each tier’s acceptance must be measured on the tasks that actually reach it rather than read from its price.

### Direct Routing and Conditional Escalation

^sec-vol3-agent-economics-direct-routing-escalation

Choosing a model tier before generation risks assigning a complex task to an underpowered model or a routine query to an expensive frontier model, whereas trying a cheap tier first always pays for the failed attempt whenever the task has to escalate. A **direct router** makes the upfront choice by inspecting incoming task features prior to generation. A cascade instead speculatively executes an initial attempt on an inexpensive tier, assesses the candidate against an acceptance contract, and escalates only when that attempt fails.

For a three-tier cascade, let ${q}_j$ denote the conditional probability of acceptance at tier ${j}$, given that the request reached tier ${j}$. Assume every task not accepted at a lower tier continues to the next tier, with no additional retry, cancellation, or stopping branch. The **reach probabilities**, the probability that a task arrives at each tier, are ${r}_1=1$, ${r}_2=1-{q}_1$, and ${r}_3=(1-{q}_1)(1-{q}_2)$. This product reflects the probability chain rule rather than an assumption that tiers fail independently. Because later tiers receive only the tasks earlier tiers failed, a difficulty-filtered remainder, their conditional acceptance rates cannot be copied from full-workload benchmarks.

Let ${c^{\text{tier}}}_j$ be the mean generation and associated execution cost conditional on reaching tier ${j}$, excluding its gate, and let ${g}_j$ be that gate’s mean cost. Let ${t}_j$ and ${v}_j$ be the corresponding mean durations. Real systems must budget for prompt caching, sandbox preparation, and environment cleanup within these terms rather than pricing output tokens alone. Linearity of expectation gives

$$
\mathbb{E}[{C_{\text{cascade}}}]=\sum_{j=1}^{3}{r}_j({c^{\text{tier}}}_j+{g}_j), \qquad {p_{\text{acc}}}=\sum_{j=1}^{3}{r}_j{q}_j.
$$

For a serial cascade where all elapsed intervals are represented, the mean duration is

$$
\mathbb{E}[{T_{\text{cascade}}}]=\sum_{j=1}^{3}{r}_j({t}_j+{v}_j).
$$

The final tier must pay for its verification gate as well. With deterministic stage durations, a task reaching every tier takes $\sum_j({t}_j+{v}_j)$; with variable durations and queueing, characterizing tail percentiles requires the measured empirical distribution. A policy that stops early or leaves assessments unresolved uses its actual reach probabilities and reports those outcomes separately.

The planning metric of cost per accepted task is $\mathbb{E}[{C_{\text{cascade}}}]/{p_{\text{acc}}}$, which amortizes the cost of every reached tier across accepted outcomes rather than crediting only successful paths. Evaluating a direct frontier policy requires measuring its baseline acceptance rate across the full target cohort, since its performance on that general distribution is a different quantity from its rate on the filtered escalation subset.

**Napkin Math 19.6: Price the accepted result at every exit**

**Problem:** In a workload of 100,000 code-editing tasks, should an engineering team deploy a multi-tier cascade or route all requests directly to a frontier model to minimize cost per accepted result, and what latency penalty does the cascade incur?

**Variables:** Each candidate output undergoes an identical verification check. In order of tiers, the cascade’s model invocation and execution costs (excluding gating) are \$0.0004, \$0.0020, \$0.0600 per reached tier, with execution times of 0.25 s, 0.80 s, 4.50 s. Evaluating the verification gate costs \$0.0010 and consumes 0.40 s at each reached tier, including the final one. These figures account for container setup, execution, and cleanup overhead. This baseline assumes zero queueing delay and immediate settlement upon completion.

The cascade resolves 60,000 tasks at tier 1. Of the 40,000 tasks advancing to tier 2, it resolves 28,000. Of the 12,000 tasks advancing to tier 3, it resolves 11,400. The resulting conditional acceptance rates are 60 percent, 70 percent, 95 percent, respectively. In comparison, a direct-to-frontier policy resolves 97,000 tasks on an identical cohort. This full-workload rate is an independent baseline parameter rather than an extrapolation from the cascade’s filtered residual. It is lower than the cascade’s total acceptance even though the cascade’s top tier is the frontier model. That is because the cheaper tiers solve some tasks the frontier model fails (the tiers’ failure sets differ), and every escalated task gets another attempt under the same gate.

**Math:** The cascade successfully resolves 99,400 tasks (99.4 percent) and leaves 600 unresolved. Across the entire cohort, the cascade expends \$992, which is \$0.00992 per initiated task and \$0.00998 per accepted result. By contrast, direct frontier routing expends \$6,100, or \$0.06289 per accepted result. Lower-tier cascade attempts must be paid for regardless of whether their candidates pass verification.

Under these deterministic execution times, the cascade averages 1.718 s per task, but tasks that fail all the way through require 6.75 s. Direct frontier routing requires 4.90 s, including verification.

**Systems insight**: Direct frontier dispatch costs 6.3× as much per accepted result as the cascade because 60 percent of all tasks exit at tier 1, where execution and verification costs represent a negligible fraction of a frontier call. The cascade’s higher acceptance contributes only the ratio of the two accepted counts, 99,400 to 97,000. Nearly all of the gap is spend per initiated task. The cascade trades latency for cost. Tasks that escalate through all tiers accumulate only \$0.0044 in lower-tier tolls, but they incur 6.75 s of serialized latency compared to 4.90 s under direct dispatch. Because cost savings scale with the early exit rate, the key architectural lever is hybrid routing. Dispatching tasks predicted to fail early stages directly to the frontier tier eliminates the latency penalty for time-sensitive tasks while capturing cascade savings across the remaining workload.

The saving comes from the shape of the filter rather than from any one tier’s price. Reach falls tier by tier because accepted results exit where they are produced, while every reached gate charges its assessment whether or not its candidate passes ([[01 Books/Volume III - Agentic Systems/06 Agents at Scale/19 Agent Economics/V3-19.06 Model Cascades and Routing#^fig-vol3-model-cascade-flowchart|figure 4]]). The same structure explains the latency penalty, since a task that escalates through every tier pays every gate’s duration in series.

### Gate Errors and the Decision to Continue

^sec-vol3-agent-economics-gate-errors

When a cheap tier-1 lint check accepts a patch that breaks authentication in production, the cascade cost log records a sub-cent exit while the engineering team pays to diagnose and roll back an outage. Cost accounting records gate verdicts even when a gate is mistaken, because an observed accepted result is not automatically a correct result across every operational dimension. To price downstream consequences, define $\text{FA}$ as the event that an initiated task is accepted by the gate yet violates an independently assessed operational criterion. Define ${C_{\text{harm}}}$ as its additional harm or remediation cost not already counted in the execution costs. The expected cost per initiated task including this risk term is

$$
\mathbb{E}[{C_{\text{with\ harm}}}] =\mathbb{E}[{C_{\text{cascade}}}]+\Pr(\text{FA})\,\mathbb{E}[{C_{\text{harm}}}\mid \text{FA}].
$$

The harm term complements the cost per accepted task rather than redefining it, with its probability evaluated over initiated tasks. If an engineering team measures a false-accept fraction solely among accepted results, they must multiply that fraction by the overall acceptance probability to recover $\Pr(\text{FA})$. Similarly, the false acceptance rate of **[[01 Books/Volume III - Agentic Systems/01 The Model/03 Test-Time Compute/V3-03.05 Verifying Candidates#^sec-vol3-test-time-compute-check-precision|How base rates limit check precision]]**, evaluated exclusively among invalid candidates, relies on a different conditioning denominator. Catastrophic or irreparable consequences must remain constrained by strict operational authority and safety limits, regardless of their expected monetary value.

When escalating across tiers, the runtime should pass bounded diagnostic evidence and the identity of the failed candidate, treated as untrusted input, rather than forwarding the full conversational transcript. Forwarding full failure transcripts inflates prompt token spend at the expensive tier and risks biasing the stronger model to repeat earlier mistakes. The orchestration layer must also distinguish pending or unavailable assessments from definitive rejections, ensuring that transient infrastructure faults do not trigger unnecessary tier escalation. A passing assessment yields an accepted-candidate record; publication still verifies current dependencies, operational authority, and required effect authorization before applying changes.

A learned router can estimate the probability and cost of each eligible remaining policy from offline evaluation data, for example by training on preference comparisons between stronger and weaker models ([[01 Books/Volume III - Agentic Systems/06 Agents at Scale/19 Agent Economics/V3-19.15 Source References#^ref-ong2024routellm|Ong et al. 2024]]). However, training records reflect only the configurations that were actually executed; logs collected under a selective routing policy omit counterfactual outcomes for untried alternatives.[[01 Books/Volume III - Agentic Systems/06 Agents at Scale/19 Agent Economics/V3-19.16 Footnotes#^fn5|⁵]] Whatever the estimates come from, the router applies one decision rule. For a simple monetary objective with acceptance probabilities ${p_{X}},{p_{Y}}$, expected additional costs ${C_{X}},{C_{Y}}$, and outcome value ${V}$ (with rejected outcomes valued at zero), policy ${Y}$ is economically preferable whenever

$$
{V}({p_{Y}}-{p_{X}})>{C_{Y}}-{C_{X}}.
$$

For direct routing, this decision rule compares two complete end-to-end policies before execution begins. After an initial cheap attempt fails, however, the router compares the remaining escalation policy against stopping or invoking human fallback, treating already incurred spend as a sunk cost. A cheap-then-frontier policy carries its own reach-weighted cost and compound duration, not merely the cheap model’s nominal price. In all cases, the decision rule must operate inside remaining budget, deadline, and operational authority limits; positive expected value never licenses spending past a hard limit or substitutes for explicit authorization.

System load further reshapes this comparison. Waiting for an overloaded model tier consumes wall-clock time and holds open active execution environments, such as containerized sandboxes. When the queue for an inexpensive tier backs up, routing a task directly to an idle frontier model can yield lower total cost and lower wall-clock latency, even at a higher nominal inference price. Production routers must therefore incorporate real-time queue depths and resource contention metrics into their execution-cost and completion-time estimates, linking dispatch decisions directly to underlying queuing and service capacity models.
