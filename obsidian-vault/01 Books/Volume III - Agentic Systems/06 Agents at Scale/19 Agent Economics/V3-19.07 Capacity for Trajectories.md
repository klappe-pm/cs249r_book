# V3-19.07 Capacity for Trajectories

[[Home|Home]] · [[01 Books/Volume III - Agentic Systems/Volume 3 Overview|Volume Overview]] · [[01 Books/Volume III - Agentic Systems/06 Agents at Scale/19 Agent Economics/V3-19 Agent Economics|V3-19 Agent Economics]]

^sec-vol3-agent-economics-capacity

Admitting one 20-minute repair trajectory per minute holds 20 sandboxes open concurrently while sending 60 model calls per minute to the inference pool. Sizing an agent platform therefore differs from sizing a stateless web service. Work enters the platform as a multi-step trajectory, but the operations that saturate hardware consist of individual model calls, tool executions, and resource leases, each with its own arrival rate, holding interval, and concurrency limit. A routing policy shapes the traffic arriving at each downstream model, tool sandbox, and external API, and capacity planning determines whether those services can sustain the offered load within strict latency and resource budgets.

### Residence, Service, and Quota

^sec-vol3-agent-economics-residence-service-quota

A trajectory suspended for five minutes on an integration test or human approval consumes zero model-accelerator cycles while continuing to occupy a container lease and retained KV-cache memory. Because each resource is held over a different interval, a single concurrency counter cannot size the platform. In this capacity model, each admitted trajectory executes one distinct task policy, including its internal retries and delegated calls. For a stable population with finite long-run averages, Little’s law (theorem [5.1](https://mlsysbook.ai/vol3/05_kv_cache/05_kv_cache.html#thrm-05-littles-law)) relates the mean number of admitted trajectories present, ${N_{\text{traj}}}$, to their admission rate ${\lambda_{\text{traj}}}$ and mean time from admission to departure, $\mathbb{E}[{T_{\text{traj}}}]$:

$$
{N_{\text{traj}}} = {\lambda_{\text{traj}}}\mathbb{E}[{T_{\text{traj}}}]. \tag{4}
$$

^eq-vol3-trajectory-concurrency

↩︎ Little’s law, and the residency tax that a tool wait imposes on KV-cache occupancy, are established in **[[01 Books/Volume III - Agentic Systems/02 Agent Memory/05 KV Cache Management/V3-05.09 Capacity for Agent Workloads#^sec-vol3-kv-workload-dynamics|Residence time and service rate]]**.

Applying Little’s law requires rigorous accounting of system boundaries. Trajectories rejected at admission never enter the system population, whereas admitted trajectories that eventually fail or abort contribute fully to concurrency while active. Little’s law must be applied separately at each resource boundary (sandbox leases, retained key-value cache memory, active model inference workers, and pending external API calls), with the arrival rate and holding interval that govern that boundary.

↪ Little’s law applied per resource boundary to trajectories with tool waits, contrasting live trajectories with the attention-state memory they retain, is developed in **[[05 Reference/Volume III - Agentic Systems/V3 Inference Cost Foundations#^sec-vol3-appendix-inference-queueing|Queueing for Trajectories]]**.

Because Little’s law governs only long-run expectations, engineering a platform for high percentiles requires analyzing the underlying occupancy distributions. Peak concurrency and latency tail buffers depend heavily on state-retention policies and burst dynamics. Under stable flow, the overall demand for model calls depends on the average number of invocations per admitted trajectory, including internal retries, reflection loops, and delegated sub-agent calls: ${\lambda_{\text{arr}}}={\lambda_{\text{traj}}}\mathbb{E}[{N_{\text{call}}}]$. In practice, these model invocations rarely arrive as smooth Poisson streams. When an agent cohort encounters a shared external bottleneck or completes a parallel tool phase simultaneously, downstream inference clusters experience severe arrival bursts.

Task acceptance couples operational demand directly to the economic balance sheet. Delivering accepted tasks at goodput ${\mathcal{G}}$ when only an acceptance fraction ${\alpha}$ of admitted tasks succeeds requires the platform to admit

$$
{\lambda_{\text{traj}}}=\frac{{\mathcal{G}}}{{\alpha}}, \qquad {\rho_{\text{serv}}}({\alpha})=\frac{{\mathcal{G}}\,\mathbb{E}[{N_{\text{call}}}]}{{\alpha}\,{N_{\text{workers}}}{\mu}}
$$

for a pool of ${N_{\text{workers}}}$ model workers, each completing ${\mu}$ calls per unit time; ${\rho_{\text{serv}}}$ is then the fraction of the pool’s call capacity the admitted trajectories demand. The trajectory goodput ${\mathcal{G}}$ is the primary deliverable of the platform; failed or rejected tasks consume scarce cluster capacity without producing realized value.

**Definition 19.3: Trajectory goodput**

***Trajectory goodput*** is the number of distinct tasks accepted under a declared contract per unit of elapsed observation time.

1.  **Significance**: It represents the core throughput metric for sizing an agent platform. At acceptance fraction ${\alpha}$, every unit of goodput requires admitting $1/{\alpha}$ trajectories, so acceptance governs both the spending multiplier in effective cost ${C_{\text{eff}}}$ and the offered server load ${\rho_{\text{serv}}}({\alpha})$.
2.  **Distinction**: Token, call, and raw trajectory throughput measure computational work performed; trajectory goodput isolates accepted outcomes. Similarly, goodput per dollar divides the accepted count by total cost rather than elapsed observation time.
3.  **Common pitfall**: Counting redundant retries on a single task, or runs that complete outside the declared contract, as incremental goodput.

Sizing a concrete fleet from its trajectory admission rate means checking each resource boundary on its own unit of demand rather than looking for a single bottleneck counter. An admitted stream that fits comfortably inside its sandbox pool can still saturate its model workers or exhaust a provider’s token-per-minute allowance, as the steady-state fleet calculation below shows.

**Napkin Math 19.7: One arrival rate, different resource demands**

**Problem**: A hypothetical fleet has a trajectory admission rate of 1 per minute; an admitted trajectory remains present for 20 minutes on average. It makes 60 calls on average, each consuming 30,000 quota-accounted tokens on average across calls. In this scenario the provider counts input and output tokens, including cached input, toward a shared token-rate allowance of 2,000,000 tokens per minute. What mean concurrency and token demand does this imply?

**Math**: Apply [[01 Books/Volume III - Agentic Systems/06 Agents at Scale/19 Agent Economics/V3-19.07 Capacity for Trajectories#^eq-vol3-trajectory-concurrency|equation 4]] to admitted trajectories, and multiply the long-run call rate by mean tokens per call. The token average is call-weighted, not the unweighted mean of per-task averages.

**Result**: Mean trajectory concurrency is 20, while the mean call arrival rate is 60 calls per minute. The mean token demand is 1,800,000 per trajectory, giving a token rate of 1,800,000 tokens per minute and using 90 percent of the allowance. The quota alone permits a trajectory admission rate of at most 1.11 per minute at this workload mix in the long run.

**Systems insight**: The token quota sets the admission ceiling calculated here; concurrency, memory, sandbox, reviewer, and model-service limits still require their own checks. Holding goodput fixed, admissions scale as $1/{\alpha}$, so a relative drop in acceptance of 10 percent pushes mean demand to the full allowance, leaving no headroom for bursts. A model or prompt change that lowers acceptance is therefore also a capacity change. Analyzing burst fit, deadline feasibility, and sandbox residency requires validating the quota’s actual window semantics against production traces on top of these steady-state means.

Upstream provider quotas count requests, tokens, or concurrent connections across sliding windows of varying duration, and admission control must match the specific contract. A billing discount on cached input does not relax a token-rate limit, which still counts those tokens at full weight, so prefix caching lowers the bill without raising this admission ceiling. Within the platform, reserving capacity for active trajectories can prevent wasted partial work. Such admission and scheduling decisions must depend on forward-looking attributes: remaining resource requirements, deadlines, tenant fairness, and expected task value. Money already spent is a sunk cost and provides no justification on its own for prioritizing a trajectory.

↩︎ The gates that admit each model call against its provider’s quota windows are established in **[[01 Books/Volume III - Agentic Systems/04 The Agent Runtime/10 The Agent Harness/V3-10.09 Scheduling Concurrent Trajectories#^sec-vol3-agent-harness-quota-capacity-gates|Quota and capacity gates]]**.

### The Price of Queueing

^sec-vol3-agent-economics-queueing-price

In a shared platform, an agent’s model calls compete in a serving pool alongside every other active trajectory. If the serving cluster runs at 70 percent utilization, pushing utilization to 90 percent lowers the amortized hardware cost per call. The resulting throughput gains appear immediately on the infrastructure bill. The accompanying queue delays do not, because waiting time accumulates silently across every serial step of an active trajectory while sandboxes, execution environments, and attention state remain held.

The simplest model that quantifies this trade-off is a nonpreemptive, first-come-first-served queue fed by a stationary renewal stream. Interarrival times are independent and identically distributed with mean $1/{\lambda_{\text{arr}}}$, and service times ${T_{\text{svc}}}$ are independent and identically distributed with finite variance. The sequences are mutually independent, with no batching, priority preemption, abandonment, or feedback. Let ${c_{a}}^2$ and ${c_{s}}^2$ denote the squared coefficients of variation[[01 Books/Volume III - Agentic Systems/06 Agents at Scale/19 Agent Economics/V3-19.16 Footnotes#^fn6|⁶]] (variance divided by squared mean) of interarrival and service times, and let ${\rho_{\text{serv}}}={\lambda_{\text{arr}}}\mathbb{E}[{T_{\text{svc}}}]<1$ denote server utilization. For this single-server system, the **Kingman approximation**[[01 Books/Volume III - Agentic Systems/06 Agents at Scale/19 Agent Economics/V3-19.16 Footnotes#^fn7|⁷]] ([[01 Books/Volume III - Agentic Systems/06 Agents at Scale/19 Agent Economics/V3-19.15 Source References#^ref-kingman1961single|Kingman 1961]]) estimates the mean waiting time *before* service begins:

$$
\mathbb{E}[{T_{\text{queue}}}] \approx \frac{{c_{a}}^2+{c_{s}}^2}{2}\frac{{\rho_{\text{serv}}}}{1-{\rho_{\text{serv}}}}\mathbb{E}[{T_{\text{svc}}}]. \tag{5}
$$

^eq-vol3-kingman-gg1

![[90 Assets/Volume III/19 Agent Economics/Vol3 Economics Queue Wait Margin de9c3a4e8f.svg]]

*Curve of mean wait against utilization that stays low and then rises steeply. A blue dot at 70 percent utilization is labeled 7 seconds and a red dot at 90 percent is labeled 27 seconds.*

*Near saturation, a little more utilization buys a lot more waiting.*

The factor ${\rho_{\text{serv}}}/(1-{\rho_{\text{serv}}})$ explains why queueing delays escalate steeply near full utilization. [[01 Books/Volume III - Agentic Systems/06 Agents at Scale/19 Agent Economics/V3-19.07 Capacity for Trajectories#^eq-vol3-kingman-gg1|Equation 5]] is a heavy-traffic approximation to the mean rather than an exact expectation or a percentile. **[[05 Reference/Volume III - Agentic Systems/V3 Mathematical Foundations#^sec-vol3-appendix-math-queueing|Heavy-tailed queueing theory]]** derives the corresponding Pollaczek–Khinchine waiting-time formula under general service distributions, and Boon et al. ([[01 Books/Volume III - Agentic Systems/06 Agents at Scale/19 Agent Economics/V3-19.15 Source References#^ref-boon2023heavy|2023]]) provides formal derivations and error bounds. Capturing correlated agent bursts, dynamic continuous batching, worker pools, and shared quotas requires queueing networks or trace-driven discrete-event simulations. Substituting an aggregate cluster service rate into a single-server formula does not turn it into a valid multi-server model. Two workable estimates remain for a pool. When a dispatcher pins each trajectory to one endpoint, every endpoint is its own single-server queue fed by its share of the arrivals, and [[01 Books/Volume III - Agentic Systems/06 Agents at Scale/19 Agent Economics/V3-19.07 Capacity for Trajectories#^eq-vol3-kingman-gg1|equation 5]] applies per endpoint. When workers share one queue, a trace-driven simulation of the pool, or the multi-server models in the further reading, replaces the single-server estimate.

**Napkin Math 19.8: Pricing mean queue delay**

**Problem**: In the hypothetical single-server model above, mean service takes 1 second, arrival variability ${c_{a}}^2$ is 2, and service variability ${c_{s}}^2$ is 4. A waiting call holds resources costing \$0.002 per second. How do estimated mean wait and holding cost change between 70 percent and 90 percent utilization?

**Math**: Apply [[01 Books/Volume III - Agentic Systems/06 Agents at Scale/19 Agent Economics/V3-19.07 Capacity for Trajectories#^eq-vol3-kingman-gg1|equation 5]], then multiply mean wait by the stated linear holding rate. The rate covers only resources retained during the queue interval, excluding model service, tool execution, and any separate valuation of user delay.

**Result**: Estimated mean wait rises from 7 seconds to 27 seconds, and the corresponding holding charge rises from \$0.014 to \$0.054 per call.

**Systems insight**: Moving from 70 percent to 90 percent utilization buys 28.6 percent more throughput from the same server, but it multiplies mean wait by 3.9× because the ${\rho_{\text{serv}}}/(1-{\rho_{\text{serv}}})$ factor dominates near saturation. While the holding charge for an isolated call appears small, this delay compounds across every serial step of an agent trajectory. Halving the variability term $({c_{a}}^2+{c_{s}}^2)/2$, for example by smoothing bursty admissions or standardizing prompt lengths, halves the wait at any utilization and provides a cost-effective lever before purchasing reserve capacity. Sizing that reserve ultimately depends on enforcing tail latency targets at measured percentiles.

![[90 Assets/Volume III/19 Agent Economics/Vol3 Economics Tail Compounding Margin 8dd13ab42b.svg]]

*Rising curve of the probability of meeting at least one per-call tail delay against the number of serial calls, approaching a dashed line labeled certain. A red dot at 30 calls is labeled 0.26.*

*A one-in-a-hundred delay per call becomes routine across a long trajectory.*

Mean metrics also obscure how tail latency compounds across multi-step execution. If each of ${n}$ serial calls independently exceeds its 99th-percentile latency with probability ${p_{\text{tail}}}$, the trajectory encounters at least one such delay with probability $1-(1-{p_{\text{tail}}})^{n}$, which is the horizon compounding of **[[01 Books/Volume III - Agentic Systems/00 Introduction/01 Introduction/V3-01.07 Reliability and Cost Over a Horizon#^eq-vol3-horizon-compounding|equation 1.1]]** applied to latency rather than to correctness. For a trajectory of 30 serial calls, each with tail probability 0.01, the probability of experiencing at least one tail delay reaches 0.26. This is the probability of encountering a per-call tail event, not the probability of missing a trajectory deadline; the latter requires the complete duration distribution and the declared deadline. Sizing a fleet solely for acceptable per-call latency can therefore conceal end-to-end delays. This dynamic is the serial equivalent of the fan-out straggler problem in distributed systems ([[01 Books/Volume III - Agentic Systems/06 Agents at Scale/19 Agent Economics/V3-19.15 Source References#^ref-dean2013tail|Dean and Barroso 2013]]). Correlated delays, such as a shared queue that remains backlogged across successive steps, alter the exact probability but not the operational conclusion. That conclusion is to provision and schedule against trajectory-level percentiles rather than per-call means (principle [[01 Books/Volume III - Agentic Systems/06 Agents at Scale/V3 Agents at Scale Principles#^pri-vol3-trajectory-goodput|15]]).

↩︎ The parallel counterpart, an all-results barrier exposed to fan-out stragglers, is established in **[[01 Books/Volume III - Agentic Systems/06 Agents at Scale/18 Multi-Agent Coordination/V3-18.08 Bounding In-Flight Work#^sec-vol3-multiagent-hedged-requests|Hedged requests and speculative work]]**.

Integrating queueing delays completes the model-routing framework of [[01 Books/Volume III - Agentic Systems/06 Agents at Scale/19 Agent Economics/V3-19.06 Model Cascades and Routing#^sec-vol3-agent-economics-cascades|section 19.5]]. A dynamic router compares eligible *remaining paths*, each priced with its queue and service delays, retained-resource costs, verification checks, and possible escalations, under an identical acceptance contract. Because dispatching decisions alter the arrival rates that produced the initial delay estimates, the router must refresh queue projections whenever traffic shifts. Separating interactive and batch pools, or enforcing priority scheduling, protects latency-bound work at a quantifiable cost in fairness and cluster utilization.

### Dedicated Capacity Vs. Per-Token APIs

^sec-vol3-agent-economics-dedicated-vs-api

When an agent application’s token consumption becomes sustained and predictable, engineering teams must choose between paying on demand through per-token APIs and provisioning private infrastructure. Consider a dedicated deployment incurring a fixed infrastructure expense of \$28 per hour plus \$2 per million output tokens in marginal operating costs. Such private infrastructure represents **dedicated capacity**, a financial commitment paid continuously regardless of whether accelerators are busy or idle. The economic decision therefore turns entirely on sustained utilization.[[01 Books/Volume III - Agentic Systems/06 Agents at Scale/19 Agent Economics/V3-19.16 Footnotes#^fn8|⁸]] This comparison requires a strictly matched workload: equivalent task capability, acceptance criteria, data-privacy constraints, and feasible tail latency under representative traffic mixtures. A shared model architecture or nominal token rate alone does not establish equivalence.

Fix the workload’s input/output token ratio and prompt-cache hit rate. Because dedicated capacity is sized by its output generation rate, we put the API tariff on the same per-output-token basis by folding each output token’s associated input charges into a single effective price ${c_{\text{API}}}$. This effective price adds to the API’s output-token rate the workload’s input-to-output token ratio multiplied by the average input price, where that average blends the uncached and cached input rates by the prompt-cache hit rate. Let ${c_{\text{ded}}}$ be the variable cost per output token on dedicated capacity, and let ${C_{\text{fixed}}}$ be the complete hourly fixed cost of the private deployment, including amortized hardware leases, provisioned failover headroom, and operational maintenance. Shared application costs cancel only when genuinely identical across both options, whereas differential tool, verification, network egress, or support fees must enter the balance sheet. Let ${\mu_{\text{out}}}$ denote the measured feasible output token generation rate (in tokens per second) under the target latency objective, a token rate distinct from the per-worker call rate ${\mu}$ of the capacity model. Let ${u}$ represent the fraction of that reference capacity the workload actually delivers. Unlike the queueing utilization ${\rho_{\text{serv}}}$, which measures offered load against raw service capacity, ${u}$ is measured against a rate already chosen to meet the latency target, so ${u}=1$ still meets that target by construction. In one hour, the system produces $3600{\mu_{\text{out}}} {u}$ output tokens, costing $3600{\mu_{\text{out}}} {u}\,{c_{\text{API}}}$ via a pay-as-you-go API and ${C_{\text{fixed}}}+3600{\mu_{\text{out}}} {u}\,{c_{\text{ded}}}$ on dedicated hardware. When ${c_{\text{API}}}>{c_{\text{ded}}}$, the two expenditure curves cross at the **break-even utilization**

$$
{u^*} = \frac{{C_{\text{fixed}}}}{3600{\mu_{\text{out}}}({c_{\text{API}}}-{c_{\text{ded}}})}. \tag{6}
$$

^eq-vol3-breakeven-utilization

![[90 Assets/Volume III/19 Agent Economics/Vol3 Economics Breakeven Margin 2e631f99a1.svg]]

*Two cost-per-hour lines against delivered load. An orange per-token API line starts at zero and rises steeply; a blue dedicated line starts at a fixed cost and rises gently. They cross at a dot above a dashed marker labeled u star.*

*Owned capacity wins only above the load that repays its fixed cost.*

If ${u^*}>1$, the dedicated deployment cannot break even within its physical capacity envelope; if ${c_{\text{API}}}\le {c_{\text{ded}}}$ while ${C_{\text{fixed}}}>0$, self-hosting remains permanently more expensive. Execution deadlines and concurrency limits must be verified separately at the target operating point.

**Napkin Math 19.9: Pricing idle capacity and cached input**

**Problem**: Assume a hypothetical matched-capability workload uses 5 input tokens per output token, with 80 percent of inputs billed at the cached rate. API prices per million tokens are \$3 for uncached input, \$0.3 for cached input, and \$15 for output. A dedicated deployment costs \$28 per hour for its provisioned capacity and operations, plus \$2 per million output tokens for differential variable costs. Its feasible reference output rate is 1,200 tokens/s under the same mix, reserve policy, and latency target. Which alternative costs less at 52 percent of that rate?

**Math**: Weight the two input prices by their billed volume fractions, then incorporate associated input costs into ${c_{\text{API}}}$. Apply [[01 Books/Volume III - Agentic Systems/06 Agents at Scale/19 Agent Economics/V3-19.07 Capacity for Trajectories#^eq-vol3-breakeven-utilization|equation 6]]. Both options are feasible at this operating point, and shared tool, evaluation, and application costs cancel.

**Result**: Weighted input costs \$0.84 per million input tokens. Factoring in associated inputs, the API costs \$19.2 per million output tokens. The cost crossing occurs at 37.7 percent of reference capacity. At the stated load, generating 2,246,400 output tokens per hour costs \$43.13 through the API vs. \$32.49 on dedicated capacity, saving \$10.64 per hour.

**Systems insight**: The economic break-even threshold shifts directly with the API’s effective token price. Without cached input, the same workload would cross at 23.1 percent of reference capacity instead of 37.7 percent. Aggressive prefix caching by API providers erodes the economic incentive to own capacity. Teams should commit to dedicated capacity only when sustained production load reliably exceeds ${u^*}$ with the headroom demanded by latency targets. Operating below the crossing wastes capital on idle silicon, while owning hardware places queueing congestion, node failures, and capacity management entirely on internal engineering teams.

**Checkpoint 19.2: Check the capacity boundary**

Before bounding what an individual trajectory may spend, evaluate the platform capacity model:

**Residence vs. service**: Why can mean trajectory concurrency remain high while model-worker accelerator occupancy is low?

**Queue headroom**: With the variability modeled in notebook [19.8](https://mlsysbook.ai/vol3/19_agent_economics/19_agent_economics.html#nbk-18-sizing-queue-capacity-and-tail), what utilization keeps the estimated mean wait below one mean service time? How does that headroom shift if arrivals come in correlated cohorts?

**Acceptance and load**: If a model change lowers acceptance while goodput is held fixed, how does offered load ${\rho_{\text{serv}}}({\alpha})$ scale, and at what relative drop does the fleet in notebook [19.7](https://mlsysbook.ai/vol3/19_agent_economics/19_agent_economics.html#nbk-18-trajectory-residence-and-quota) exhaust its token quota?

**Cache and crossing**: Why does cached-input pricing alter the dedicated vs. API break-even crossing without changing a provider’s token-rate quota?

Capacity planning sizes the collective infrastructure required to sustain admitted workload streams. It says nothing about how much any one trajectory, or the tree of children it delegates to, may spend. That bound needs a different mechanism, a budget account that admits and settles every charge atomically.
