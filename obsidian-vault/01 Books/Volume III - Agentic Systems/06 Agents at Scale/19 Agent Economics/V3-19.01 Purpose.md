*Why can a model with a tenth of the token price make an agent cost more for every task it finishes?*

A model whose tokens cost a tenth as much looks like a large saving on the provider’s invoice and can still raise the price of every task the agent finishes. Token charges are only part of the account. An attempt also holds a sandbox open while its tools run, invokes tests and external services, waits for shared capacity, and may end in human review, and an attempt that fails pays for all of it without producing anything that ships. A cheaper model that needs more turns, retries more often, or passes assessment less often spreads those charges across fewer accepted tasks, so the bill per accepted result can rise while the token bill falls. The account that matters is kept over a cohort of tasks under one declared contract. It charges everything every attempt incurred, failed and canceled work included, to the tasks that contract accepted, and it keeps work still pending visible rather than dropping it. Reuse, retries, faster execution, model routing, added capacity, and delegation are each worth buying only when they lower that ratio or meet a deadline the objective values, and which lever matters depends on which term of the account dominates. Capacity enters the same account, because concurrent trajectories hold model servers, sandboxes, and reviewers while they wait, and an undersized pool turns waiting into cost. Horizon, state, and authority each set the closure a task must pay for, and an agent service is sustainable only when the accepted work it delivers covers that price.

**Learning Objectives**

- Calculate cohort cost per accepted task while distinguishing attempt costs, human outcomes, and unsettled liabilities
- Evaluate prefix caching, compaction, and retry purchases under explicit billing and acceptance assumptions
- Diagnose which acceleration shortens a trajectory enough to pay for itself from the model’s share of its measured critical path
- Evaluate model cascades under a common acceptance contract, including assessment costs and false-accept risk
- Size resource occupancy for a target trajectory goodput (accepted tasks per unit time) and compare provisioning choices under declared workload, quota, and queueing assumptions
- Design atomic budget reservations that preserve spending bounds across concurrent work and late settlement
- Select a loop cap or architecture by pricing automated and human-completed outcomes under one acceptance contract
