# V3-19.09 Pricing the Choice of Architecture

[[Home|Home]] · [[01 Books/Volume III - Agentic Systems/Volume 3 Overview|Volume Overview]] · [[01 Books/Volume III - Agentic Systems/06 Agents at Scale/19 Agent Economics/V3-19 Agent Economics|V3-19 Agent Economics]]

^sec-vol3-agent-economics-selection

A spending bound makes a proposed architecture admissible under its budget, but cost per accepted task determines whether it is worth deploying. When choosing among deterministic code, a fixed model-assisted workflow, a bounded loop, and multi-agent delegation, system architects often frame the decision as an ideological debate between programmatic control and model autonomy. In production systems engineering, however, this decision is an empirical optimization problem governed by an acceptance contract. The engineering objective comes first, because minimizing expected financial cost subject to a completion requirement selects a different architecture than minimizing tail latency or deadline misses under a spending ceiling.

### Design Alternatives

^sec-vol3-selection-spectrum

A fixed workflow that unconditionally calls a frontier model on every ticket can cost more than an adaptive, bounded loop that resolves simple cases on turn one with a lightweight model. A single model call proposing a database deletion likewise requires the same authorization checks as a ten-turn agent, and parallel delegated trajectories can cut wall-clock time while inflating total token consumption. Architectural alternatives differ primarily in who selects the next operation, as [[01 Books/Volume III - Agentic Systems/06 Agents at Scale/19 Agent Economics/V3-19.09 Pricing the Choice of Architecture#^tbl-vol3-architecture-spectrum|table 6]] summarizes, yet control authority alone settles neither execution cost, permitted environmental side effects, nor end-to-end latency.

↩︎ Foundational trade-offs between fixed workflows and model-directed loops are established in **[[01 Books/Volume III - Agentic Systems/00 Introduction/01 Introduction/V3-01.07 Reliability and Cost Over a Horizon#^sec-vol3-intro-workflow-vs-loop|Workflows vs. model-directed loops]]**.

| Design                        | Who selects the next operation                       | Incremental obligation to price                                                     |
|:------------------------------|:-----------------------------------------------------|:------------------------------------------------------------------------------------|
| Deterministic program         | Program control flow                                 | Maintenance, execution, assessment, and exception handling                          |
| Fixed model-assisted workflow | Program control flow around model calls              | Model usage, output validation, and fallback paths                                  |
| Bounded model-directed loop   | Model proposals admitted by the runtime              | Repeated observations, revision, stopping, and effect settlement                    |
| Delegated trajectories        | Coordinator and children within assignment contracts | Child execution, communication, integration, cancellation, and residual liabilities |

Table 6: **Architecture Choices Under One Task Contract**: The control structure changes the work to account for, not the required acceptance evidence or authorized effects.

^tbl-vol3-architecture-spectrum

Every design can carry state, require crash recovery, and demand substantive verification. A rigorous comparison therefore holds the acceptance contract strictly constant across candidate designs. Verification is neither relaxed for a single model call nor tightened for an agentic loop or a delegated system.

### Task Obligations

^sec-vol3-selection-questions

Whether a dependency upgrade runs as a deterministic script or a multi-agent tree, it still has to pass the regression suite, retain workspace state across retries, and hold authorization before merging. The H·S·A exposures that have organized this book, together with the closure evidence the contract demands, do not dictate an architecture. They name the obligations each candidate design must pay for:

- **Horizon**: A longer execution horizon expands the trajectory space, multiplying opportunities for resource consumption, transient network faults, and compounding drift. In cost accounting, these costs are priced not by hypothetical maximum depth, but by the expected distribution of steps that the runtime policy actually reaches.
- **State**: State incurs retrieval overhead, storage retention, consistency maintenance, and checkpoint recovery mechanisms wherever it resides. An adaptive loop justifies this stateful overhead only when fresh observations yield updates that substantially improve downstream decisions, generating enough value to repay their operational cost.
- **Authority**: The scope of authority determines which operations demand formal admission, evidence collection, and two-phase settlement. Irreversible actions, such as modifying financial records, sending customer emails, or deleting cloud infrastructure, require an explicit authorization and risk policy, often incorporating cryptographic audit trails or human approval gates. Any unresolved effect or outstanding external reservation remains an open liability on the account, regardless of the expected inference bill.
- **Closure evidence**: Closure evidence governs what repeated attempts can actually establish. An automated, executable check, such as a compiler, test suite, or schema validator, supplies fast, inexpensive, and highly specific feedback. The economic value of this feedback depends directly on the test harness coverage and its false-acceptance rate. When no automated check exists, an agent must seek closure through additional observations, queries to independent data sources, or escalation to human review. Human review, however, introduces its own substantial hourly costs, queueing delays, and error rates. Routing an ambiguous task to a person does not count as completing it. The task counts as completed only when the human reviewer finishes the work and fulfills the acceptance contract.

### When a Bounded Loop Beats a Single Call

^sec-vol3-selection-economics

Pricing those obligations on the dependency-upgrade service shows when a multi-turn loop earns its keep over simpler designs. The engineering team can deploy a deterministic rule-based rewriter, a single frontier model call, or a smaller, cheaper model that retries for up to three turns within a bounded loop before escalating failed tickets to a human engineer. The deterministic rewriter and the single model call each have a single static acceptance rate, whereas the loop’s overall acceptance rate and aggregate spend depend on the exact distribution of turns it reaches before stopping.

For a bounded loop, let ${q}_t$ denote the probability that turn ${t}$ produces an accepted candidate, conditional on reaching turn ${t}$ after rejections on all preceding turns. Assuming that every reached turn undergoes a complete acceptance assessment, that execution halts immediately upon acceptance, and that the runtime enforces a hard budget of ${H_{\max}}$ turns, the cumulative automated acceptance probability is:

$$
{p_{\text{loop}}}({H_{\max}}) = 1 - \prod_{t=1}^{{H_{\max}}} (1 - {q}_t) \tag{7}
$$

^eq-vol3-loop-success

As with model cascading, this formulation applies the probability chain rule rather than assuming independence across turns. The system reaches turn ${t}$ with probability ${r}_t=\prod_{j<t}(1-{q}_j)$, where ${r}_1=1$. If ${C}_t$ represents the comprehensive cost of turn ${t}$ (model inference, tool execution, sandbox lease duration, and the assessment check), the expected automated cost per incoming ticket is $\sum_{t=1}^{{H_{\max}}} {r}_t {C}_t$. When turns incur an identical per-turn cost ${C_{\text{turn}}}$, this expectation simplifies to ${C_{\text{turn}}} \sum_{t=1}^{{H_{\max}}} {r}_t$, where $\sum_{t=1}^{{H_{\max}}} {r}_t$ is the expected number of executed turns. Operational exceptions, such as test harness outages or runtime abandonment triggered by safety filters, introduce distinct terminal states that must be accounted for as separate branches rather than conflated with normal test failures.

Accounting becomes more complex when human escalation enters the operational policy. Suppose an automated tier achieves acceptance probability ${p}$ at expected cost ${c}$, and routes every rejected ticket to a human fallback workflow that costs ${C_{h}}$ and achieves conditional resolution probability ${h}$. The expected total expenditure per incoming ticket is ${c}+(1-{p}){C_{h}}$, while the combined resolution probability across automated and human tiers is ${p}+(1-{p}){h}$. Dividing expected spend by combined resolution yields the true cost per resolved ticket under the end-to-end policy:

$$
\frac{{c} + (1 - {p}) {C_{h}}}{{p} + (1 - {p}) {h}}
$$

 In contrast, the isolated metric ${c}/{p}$ captures only the cost per automated acceptance. Conflating these two metrics obscures the true cost structure of the system, particularly when automated acceptance is low. Neither ratio is well-defined when its respective denominator reaches zero. The same account sets a bounded loop’s cap. When the fallback resolves every ticket it receives (${h}=1$), the combined resolution probability in the denominator is fixed at one regardless of how many turns the loop runs, so adding turn ${t}$ changes only the numerator. A ticket reaches turn ${t}$ with probability ${r}_t$ after failing all earlier turns. Stopping before turn ${t}$ sends that entire ${r}_t$ remainder to the human fallback, whereas running turn ${t}$ pays ${r}_t{C}_t$ to resolve a fraction ${q}_t$ of that remainder automatically and avoids ${r}_t{q}_t{C_{h}}$ of fallback spend. Because ${r}_t$ multiplies both terms, it cancels out, and extending the cap to turn ${t}$ lowers expected spend per resolved ticket whenever ${q}_t{C_{h}}>{C}_t$. When the human fallback is imperfect (${h}<1$), this simple comparison no longer suffices because an extra automated turn also raises the combined resolution probability in the denominator. Even at ${h}=1$, this adjacent-cap comparison assumes immediate fallback and unchanged earlier turns rather than serving as a general stopping rule for policies with further contingent choices. The executable model in notebook [19.11](https://mlsysbook.ai/vol3/19_agent_economics/19_agent_economics.html#nbk-18-architectural-frontier-migration-under-cost) illustrates why keeping these two metrics separate is essential for sound architectural selection.

**Napkin Math 19.11: Pricing three designs for dependency upgrades**

**Problem**: A software engineering team manages an influx of 10,000 dependency-upgrade pull requests each month. Every upgrade requires code modifications that must pass an identical build and regression test suite to be accepted. The team considers three architectural options:

1.  A deterministic rule-based rewriter that costs \$0.001 per attempt before verification and achieves automated acceptance on 32 percent of tickets.
2.  A single frontier model call that costs \$0.045 before verification and accepts 68 percent of tickets.
3.  A bounded loop powered by a lightweight model that costs \$0.006 per reached turn before verification. This loop halts immediately upon acceptance or caps execution at three turns, with conditional acceptance probabilities of 0.55 on turn 1, 0.42 on turn 2, and 0.30 on turn 3.

Every candidate generated by any automated tier incurs an identical \$0.002 charge for build and regression testing. Automated costs comprehensively include model inference, process execution, sandbox container reservation, and workspace teardown. Any ticket rejected by automation escalates to a human engineer; human fallback incurs \$35 per ticket (including manual triage, debugging, and final verification sign-off) and resolves 90 percent of escalated tickets. Under these population expectations, which architecture minimizes the total cost per resolved ticket while satisfying a mandatory service floor of 95 percent combined resolution?

**Math**: Applying [[01 Books/Volume III - Agentic Systems/06 Agents at Scale/19 Agent Economics/V3-19.09 Pricing the Choice of Architecture#^eq-vol3-loop-success|equation 7]], the bounded loop achieves an automated acceptance rate of 81.73 percent while executing 1.711 turns on average per ticket. The cost breakdown in [[01 Books/Volume III - Agentic Systems/06 Agents at Scale/19 Agent Economics/V3-19.09 Pricing the Choice of Architecture#^tbl-vol3-architecture-decision|table 7]] separates automated acceptance from combined resolution, preventing expensive human resolutions from masquerading as automated successes.

| Expected quantity                                     |  Rewriter | Single call | Bounded loop |
|:------------------------------------------------------|----------:|------------:|-------------:|
| Automated spend per incoming ticket, including checks |   \$0.003 |     \$0.047 |   \$0.013688 |
| Automated spend per automated acceptance              | \$0.00937 |   \$0.06912 |    \$0.01675 |
| Resolution probability, including human fallback      |   93.200% |     96.800% |      98.173% |
| Combined spend per incoming ticket                    |  \$23.803 |    \$11.247 |   \$6.408188 |
| Combined spend per resolved ticket                    |   \$25.54 |     \$11.62 |       \$6.53 |

Table 7: **Dependency-Upgrade Decision Account**: Expected spend includes rejected attempts and unsuccessful human fallback; the automated and combined denominators represent different outcomes.

^tbl-vol3-architecture-decision

**Systems insight**: When human escalation follows automated rejection, fallback labor dominates the economics of the system. Because human handling costs \$35, each percentage point of automated acceptance saves \$0.35 in expected fallback expenditure per incoming ticket, several times the entire automated spend of any candidate architecture. Although the deterministic rewriter looks attractive under isolated automated accounting (\$0.00937 per automated acceptance), it yields the highest combined cost (\$25.54 per resolved ticket) and fails to satisfy the 95 percent resolution floor. The bounded loop wins (\$6.53 per resolved ticket) because its second and third retry turns successfully salvage rejections that would otherwise trigger human intervention. Across 10,000 monthly tickets, the loop delivers an expected total spend of \$64,081.88 for 9,817.3 successfully resolved upgrades. This ranking directly reflects the conditional acceptance yield of subsequent turns relative to human triage costs, relationships that engineering teams must empirically benchmark in production.

This steady-state model assumes that all verification jobs complete cleanly and that all resource charges settle immediately. In a live production evaluation, however, telemetry systems must track in-flight runs, timeout aborts, orphaned sandbox containers, and pending verifier verdicts at the observation cutoff, charging every initiated attempt to the cost total. Human fallback must be evaluated against the exact same task contract as automation. Human reviewers must pass the identical build and regression suite rather than being granted informal operational waivers.

Furthermore, expected cost per resolved ticket does not tell the whole operational story. The model leaves queueing dynamics, completion-time distributions, and human engineering bandwidth unmodeled. If incoming tickets arrive in sudden bursts, or if the engineering staff has fixed capacity, routing even a small fraction of failures to human triage can saturate the queue and introduce long review delays. Under stringent service-level agreements or strict staffing limits, a team may deliberately select a direct frontier model call over a bounded loop or rewriter, even at higher automated spend, when measured acceptance is higher and fewer tickets escape to human queues. In the dependency-upgrade scenario, the bounded loop has higher automated acceptance than the single call, so it also sends fewer tickets to human fallback.

Multi-agent delegation obeys this same objective-first economic evaluation when benchmarked against a capable single controller. Decomposing a complex task across a coordinator and specialized child agents pays the coordination tax (principle [[01 Books/Volume III - Agentic Systems/06 Agents at Scale/V3 Agents at Scale Principles#^pri-vol3-coordination-tax|14]]) in two places. Handoffs, context delivery, and integration on the critical path enter **[[01 Books/Volume III - Agentic Systems/06 Agents at Scale/18 Multi-Agent Coordination/V3-18.03 The Delegation Trade-Off#^eq-vol3-multiagent-speedup|equation 18.1]]** as the overhead term that holds any speedup below its serial-fraction ceiling, the same form of ceiling that capped faster inference at $1/(1-{f})$ in notebook [19.5](https://mlsysbook.ai/vol3/19_agent_economics/19_agent_economics.html#nbk-18-critical-path-investment). Token amplification and child work off the critical path enter the numerator of cost per accepted task whether or not they shorten the task. Whether a given workload repays that premium, by meeting a deadline a sequential loop would miss or by higher conditional acceptance on subproblems, is the matched delegation decision of **[[01 Books/Volume III - Agentic Systems/06 Agents at Scale/18 Multi-Agent Coordination/V3-18.10 Benchmarking Against a Single Agent#^sec-vol3-multiagent-delegation-decision|A complete delegation decision]]**. In this accounting, delegation is one more architecture priced under the same contract. Economic viability does not require an architecture to dominate every competing design on both cost and latency simultaneously. Pareto dominance is sufficient, but in real systems engineering, meeting a critical operational deadline under an admissible budget is what decides the deployment.

### Payback of an Adaptation Investment

^sec-vol3-selection-adaptation-payback

The comparisons so far priced only per-task operating spend, but replacing a general model with a smaller fine-tuned one also carries a one-time engineering bill that must be recovered before the switch saves money. Let ${C_{\text{invest}}}$ denote that one-time cost of dataset curation, training compute, and offline evaluation, and let ${C_{\text{task, base}}}$ and ${C_{\text{task, adapt}}}$ be the expected operating costs per assigned task under the baseline and adapted configurations, including failed attempts, retries, and downstream human review or recovery. Assuming stationary unit costs over the planning horizon, a positive per-task saving ${\Delta} {C_{\text{task}}} = {C_{\text{task, base}}} - {C_{\text{task, adapt}}}$ yields the continuous break-even threshold:

$$
{N^*} = \frac{{C_{\text{invest}}}}{{C_{\text{task, base}}} - {C_{\text{task, adapt}}}} = \frac{{C_{\text{invest}}}}{{\Delta} {C_{\text{task}}}}, \qquad {\Delta} {C_{\text{task}}} > 0. \tag{8}
$$

^eq-vol3-breakeven-volume

The relation is the payback horizon of context compaction ([[01 Books/Volume III - Agentic Systems/06 Agents at Scale/19 Agent Economics/V3-19.04 Context Reuse and Retry Budgets#^sec-vol3-agent-economics-compaction-price|section 19.3.2]]) moved up one scale. There a one-time summarization surcharge is recovered over the remaining calls of one trajectory; here a one-time engineering investment is recovered over the tasks of a deployment. Because tasks arrive in whole units, the investment is recovered at $\lceil {N^*} \rceil$ tasks. If the per-task saving is nonpositive (${\Delta} {C_{\text{task}}} \le 0$), the investment never breaks even on operating cost, and a more expensive configuration must be justified by quality, latency tails, a required capability, or reduced risk. If the two configurations accept different fractions of tasks, cost per assigned task misleads, and the comparison must use cost per accepted task (**[[01 Books/Volume III - Agentic Systems/00 Introduction/01 Introduction/V3-01.07 Reliability and Cost Over a Horizon#^eq-vol3-cost-per-accepted-task|equation 1.2]]**).

**Napkin Math 19.12: Conditional payback of a smaller adapted model**

**Scenario**: A cheaper model can repay its adaptation only where model tokens are a large share of each attempt, which rules out workloads whose cost is dominated by human approval or sandbox holding. Consider instead a short, high-volume workload whose token bill a smaller model does change. An engineering team is evaluating a smaller fine-tuned model for automated code review. Both configurations must meet identical acceptance criteria and latency bounds with equivalent acceptance rates, which offline and shadow evaluation must establish before any cost model can justify a rollout. Step and token figures are averages across all assigned reviews, including failed attempts and retries.

**Variables**: The generalist baseline consumes 12,000 billable input tokens and 800 billable output tokens per step across an average of 5 steps. Inference pricing stands at \$3 per million input tokens and \$15 per million output tokens. In contrast, the smaller adapted model operates with a distilled prompt consuming 1,500 input tokens and 800 output tokens across 4 steps, billed at \$1 and \$4 per million tokens, respectively. These figures assume standard on-demand token billing without prompt-caching discounts.

The upfront engineering investment entails \$4,000 for data collection and curation, \$500 for training compute, and \$8,000 for rigorous evaluation and benchmark verification, totaling an incremental expenditure of \$12,500. The expected deployment workload is 20,000 assigned reviews per month. All ancillary costs (developer tooling, human escalation, and infrastructure maintenance) are assumed equivalent across configurations and cancel out of the marginal equation.

**Math**: Executing a single step under the baseline policy costs 12,000 × \$3 plus 800 × \$15 per million tokens, yielding \$0.048 per step and an expected task cost of \$0.2400. A step under the adapted policy costs \$0.0047, totaling \$0.0188 per task. Under stationary cost assumptions, the intervention produces an incremental operational saving of \$0.2212 per assigned task. Applying [[01 Books/Volume III - Agentic Systems/06 Agents at Scale/19 Agent Economics/V3-19.09 Pricing the Choice of Architecture#^eq-vol3-breakeven-volume|equation 8]] yields a continuous break-even threshold of approximately 56,509.95 tasks. The discrete integer crossing occurs at 56,510 tasks. At steady monthly volume, this investment pays for itself in approximately 2.83 months.

**Systems insight**: Because break-even duration equals ${N^*}$ divided by monthly volume, the economic horizon is governed by deployment volume and verification overhead rather than raw training compute. Evaluation accounts for 64 percent of the initial investment, whereas training compute represents just 4 percent. On the operational side, reduced input tokens generate 79 percent of the net per-task savings, so the case depends on the adapted model staying competent under a much shorter prompt. If traffic drops to a quarter of anticipated volume (5,000 reviews per month), the payback timeline stretches to 11.3 months. These savings apply per assigned task. They translate to cost per accepted task only as long as end-to-end acceptance rates remain equal (**[[01 Books/Volume III - Agentic Systems/00 Introduction/01 Introduction/V3-01.07 Reliability and Cost Over a Horizon#^eq-vol3-cost-per-accepted-task|equation 1.2]]**). If the adapted model’s acceptance falls, the comparison must be rerun on cost per accepted task. The human escalation and review costs assumed above to cancel no longer cancel, because each rejected review now creates human work the baseline did not.

An adaptation is therefore priced like any other architecture change on the task account, with its one-time engineering cost spread over the volume it serves and its operating saving conditional on unchanged acceptance. The dependency-upgrade and code-review comparisons each priced candidate designs for a single focused task; stepping back to diverse production workloads shows how the same account behaves when different cost terms dominate the numerator.
