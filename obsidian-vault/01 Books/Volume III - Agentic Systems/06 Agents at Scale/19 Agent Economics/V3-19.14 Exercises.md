# V3-19.14 Exercises

[[Home|Home]] · [[01 Books/Volume III - Agentic Systems/Volume 3 Overview|Volume Overview]] · [[01 Books/Volume III - Agentic Systems/06 Agents at Scale/19 Agent Economics/V3-19 Agent Economics|V3-19 Agent Economics]]

^sec-vol3-agent-economics-exercises

Every given below is a hypothetical problem input rather than a price, rate, or measurement of a real system. Each problem ends in a decision, and the calculation exists to justify it.

1.  **A cheaper configuration and a moving denominator (Basic).** A repair cohort of ${N}=200$ tickets runs one bounded attempt per ticket under a fixed contract. Configuration A accepts $120$ tickets; its accepted tasks cost ${C_{\text{succ}}}=\$1.20$ on average and its nonaccepted tasks ${C_{\text{fail}}}=\$1.80$, since they run to the turn cap. For configuration B under the same contract, accepted tickets cost $30$ percent less than A’s accepted-ticket mean, and rejected tickets cost $30$ percent less than A’s nonaccepted-ticket mean.
    1.  Compute ${C_{\text{eff}}}$ for A from the cohort total and again from ${C_{\text{succ}}}+\frac{1-{\alpha}}{{\alpha}}{C_{\text{fail}}}$, and state how many nonaccepted tasks each accepted repair carries.
    2.  Find the smallest number of accepted tickets at which B costs no more per accepted task than A.
    3.  At the reporting cutoff B has $85$ accepted, $105$ rejected, and $10$ pending tickets, with the accepted and rejected groups having B’s stated conditional means. Each of the ten identified pending tickets has final charges of $\$1.26$, which are not repriced when its verdict arrives. All invoices, cleanup, and other costs are final; only acceptance verdicts remain pending. Compute the range of B’s provisional ${C_{\text{eff}}}$ over the possible verdicts, and decide what the report may claim about B against A before the pending assessments complete. If a pending operation can still incur charges, what liability evidence is needed before this range bounds the final account?
2.  **When compaction pays (Basic).** A repair trajectory holds ${S}=40{,}000$ input tokens at a decision point. Compaction would reduce it to ${S_{\text{comp}}}=8{,}000$ tokens with a summary call that emits ${K_{\text{sum}}}=2{,}000$ tokens, and the first compacted call misses the cache completely. The tariff charges ${c_{\text{in}}}=\$3$, ${c_{\text{cache}}}=\$0.30$, and ${c_{\text{out}}}=\$15$ per million tokens.
    1.  Compute the upfront increment ${U}$, the per-call saving ${s}$, and the payback horizon ${N_{\min}}$.
    2.  Five ordinary calls remain, including the first compacted one. Compute the price difference between compacting and keeping the context, and decide whether to compact on price alone.
    3.  The provider halves the cached rate to $\$0.15$ per million. Recompute ${N_{\min}}$ and decide whether compaction pays with ten calls remaining. Explain why a cheaper cache pushes the profitable compaction point earlier in a trajectory, and name two reasons other than price that could still justify compacting.
3.  **How many turns before a person takes over (Basic).** A repair loop costs ${C_{\text{turn}}}=\$0.50$ per reached turn, including assessment and sandbox holding, and stops at the first accepted candidate. Its conditional acceptance probabilities for turns one through five are $0.50$, $0.30$, $0.20$, $0.10$, and $0.01$. Every ticket the loop leaves unaccepted goes to an engineer who always resolves it for ${C_{h}}=\$30$.
    1.  Using [[01 Books/Volume III - Agentic Systems/06 Agents at Scale/19 Agent Economics/V3-19.09 Pricing the Choice of Architecture#^eq-vol3-loop-success|equation 7]], tabulate ${p_{\text{loop}}}({H_{\max}})$, the expected number of turns, and the automated spend per ticket for ${H_{\max}}=1$ through $5$.
    2.  Compute the combined spend per resolved ticket for each ${H_{\max}}$ and choose the cap. Confirm from the table that the chosen cap is the last turn satisfying the marginal rule ${q}_t{C_{h}}>{C_{\text{turn}}}$ of [[01 Books/Volume III - Agentic Systems/06 Agents at Scale/19 Agent Economics/V3-19.09 Pricing the Choice of Architecture#^sec-vol3-agent-economics-selection|section 19.8]], and explain why that rule holds only while the engineer resolves every ticket the loop leaves.
    3.  A triage queue replaces the engineer and resolves every rejected ticket for ${C_{h}}=\$2$. Choose the cap again. Explain why ranking the caps by automated spend per automated acceptance would have chosen wrongly in both cases.
4.  **Retry, a stronger model, or a router (Intermediate).** A cheap configuration costs ${c}=\$0.20$ per assessed attempt and may retry sequentially up to ${k}=4$ times. Half the incoming tasks are easy, which the cheap configuration accepts with ${p}=0.8$ per attempt; the other half are hard, with ${p}=0.1$. A stronger configuration costs $\$0.90$ per attempt, is run once, and accepts easy tasks with probability $0.95$ and hard tasks with $0.55$. Attempts are conditionally independent given the task.
    1.  A planner averages the cheap configuration’s rates to ${p}=0.45$. Compute the coverage and cost per accepted task this predicts for sequential retry.
    2.  Compute the actual coverage, expected attempts per task, and cost per accepted task for sequential retry over the mixed population, and compare it with running the stronger configuration on every task.
    3.  A direct router can identify each task’s class before generation. Price the policy that retries easy tasks on the cheap configuration and sends hard tasks to the stronger one. Decide among the three policies when the declared objective is minimum cost per accepted task subject to at least $75$ percent coverage, and state what evidence the router’s class labels require before the comparison holds.
5.  **Recomputing the critical path (Intermediate).** A repair episode has these dependencies and fixed durations. Sandbox creation ($20$ s) precedes a planning call ($12$ s). Two branches then run concurrently and must both finish before the test suite: a dependency build ($60$ s), and static analysis ($25$ s) followed by a model review of its findings ($15$ s). The test suite ($120$ s) is followed by a summary call ($8$ s) and snapshot export ($10$ s). For the speculation comparisons in parts (b) and (d), all stated model-call intervals consist entirely of decode with negligible prefill or queueing overhead; every other duration stays fixed.
    1.  Compute ${T_{\text{crit}}}$ with [[01 Books/Volume III - Agentic Systems/06 Agents at Scale/19 Agent Economics/V3-19.05 Critical-Path Latency#^eq-vol3-critical-path|equation 2]], identify the critical path, and compute the model share ${f}$ on that path.
    2.  A speculative decoder drafts ${\gamma}=4$ tokens per round at ${\beta}=0.1$ of a target step and accepts ${\bar{m}}=2.2$ tokens per round. Compute ${S_{\text{spec}}}$ and its break-even ${\bar{m}}$, apply the speedup to the model-call intervals under the stated approximation, and compute the new episode time.
    3.  A build cache instead cuts the build to $10$ s. Compute the time saved and explain why it is not $50$ s.
    4.  With the build cache in place, the suite is split into two concurrent shards of $60$ s plus $5$ s to merge results. Compute the episode time, then the time with speculation added. Rank the three interventions by elapsed time saved, and name the cost that sharding adds to the account.
6.  **A cascade under a deadline (Intermediate).** Small repair tasks may run a two-tier cascade. Tier 1 costs ${c^{\text{tier}}}_1=\$0.05$ and ${t}_1=3$ s and accepts ${q}_1=0.50$ of tasks; tier 2 costs ${c^{\text{tier}}}_2=\$0.60$ and ${t}_2=12$ s and accepts ${q}_2=0.80$ of the tasks that reach it. Each gate costs ${g}=\$0.02$ and takes ${v}=30$ s. Direct frontier execution costs $\$0.62$ with its gate, takes $42$ s, and accepts $0.82$ of the full cohort.
    1.  Compute the cascade’s expected cost per initiated task, acceptance probability, cost per accepted task, and mean duration, and compare each with direct execution.
    2.  A team proposes replacing tier 1’s gate with a lint check costing $\$0.005$ that exits the same share of tasks, but a fraction ${\phi}$ of those exits violate the independently assessed criterion, each costing $\$4$ to find and repair. Find the ${\phi}$ at which the harm term erases the gate saving, and evaluate the proposal at a measured ${\phi}=0.04$.
    3.  The service now requires $90$ percent of tasks to finish within $60$ s. Show that the cascade fails. A router sends $45$ percent of tasks directly to the frontier tier, where they are accepted with probability $0.70$; tier 1 accepts $0.85$ of the rest, and tier 2 accepts $0.80$ of what it receives. Check the deadline, and compute the routed policy’s cost per accepted task against both alternatives.
7.  **The support agent’s binding constraint (Intermediate, second-lighthouse transfer).** Apply the chapter’s capacity model to the support agent. The service must deliver trajectory goodput ${\mathcal{G}}=30$ accepted sessions per minute at acceptance fraction ${\alpha}=0.75$. An admitted session stays present for $4$ minutes on average, including customer think time, and makes $5$ serial model calls. Plan the model endpoint as the single-server queue of [[01 Books/Volume III - Agentic Systems/06 Agents at Scale/19 Agent Economics/V3-19.07 Capacity for Trajectories#^eq-vol3-kingman-gg1|equation 5]] with $\mathbb{E}[{T_{\text{svc}}}]=0.25$ s, ${c_{a}}^2=2$, and ${c_{s}}^2=1$. The response target is a mean of at most $1.0$ s of wait plus service per call. Each attempt costs $\$0.30$ in total.
    1.  Compute ${\lambda_{\text{traj}}}$, the mean session concurrency, the call arrival rate ${\lambda_{\text{arr}}}$, ${\rho_{\text{serv}}}$, and the mean response per call. Decide whether the target is met.
    2.  Find the largest ${\rho_{\text{serv}}}$ that meets the target, and the goodput it permits at ${\alpha}=0.75$.
    3.  Evaluate three levers: smoothing admissions to ${c_{a}}^2=1$; smoothing plus raising acceptance to ${\alpha}=0.85$; and a second, independent endpoint that takes half the sessions with unchanged variability. State which meet the target, and what happens to ${\rho_{\text{serv}}}$ if acceptance instead falls to ${\alpha}=0.60$ at the same goodput.
    4.  Compute ${C_{\text{eff}}}$ and the probability that a session meets at least one per-call tail delay when each call exceeds its 99th percentile independently with probability $0.01$, and compare with a $30$-call repair trajectory. Explain why this workload’s binding constraint differs from the repair agent’s, and why a design that meets a mean target can still miss a percentile target.
8.  **Renting or owning capacity for research fan-out (Advanced, critique and redesign).** The research agent reads $20$ input tokens per output token, and only $30$ percent of its input is billed at the cached rate because its sources vary. The API charges $\$3$ per million uncached input tokens, $\$0.30$ per million cached input tokens, and $\$15$ per million output tokens. A matched dedicated deployment costs ${C_{\text{fixed}}}=\$40$ per hour plus ${c_{\text{ded}}}=\$4$ per million output tokens, with a feasible reference output rate ${\mu_{\text{out}}}=800$ tokens per second at the latency target. The workload runs at ${u}=0.6$ for $8$ hours a day and is idle otherwise.
    1.  Compute ${c_{\text{API}}}$ per output token including its associated input, and ${u^*}$ from [[01 Books/Volume III - Agentic Systems/06 Agents at Scale/19 Agent Economics/V3-19.07 Capacity for Trajectories#^eq-vol3-breakeven-utilization|equation 6]].
    2.  A colleague argues that because the busy-hour load exceeds ${u^*}$, the team should commit to dedicated capacity around the clock. Compute the daily cost of the API and of that commitment, and critique the argument.
    3.  Redesign the commitment so that owning capacity pays, and compute its daily cost and saving.
    4.  Reordering sources to keep a stable prefix raises the cached share to $80$ percent. Recompute ${c_{\text{API}}}$, ${u^*}$, and the saving from part (c). Decide which change the team should make first, and name two conditions the comparison must hold fixed for either conclusion to stand.
9.  **A remediation budget under concurrency and cancellation (Advanced, redesign).** A remediation task has ${C_{\max}}=\$30$, of which $\$4$ of diagnosis has settled. Two children, log analysis and canary replay, each request a $\$15$ allocation at the same moment.
    1.  The coordinator reads the free balance, checks it against each request, and then records both reservations. Compute the overcommitment, and state the budget transition that prevents it and the largest allocation the second child can receive.
    2.  After the fix, log analysis receives $\$15$ and canary replay $\$11$. Log analysis closes with $\$9$ settled. An operator then cancels canary replay when it has $\$3$ settled and a remote load test outstanding with an enforced bound of $\$6$; that test later bills $\$4.20$. Tabulate the root’s ${E}$, ${R}$, and ${F}$ at each boundary, and compute what a release at local stop would return against what may safely be returned.
    3.  A trailing-window limit allows ${C_{\text{win}}}=\$8$ of admitted bounds per ${T_{\text{win}}}=10$ minutes across all children. Requests arrive at minute $0$ ($\$3$), minute $2$ ($\$4$), minute $6$ ($\$2$), and minute $11$ ($\$2$); the first operation settles at minute $1$ for $\$1$. Decide each admission, and show what an implementation that credits early settlement back to the window would admit instead.
    4.  The agent now proposes renting a load-generation cluster whose provider enforces no duration or spending cap. Redesign the admission path so the task keeps a hard ceiling, and explain why an operator’s approval of the production change does not substitute for a budget reservation.

**What’s Next: From priced parts to one trajectory**

Who owns each transition between context, tools, execution, assessment, and settlement, and what evidence permits the next one? A policy this chapter justifies on price still crosses every one of those boundaries. **[[01 Books/Volume III - Agentic Systems/07 Synthesis/20 Conclusion/V3-20 Conclusion#^sec-vol3-conclusion|Conclusion]]** first returns to the three questions the introduction posed, answering the first with the cost per accepted task priced here. It then follows one remediation incident through the whole system, naming the owner and the record of each transition, putting the attempt on this chapter’s account, and examining the failures that occur at the seams. It ends at the specification boundary, where the runtime can enforce and measure a declared contract but cannot decide whether that contract captures what the task was for.
