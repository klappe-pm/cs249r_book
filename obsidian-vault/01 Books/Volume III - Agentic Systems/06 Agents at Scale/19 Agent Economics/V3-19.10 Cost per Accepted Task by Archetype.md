# V3-19.10 Cost per Accepted Task by Archetype

[[Home|Home]] · [[01 Books/Volume III - Agentic Systems/Volume 3 Overview|Volume Overview]] · [[01 Books/Volume III - Agentic Systems/06 Agents at Scale/19 Agent Economics/V3-19 Agent Economics|V3-19 Agent Economics]]

^sec-vol3-agent-economics-synthesis

Even within the model-inference bill alone, token volume and dollar spend diverge sharply across production deployments. On the multi-provider gateway introduced in **[[01 Books/Volume III - Agentic Systems/00 Introduction/01 Introduction/V3-01.02 The Evolution of Machine Learning Systems#^sec-vol3-intro-evolution|The Evolution of Machine Learning Systems]]**, open-weight models serve 71.5 percent of all tokens while accounting for 22.6 percent of inference expenditure, whereas closed-weight frontier models carry the remaining 28.5 percent of tokens and 77.4 percent of spend (8.6× higher average spend per token). [[01 Books/Volume III - Agentic Systems/06 Agents at Scale/19 Agent Economics/V3-19.10 Cost per Accepted Task by Archetype#^fig-vol3-gateway-use-case-volume-vs-spend|Figure 6]] shows how that tier split separates token volume from dollar spend across eight workload classes on the same gateway.

Wherever the hatched spend bar extends past the solid volume bar, the workload concentrates on higher-priced model tiers; where the spend bar is shorter, routine turns run on inexpensive tiers. High-volume personal assistants route routine turns to inexpensive tiers, consuming 40 percent of gateway tokens for 19.6 percent of total spend (0.49× the gateway average per token). Coding agents balance exploration on fast tiers against patch synthesis on stronger models (21.8 percent of tokens and 20.4 percent of spend). Back-office automation concentrates on premium reasoning models, drawing 15 percent of spend from 5.8 percent of tokens (2.59× the gateway average, or 5.3× as much per token as personal assistants).

Yet the gateway invoice captures only the model-token term of an attempt. Widening the boundary to the whole-trajectory account across the book’s four lighthouse workloads shows how those token bills interact with sandbox residency, external tool fees, verification, and human review. The software repair agent holds a sandbox and an expanding workspace context over long multi-turn trajectories, whereas the research agent fans out parallel requests across untrusted external sources. The remediation agent exercises high-privilege authority over live production infrastructure under human approval, and the customer support agent processes high volumes of brief sessions against tight latency targets. Because a different cost term dominates each of these four archetypes, each workload responds to a different optimization lever.

**Lighthouse 19.1: Cost per accepted task by archetype**

Each lighthouse workload executes an attempt under its specific acceptance contract, priced in [[01 Books/Volume III - Agentic Systems/06 Agents at Scale/19 Agent Economics/V3-19.10 Cost per Accepted Task by Archetype#^tbl-vol3-lighthouse-economics|table 8]] using the cost framework developed throughout this chapter. For the repair agent, the execution horizon reflects the step limit of the registry’s reference coding harness, with token costs derived from the prefix-cached, append-only trajectory in notebook [19.2](https://mlsysbook.ai/vol3/19_agent_economics/19_agent_economics.html#nbk-18-prefix-cache-dollars). This repair row is a scenario of its own, independent of the opening cohort of notebook [19.1](https://mlsysbook.ai/vol3/19_agent_economics/19_agent_economics.html#nbk-18-the-token-price-fallacy-in-automated), whose short attempts and acceptance counts were chosen only to isolate the token-price effect. For the research agent, model costs scale according to the 15× token fan-out multiple reported for a production multi-agent research system ([[01 Books/Volume III - Agentic Systems/06 Agents at Scale/19 Agent Economics/V3-19.15 Source References#^ref-anthropic2025multiagent|Hadfield et al. 2025]]). The multiple is treated here as one measured baseline rather than a universal constant, and it is applied to a standard conversational turn at the chapter’s base tariff. For the remediation agent, human costs reflect an on-call engineer reviewing and approving a high-stakes production patch. For the support agent, every failed or rejected session triggers an immediate escalation handoff to a human representative at \$4. The support account counts automated accepted sessions in its denominator and includes handoff setup for rejected sessions in its numerator. Subsequent human resolution, its costs, and its completion delays lie outside this account. A combined service must add those costs and count verified human completions separately, as in [[01 Books/Volume III - Agentic Systems/06 Agents at Scale/19 Agent Economics/V3-19.09 Pricing the Choice of Architecture#^sec-vol3-selection-economics|section 19.8.3]]. All remaining inputs represent baseline scenario values.

**Systems insight**: The economic trade-off between cheaper inference and higher task accuracy follows a direct mathematical relationship. Halving the unit token price saves half of the model’s share of the attempt. In contrast, raising the acceptance probability from ${p}$ to ${p}+{\Delta} {p}$ saves the fraction ${\Delta} {p}/({p}+{\Delta} {p})$ across all fixed per-attempt cost components in the repair, research, and remediation rows. Support also saves handoff setup as fewer sessions are rejected. Under these held-cost assumptions, higher acceptance gives the greater reduction whenever the model’s share of total attempt cost falls below $2{\Delta} {p}/({p}+{\Delta} {p})$.

For the software repair agent, this decision threshold sits at 40 percent. Because model tokens account for only 33.2 percent of the repair agent’s attempt cost, improving acceptance by 10 percentage points saves more money than cutting token tariffs in half under those assumptions. Selecting an intervention also requires pricing the work and latency that produce the acceptance improvement. In this account, the repair agent is economically bound by holding its sandbox and tools across 30 turns. That is non-model work again, much as tests and sandbox lifecycle rather than model calls dominated elapsed time in notebook [19.5](https://mlsysbook.ai/vol3/19_agent_economics/19_agent_economics.html#nbk-18-critical-path-investment), although here the binding term is residency cost rather than test time.

Each archetype exposes a different binding constraint. Only the research agent is bound by token expenditure, with model calls consuming 85.7 percent of its attempt cost due to combinatorial sub-agent fan-out. Prefix caching, context compaction, and bounded search horizons are its primary economic levers. The remediation agent is dominated by senior engineer approval time. The approval escrow of **[[01 Books/Volume III - Agentic Systems/04 The Agent Runtime/10 The Agent Harness/V3-10.08 Approval Gates#^sec-vol3-agent-harness-held-proposals|Held proposals]]** makes each wait cheap to hold but does not shorten it. Systems architects shorten review by binding each approval to one exact configuration version and by grouping several reversible actions under one approval, balanced against the expected penalty of irreversible failures. Finally, the customer support agent is dominated by escalation handoffs. Because every failed session incurs an escalation fee, higher acceptance both raises the denominator and removes human labor from the numerator. Because the support agent’s cost per accepted task is well under a dollar, its interactive latency objective still requires separate fleet-capacity checks using the queueing models of [[01 Books/Volume III - Agentic Systems/06 Agents at Scale/19 Agent Economics/V3-19.07 Capacity for Trajectories#^sec-vol3-agent-economics-capacity|section 19.6]]. A small financial ratio alone does not establish fleet saturation.

When transitioning from architectural design evaluations to continuous production monitoring, an engineering team must maintain consistent accounting boundaries within its operational reporting. Complete cost accounting must track aborted and canceled attempts, idle headroom in shared hosting pools, human review and escalation labor within the task contract, and unresolved liabilities at the accounting cutoff. Preserving this rigorous boundary allows teams to re-evaluate architectural rankings as operating conditions evolve. When an inference provider slashes model pricing, a product release shifts the difficulty distribution of incoming tasks, or an organization updates its safety and approval policies, the matched economic comparison must be re-run. A design that appears cheaper on paper should only be promoted to production after its sandbox environments, test runners, and reviewer pools have been validated under realistic peak admission rates.
