# V3-18.11 Fallacies and Pitfalls

[[Home|Home]] · [[01 Books/Volume III - Agentic Systems/Volume 3 Overview|Volume Overview]] · [[01 Books/Volume III - Agentic Systems/06 Agents at Scale/18 Multi-Agent Coordination/V3-18 Multi-Agent Coordination|V3-18 Multi-Agent Coordination]]

^sec-vol3-multi-agent-fallacies

Every pitfall below comes from treating a delegation boundary as a guarantee that the runtime never actually enforced. Splitting work across agents does not by itself speed up completion, prevent semantic conflicts, decorrelate reviewer errors, or settle in-flight operations when a parent stops. Each of those assumptions carries a measurable cost in latency, token spend, or broken repository state.

**Fallacy**: *More workers necessarily improve the task outcome.*

Adding parallel workers introduces synchronization overhead, resource contention, and coordination traffic that quickly erode parallel speedup. In the fixed-partition screen (notebook [18.1](https://mlsysbook.ai/vol3/18_multi_agent/18_multi_agent.html#nbk-17-the-amdahl-limits-of-multi-agent)), deploying eight workers consumes 0.440 of the serial execution time, compared to 0.433 achieved at an optimal configuration of 6 workers. Beyond this point, coherency cost dominates: under the universal scalability law with those same coefficients, scaling out to 41 workers runs slower than executing the workload on a single agent. Furthermore, economic cost escalates even when latency stalls. A multi-agent delegation multiple of 3.75× in token consumption pays for itself only on difficult tasks that an isolated single agent completes less than 26.7 percent of the time. Rather than assuming that parallelism is beneficial, system designers must benchmark candidate topologies under identical token budgets and time ceilings against a declared task objective.

**Pitfall**: *Treating a clean merge as evidence that concurrent changes remain compatible.*

A successful textual merge only verifies that concurrent workers avoided line collisions in shared files, leaving cross-file semantic dependencies completely unvalidated. Across a repository of 1,000 files where each worker performs 10 edits, two concurrent workers touch completely disjoint files 90.4 percent of the time. As a result, nearly every pair of concurrent patches merges cleanly from the perspective of version control, even when a caller and an interface worker introduce mutually incompatible API contracts. Coordination systems must validate semantic dependencies and test candidate integration trees within an isolated environment before guarded publication ([[01 Books/Volume III - Agentic Systems/06 Agents at Scale/18 Multi-Agent Coordination/V3-18.06 Optimistic Concurrency Control#^sec-vol3-multiagent-concurrency|section 18.5]]).

**Fallacy**: *Agreement among reviewers proves correctness.*

Consensus among language-model reviewers reflects the correlation of their errors rather than an objective proof of correctness. When multiple reviewer agents share underlying model families, prompt templates, or reasoning biases, their mistakes correlate positively. For instance, five reviewers with an individual false-approval rate of 20 percent would reach an erroneous majority verdict only 5.79 percent of the time under strict statistical independence. Under the assumed beta mixture, in which reviewers share each candidate’s difficulty, that false-majority rate jumps to 16.68 percent. Adding more reviewers of the same kind cannot eliminate this shared bias, because error rates quickly level off near an irreducible floor of 15.0 percent ([[01 Books/Volume III - Agentic Systems/06 Agents at Scale/18 Multi-Agent Coordination/V3-18.07 Correlated Ensemble Failures#^sec-vol3-multiagent-consensus|section 18.6]]). Production architectures must treat subjective reviewer voting as an advisory signal and isolate it strictly from deterministic, guarded verification checks.

**Pitfall**: *Closing the parent task and releasing its reservations as soon as cancellation is requested.*

A cancellation request immediately closes admission for new subtasks, but active execution, external tool calls, and downstream token charges drain asynchronously. Discarded work is not a rounding error. In the delegation of [[01 Books/Volume III - Agentic Systems/06 Agents at Scale/18 Multi-Agent Coordination/V3-18.10 Benchmarking Against a Single Agent#^tbl-vol3-multiagent-decision-costs|table 9]], failed attempts and canceled speculative branches account for \$6 of \$18 in delegated spend, and that table is complete only because every one of those charges stayed owned until it settled. If the parent task releases its accounting context and budget allocations the moment cancellation is requested, the trailing operations continue running without an owner, and their charges land outside any budget as untracked spend and leaked resources. Robust orchestrators close admission at the entry gates, broadcast cancellation signals downstream, and maintain accounting context and state reservations until all active operations, diagnostic evidence, and billing charges have fully settled under the settlement rule.

**Fallacy**: *If each worker usually finishes on time, the fan-out usually finishes on time.*

Synchronizing multiple concurrent workers behind an all-results barrier compounds tail latency exponentially. Because every worker must complete before the barrier can lift, the overall probability of on-time completion multiplies each individual worker’s success rate. With an individual on-time probability of 90 percent per worker, a fan-out of 8 workers completes within the deadline only 43.05 percent of the time, and barrier success falls below even odds once the fleet reaches 7 workers ([[01 Books/Volume III - Agentic Systems/06 Agents at Scale/18 Multi-Agent Coordination/V3-18.08 Bounding In-Flight Work#^sec-vol3-multiagent-backpressure|section 18.7]]). Where the system contract accepts interchangeable or quorum-based results, implementing a stopping rule restores 96.19 percent. Where every subtask is strictly required, the orchestrator must reduce fan-out width to keep tail latency bounded.
