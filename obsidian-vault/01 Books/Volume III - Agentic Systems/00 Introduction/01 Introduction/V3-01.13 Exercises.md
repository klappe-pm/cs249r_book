# V3-01.13 Exercises

[[Home|Home]] · [[01 Books/Volume III - Agentic Systems/Volume 3 Overview|Volume Overview]] · [[01 Books/Volume III - Agentic Systems/00 Introduction/01 Introduction/V3-01 Introduction|V3-01 Introduction]]

^sec-vol3-01-introduction-exercises-4497ad35

All quantities below are hypothetical problem givens, chosen to be round, and describe no particular model, provider, or deployment. Unless a problem says otherwise, assume step errors are independent, each attempt costs the same in expectation, and “accepted” means accepted under a fixed task contract.

**1. How far can an unchecked repair run? (Basic).** A repair agent takes steps that are each correct with probability 0.98, and no step is checked before the next one builds on it.

1.  Using [[01 Books/Volume III - Agentic Systems/00 Introduction/01 Introduction/V3-01.07 Reliability and Cost Over a Horizon#^eq-vol3-horizon-compounding|equation 1]], compute task success at horizons of 20 and 60 turns.
2.  Find the per-step error rate that would hold the 60-turn task at 80 percent success, and how many times lower it is than the current error rate. Confirm the answer with the small-${\epsilon}$ form ${\epsilon} {H} \approx -\ln(0.8)$.
3.  Instead of a better model, the runtime inserts a check after every 10 turns that detects any incorrect step in that segment and reruns the segment from the preceding checkpoint. Assuming the check is perfect and free, compute the expected number of turns to finish the 60-turn task, and compare it with rerunning the whole unchecked trajectory until it succeeds. State which lever to pull first and the property of the check on which the answer depends.

**2. A token discount or a better context? (Basic).** An attempt at a repair task spends \$1.80 on model tokens and \$1.20 on the sandbox, tests, and review, and 25 percent of attempts are accepted. The runtime retries until acceptance.

1.  Compute the expected number of attempts and the cost per accepted task from [[01 Books/Volume III - Agentic Systems/00 Introduction/01 Introduction/V3-01.07 Reliability and Cost Over a Horizon#^eq-vol3-cost-per-accepted-task|equation 2]].
2.  A provider offers a 40 percent token discount; alternatively, a context change raises acceptance to 30 percent at the original attempt cost. Compute the cost per accepted task under each, and under both together.
3.  Find the acceptance probability that matches the discount. Explain why the comparison comes out differently from the chapter’s notebook ([1.2](https://mlsysbook.ai/vol3/01_introduction/01_introduction.html#nbk-01-cost-per-accepted)), naming the quantity that decides which lever wins.

**3. Placing a workload in H·S·A (Basic).** A log-triage agent runs about 15 turns per incident and spends \$0.50 on tokens per task. Consider three versions of its grant: (i) it reads logs and writes a summary to a scratch file; (ii) it also opens tickets in a shared tracker, and any ticket can be closed again; (iii) it also rolls back a production deployment, and a wrong rollback causes an outage costing \$2,000 that cannot be undone. In version (iii), 2 percent of rollbacks are wrong.

1.  Place each version on the state and authority axes of [[01 Books/Volume III - Agentic Systems/00 Introduction/01 Introduction/V3-01.05 The H·S·A Exposure Space#^fig-vol3-hsa-taxonomy|figure 4]], justifying each level by the consequence of the operation rather than its name.
2.  For version (iii), compute the expected loss per task from wrong rollbacks and express it as a multiple of the token cost.
3.  A human approval step costs \$5 per rollback and stops 90 percent of wrong ones. Compute the expected cost per task with approval, and state which exposure binds this workload.
4.  Specify all five task-contract fields for version (iii): the useful incident-triage goal and its negative scope, the deployment baseline and execution environment, the permitted actions and approval boundary, bounded diagnostic observations, and independent completion criteria. Name one limitation of the evidence that would require an unresolved outcome or further review.

**4. Paying for checks inside the horizon (Intermediate).** A repair task runs 50 turns with per-step error 0.02. Each turn costs \$0.04 in tokens, and each attempt pays \$1.00 to prepare a fresh sandbox. Under policy A, only a sealed test at the end checks the result; it accepts an attempt exactly when every step was correct, and a rejected attempt restarts from scratch. Under policy B, the same sandbox is kept and a check costing \$0.10 runs after every 10 turns, detecting any incorrect step in that segment, which is then rerun from the last checkpoint.

1.  Compute the acceptance probability and cost per accepted task under policy A.
2.  Compute the expected cost per accepted task under policy B, and the expected number of turns under each policy.
3.  Find the per-check cost at which policy B stops paying. Then critique the assumption that makes B look so strong: what happens to the calculation if the intermediate check is a visible test the agent can fit its patch to? Specify independent final evidence and concurrency coverage appropriate to this repair, using [[01 Books/Volume III - Agentic Systems/00 Introduction/01 Introduction/V3-01.06 Invariant Closure#^sec-vol3-intro-invariant-closure|section 1.5.2]], and explain why sealing a sequential test alone would not establish that the race condition is fixed.

**5. When the loss is not in the tokens (Intermediate; second lighthouse).** The remediation agent (lighthouse [1.3](https://mlsysbook.ai/vol3/01_introduction/01_introduction.html#lhs-vol3-remediation-agent)) spends \$2 on tokens per attempt. Each attempt ends by proposing one configuration change that is correct with probability 0.95; a wrong change that commits costs \$20,000 in total. A human approval step costs \$15 of on-call time and delays restoration by 6 minutes, during which the ongoing incident costs \$50 per minute. Approval passes every correct change and rejects 90 percent of wrong ones; a rejected proposal starts a new attempt, which needs approval again.

1.  Without approval, every proposal commits. Compute the expected cost per task, including expected loss.
2.  With approval, compute the probability that an attempt ends in a commit, the expected attempts per task, the expected attempt spend, the probability the committed change is wrong, and the total expected cost per task.
3.  Find the incident cost per minute above which approval stops paying.
4.  For the repair agent, the chapter’s levers were acceptance and horizon. Show numerically why halving the token price is irrelevant here, and name the binding constraint of this lighthouse and the H·S·A level that makes it bind.

**6. Workflow, loop, or both? (Intermediate).** A fixed workflow attempt for a support query costs \$0.60; a model-directed loop attempt costs \$1.80. Half the queries are routine, where the workflow is accepted with probability 0.55 and the loop with 0.80. The other half are novel, where the workflow is accepted with probability 0.05 and the loop with 0.70. Each query retries its chosen architecture until acceptance.

1.  Compute the cost per accepted task for each architecture on each query class, and the mean over the population if every query uses the workflow, and if every query uses the loop.
2.  A perfect router sends routine queries to the workflow and novel ones to the loop. Compute the mean cost per accepted query.
3.  A real router costs \$0.05 per query and sends a fraction ${m}$ of each class to the wrong architecture. Compute the mean cost at ${m} = 0.05$ and find the ${m}$ at which routing stops beating the loop alone. What does this imply about where to spend engineering effort, and what must be held fixed for the comparison to be valid?

**7. Retries that repeat one mistake (Advanced).** Each attempt at a repair task costs \$2.00, and the retry budget allows 4 attempts. Of the tasks, 70 percent are easy, accepted with probability 0.70 per attempt; the rest are hard, and because each retry rereads the transcript and repeats the same mistaken hypothesis, a hard task is accepted with probability only 0.05 per attempt. Assume attempts are independent conditional on the task being easy or hard; the repeated hypothesis lowers the hard-task acceptance rate rather than making every retry share one outcome. Tasks not accepted within the budget end unresolved, and their spend stays in the numerator.

1.  An analyst pools the cohort into one mean acceptance probability and applies the independent-retry formulas. Compute the pooled acceptance, the predicted success within the budget, and the predicted cost per accepted task.
2.  Compute the actual acceptance within the budget, expected spend per task, and measured cost per accepted task under the mixture. By what factor did the pooled model understate the cost?
3.  Redesign the retry. Retries 2 through 4 start from a fresh context carrying only the failed test result, which costs \$2.50 per retry and raises a hard task’s retry acceptance to 0.25 while leaving easy tasks unchanged. Compute the new acceptance within the budget and cost per accepted task, and argue whether the redesign should ship. What does the result say about treating ${p_{\text{acc}}}$ as a property of the model rather than of the whole attempt?

**8. Should the repair agent deploy its own patches? (Advanced).** A team produces 200 patches that pass the sealed tests each day. Of these patches, 3 percent are in fact wrong. A wrong patch that reaches production costs \$5,000. The team proposes letting the agent deploy directly. Two alternatives are on the table. A reviewer costing \$25 per patch catches 80 percent of the wrong patches that tests pass, and waiting for review delays each patch by 4 hours valued at \$10 per hour. Alternatively, a canary rollout costing \$5 per patch exposes a small slice of traffic and rolls back automatically, bounding the cost of a wrong patch to \$500.

1.  Place the direct-deploy proposal and the canary design on the H·S·A authority axis, and explain what changes the level.
2.  Compute the expected cost per patch and per day for direct deploy, review, and canary.
3.  Critique the canary design. Name one class of change for which automatic rollback is not an adequate reversal, state what authority level such a change returns to, and specify the contract change (permitted actions and completion criteria) that should route it back to review.

**What’s Next: From task responsibilities to the model call**

Of the six phases every turn passes through, only the second belongs to the model, and each of the other five depends on knowing exactly what that phase delivers. When the repair agent asks to inspect the connection pool, the runtime may receive a complete tool request, a request cut off before it is whole, or no response at all, and each outcome requires different handling. **[[01 Books/Volume III - Agentic Systems/01 The Model/02 The Foundation Model/V3-02 The Foundation Model#^sec-vol3-foundation-model|The Foundation Model]]** follows that single inspection call from request to bill, establishing what one call reads, how its status reports completion, and what it costs. The single call sits at the origin of the H·S·A exposure space, so this invocation contract becomes the baseline from which every later part measures a departure.
