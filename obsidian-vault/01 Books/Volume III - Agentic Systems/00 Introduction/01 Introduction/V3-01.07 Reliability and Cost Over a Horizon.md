# V3-01.07 Reliability and Cost Over a Horizon

[[Home|Home]] · [[01 Books/Volume III - Agentic Systems/Volume 3 Overview|Volume Overview]] · [[01 Books/Volume III - Agentic Systems/00 Introduction/01 Introduction/V3-01 Introduction|V3-01 Introduction]]

^sec-vol3-intro-horizon-arithmetic

A realistic agentic trajectory spans dozens of sequential turns, with each turn building directly on observations and state modifications from the preceding step. A misdiagnosed deadlock trace leads the model to inspect the wrong source file; an erroneous patch to that file cascades into compilation failures or silently corrupts unrelated business logic. Because foundation models generate fluent rationalizations even when mistaken, nothing in the model’s generated text signals the exact moment execution derailed. An extended autonomous task can easily fail even when individual per-turn accuracy appears high.

The invariant closure principle ([[01 Books/Volume III - Agentic Systems/00 Introduction/01 Introduction/V3-01.06 Invariant Closure#^pri-invariant-closure|1]]) stated the cost of leaving steps unchecked without deriving it. Elementary probability supplies the derivation and shows why the principle places verifiers inside the execution loop. Suppose each of ${H}$ sequential steps is correct with probability $1-{\epsilon}$, errors are statistically independent, and no step is verified before subsequent steps build upon it. The overall task succeeds only if every intermediate step succeeds:

$$
\Pr(\text{success}) = (1-{\epsilon})^{{H}} \tag{1}
$$

^eq-vol3-horizon-compounding

[[01 Books/Volume III - Agentic Systems/00 Introduction/01 Introduction/V3-01.07 Reliability and Cost Over a Horizon#^eq-vol3-horizon-compounding|Equation 1]] applies classic series-reliability analysis[[01 Books/Volume III - Agentic Systems/00 Introduction/01 Introduction/V3-01.15 Footnotes#^fn11|¹¹]] to the sequential turns of a trajectory. The resulting reliability collapse is **horizon compounding**. Statistical independence is an idealization; in practice, errors correlate and models occasionally recover from bad states. Under the independent-step model without recovery, unchecked success decays exponentially with horizon length (notebook [1.1](https://mlsysbook.ai/vol3/01_introduction/01_introduction.html#nbk-01-horizon-compounding)); correlation and recovery can change the shape of that decline.

**Napkin Math 1.1: Horizon compounding**

**Problem**: Each step of a repair agent is correct with probability 99 percent, independently, and no step is checked before the next one builds on it. What fraction of tasks succeed at horizons of 10 and 100 turns, and what per-step error rate would hold the longer horizon at 90 percent success?

**Math**: By [[01 Books/Volume III - Agentic Systems/00 Introduction/01 Introduction/V3-01.07 Reliability and Cost Over a Horizon#^eq-vol3-horizon-compounding|equation 1]], $0.99^{10} \approx 0.904$, so 90.4 percent of tasks succeed at 10 turns, while $0.99^{100} \approx 0.366$ leaves 36.6 percent at 100 turns. Holding 90 percent at 100 turns requires ${\epsilon} = 1 - 0.90^{1/100} \approx 0.00105$, about 0.11 percent per step and 9.5× lower than the assumed 1 percent. For small ${\epsilon}$, $(1-{\epsilon})^{H} \approx e^{-{\epsilon} {H}}$, and the requirement reads ${\epsilon} {H} \approx 0.105$ at any horizon.

**Systems insight**: The product ${\epsilon} {H}$, not step accuracy alone, sets task reliability. A tenfold longer horizon demands a tenfold lower per-step error to hold the same success rate. The cheaper engineering knob is usually the unchecked horizon itself: a check that reliably detects an error, followed by restoration and a successful retry, bounds the segment over which that error can compound. Under the notebook’s independence assumption, perfect detection and restoration turn the extended task into a sequence of short segments. Supplying and enforcing those checks is the primary responsibility of the runtime, as formalized by the invariant closure principle ([[01 Books/Volume III - Agentic Systems/00 Introduction/01 Introduction/V3-01.06 Invariant Closure#^pri-invariant-closure|1]]).

The horizon in [[01 Books/Volume III - Agentic Systems/00 Introduction/01 Introduction/V3-01.07 Reliability and Cost Over a Horizon#^eq-vol3-horizon-compounding|equation 1]] belongs to the delegated task, and the tasks delegated to agents have lengthened quickly. Kwa et al. ([[01 Books/Volume III - Agentic Systems/00 Introduction/01 Introduction/V3-01.14 Source References#^ref-kwa2025measuring|2025]]) express that length in human terms. They time people with the relevant expertise on a suite of software and research tasks, then report each model’s 50 percent time horizon at release: the length of task, in expert time, that an agent built on the model completes in half its attempts ([[01 Books/Volume III - Agentic Systems/00 Introduction/01 Introduction/V3-01.07 Reliability and Cost Over a Horizon#^fig-vol3-measured-task-horizon|figure 5]]).

^fig-vol3-measured-task-horizon

Among the frontier models in [[01 Books/Volume III - Agentic Systems/00 Introduction/01 Introduction/V3-01.07 Reliability and Cost Over a Horizon#^fig-vol3-measured-task-horizon|figure 5]], the 50 percent horizon grew from about 3 seconds to about 17 hours, a factor of roughly 19,000. That is a doubling every 6.2 months across the whole period and every 4.2 months since 2023. [[01 Books/Volume III - Agentic Systems/00 Introduction/01 Introduction/V3-01.07 Reliability and Cost Over a Horizon#^eq-vol3-horizon-compounding|Equation 1]] turns that trend into an engineering obligation. A task that occupies an expert for hours instead of minutes spans many more dependent steps, and at a fixed per-step error, doubling the unchecked horizon squares the probability of success. Each doubling of the delegated task therefore requires either halving the per-step error or inserting checks that halve the unchecked segment between them. Model improvement supplies the first; only the runtime can supply the second, and the measured horizon is itself a reminder that half the attempts at that length still fail.

Checking converts a silent failure into an explicit, rejected attempt, and a rejected attempt still consumes real resources. Suppose each attempt at a task is accepted with probability ${p_{\text{acc}}}$, independently of earlier attempts, and the runtime retries until one is accepted. The number of attempts follows a geometric distribution with mean $1/{p_{\text{acc}}}$. Each attempt costs ${C_{\text{attempt}}}$ in expectation, tallying model tokens, tool execution, test sandboxes, and human review, all of which a failed attempt consumes as completely as a successful one. The expected **cost per accepted task** is therefore

$$
{C_{\text{eff}}} = \frac{\mathbb{E}[{C_{\text{attempt}}}]}{{p_{\text{acc}}}} \tag{2}
$$

^eq-vol3-cost-per-accepted-task

Production runtimes bound the retry budget, so some tasks end without acceptance, and their spend must still be charged to the tasks that were accepted. Measured over a cohort, cost per accepted task is therefore the total spend of every initiated task divided by the number accepted; the accounting pitfall in [[01 Books/Volume III - Agentic Systems/00 Introduction/01 Introduction/V3-01.10 Fallacies and Pitfalls#^sec-vol3-intro-fallacies|section 1.9]] prices what omitting the failed attempts hides. Even in its expected-value form, [[01 Books/Volume III - Agentic Systems/00 Introduction/01 Introduction/V3-01.07 Reliability and Cost Over a Horizon#^eq-vol3-cost-per-accepted-task|equation 2]] exposes a trade-off between unit model pricing and acceptance, which notebook [1.2](https://mlsysbook.ai/vol3/01_introduction/01_introduction.html#nbk-01-cost-per-accepted) prices for the repair task.

↪ Whole-trajectory cost and the cohort cost per accepted task, which charges failed and abandoned attempts to the accepted ones, are developed in **[[01 Books/Volume III - Agentic Systems/06 Agents at Scale/19 Agent Economics/V3-19.03 Cost per Accepted Task#^sec-vol3-agent-economics-accounting|Cost per Accepted Task]]**.

**Napkin Math 1.2: Cheaper tokens or more acceptances**

**Problem**: An attempt at the repair task spends \$1.30 on model tokens and \$0.80 on the sandbox, tests, and review, and is accepted with probability 40 percent. Which lowers cost per accepted task more: halving the token price, or raising acceptance to 60 percent through a context or tool improvement?

**Math**: The baseline attempt costs \$2.10, and the task needs 2.5 attempts on average, so [[01 Books/Volume III - Agentic Systems/00 Introduction/01 Introduction/V3-01.07 Reliability and Cost Over a Horizon#^eq-vol3-cost-per-accepted-task|equation 2]] gives $2.10 / 0.40 = 5.25$ dollars per accepted task. Halving the token price lowers the attempt to \$1.45, giving $1.45 / 0.40 \approx 3.63$ dollars. Raising acceptance at the original attempt cost gives $2.10 / 0.60 = 3.50$ dollars. To find the acceptance that the price cut is worth, set the cheaper attempt’s cost per accepted task, ${C_{\text{attempt}}}(1 - \text{cut} \times \text{share})/{p_{\text{acc}}}$, equal to ${C_{\text{attempt}}}/{p'}$ at the original attempt cost. Here cut is the fraction of the token price removed, and share is the tokens’ share of the attempt cost. Solving gives ${p'} = {p_{\text{acc}}}/(1 - \text{cut} \times \text{share})$. Tokens are 62 percent of the attempt, so the price cut matches an acceptance of $0.40 / (1 - 0.5 \times 0.62) \approx 0.58$.

**Systems insight**: The two levers act on different amounts. A price cut can remove at most the share of the attempt that the cheaper component represents, whereas acceptance divides the whole attempt cost (sandbox, tests, and review included). Halving the token price removes half of the tokens’ 62 percent share, which is worth the same as gaining 18 percentage points of acceptance (40 percent to 58 percent). Here the two options land close together, and the acceptance gain wins only narrowly. Any context enhancement or tool refinement that lifts acceptance beyond that threshold outperforms the price cut, because higher acceptance amortizes sandbox overhead, environment setup, and verification spend across fewer discarded runs. Systems engineers must evaluate interventions by their net effect on ${C_{\text{eff}}}$ under a rigorous acceptance contract, never by inference tariffs alone.

[[01 Books/Volume III - Agentic Systems/00 Introduction/01 Introduction/V3-01.07 Reliability and Cost Over a Horizon#^eq-vol3-horizon-compounding|Equation 1]] and [[01 Books/Volume III - Agentic Systems/00 Introduction/01 Introduction/V3-01.07 Reliability and Cost Over a Horizon#^eq-vol3-cost-per-accepted-task|equation 2]] form the quantitative basis for systems design throughout this book. [[01 Books/Volume III - Agentic Systems/00 Introduction/01 Introduction/V3-01.07 Reliability and Cost Over a Horizon#^eq-vol3-horizon-compounding|Equation 1]] shows how fast reliability falls across unchecked trajectories, and [[01 Books/Volume III - Agentic Systems/00 Introduction/01 Introduction/V3-01.07 Reliability and Cost Over a Horizon#^eq-vol3-cost-per-accepted-task|equation 2]] sets the economic bar for any runtime mechanism. An intermediate check, a sandbox boundary, or a prompt cache (which reuses the serving state already computed for a repeated prompt prefix, so later calls skip recomputing it) changes both terms of that ratio. Writing ${C'_{\text{attempt}}}$ and ${p'_{\text{acc}}}$ for the attempt cost and acceptance with the mechanism in place, the mechanism pays for its overhead only when ${C'_{\text{attempt}}}/{p'_{\text{acc}}} < {C_{\text{attempt}}}/{p_{\text{acc}}}$. Equivalently, it must lower $\mathbb{E}[{C_{\text{attempt}}}]$ by a larger fraction than it reduces ${p_{\text{acc}}}$, or raise ${p_{\text{acc}}}$ by a factor that outweighs its added per-attempt cost.

Neither equation depends on how control decisions are chosen. Both apply to deterministic programs with fixed control flow and to autonomous loops where foundation models choose each next action. That neutrality makes them the right tools for choosing between fixed workflows and model-directed loops.

### Workflows Vs. Model-Directed Loops

^sec-vol3-intro-workflow-vs-loop

In the software repair task, letting a foundation model decide how to clone the repository, install dependencies, and start the linter wastes tokens and adds failure points to steps whose order never changes. Hardcoding a script to diagnose an unknown race condition, by contrast, breaks the moment the deadlock trace points to an unexpected file. The first group of steps fits a **fixed workflow**, where the sequence of operations is specified in advance. Diagnosing the race condition requires inspecting source files, forming test hypotheses, and reading logs that depend on what the previous turn uncovered. A **model-directed loop**, in which the model chooses its next tool call from intermediate observations, earns its keep at those open-ended branches whenever its acceptance gains outweigh the variance and token cost it adds.

A fixed workflow is not limited to a straight-line script: it can take conditional branches, manage retries, and run parallel tasks as long as its control graph is fixed in advance. Because the model calls inside a workflow are still stochastic, a fixed control graph guarantees neither identical outputs nor bounded latency. When comparing a fixed workflow with a model-directed loop, engineers must test both under the same task contract and acceptance checks. By [[01 Books/Volume III - Agentic Systems/00 Introduction/01 Introduction/V3-01.07 Reliability and Cost Over a Horizon#^eq-vol3-cost-per-accepted-task|equation 2]], a model-directed loop pays for itself only when its open-ended exploration raises ${p_{\text{acc}}}$ by a larger factor than its extra model calls raise $\mathbb{E}[{C_{\text{attempt}}}]$.

↪ Economic feasibility envelopes for choosing between deterministic workflows and model-directed loops are developed in **[[01 Books/Volume III - Agentic Systems/06 Agents at Scale/19 Agent Economics/V3-19.09 Pricing the Choice of Architecture#^sec-vol3-selection-economics|When a bounded loop beats a single call]]**.

Authorization policies stay separate from whichever control structure chooses the steps. A fixed workflow can require human approval before running an external side effect, just as a model-directed diagnostic loop can run without prompts inside an isolated sandbox. The runtime allows exploratory actions inside a safe boundary while gating irreversible operations such as production deployment. An approved grant only authorizes an action to run; it does not show that the resulting state is correct, which still requires independent verification.

**Checkpoint 1.1: Changing the task boundary**

A repair agent can inspect and edit a disposable working copy and return a patch for review. Its contract grants no access to the deployment service.

**Reading the boundary**

**Authority**: The task now includes deployment. Which authority level does it move to, and which permission decision must be added that no passing test can supply?

**State**: The workspace turns out to hold the only copy of unrelated edits. Which exposure changes, and which single change to the grant removes it?

**Acceptance evidence**: A sealed sequential test passes after the race-condition repair. Which claim does it support, and what further evidence would you require before accepting?

**Control policy**: Which steps of the repair would you assign to a fixed workflow, and which decision must stay model-directed?

**Pricing the choice**

**Cost per accepted task estimate**: An attempt costs \$2.10, of which \$0.80 is sandbox, tests, and review, and 40 percent of attempts are accepted. Estimate the cost per accepted task if you halve that \$0.80, and again if a tool change raises acceptance by 10 percentage points. Which would you fund?
