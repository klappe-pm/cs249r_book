# V3-16.14 Fallacies and Pitfalls

[[Home|Home]] · [[01 Books/Volume III - Agentic Systems/Volume 3 Overview|Volume Overview]] · [[01 Books/Volume III - Agentic Systems/05 Learning from Trajectories/16 Trajectory Fine-Tuning/V3-16 Trajectory Fine-Tuning|V3-16 Trajectory Fine-Tuning]]

^sec-vol3-fine-tuning-fallacies

A fine-tuning pipeline can optimize its nominal loss objective with complete numerical fidelity while departing from the intended operational behavior. In agent systems, where training corpora interweave multi-turn plans, tool invocations, and environment feedback, subtle flaws emerge at the interfaces between admitted evidence, context assembly, loss masking, and deployment evaluation. Each of the following mistakes produces a concrete failure mode, and each carries a quantifiable engineering penalty illustrated in this chapter’s worked examples.

**Fallacy**: *Every model response in an accepted trajectory is a desirable training target.*

When an agent successfully recovers from an error, the recorded trajectory necessarily contains the initial flawed or refused proposal alongside the eventual correction. That initial misstep is essential as conditioning context, because it explains why the correction was required, but it is the one action the policy must never learn to emit. In the inventory of notebook [16.1](https://mlsysbook.ai/vol3/16_fine_tuning/16_fine_tuning.html#nbk-15-gradient-budget-and-compute-allocation), the refused positions number 160 tokens, 20 percent as many as the 800 selected response targets. An unselective mask over all assistant turns therefore puts 160 of the 960 response positions to work reproducing the malformed call. That share is a minority of the response weight, yet all of it rewards the refused proposal directly, in every recovery record that contains one. Systems must instead implement target admission masks ([[01 Books/Volume III - Agentic Systems/05 Learning from Trajectories/16 Trajectory Fine-Tuning/V3-16.05 Observation Loss Masking#^sec-vol3-fine-tuning-loss-masking|section 16.4]]): retain rejected proposals and environment observations in the prefix context so the agent learns to condition on failure signals, but restrict direct imitation loss to admitted response targets.

**Pitfall**: *Treating efficient batching as a change with no learning consequences.*

Packing multiple variable-length trajectories into a single contiguous buffer raises occupancy, but it alters training dynamics unless the execution kernel accounts for sequence boundaries. In notebook [16.2](https://mlsysbook.ai/vol3/16_fine_tuning/16_fine_tuning.html#nbk-vol3-packing-occupancy), sequence packing raises token occupancy from 19.1 percent to 76.6 percent. However, applying a standard causal attention mask across that packed buffer allows subsequent examples to attend across episode boundaries to unrelated earlier sequences. Loss normalization alters optimization just as profoundly: standard token-level averaging gives the long example 32× the short one’s weight, whereas per-example averaging weights both examples equally. Always configure block-diagonal attention with per-example position coordinates and reset label shifts ([[01 Books/Volume III - Agentic Systems/05 Learning from Trajectories/16 Trajectory Fine-Tuning/V3-16.06 Batching Variable-Length Trajectories#^sec-vol3-fine-tuning-batching|section 16.5]]), explicitly declare loss reduction semantics, and verify real execution throughput on target accelerator kernels before claiming a speedup.

**Fallacy**: *Recovery examples and schema perturbations make a fine-tuned agent robust to the states it will meet.*

Synthesized recovery trajectories and perturbed schemas broaden training coverage, but they do not provide a blanket guarantee against distribution shift. Theoretical bounds on policy regret depend on where state error rates are evaluated. Over an execution horizon of 30 steps with a 2 percent per-step disagreement rate, theorem [16.1](https://mlsysbook.ai/vol3/16_fine_tuning/16_fine_tuning.html#thrm-15-imitation-regret) bounds excess cost at 18 units on reference demonstration states, but at 0.6 units on states generated by the learner itself. Furthermore, that tighter bound holds only while the disadvantage of each individual deviation remains near one, a condition that fails as soon as one unexpected tool error can spoil the rest of the task. Robust agent systems require sampling and labeling states actually reached by the learner, maintaining end-to-end coherence between schema transformations, tool calls, and parser observations ([[01 Books/Volume III - Agentic Systems/05 Learning from Trajectories/16 Trajectory Fine-Tuning/V3-16.08 Schema Robustness#^sec-vol3-fine-tuning-schemas|section 16.7]]), and stress-testing the policy under held-out operational shifts.

**Pitfall**: *Reading a preference pair’s logistic score as the probability that the preferred repair succeeds.*

The implicit reward margin computed in direct preference optimization reflects pairwise discrimination on historical data, not absolute operational success. In notebook [16.4](https://mlsysbook.ai/vol3/16_fine_tuning/16_fine_tuning.html#nbk-15-preference-margin), identical trajectory log-probabilities yield a logistic score of 0.646 at ${\beta}=$ 0.2, yet that score rises to 0.769 when the preference scale doubles to ${\beta}=$ 0.4. A metric that shifts this much with an optimization hyperparameter measures relative fitting margin rather than task competence. Furthermore, that margin can widen because the objective suppresses the dispreferred trajectory rather than improving the execution likelihood of the preferred one. Reliable acceptance rates cannot be read off training curves; they must be measured directly through closed-loop environment trials ([[01 Books/Volume III - Agentic Systems/05 Learning from Trajectories/16 Trajectory Fine-Tuning/V3-16.12 Evaluating a Fine-Tuned Agent#^sec-vol3-fine-tuning-benchmarking|section 16.11]]).

**Fallacy**: *Training a small fraction of the parameters shrinks training memory in proportion.*

Parameter-efficient fine-tuning cuts the number of updated weights to a small fraction, but accelerator memory does not scale down linearly with the parameter fraction. In notebook [16.5](https://mlsysbook.ai/vol3/16_fine_tuning/16_fine_tuning.html#nbk-15-adapter-state-inventory), low-rank adapters account for 0.10 percent of total model parameters, yet the training job still requires 12.6 percent of the parameter-associated memory consumed by a full fine-tuning run. This 7.9× reduction is governed by bytes per parameter across base and adapter states rather than the parameter count alone. The frozen base weights must remain resident in memory throughout forward and backward passes, accounting for 99.2 percent of what remains, while dynamic activation buffers and workspace memory scale with sequence length for both approaches. Dimension adapter training hardware by accounting for frozen base representations and empirically measured activation peaks ([[01 Books/Volume III - Agentic Systems/05 Learning from Trajectories/16 Trajectory Fine-Tuning/V3-16.11 Parameter-Efficient Fine-Tuning#^sec-vol3-fine-tuning-peft|section 16.10]]).

**Pitfall**: *Promoting the artifact with the lowest recorded loss.*

Selecting an agent checkpoint based purely on validation loss conflates prefix prediction with closed-loop execution competence. In notebook [16.1](https://mlsysbook.ai/vol3/16_fine_tuning/16_fine_tuning.html#nbk-15-gradient-budget-and-compute-allocation), an unmasked causal evaluation averages across 16,383 prediction terms per example. Tool outputs that the policy never emits occupy 77.1 percent of the stored input. In contrast, a selective objective evaluates only the 800 target positions where the model generates actions. A checkpoint with lower overall loss may simply fit the repetitive parts of that environment text more tightly without improving decision quality. Even when measured exclusively on action tokens, offline likelihood improvements do not guarantee that the policy will avoid deadlocks or handle schema variations during execution. Always qualify production candidates by measuring scoped task acceptance, effective cost ${C_{\text{eff}}}$, schema compliance, and retained foundational capabilities ([[01 Books/Volume III - Agentic Systems/05 Learning from Trajectories/16 Trajectory Fine-Tuning/V3-16.12 Evaluating a Fine-Tuned Agent#^sec-vol3-fine-tuning-benchmarking|section 16.11]]), while maintaining strict runtime validation checks regardless of training performance.
