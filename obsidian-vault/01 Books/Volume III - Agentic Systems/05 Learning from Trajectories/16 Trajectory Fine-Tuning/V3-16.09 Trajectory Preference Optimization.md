# V3-16.09 Trajectory Preference Optimization

[[Home|Home]] · [[01 Books/Volume III - Agentic Systems/Volume 3 Overview|Volume Overview]] · [[01 Books/Volume III - Agentic Systems/05 Learning from Trajectories/16 Trajectory Fine-Tuning/V3-16 Trajectory Fine-Tuning|V3-16 Trajectory Fine-Tuning]]

^sec-vol3-fine-tuning-dpo

Consider two independently collected continuations of the connection-pool repair that start from identical diagnostic context and both resolve the leak. One continuation runs the entire integration test suite after every minor edit, while the other runs targeted unit tests and executes the full suite only upon completion. Supervised imitation of both trajectories teaches the agent wasteful verification habits alongside disciplined ones; training only on the efficient path discards the signal that the slower trajectory was functionally correct yet needlessly expensive. A preference comparison captures this trade-off directly, steering the policy toward the efficient trajectory without treating the slower candidate as a failed repair. Not every pair of recorded interactions forms an admissible preference comparison: in the recovery demonstration, for instance, the malformed proposal `e2` and the subsequent correction `e4` cannot be paired as preferences, because the correction conditioned on environmental error messages that `e2` never saw. Valid comparisons require alternative decisions evaluated under identical available information.

↩︎ The preference-pair admission predicates, which require competing trajectories to branch from identical conditioning contexts, are established in **[[01 Books/Volume III - Agentic Systems/05 Learning from Trajectories/15 Trajectory Curation/V3-15.06 Staged Verifier Cascades#^sec-vol3-verifier-funnel|Admission predicates]]**.

### The Trajectory Preference Loss

^sec-vol3-fine-tuning-preference-loss

Let ${c}$ identify the shared comparison context and evaluation criterion, and let ${\tau_w}$ and ${\tau_l}$ denote the preferred and dispreferred trajectory records. Unlike a supervised fine-tuning mask that zeros out dispreferred attempts, a preference comparison selects the divergent policy spans in both records and contrasts their log-probability ratios against a frozen starting checkpoint $\pi_{\text{ref}}$ (typically the supervised fine-tuned checkpoint). These subscripts reflect the relative ranking assigned during curation rather than binary correctness, and the word “reference” now names a different role than it did for $\pi^*$ in [[01 Books/Volume III - Agentic Systems/05 Learning from Trajectories/16 Trajectory Fine-Tuning/V3-16.07 Exposure Bias#^sec-vol3-fine-tuning-exposure-bias|section 16.6]]. There, $\pi^*$ was the expert whose actions defined correct behavior, whereas here $\pi_{\text{ref}}$ is only the starting checkpoint the update is measured against. Both the active policy ${\pi_\theta}$ and $\pi_{\text{ref}}$ must assign nonzero probability to all scored tokens under the specified input prefixes.

For each trajectory, let ${h}_t$ denote the rendered context prefix used to predict action token ${x}_t$, and let ${\mathcal{A}}({\tau})$ denote the set of policy-generated token indices selected by the preference objective. Define the reference-relative score as

$$
{\Delta_\theta}({\tau};{c})=\sum_{t\in{\mathcal{A}}({\tau})} \log\frac{{\pi_\theta}({x}_t\mid {h}_t)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}({x}_t\mid {h}_t)}.
$$

Environment observations interleaved within ${h}_t$ condition subsequent predictions but carry no policy likelihood terms. The token selection set ${\mathcal{A}}({\tau})$ is customized specifically for each comparison. The dispreferred trajectory is evaluated solely to construct a negative contrast; it never serves as an imitation target.

Earlier reinforcement learning from human feedback pipelines fit a separate reward model to pairwise comparisons and then optimized the policy against that learned surrogate using reinforcement learning ([[01 Books/Volume III - Agentic Systems/05 Learning from Trajectories/16 Trajectory Fine-Tuning/V3-16.18 Source References#^ref-christiano2017deep|Christiano et al. 2017]]; [[01 Books/Volume III - Agentic Systems/05 Learning from Trajectories/16 Trajectory Fine-Tuning/V3-16.18 Source References#^ref-ouyang2022instructgpt|Ouyang et al. 2022]]). **Direct preference optimization (DPO)** bypasses the reward model entirely by optimizing a logistic loss directly over the difference of policy-to-reference log-probability ratios ([[01 Books/Volume III - Agentic Systems/05 Learning from Trajectories/16 Trajectory Fine-Tuning/V3-16.18 Source References#^ref-rafailov2023dpo|Rafailov et al. 2023]]). Using the standard sigmoid function ${\sigma}({z})=1/(1+e^{-{z}})$, a positive preference scale ${\beta}$ that sets how strongly the objective resists drift from $\pi_{\text{ref}}$, and a dataset ${\mathcal{D}}$ of curated comparison pairs, the resulting objective over selected tokens is

$$
{\mathcal{L}_{\text{DPO}}}=-\mathbb E_{({c},{\tau_w},{\tau_l})\sim{\mathcal{D}}} \log{\sigma}\!\left({\beta}[{\Delta_\theta}({\tau_w};{c})-{\Delta_\theta}({\tau_l};{c})]\right). \tag{7}
$$

^eq-vol3-dpo-trajectory

The classic DPO derivation applies to single-turn responses generated for an identical prompt under the Bradley–Terry preference model[[01 Books/Volume III - Agentic Systems/05 Learning from Trajectories/16 Trajectory Fine-Tuning/V3-16.19 Footnotes#^fn6|⁶]] ([[01 Books/Volume III - Agentic Systems/05 Learning from Trajectories/16 Trajectory Fine-Tuning/V3-16.18 Source References#^ref-bradley1952rank|Bradley and Terry 1952]]) and penalizes drift from $\pi_{\text{ref}}$ with a Kullback–Leibler divergence, the expected log-ratio of two distributions’ probabilities, which is zero only when they match. The penalty is taken in the “reverse” direction, with the expectation over the trained policy’s own outputs rather than the reference’s. In outline, the best policy under that penalty is the reference reweighted by the exponentiated reward and divided by a per-context normalizing term (the partition function) that makes its probabilities sum to one. Solving that relation for the reward expresses it as ${\beta}$ times the policy-to-reference log ratio plus ${\beta}$ times the logarithm of the normalizer. Because both members of a pair share the same context, they share the same normalizer, and it drops out when one score is subtracted from the other, which is all the Bradley–Terry model ever uses. Consequently, the implicit reward is identified only up to an additive constant per prompt context. Applying this framework to multi-step agent trajectories by scoring selected token spans ${\mathcal{A}}({\tau})$ is an engineering surrogate built on that foundation, and closed-loop evaluation must validate it.

↩︎ The Kullback–Leibler divergence, used there to compare a baseline’s and a candidate’s tool-call distributions, is established in **[[01 Books/Volume III - Agentic Systems/04 The Agent Runtime/14 Operating Agents/V3-14.06 Staged Releases#^sec-vol3-evaluation-releases-shadow|Offline comparison and shadow evidence]]**.

The full probability of generating a multi-turn trajectory is the product of the policy’s probabilities on its action tokens and the environment’s transition probabilities on the interleaved observations. When the preference objective forms the ratio of a single recorded trajectory’s probability under ${\pi_\theta}$ to its probability under $\pi_{\text{ref}}$, both the numerator and the denominator evaluate the same historical observations, so the stationary environment transition terms divide out and leave only the sum of action log-probability ratios in ${\Delta_\theta}({\tau};{c})$. Between two alternative trajectories ${\tau_w}$ and ${\tau_l}$, however, the observed states diverge after the first different tool call. Those divergent observations alter the conditioning context ${h}_t$ for all downstream actions, ensuring that environmental feedback continues to shape the cumulative score difference even though the policy assigns no explicit probability to environment outputs.

For a single comparison pair, define the **preference margin** ${z}={\beta}({\Delta_w}-{\Delta_l})$ (a new use of ${z}$, unrelated to the rationale tokens of [[01 Books/Volume III - Agentic Systems/05 Learning from Trajectories/16 Trajectory Fine-Tuning/V3-16.05 Observation Loss Masking#^sec-vol3-fine-tuning-optional-rationale|section 16.4.4]]), where ${\Delta_w}$ and ${\Delta_l}$ represent the reference-relative log-likelihood sums for the preferred and dispreferred trajectories. Differentiating the loss in [[01 Books/Volume III - Agentic Systems/05 Learning from Trajectories/16 Trajectory Fine-Tuning/V3-16.09 Trajectory Preference Optimization#^eq-vol3-dpo-trajectory|equation 7]] with respect to the policy parameters ${\theta}$ while holding the reference policy fixed yields

$$
\nabla_{\theta}{\mathcal{L}_{\text{DPO}}}=-{\beta}{\sigma}(-{z}) \left(\sum_{t\in{\mathcal{A}}({\tau_w})}\nabla_{\theta}\log{\pi_\theta}({x}_t\mid {h}_t) -\sum_{t\in{\mathcal{A}}({\tau_l})}\nabla_{\theta}\log{\pi_\theta}({x}_t\mid {h}_t)\right). \tag{8}
$$

^eq-vol3-dpo-gradient

The gradient has three systems consequences. First, identical selected tokens sharing an identical conditioning prefix contribute equal gradient vectors to both sums in [[01 Books/Volume III - Agentic Systems/05 Learning from Trajectories/16 Trajectory Fine-Tuning/V3-16.09 Trajectory Preference Optimization#^eq-vol3-dpo-gradient|equation 8]] and cancel exactly. A shared prefix therefore suffers no negative gradient updates simply because it also appears at the start of the dispreferred record. Once the paths diverge, identical tokens no longer cancel because their conditioning histories differ. Second, because shared model parameters parameterize all tokens, updating weights on selected positions also shifts likelihoods on unselected positions. Third, the full scalar coefficient multiplying the difference of gradient sums is ${\beta}{\sigma}(-{z})$; ${\sigma}(-{z})=1-{\sigma}({z})$ is its saturation factor. At the reference policy, both scores are zero (${z}=0$), so the factor is $0.5$ and the coefficient is ${\beta}/2$. At fixed ${\beta}$, increasing a positive margin reduces both quantities, dampening the contribution of pairs the policy already distinguishes. The coefficient alone does not determine the full gradient norm or the optimizer step, which also depend on the gradient vectors and optimization state.

**Napkin Math 16.4: How hard one comparison pulls**

**Problem**: After some training, the policy has moved relative to the reference on one admitted comparison. What are the pair’s margin, loss, saturation factor, and full gradient coefficient, and how do they change if ${\beta}$ doubles?

**Variables**: The selected-token log-probability sums are -8 for the active policy and -10 for the reference on the preferred record; the dispreferred record’s sums are -11 and -10. Use ${\beta}=$ 0.2. These are hypothetical, dimensionless natural-log quantities.

**Math**: The reference-relative scores are 2 and -1, giving margin ${z}=$ 0.6, logistic score ${\sigma}({z})=$ 0.646, and pair loss 0.437, down from 0.693 at ${z}=0$. The saturation factor ${\sigma}(-{z})$ falls from 0.5 to 0.354, while the full coefficient ${\beta}{\sigma}(-{z})$ falls from 0.1 to 0.0709. At ${\beta}=$ 0.4, the same log-probabilities give ${z}=$ 1.2, a logistic score of 0.769, a saturation factor of 0.231, and a full coefficient of 0.0926.

**Systems insight**: Changing ${\beta}$ changes both the margin and the gradient prefactor. In this fixed-record comparison, doubling it reduces the saturation factor from 0.354 to 0.231 but increases the full coefficient from 0.0709 to 0.0926. This local coefficient comparison establishes neither the eventual policy’s divergence from the reference nor the size of its optimizer step. The logistic score ${\sigma}({z})$ rises with ${\beta}$ for the same log-probabilities, reflecting the model’s fit to this comparison rather than the operational success probability of the repair. Furthermore, the margin can increase either by boosting the likelihood of the preferred trajectory or by suppressing the dispreferred trajectory; only closed-loop execution can verify whether the resulting policy generalizes or merely avoids the dispreferred path.

Scoring an entire trajectory spreads credit and blame across every divergent token in the interaction, whereas narrowing ${\mathcal{A}}({\tau})$ to the branch point where the competing tool calls or edits first diverge focuses the gradient on that decision. Because log-probability ratios can be positive or negative, summing over longer sequences does not bias the margin in one direction, but it does increase score variance. Teams commonly test two modifications to control length sensitivity: dividing each score by its selected-token count $|{\mathcal{A}}({\tau})|$, and adding an auxiliary supervised imitation loss on the preferred record ${\tau_w}$ to keep the policy from drifting into low-likelihood outputs. Both choices are validated on closed-loop trials.

### Reference Log-Probability Caching

^sec-vol3-fine-tuning-reference-caching

Whichever token span ${\mathcal{A}}({\tau})$ the objective scores, evaluating ${\Delta_\theta}({\tau};{c})$ online requires holding a frozen copy of $\pi_{\text{ref}}$ in accelerator memory alongside the active policy and running reference forward passes on every preferred and dispreferred record. Because $\pi_{\text{ref}}$ and the training dataset are fixed across a DPO run, the pipeline can precompute and cache the reference policy’s log-probabilities for all selected action tokens ahead of time. When token masks and aggregation rules are also fixed, storing only the scalar sum ${\Delta_{\text{ref}}}({\tau})$ avoids saving per-token arrays or materializing full-vocabulary logits. To keep cached sums from going silently stale across experiments, the cache key must bind every input that affects the number: the reference weights and any adapter configuration ([[01 Books/Volume III - Agentic Systems/05 Learning from Trajectories/16 Trajectory Fine-Tuning/V3-16.11 Parameter-Efficient Fine-Tuning#^sec-vol3-fine-tuning-peft|section 16.10]]), the tokenizer and chat template, the exact token sequences and selected indices, the attention, position, and truncation settings, and the floating-point precision mode (such as FP16 vs. BF16). Changing data preprocessing or token selection invalidates cached sums even when the reference weights have not changed. Pipelines therefore check cached values against live recomputations on sampled batches within a declared numerical tolerance (for example, $10^{-5}$) rather than assuming bitwise identity across accelerators or kernels.

Caching drops the reference weights and their forward passes from the training loop, trading preprocessing compute, disk space, and host-to-device bandwidth for accelerator memory. That removes the reference model’s weight and activation footprint, while the active policy’s weights, optimizer state (the running moments an adaptive optimizer keeps for each trained parameter), gradient buffers, and forward activations remain on the device. [[01 Books/Volume III - Agentic Systems/05 Learning from Trajectories/16 Trajectory Fine-Tuning/V3-16.11 Parameter-Efficient Fine-Tuning#^sec-vol3-fine-tuning-peft|Section 16.10]] itemizes those remaining arrays. Preference optimization changes the loss function applied to a curated corpus, while on-policy labeling and fault injection change which states the corpus covers. When the bottleneck at serving time is instead the cost of carrying a long procedural prompt or running a large teacher model on every turn, policy distillation moves that behavior into a smaller student ([[01 Books/Volume III - Agentic Systems/05 Learning from Trajectories/16 Trajectory Fine-Tuning/V3-16.10 Policy Distillation#^sec-vol3-fine-tuning-distillation|section 16.9]]).
