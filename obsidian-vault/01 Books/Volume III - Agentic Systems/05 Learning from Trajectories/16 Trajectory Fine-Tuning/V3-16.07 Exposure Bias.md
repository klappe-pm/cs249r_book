# V3-16.07 Exposure Bias

[[Home|Home]] · [[01 Books/Volume III - Agentic Systems/Volume 3 Overview|Volume Overview]] · [[01 Books/Volume III - Agentic Systems/05 Learning from Trajectories/16 Trajectory Fine-Tuning/V3-16 Trajectory Fine-Tuning|V3-16 Trajectory Fine-Tuning]]

^sec-vol3-fine-tuning-exposure-bias

Suppose a repair policy trained on this corpus is placed in a fresh bounded trial and meets a version conflict instead of the malformed-argument rejection recorded in `trace_r19_01_redacted_v1`. A plausible failure is that the policy repeats its previously accepted edit without first querying compatible state evidence. The runtime’s conditional-update guard then blocks the stale write, and the trial exhausts its execution allowance without completing the repair. The training corpus never included that state, so nothing in it taught a correction. While serialization and loss masking preserve the demonstrated decision problem, production deployment dictates which states the agent actually encounters. A policy trained only under teacher forcing never observes its own compounding errors during optimization. In standard text generation, an unexpected token merely drifts stylistic phrasing. In an autonomous agent, an errant action mutates the external environment and pushes the agent into states that the demonstration corpus never covered.

**Definition 16.3: Exposure bias**

***Exposure bias*** is the mismatch between training a sequential policy on reference prefixes and running it on prefixes produced by its own earlier outputs ([[01 Books/Volume III - Agentic Systems/05 Learning from Trajectories/16 Trajectory Fine-Tuning/V3-16.18 Source References#^ref-bengio2015scheduledsampling|Bengio et al. 2015]]; [[01 Books/Volume III - Agentic Systems/05 Learning from Trajectories/16 Trajectory Fine-Tuning/V3-16.18 Source References#^ref-ranzato2016sequence|Ranzato et al. 2016]]).

1.  **Significance**: It converts a per-step error rate into a horizon-dependent cost. Unchecked horizon compounding (**[[01 Books/Volume III - Agentic Systems/00 Introduction/01 Introduction/V3-01.07 Reliability and Cost Over a Horizon#^sec-vol3-intro-horizon-arithmetic|Reliability and Cost over a Horizon]]**) already makes the failure probability roughly ${H}{\epsilon}$ for a small per-step rate ${\epsilon}$. When that rate is measured solely on reference states, the best available bound on excess task cost is ${H}^2{\epsilon}$ (theorem [16.1](https://mlsysbook.ai/vol3/16_fine_tuning/16_fine_tuning.html#thrm-15-imitation-regret)), one further factor of ${H}$.
2.  **Distinction**: It reflects a structural property of the training distribution rather than an isolated mistake. A policy can err on states the corpus covers and recover on states it never showed.
3.  **Common pitfall**: Interpreting low offline validation loss on recorded prefixes as evidence that deployed execution will succeed. Offline loss samples only the reference trajectory, revealing nothing about the states the deployed policy visits once it deviates.

Offline training corpora can include recovery trajectories, allowing an agent that departs from an ideal demonstration to re-enter a familiar state or discover an alternate valid solution. Exposure bias,[[01 Books/Volume III - Agentic Systems/05 Learning from Trajectories/16 Trajectory Fine-Tuning/V3-16.19 Footnotes#^fn4|⁴]] however, concerns how thoroughly the corpus covers the state distribution generated by the deployed policy itself, including unexpected edge states like the version conflict.

### State Distributions and Imitation Regret

^sec-vol3-fine-tuning-imitation-regret

An idealized finite-horizon model makes the cost of missing states like the version conflict exact. This derivation follows the imitation-learning notation locally. Let ${t}$ count discrete decision steps rather than token positions. Let ${s}$ denote the complete environment state of the decision process (including interaction history and system variables the agent cannot directly observe, whereas ${s}_{t}$ in definition [16.1](https://mlsysbook.ai/vol3/16_fine_tuning/16_fine_tuning.html#dfn-action-loss-masking) named only the policy’s visible token context). View the trained policy $\pi$ (corresponding to ${\pi_\theta}$) and the deterministic expert reference $\pi^*$ as maps from states to actions over a ${H}$-step horizon under identical initial states and transition dynamics. If ${d_\pi}^t$ is the distribution of states visited before step ${t}$ under $\pi$, the horizon-averaged *state distribution* is ${d_\pi} = {H}^{-1}\sum_{t=1}^{H} {d_\pi}^t$.

Let ${C}({s},{a})\in[0,1]$ be a normalized immediate task cost and ${J}(\pi)$ its expected sum over the horizon. Let ${\mathcal{L}}({s},\pi)=\Pr_{{a}\sim\pi(\cdot\mid {s})}[{a}\ne\pi^*({s})]$ measure the probability of choosing an action different from the reference at decision time, a conservative proxy for task error since several distinct actions may be acceptable, and a decision-step quantity distinct from token-level cross-entropy. Finally, the *cost-to-go* ${Q_t^{\pi^*}}({s},{a})$ is the expected cost from step ${t}$ through ${H}$ after taking ${a}$ in ${s}$ and following the reference $\pi^*$ thereafter.

**Theorem 16.1: Imitation regret bounds**

*The horizon penalty of imitation depends on which states the error is measured on.* For a policy $\pi$ imitating a deterministic reference $\pi^*$ over ${H}$ steps with per-step cost in $[0,1]$, Ross et al. ([[01 Books/Volume III - Agentic Systems/05 Learning from Trajectories/16 Trajectory Fine-Tuning/V3-16.18 Source References#^ref-ross2011reduction|2011]]) bound the excess task cost in two ways.

**Reference-state error** (their Theorem 2.1, first proved by Ross and Bagnell ([[01 Books/Volume III - Agentic Systems/05 Learning from Trajectories/16 Trajectory Fine-Tuning/V3-16.18 Source References#^ref-ross2010efficient|2010]])). With disagreement measured on the reference policy’s states, ${\epsilon_E}=\mathbb E_{{s}\sim {d_{\pi^*}}}[{\mathcal{L}}({s},\pi)]$,

$$
{J}(\pi)-{J}(\pi^*)\le \min\{{H},\;{H}^2{\epsilon_E}\}. \tag{5}
$$

^eq-vol3-quadratic-derivation

**Learner-state error** (their Theorem 2.2). With disagreement measured on the learner’s states, ${\epsilon_\pi}=\mathbb E_{{s}\sim {d_\pi}}[{\mathcal{L}}({s},\pi)]$, and every deviation followed by reference recovery costing at most ${u}$ extra, ${Q_t^{\pi^*}}({s},{a})-{Q_t^{\pi^*}}({s},\pi^*({s}))\le {u}$ at every learner-reachable state,

$$
{J}(\pi)-{J}(\pi^*)\le \min\{{H},\;{u} {H}{\epsilon_\pi}\}. \tag{6}
$$

^eq-vol3-linear-regret-bound

- **Horizon** (${H}$): decision steps in the task; because each step costs at most one, ${H}$ also caps both bounds.
- **State distributions** (${d_{\pi^*}}$, ${d_\pi}$): the environment states the reference or the learner visits, averaged over the horizon.
- **Disagreement rates** (${\epsilon_E}$, ${\epsilon_\pi}$): probability of an action other than the reference’s, averaged over reference-visited vs. learner-visited states.
- **Deviation disadvantage** (${u}\ge0$): the largest extra expected cost of one deviation followed by reference recovery, an assumption about the task rather than a measured retry count.

The same error rate therefore carries different horizon penalties. At ${H}=$ 30 and a 2 percent disagreement rate, the reference-state bound is 18 cost units and the learner-state bound with ${u}=$ 1 is 0.6, a gap of 30×, which is ${H}/{u}$. The linear bound holds only while ${u}$ stays independent of ${H}$; one action that can spoil the rest of the task makes ${u}$ grow with ${H}$ and restores the quadratic dependence.

Both bounds have a direct physical reading. For the reference-state bound, couple the environment randomness of the two policies until they first choose different actions. Across ${H}$ decision steps, the probability that the learner ever diverges from the reference path is at most the sum of per-step disagreement probabilities, ${H}{\epsilon_E}$. Because an offline corpus contains only reference-visited states, this coupling argument makes no assumptions about how the learner behaves once it leaves the reference trajectory, so a single divergence can cost up to one unit on each of the remaining ${H}$ steps and multiply ${H}{\epsilon_E}$ by a second factor of ${H}$. When the corpus instead covers the learner’s own state distribution with disagreement rate ${\epsilon_\pi}$, a slip at step ${t}$ moves the agent into a state where it still knows how to follow the reference recovery. Each deviation therefore adds at most ${u}$ extra cost rather than spoiling the remaining horizon, and summing that penalty across ${H}$ steps yields ${u}{H}{\epsilon_\pi}$. **[[05 Reference/Volume III - Agentic Systems/V3 Mathematical Foundations#^sec-vol3-appendix-math-compounding-error|Conditional error bounds in trajectory imitation]]** derives the reference-state bound by coupling and the learner-state bound through telescoping policy comparisons.

Unchecked runtime step errors compound as $(1-{\epsilon})^{H}$ (**[[01 Books/Volume III - Agentic Systems/00 Introduction/01 Introduction/V3-01.07 Reliability and Cost Over a Horizon#^eq-vol3-horizon-compounding|equation 1.1]]**), and for small ${\epsilon}$ this is close to $1-{H}{\epsilon}$, so the chance of some failure grows roughly as ${H}{\epsilon}$. Theorem [16.1](https://mlsysbook.ai/vol3/16_fine_tuning/16_fine_tuning.html#thrm-15-imitation-regret) adds the training-side consequence, since imitation whose error is measured on the reference distribution is bounded only by ${H}^2{\epsilon}$, a second factor of ${H}$. The two rates are analogous rather than identical. The introduction’s ${\epsilon}$ is a probability of error, while the disagreement rate counts every departure from the reference, harmless ones included, and so upper-bounds that error probability.

*Same 2 percent error: 18 cost units on reference states, 0.6 on learner states.*

The bounds are stated in normalized cost units. If each step spent off the reference path costs one unit and every other step costs nothing, the reference-state bound of 18 units caps the expected number of wasted turns at 18 of 30. The learner-state bound of 0.6 caps it below one. Wasted turns reach the familiar evaluation quantities directly, since they raise the expected attempt cost in ${C_{\text{eff}}}$ and, once they exhaust the turn budget, lower the acceptance probability. The deviation disadvantage ${u}$ is an assumption about the task and its reference, not a property of the trained learner; it asks how much extra cost one slip adds when the reference takes over afterward. A repair edit that the runtime refuses before dispatch, or that a version guard blocks, costs a small, fixed number of extra turns to correct however long the task is (in the recorded trace, one schema inspection and one reissued call). A small ${u}$ that does not grow with ${H}$ is therefore plausible for the repair agent. Retrospective recovery records help only to the extent that they populate the training corpus with learner-visited states and keep ${u}$ small, but an offline dataset alone cannot guarantee that either assumption holds during deployment.

**Napkin Math 16.3: The same error rate under different assumptions**

**Problem**: An illustrative task has horizon 30 and immediate cost bounded between zero and one. Compare a 2 percent disagreement rate on reference-policy states with the same numerical rate on learner-policy states. What changes if the latter also satisfies a deviation-disadvantage bound of ${u}=$ 1, and how accurate would offline imitation have to be to match it?

**Math**: The reference-state bound multiplies ${H}^2=$ 900 by ${\epsilon_E}=$ 0.02, giving 18 normalized cost units, below the cap of 30. The learner-state bound multiplies ${u} {H}=$ 30 by the same rate, giving 0.6. If the disadvantage instead reaches ${u}={H}$, the learner bound returns to 18. Matching the learner-state bound on reference states requires ${\epsilon_E}$ equal to that bound divided by ${H}^2$, about 0.07 percent.

**Result**: At this horizon the two bounds differ by 30× for the same measured error rate.

**Systems insight**: The gap is ${H}/{u}$, so it grows linearly with the horizon. Offline imitation must be ${H}/{u}$ times more accurate on reference states to carry the guarantee that labeling learner-visited states provides. The reference-state bound also saturates at its cap once ${H}\ge 1/{\epsilon_E}$, here 50 steps, beyond which it constrains nothing. Long-horizon agents with cheap, recoverable deviations are therefore where on-policy labeling pays most. When one deviation can spoil the task (${u}$ near ${H}$), the learner-state bound is no better than the quadratic one, and the runtime checks that invariant closure (principle [[01 Books/Volume III - Agentic Systems/00 Introduction/01 Introduction/V3-01.06 Invariant Closure#^pri-invariant-closure|1]]) requires carry the reliability.

### Dataset Aggregation

^sec-vol3-fine-tuning-dagger

A fixed offline corpus never shows the policy how to recover from states that only the learner’s own actions reach, such as the version conflict. **Dataset Aggregation (DAgger)** closes this coverage gap by rolling out trajectories under successive learned policies. It queries an authorized reference labeler at the states they actually visit, adds those labeled transitions to the training corpus, and retrains the policy[[01 Books/Volume III - Agentic Systems/05 Learning from Trajectories/16 Trajectory Fine-Tuning/V3-16.19 Footnotes#^fn5|⁵]] ([[01 Books/Volume III - Agentic Systems/05 Learning from Trajectories/16 Trajectory Fine-Tuning/V3-16.18 Source References#^ref-ross2011reduction|Ross et al. 2011]]). The resulting dataset is **on-policy data**, drawn from the state distribution of the policy being trained. Executing a learner action in the environment and generating its reference label are separate operations, and early collection rounds mix them. In round ${k}$, the collection worker executes the reference action with probability ${\lambda}_k$ and the learner’s action otherwise, decaying ${\lambda}_k$ toward zero across rounds. This schedule shifts the collected states toward the learner’s own distribution, where theorem [16.1](https://mlsysbook.ai/vol3/16_fine_tuning/16_fine_tuning.html#thrm-15-imitation-regret) measures ${\epsilon_\pi}$. Because aggregation broadens coverage without making the training and learner distributions identical, and because the linear bound holds only where ${u}$ stays small, each retrained round is checked on fresh rollouts rather than assumed to satisfy the bound.

When the learner reaches an off-distribution state like the version conflict, the labeler (typically a larger foundation model or a search procedure with an expanded budget) proposes a corrective action, which can itself be wrong. To avoid training recovery in the wrong direction, the curation pipeline executes the proposed step inside an isolated sandbox and admits the label only if automated verification passes under the predicates of **[[01 Books/Volume III - Agentic Systems/05 Learning from Trajectories/15 Trajectory Curation/V3-15.06 Staged Verifier Cascades#^sec-vol3-verifier-funnel|Admission predicates]]**. A passing continuation confirms that the label works, not that it is the only valid action. Because steps that look superficially valid can still rely on ungrounded assumptions or violated preconditions, the pipeline records ground-truth observations, enforces tool contracts, and tracks lineage for disputed labels. After retraining shifts the policy’s visited states, the updated checkpoint is evaluated on fresh bounded rollouts and held-out suites rather than on the accumulated training buffer.

Waiting for live rollouts to reach every rare failure mode is slow when the harness team already knows which infrastructure faults will occur. **Fault injection** supplies those off-path states deliberately rather than waiting for the learner to reach them. The fault conditions a collection fixture must reproduce faithfully, and the causal distinction each one preserves, are established in **[[01 Books/Volume III - Agentic Systems/05 Learning from Trajectories/15 Trajectory Curation/V3-15.07 Recovery Demonstration Curation#^sec-vol3-trajectory-curation-fault-injection|Fault injection]]**. The training-specific question is where the mask falls. In a fault-injected record, the synthesized error is presented as an observation with loss mask ${m}_{t} = 0$, and the diagnosis and corrective tool invocation that follow are targets with ${m}_{t} = 1$, so the gradient lands on decision logic that nominal demonstrations never exercise. The selected targets inherit the fixture’s causal distinctions, so a record that answers a lost acknowledgment with a blind retry teaches the duplicate effect that settlement before retry (principle [[01 Books/Volume III - Agentic Systems/03 Tool Use/V3 Tool Use Principles#^pri-vol3-exactly-once-settlement|8]]) exists to prevent. Fault injection covers the failures the harness designers anticipated, whereas on-policy collection uncovers the ones the model’s own behavior produces ([[01 Books/Volume III - Agentic Systems/05 Learning from Trajectories/16 Trajectory Fine-Tuning/V3-16.07 Exposure Bias#^fig-vol3-exposure-bias-dagger|figure 4]]). Both are choices of behavior source in the sense of [[01 Books/Volume III - Agentic Systems/05 Learning from Trajectories/16 Trajectory Fine-Tuning/V3-16.03 Scope of Supervised Fine-Tuning#^sec-vol3-fine-tuning-adaptation-choices|section 16.2.2]], and both still need a step-level label for every selected decision. Reinforcement learning from verifiable rewards drops that requirement by scoring fresh rollouts against terminal outcomes instead.

↪ Optimizing a policy against verifiable outcomes on fresh rollouts, without step-level labels, is developed in **[[01 Books/Volume III - Agentic Systems/05 Learning from Trajectories/17 Reinforcement Learning from Verifiable Rewards/V3-17.03 Foundations of Verifiable Rewards#^sec-vol3-rlvr-need|Foundations of Verifiable Rewards]]**.

![[90 Assets/Volume III/16 Fine Tuning/Dagger Fault Injection 5095150f9f.svg]]

Figure 4: **Exposure Bias and On-Policy Labeling**: Training only on recorded reference states (a) leaves any state the learner reaches after a deviation without a recorded correction, and the applicable bound, [[01 Books/Volume III - Agentic Systems/05 Learning from Trajectories/16 Trajectory Fine-Tuning/V3-16.07 Exposure Bias#^eq-vol3-quadratic-derivation|equation 5]], grows with ${H}^2{\epsilon_E}$. Each labeling round (b) runs the current policy, mixed with the reference at probability ${\lambda}_k$, labels the states it visits, keeps a label only if the sandbox continuation passes the verifier, aggregates, and retrains. The bound in [[01 Books/Volume III - Agentic Systems/05 Learning from Trajectories/16 Trajectory Fine-Tuning/V3-16.07 Exposure Bias#^eq-vol3-linear-regret-bound|equation 6]] applies only when error is measured on learner states and each deviation’s disadvantage is bounded by ${u}$; aggregation broadens coverage but does not by itself make the training and learner distributions equal.

**Checkpoint 16.2: Coverage after faithful imitation**

**Distributions**: Why can low disagreement on reference-policy states coexist with poor behavior on a new policy’s states, even when the corpus includes recovery?

**Saturation estimate**: With a disagreement rate of 2 percent on reference states, at what horizon does the reference-state bound reach its cap ${H}$, and what does that bound imply about a task twice as long?

**Consequences**: What does ${u}$ bound, and for a remediation step that cannot be undone, is ${u}$ expected near one or near ${H}$?

**Evidence**: Why is a passing continuation useful evidence for a corrective label without proving that the label is uniquely correct?

**Collection choices**: How do labeled aggregation, fault injection, and fresh reward-scored rollouts differ in the information they require?

State coverage encompasses both environment variables and the tool contracts presented at each decision step. An agent may recover reliably from familiar runtime exceptions yet fail persistently by emitting deprecated argument names following an interface update. Schema robustness tests this narrower sensitivity to structured tool definitions.
