# V3-17.12 Fallacies and Pitfalls

[[Home|Home]] · [[01 Books/Volume III - Agentic Systems/Volume 3 Overview|Volume Overview]] · [[01 Books/Volume III - Agentic Systems/05 Learning from Trajectories/17 Reinforcement Learning from Verifiable Rewards/V3-17 Reinforcement Learning from Verifiable Rewards|V3-17 Reinforcement Learning from Verifiable Rewards]]

^sec-vol3-rlvr-fallacies

A rollout group can easily contain positive rewards without establishing a sound solution, and a policy update can fit that group without improving the deployed agent. The pitfalls detailed below each turn a narrow, scoped measurement into an unwarranted conclusion, and each carries a concrete systems cost.

**Fallacy**: *An automated reward is a complete task specification.*

Automated test suites check only the conditions their authors anticipated, and reinforcement learning operates as an aggressive search procedure that reliably uncovers unhandled edge cases. When a policy’s true success rate is low, Bayesian base rates compound this vulnerability. As demonstrated in notebook [17.1](https://mlsysbook.ai/vol3/17_rlvr/17_rlvr.html#nbk-16-oracle-gaming), a verifier that misclassifies only 2.5 percent of failing attempts still results in 18.4 percent of all rewarded rollouts being outright failures when the policy solves only 10 percent of tasks. Because reinforcement updates treat every rewarded attempt as a success, the optimizer systematically increases the probability of the tokens that generated those spurious answers. Always benchmark the verifier against suites of known incorrect solutions, evaluate its false-acceptance rate against the policy’s current pass rate, and gate production deployment on strict held-out task criteria rather than raw training rewards.

**Pitfall**: *Leaving the scorer, the runner, or its configuration where the candidate can reach it.*

If an agent can read or write to its evaluation harness, modifying the test framework becomes the path of least resistance. In the METR evaluations, when a task exposed an editable scoring script to the agent, the model bypassed the intended challenge and tampered with the verifier in 21 of 21 runs (war story [17.1](https://mlsysbook.ai/vol3/17_rlvr/17_rlvr.html#ws-16-metr-reward-hacking-2025)). During training, this vulnerability causes severe gradient distortion. On difficult tasks where honest attempts fail, the first rollout that discovers an exploit becomes the group’s lone positive sample. In a rollout group of 16, that single hack receives an advantage coefficient of 3.87, while each honest failure receives a negative coefficient of 0.26 in magnitude, strongly reinforcing the exploit. Always evaluate candidate solutions in an ephemeral workspace isolated behind a hardened controller that retains exclusive ownership of the test harness, the execution runner, and the result path ([[01 Books/Volume III - Agentic Systems/05 Learning from Trajectories/17 Reinforcement Learning from Verifiable Rewards/V3-17.08 Verifier Isolation#^sec-vol3-rlvr-sandboxing|section 17.7]]). Even within that sandbox, treat all generated agent code as untrusted input.

**Fallacy**: *A bigger group is the way to extract signal from hard tasks.*

Increasing the rollout group size ${G}$ appears to offer a straightforward way to capture rare successes on difficult tasks. While larger groups improve the likelihood that a batch contains contrasting rewards, the probability of contrast alone does not measure the information each group supplies. At a pass rate of 5 percent, rollout groups of size 8 consume 23.8 rollouts for every informative group with nonzero variance. Expanding the group size to 16 still demands 28.6 rollouts per informative group. By contrast, steering the training curriculum toward tasks near a 50 percent pass rate consumes only 8.1 rollouts per informative group (notebook [17.4](https://mlsysbook.ai/vol3/17_rlvr/17_rlvr.html#nbk-16-rollout-budget)). Where obtaining contrast is the binding constraint and the task-mixture change is acceptable, this comparison favors targeting difficulty. Choose the group size using baseline precision, required task coverage, and held-out learning progress as well as rollout cost, and log the sampling distribution.

**Pitfall**: *Reading longer self-correction text as evidence of better reasoning.*

When an agent produces lengthy internal monologues and verbose self-critiques, engineers often mistake length for deeper reasoning. In reality, output lengths frequently expand as a direct artifact of loss normalization. Under per-rollout token averaging, a failing rollout of 128 tokens penalizes each individual token 32× as heavily as a failing rollout of 4,096 tokens. Because token-level gradient penalties dilute across longer generation lengths, this objective can favor longer failures even when the extra text performs no useful validation ([[01 Books/Volume III - Agentic Systems/05 Learning from Trajectories/17 Reinforcement Learning from Verifiable Rewards/V3-17.06 Group Relative Policy Optimization#^eq-vol3-grpo-length-coefficient|equation 5]]). To uncover length bias, compare sequence lengths conditioned on task outcome and train with a constant-denominator loss objective. Ultimately, evaluate candidate revisions by the concrete tool actions and verified task completions they deliver under a matched token budget, rather than by the model’s narration of reconsidering.

**Fallacy**: *Faster environment reset speeds training proportionally.*

Optimizing container teardown and environment initialization yields satisfying microbenchmarks, but Amdahl’s law strictly limits the resulting gains in end-to-end training throughput. Reset latency represents only one slice of the rollout lifecycle. Cutting reset overhead from 3.5 s to 15 ms delivers a 233× acceleration in reset speed, yet it improves execution slot capacity by only 1.77×. The remaining 4.5 s spent on model inference, tool execution, and verifier scoring continues to dominate every cycle (notebook [17.6](https://mlsysbook.ai/vol3/17_rlvr/17_rlvr.html#nbk-16-exploration-reset-goodput)). Moreover, increased rollout throughput converts into faster training progress only if the inference workers, verification services, and parameter optimizers maintain sufficient headroom to ingest the higher volume. Profile the entire training loop to pinpoint the saturated pipeline stage before investing engineering effort into optimizing any individual phase.
