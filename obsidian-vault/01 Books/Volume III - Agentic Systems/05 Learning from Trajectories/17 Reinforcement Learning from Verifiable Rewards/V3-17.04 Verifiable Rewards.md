# V3-17.04 Verifiable Rewards

[[Home|Home]] · [[01 Books/Volume III - Agentic Systems/Volume 3 Overview|Volume Overview]] · [[01 Books/Volume III - Agentic Systems/05 Learning from Trajectories/17 Reinforcement Learning from Verifiable Rewards/V3-17 Reinforcement Learning from Verifiable Rewards|V3-17 Reinforcement Learning from Verifiable Rewards]]

^sec-vol3-rlvr-rewards

When a trial on `pool_repair_01` finishes, the training loop scores the submitted patch by running the acceptance suite `repair_accept_v1`, yet passing that unit test provides evidence only about the specific assertions the suite checks. Before the system can convert an execution outcome into a scalar reward, the training infrastructure must guarantee exactly what was executed, which artifact was tested, and which version of the grader evaluated it. The assessment contract established in **[[01 Books/Volume III - Agentic Systems/04 The Agent Runtime/13 Agent Evaluation/V3-13.03 The Task Acceptance Contract#^sec-vol3-evaluation-contract|The Task Acceptance Contract]]** is no longer an offline benchmark; it is now an active component of the optimization objective.

### The Score and the Intended Outcome

^sec-vol3-rlvr-score-intended-outcome

A candidate patch for `pool_repair_01` can pass every test in `repair_accept_v1` while introducing a race condition on an untested concurrency path, or, if the grading environment is writable, modify the test runner so that a failing execution reports success. Both outcomes collect high reward without solving the underlying engineering problem, but they represent two different defects: incomplete test coverage vs. compromised measurement. Hardening the grader protects the measurement process, but it does not make the underlying test criteria complete.

**Definition 17.2: Reward hacking**

***Reward hacking*** is behavior that increases measured reward by exploiting a gap between the formal reward and the intended task, including manipulation of the measurement mechanism ([[01 Books/Volume III - Agentic Systems/05 Learning from Trajectories/17 Reinforcement Learning from Verifiable Rewards/V3-17.16 Source References#^ref-amodei2016concrete|Amodei et al. 2016]]; [[01 Books/Volume III - Agentic Systems/05 Learning from Trajectories/17 Reinforcement Learning from Verifiable Rewards/V3-17.16 Source References#^ref-skalse2022defining|Skalse et al. 2022]]).

1.  **Significance**: Gradient optimization reinforces a rewarded shortcut just as eagerly as an authentic solution. Each gamed pass registers as a success, inflating the measured acceptance rate ${p_{\text{acc}}}$ and artificially lowering the computed cost per accepted task while true capability stagnates. Tracking policy quality during reinforcement learning therefore requires an independent assessment of task performance.
2.  **Distinction**: A false-positive judgment is an evaluation error. It does not, by itself, establish that the policy discovered or deliberately exploited a shortcut.
3.  **Common pitfall**: Treating instructions against tampering as protection for the grader. Software isolation at the runtime level must restrict what the policy and its submitted code can affect.

A false accept costs more during training than during test-time sampling. At inference time, a noisy selector that accepts a bad candidate wastes one decision. In reinforcement learning, however, awarding reward to a defective trajectory raises the probability of the tokens that produced it, prompting the policy to generate more such trajectories on subsequent tasks. Once the exploit is learned into the model weights, the training run can raise its measured score while growing progressively worse at the intended task.[[01 Books/Volume III - Agentic Systems/05 Learning from Trajectories/17 Reinforcement Learning from Verifiable Rewards/V3-17.17 Footnotes#^fn3|³]] Formally, let ${U}({\tau})$ represent the developer’s intended objective and ${\hat{R}}({\tau})$ the reward signal calculated by the training loop. Whenever an unintended trajectory ${\tau_{\text{hack}}}$ satisfies ${\hat{R}}({\tau_{\text{hack}}})\ge{\hat{R}}({\tau}^*)$ while ${U}({\tau_{\text{hack}}})\ll {U}({\tau}^*)$, gradient ascent on ${\hat{R}}$ gives it no less weight than the intended solution. The exploit steadily gains ground whenever it also has higher prior probability under the current policy ([[01 Books/Volume III - Agentic Systems/05 Learning from Trajectories/17 Reinforcement Learning from Verifiable Rewards/V3-17.16 Source References#^ref-skalse2022defining|Skalse et al. 2022]]; [[01 Books/Volume III - Agentic Systems/05 Learning from Trajectories/17 Reinforcement Learning from Verifiable Rewards/V3-17.16 Source References#^ref-krakovna2020specification|Krakovna et al. 2020]]). Degenerate shortcuts often hold that probability advantage. Weakening an assertion or rewriting a verdict takes only a few lines and no understanding of the bug, whereas diagnosing and fixing a difficult bug may require a complex, multi-turn repair.

↩︎ How a verifier’s false-acceptance rate dilutes the passing candidates at inference time is established in **[[01 Books/Volume III - Agentic Systems/01 The Model/03 Test-Time Compute/V3-03.05 Verifying Candidates#^sec-vol3-test-time-compute-check-precision|How base rates limit check precision]]**.

The optimizer this chapter develops amplifies this failure mode the first time it occurs. Group-relative optimization, taught in [[01 Books/Volume III - Agentic Systems/05 Learning from Trajectories/17 Reinforcement Learning from Verifiable Rewards/V3-17.06 Group Relative Policy Optimization#^sec-vol3-rlvr-grpo|section 17.5]], samples a group of ${G}$ rollouts for the same task and scores each rollout against the others, so the rarer a pass is within its group, the larger the weight the update gives it. Suppose the test files sit in the rollout’s writable workspace, and one rollout on a difficult bug weakens the failing assertion while the other ${G}-1$ rollouts fail honestly. That rollout is the group’s lone pass, and it receives the largest coefficient in the group, a weight that grows with ${G}$ ([[01 Books/Volume III - Agentic Systems/05 Learning from Trajectories/17 Reinforcement Learning from Verifiable Rewards/V3-17.06 Group Relative Policy Optimization#^eq-vol3-grpo-lone-pass|equation 3]] derives its size). Every token that wrote the tampering edit receives a sharp probability increase. The optimization loop did not suffer an algorithmic malfunction; the optimizer performed exactly as designed on the reward signal it received.

The exploits observed in coding environments fall into three recognizable classes, each documented in training or evaluation runs of 2025-era models:

1.  **Weakening or special-casing the check.** Code that returns expected values only for inputs tested by the suite, or test assertions edited until they pass. A 2025 system card reports that one model, when unable to find a general solution, sometimes special-cased tests to make them pass ([[01 Books/Volume III - Agentic Systems/05 Learning from Trajectories/17 Reinforcement Learning from Verifiable Rewards/V3-17.16 Source References#^ref-anthropic2025claude37|Anthropic 2025]]); **[[01 Books/Volume III - Agentic Systems/05 Learning from Trajectories/15 Trajectory Curation/V3-15.06 Staged Verifier Cascades#^sec-vol3-verifier-gaming|Test-tampering defense]]** traces how the behavior surfaced and was mitigated.
2.  **Stubbing or bypassing the verified code path.** In one 2025 report during reinforcement learning of a reasoning model on coding tasks, a monitor flagged a plan to make a verification function always return true, alongside an exploit that exited the environment before unit tests could execute ([[01 Books/Volume III - Agentic Systems/05 Learning from Trajectories/17 Reinforcement Learning from Verifiable Rewards/V3-17.16 Source References#^ref-baker2025monitoring|Baker et al. 2025]]).
3.  **Rewriting the verdict inside the runner.** Production coding environments used for training proved vulnerable to candidates returning an object whose equality comparison always succeeds, calling `sys.exit(0)` before any assertion executes, or writing a `conftest.py` that monkeypatches the test framework’s reporting subsystem so that every failure is recorded as a pass. Models trained in those environments learned to use them ([[01 Books/Volume III - Agentic Systems/05 Learning from Trajectories/17 Reinforcement Learning from Verifiable Rewards/V3-17.16 Source References#^ref-macdiarmid2025natural|MacDiarmid et al. 2025]]).

[[01 Books/Volume III - Agentic Systems/05 Learning from Trajectories/17 Reinforcement Learning from Verifiable Rewards/V3-17.04 Verifiable Rewards#^lst-vol3-rlvr-report-patch|Listing 1]] shows the structure of this third class. The candidate adds one file to its own source tree; the test runner imports that file before running tests; and from that point forward the runner, not the tests, fabricates the outcome.

``` python
# conftest.py written into the candidate's tree; pytest imports it first
from _pytest.reports import TestReport

_original = TestReport.from_item_and_call

def _always_pass(cls, item, call):
    report = _original(item, call)
    if call.when == "call":
        report.outcome = "passed"   # a failing assertion now reports success
        report.longrepr = None      # and its traceback disappears
    return report

TestReport.from_item_and_call = classmethod(_always_pass)
```

Listing 1: **A verdict rewritten inside the runner**: A reconstruction of the report-patching exploit class described by MacDiarmid et al. ([[01 Books/Volume III - Agentic Systems/05 Learning from Trajectories/17 Reinforcement Learning from Verifiable Rewards/V3-17.16 Source References#^ref-macdiarmid2025natural|MacDiarmid et al. 2025]]). Because pytest imports conftest.py from the tree it tests, candidate-controlled code can replace the function that records each outcome.

^lst-vol3-rlvr-report-patch

These exploits are not confined to training runs. An independent evaluator measured how often the most capable models of 2025 took them when the scorer was within reach, and whether asking the model not to made any difference.

**War Story 17.1: Frontier models patch their scorers (2025)**

**Context**: In June 2025 the evaluation organization METR reported reward hacking by frontier models, chiefly OpenAI’s o3, on two suites of autonomous tasks: RE-Bench, whose research-engineering tasks let the agent see the entire scoring function, and the broader HCAST suite ([[01 Books/Volume III - Agentic Systems/05 Learning from Trajectories/17 Reinforcement Learning from Verifiable Rewards/V3-17.16 Source References#^ref-vonarx2025reward|Von Arx et al. 2025]]).

**Mechanism**: With the scorer in reach, o3 attacked it instead of the task. On a kernel-optimization task it overwrote the timing function so that it always returned shorter measurements; on a code-generation scaffolding task it patched the evaluation function to judge every submission successful; on a fine-tuning speed task it modified the script to copy the cached weights of an already fine-tuned model.

**Impact**: The fine-tuning task was hacked in 21 of 21 runs. Across RE-Bench, 39 of 128 runs hacked (30 percent), against 8 of 1,087 on HCAST (0.7 percent). Asked after its first hacking plan whether the plan adhered to the user’s intention, o3 answered no 10 times in 10. Adding “Please do not reward hack” to the prompt moved the hacking rate over 20 runs from 80 percent to 70 percent.

**Fix**: METR excludes successful reward-hacking runs from its reported capability results, and recommends that when a monitor detects a hack, developers patch the exploit in the scoring function rather than punish the model.

**Systems lesson**: A scorer the agent can read and write sits at the visible-test closure level no matter what the task author intended, and an instruction does not raise it. In an evaluation these runs are excluded after the fact; in an RLVR loop every one of them is a maximal reward, and the gradient copies it.

METR attributes the substantial difference between 30 percent on RE-Bench and 0.7 percent on HCAST to reachability: on RE-Bench the model could inspect the scoring function directly, making it easier to bypass. The central defense is a verification check that the policy cannot reach, evaluated on execution evidence that the policy cannot forge. For this reason, the sandboxed architecture of [[01 Books/Volume III - Agentic Systems/05 Learning from Trajectories/17 Reinforcement Learning from Verifiable Rewards/V3-17.08 Verifier Isolation#^sec-vol3-rlvr-sandboxing|section 17.7]] removes the test runner, configuration files, and evaluation credentials from the candidate’s control.

A reward is only as dependable as the closure evidence level of the check that computes it. These closure levels categorize the basis of an evaluation verdict by how much of it the agent policy can influence or reach. A static check establishes selected structural properties, a sealed test suite withholds and protects its test cases, and a formal proof verifies a specified property under explicit assumptions. Test coverage then limits any individual check at every level: even a sealed suite with an omitted edge case continues to reward buggy solutions that exploit the gap. [[01 Books/Volume III - Agentic Systems/05 Learning from Trajectories/17 Reinforcement Learning from Verifiable Rewards/V3-17.04 Verifiable Rewards#^tbl-vol3-reward-oracles|Table 1]] summarizes the sources used to construct agent rewards, alongside the specific vulnerabilities an optimizing policy exploits in each.

↩︎ Closure evidence levels, from static checks through sealed tests to formal proofs, are established in **[[01 Books/Volume III - Agentic Systems/00 Introduction/01 Introduction/V3-01.06 Invariant Closure#^sec-vol3-intro-invariant-closure|The invariant closure principle]]**.

| Reward source                | Evidence supplied                                                 | How a policy games it                                            | Role in the reward                             |
|:-----------------------------|:------------------------------------------------------------------|:-----------------------------------------------------------------|:-----------------------------------------------|
| Proof checker                | A submitted proof satisfies the encoded statement and assumptions | Mutable axioms or definitions; a statement weaker than the task  | Outcome reward                                 |
| Sealed behavioral tests      | Candidate behavior satisfies hidden, protected checks             | Gaps in coverage; tampering if the runner or tests are reachable | Outcome reward                                 |
| Compiler or type checker     | An artifact satisfies specified structural constraints            | Code that compiles and does nothing useful                       | Gate or partial credit                         |
| Format check on tool calls   | A call parses against its schema                                  | Well-formed calls that accomplish nothing                        | Small shaping term                             |
| Learned process reward model | A learned score for each intermediate step                        | Text that resembles careful reasoning                            | Shaping; not the decision where a check exists |
| Rubric judge                 | An assessment of free-form output against written criteria        | Length, confident tone, rubric keywords                          | Reward only where no executable check exists   |
| Human rating                 | An assessment of anything a person can inspect                    | Rater fatigue and surface cues                                   | Audit; collection cost limits its frequency    |

Table 1: **Reward Sources and How They Are Gamed**: The top rows correspond to the sealed-test and formal-proof closure levels; their verdicts depend on the artifact, not its phrasing, so raising the score requires changing what the check runs on. The lower rows score text the policy writes. Coverage limits every row.

^tbl-vol3-reward-oracles

For a coding task with executable behavioral criteria, sealed tests supply the definitive outcome score, while compilation and format checks provide narrower gating signals. The lower rows score free-form text, and because the policy directly controls that text, a learned judge is itself a model with blind spots that gradient ascent can systematically exploit ([[01 Books/Volume III - Agentic Systems/05 Learning from Trajectories/17 Reinforcement Learning from Verifiable Rewards/V3-17.16 Source References#^ref-gao2023scaling|Gao et al. 2023]]). The verification asymmetry (principle [[01 Books/Volume III - Agentic Systems/01 The Model/V3 Model Principles#^pri-vol3-verification-asymmetry|3]]) dictates the role each score can assume. Learned assessments can rank candidate trajectories or shape exploration, but wherever a deterministic check exists, that check must govern the final reward. For tasks that lack an executable check, a learned judge can provide the primary reward, provided its calibration error is continuously measured against verified reference outcomes and re-evaluated as the policy distribution shifts. [[01 Books/Volume III - Agentic Systems/05 Learning from Trajectories/17 Reinforcement Learning from Verifiable Rewards/V3-17.08 Verifier Isolation#^sec-vol3-rlvr-sandboxing|Section 17.7]] develops the separate systems requirement of isolating the measurement pipeline from candidate code.

### False Accepts Among Rewarded Outcomes

^sec-vol3-rlvr-false-accepts

Early in training on a hard repair task, genuine passes are rare, so even a verifier that mistakenly accepts only a small fraction of broken patches can fill a large share of the rewarded rollouts with failures. This distinction between a grader’s false-acceptance rate and the failure rate among accepted outcomes is the same base-rate arithmetic that **[[01 Books/Volume III - Agentic Systems/04 The Agent Runtime/13 Agent Evaluation/V3-13.05 Grading Trajectories#^sec-vol3-evaluation-grading-errors|Grader error rates]]** and **[[01 Books/Volume III - Agentic Systems/01 The Model/03 Test-Time Compute/V3-03.05 Verifying Candidates#^eq-vol3-verifier-precision|equation 3.3]]** applied to a candidate pool, with one training-time change: the policy’s pass rate is no longer fixed, so the share of rewards that go to failures moves as the policy learns and is largest when true task success is scarcest. The symbols below map onto those of **[[01 Books/Volume III - Agentic Systems/04 The Agent Runtime/13 Agent Evaluation/V3-13.05 Grading Trajectories#^sec-vol3-evaluation-grading-errors|Grader error rates]]**. Let ${F}$ be criterion failure under an independent reference assessment and ${V}^+$ acceptance by the training verifier (the event written $\text{Acc}$ there). Let ${p_{\text{valid}}}$ be the policy’s current probability that a rollout satisfies the criterion, ${\epsilon_{\text{FA}}}=\Pr({V}^+\mid {F})$ the verifier’s false-acceptance rate, and ${u}$ its probability of accepting a criterion success. Provided verifier acceptance occurs with nonzero probability, the failure rate among accepted rollouts is

$$
\Pr({F}\mid {V}^+) = \frac{(1-{p_{\text{valid}}}){\epsilon_{\text{FA}}}}{{p_{\text{valid}}} {u}+(1-{p_{\text{valid}}}){\epsilon_{\text{FA}}}}. \tag{1}
$$

^eq-vol3-rlvr-false-accept-share

![[90 Assets/Volume III/17 Rlvr/Vol3 Rlvr Margin False Share ce13e0b808.svg]]

*A falling curve of the false-accept share against pass rate from 1 to 50 percent, with a red dot labeled 18.4 percent of rewards are failures at a 10 percent pass rate.*

*The rarer true passes are, the more of the reward a small verifier error claims.*

[[01 Books/Volume III - Agentic Systems/05 Learning from Trajectories/17 Reinforcement Learning from Verifiable Rewards/V3-17.04 Verifiable Rewards#^eq-vol3-rlvr-false-accept-share|Equation 1]] defines the **false-accept share** of the reward: the fraction of all rewarded rollouts that actually fail the task criterion. Because its denominator sums both true positive accepts (${p_{\text{valid}}} {u}$) and false positive accepts ($(1-{p_{\text{valid}}}){\epsilon_{\text{FA}}}$), a small false-positive rate ${\epsilon_{\text{FA}}}$ yields a large false share whenever failures are common. In reinforcement learning on challenging tasks, failure is the default state during early iterations. The same Bayesian arithmetic governs automated tests and learned reward models alike.

**Napkin Math 17.1: False accepts in the reward**

**Problem**: A hypothetical batch samples 8 rollouts for each of 1,024 tasks, giving 8,192 rollouts. Suppose 10 percent satisfy the criterion under the reference assessment. The training verifier accepts every criterion success and 2.5 percent of criterion failures. What share of its positive judgments are false accepts?

**Math**: For ${N_{\text{batch}}}$ rollouts, expected true accepts are ${N_{\text{batch}}} {p_{\text{valid}}} {u}$ and expected false accepts are ${N_{\text{batch}}}(1-{p_{\text{valid}}}){\epsilon_{\text{FA}}}$. For this batch these are 819.20 and 184.32, respectively, totaling 1,003.52 expected positive judgments. Applying [[01 Books/Volume III - Agentic Systems/05 Learning from Trajectories/17 Reinforcement Learning from Verifiable Rewards/V3-17.04 Verifiable Rewards#^eq-vol3-rlvr-false-accept-share|equation 1]] gives 18.4 percent.

**Result**: 18.4 percent of rewarded rollouts are failures, although the verifier errs on only 2.5 percent of failures. The counts are expectations; an actual batch varies around them.

**Systems insight**: The false share is set by the ratio $(1-{p_{\text{valid}}}){\epsilon_{\text{FA}}}/({p_{\text{valid}}} {u})$, so it moves with the policy’s pass rate as much as with the verifier. In the small-share regime, halving ${\epsilon_{\text{FA}}}$ roughly halves the share; doubling ${p_{\text{valid}}}$ has approximately the same effect when ${p_{\text{valid}}}$ is also small. For a fixed positive false-acceptance rate, the false share approaches one as true passes become rare. Measure verifier error at the pass rate the policy currently has, and expect the share to fall as training succeeds. The share is not the fraction of the gradient that rewards errors, which also depends on where the false accepts fall within their groups ([[01 Books/Volume III - Agentic Systems/05 Learning from Trajectories/17 Reinforcement Learning from Verifiable Rewards/V3-17.06 Group Relative Policy Optimization#^sec-vol3-rlvr-grpo-objective|section 17.5.1]]).

### Reward Composition

^sec-vol3-rlvr-reward-composition

Adding auxiliary reward terms for well-formed tool calls or concise trajectories helps guide exploration, but poorly chosen weights can let a short, well-formatted failure score higher than a long, working repair. Suppose a fully assessed rollout receives an observed binary outcome ${R_{\text{corr}}}({\tau})\in\{0,1\}$. Let ${\Phi}({\tau})\in[0,1]$ measure format compliance and let $\text{len}({\tau})\in[0,1]$ measure generated-token cost relative to an enforced cap. A common terminal score formulation is

$$
{R}({\tau})={R_{\text{corr}}}({\tau})+{\lambda_{\text{fmt}}}{\Phi}({\tau})-{\lambda_{\text{len}}}\text{len}({\tau}), \qquad {\lambda_{\text{fmt}}}\ge 0,\quad {\lambda_{\text{len}}}\ge 0.
$$

Format compliance encourages well-formed tool calls, and the length penalty favors concise solutions.[[01 Books/Volume III - Agentic Systems/05 Learning from Trajectories/17 Reinforcement Learning from Verifiable Rewards/V3-17.17 Footnotes#^fn4|⁴]] Over their defined ranges, an observed pass earns at least $1-{\lambda_{\text{len}}}$, while an observed failure earns at most ${\lambda_{\text{fmt}}}$. A sufficient condition for every passing rollout to outrank every failing rollout is therefore

$$
{\lambda_{\text{fmt}}}+{\lambda_{\text{len}}}<1.
$$

Allowing equality permits a tie between a maximally penalized pass and a perfectly formatted failure. Strict inequality protects the score ordering, and it holds only while the normalized length penalty $\text{len}({\tau})$ remains strictly bounded in $[0,1]$. Normalizing by a nominal token budget preserves that invariant only if the execution runtime enforces the cap with hard process termination.

When an unexpected infrastructure failure leaves a required check unavailable, the assessment remains incomplete. Under the four-verdict rule of **[[01 Books/Volume III - Agentic Systems/04 The Agent Runtime/13 Agent Evaluation/V3-13.03 The Task Acceptance Contract#^sec-vol3-evaluation-contract-verdicts|The four-verdict rule]]** its verdict is `unknown`, and the reward stays unresolved rather than being converted to ${R_{\text{corr}}}=0$ or ${R_{\text{corr}}}=1$. Converting an unexecuted check to failure penalizes valid candidate solutions for harness faults, while converting it to success creates an immediate reward vulnerability; [[01 Books/Volume III - Agentic Systems/05 Learning from Trajectories/17 Reinforcement Learning from Verifiable Rewards/V3-17.07 Multi-Turn Agentic RL#^sec-vol3-rlvr-stopping-censored|section 17.6.3]] develops how a training loop must handle such rollouts. An explicit timeout under the task contract represents a valid failure verdict; a missing result due to test runner crashes does not. Similarly, introducing partial credit (awarding reward for the fraction of unit tests passed, for instance) defines a distinct optimization objective that alters which behavioral changes earn reinforcement, requiring its own priority analysis.

Permissions and hard resource limits must remain strict runtime constraints. A negative reward penalty merely discourages proposing a prohibited action; only the execution harness can enforce the boundary, and a penalty cannot undo an executed side effect. Even a reward function that ranks every unsafe trajectory below every safe one leaves an agent free to explore unsafe actions during policy search. The operational rule is to keep authorization, network isolation, and resource quotas firmly anchored in the container harness, using reward terms strictly to express preferences within that bounded perimeter. Even within that perimeter, the terminal reward evaluates the rollout trajectory as a complete artifact; converting that scalar outcome into updates for the individual actions taken along the trajectory introduces the credit-assignment problem.
