# V3-17.05 Credit Across Turns

[[Home|Home]] · [[01 Books/Volume III - Agentic Systems/Volume 3 Overview|Volume Overview]] · [[01 Books/Volume III - Agentic Systems/05 Learning from Trajectories/17 Reinforcement Learning from Verifiable Rewards/V3-17 Reinforcement Learning from Verifiable Rewards|V3-17 Reinforcement Learning from Verifiable Rewards]]

^sec-vol3-rlvr-credit

In the connection-pool repair, an agent might run the failing reproducer, read `src/pool.py`, and correctly identify the missed notification on connection return, only to introduce a syntax error in its final edit. A verifier that evaluates only the end state marks the entire trajectory as failed, exposing the multi-turn credit-assignment problem introduced in [[01 Books/Volume III - Agentic Systems/05 Learning from Trajectories/17 Reinforcement Learning from Verifiable Rewards/V3-17.03 Foundations of Verifiable Rewards#^sec-vol3-rlvr-baselines-credit|section 17.2.2]]: that terminal failure characterizes the trajectory as a whole, not whether the earlier diagnosis was a mistake.

The estimators that address this problem differ in what they spend to separate the two turns: nothing beyond the sampled return, a trained value model, a denser reward, or additional rollouts. Choosing among them requires keeping two quantities apart, the empirical coefficient applied in a sampled gradient update and the true expected value of an individual action.

### The Score-Function Estimator

^sec-vol3-rlvr-score-function-estimator

When an environment emits only a terminal pass or fail at the end of an episode, the simplest gradient estimator attaches that single outcome to every turn along the trajectory. Let ${H}$ denote the finite interaction horizon, ${k} \in \{0, \ldots, {H}-1\}$ the decision index, and ${h}_k$ the delivered interaction history up to turn ${k}$. Under an undiscounted objective, let ${r}_k$ represent the reward received immediately after taking action ${a}_k$. The remaining return from step ${k}$ onward, often called the *reward-to-go*, is defined as ${R_{\geq k}}=\sum_{j=k}^{{H}-1}{r}_j$. In sparse verifiable environments where only a terminal reward ${R}$ arrives at the end of the episode, every intermediate turn sees the same reward-to-go: ${R_{\geq k}}={R}$ for all ${k}$.

For a trajectory sampled from the current policy, a likelihood-ratio, or score-function, estimator of the REINFORCE form ([[01 Books/Volume III - Agentic Systems/05 Learning from Trajectories/17 Reinforcement Learning from Verifiable Rewards/V3-17.16 Source References#^ref-williams1992simple|Williams 1992]]) is

$$
{\widehat{g}}=\sum_{k=0}^{{H}-1}\nabla_{\theta}\log{\pi_\theta}({a}_k\mid {h}_k)\,[{R_{\geq k}}-{b}({h}_k)].
$$

Its expectation equals the gradient of expected total reward under standard regularity conditions ([[01 Books/Volume III - Agentic Systems/05 Learning from Trajectories/17 Reinforcement Learning from Verifiable Rewards/V3-17.16 Source References#^ref-sutton1999policy|Sutton et al. 1999]]). Because the initial task distribution, the environment transitions, and the reward mechanism do not depend on the policy parameters ${\theta}$, their probabilities have zero derivative with respect to ${\theta}$ and drop out when differentiating the log-probability of a sampled trajectory. Provided the differentiable policy has fixed support so that differentiation under the expectation integral is valid, the gradient depends on the execution environment and the verifier only through the observed return ${R_{\geq k}}$ and never requires derivatives through the container or the test suite. Within each step of the sum, the score $\nabla_{\theta}\log{\pi_\theta}({a}_k\mid {h}_k)$ is the sensitivity vector of the sampled action’s log-probability with respect to parameters ${\theta}$. Multiplying this vector by the scalar return ${R_{\geq k}}-{b}({h}_k)$ produces a step in parameter space rather than an intrinsic score of action quality ([[01 Books/Volume III - Agentic Systems/05 Learning from Trajectories/17 Reinforcement Learning from Verifiable Rewards/V3-17.16 Source References#^ref-sutton2018reinforcement|Sutton and Barto 2018]]).

The baseline ${b}({h}_k)$ can be fixed, task-specific, or predicted by a learned value model. To maintain an unbiased gradient, ${b}({h}_k)$ must not depend on the current sampled action conditional on ${h}_k$, and its parameters remain fixed when computing the policy gradient. Subtraction preserves the expectation because the expected score under the policy evaluates to zero:

$$
\mathbb E_{{a}\sim{\pi_\theta}(\cdot\mid {h})}[{b}({h})\nabla_{\theta}\log{\pi_\theta}({a}\mid {h})] ={b}({h})\nabla_{\theta}\sum_{a}{\pi_\theta}({a}\mid {h})=0.
$$

A baseline trained on earlier, independent rollouts satisfies this conditional independence property. A baseline computed from the current rollout’s own outcome does not, even if no gradient flows through it. That outcome depends on the very actions being scored, so the baseline is no longer a constant with respect to them. Group-relative estimators, which compute baselines across multiple rollouts for the same prompt, introduce sample-dependent coefficients that require separate statistical treatment.

Consider the consequences for our connection-pool repair. When an attempt fails (${R}=0$) with a zero baseline, the sampled coefficient $[{R_{\geq k}}-{b}({h}_k)]$ becomes zero for every turn, yielding zero gradient update. With a positive baseline instead (for instance, an average pass rate of $0.4$), the resulting coefficient is negative ($-0.4$), penalizing the insightful diagnostic command and the faulty edit alike. The learning algorithm does not assign positive credit to the diagnosis from this single run. Instead, credit for effective diagnostic steps emerges only statistically across many trajectories: actions that systematically lead to successful outcomes appear more frequently in winning runs than in losing ones.

Because every additional interaction turn introduces another score-function term to the summation, the variance of ${\widehat{g}}$ can grow with the trajectory length ${H}$. Even under a decaying-covariance assumption, the variance bound in **[[05 Reference/Volume III - Agentic Systems/V3 Mathematical Foundations#^sec-vol3-appendix-math-credit-assignment|Credit assignment variance in trajectory optimization]]** grows linearly with ${H}$, so longer decision horizons can demand substantially more rollouts to extract a clean training signal. Long horizons therefore cost the learner twice. Under the horizon-compounding model (**[[01 Books/Volume III - Agentic Systems/00 Introduction/01 Introduction/V3-01.07 Reliability and Cost Over a Horizon#^eq-vol3-horizon-compounding|equation 1.1]]**), successful trajectories become exponentially rarer, so fewer rollouts carry a positive signal, and each one that does arrives with a gradient estimate whose variance can grow with ${H}$.

### Value, Advantage, and Process Scores

^sec-vol3-rlvr-value-advantage-process

To distinguish an insightful diagnostic command from a later syntax error on a single rollout, the estimator needs a baseline that updates after each turn rather than once per prompt. For the same continuation policy and reward definition, define

$$
{V^{\pi}}({h}_k)=\mathbb E_\pi[{R_{\geq k}}\mid {h}_k],\qquad {Q^{\pi}}({h}_k,{a}_k)=\mathbb E_\pi[{R_{\geq k}}\mid {h}_k,{a}_k],\qquad {A^{\pi}}({h}_k,{a}_k)={Q^{\pi}}({h}_k,{a}_k)-{V^{\pi}}({h}_k).
$$

The state value ${V^{\pi}}({h}_k)$ averages over the policy’s next action and subsequent interaction, whereas the action value ${Q^{\pi}}({h}_k,{a}_k)$ conditions explicitly on that chosen action. Their difference, the **advantage**, measures expected return relative to the policy’s default behavior at that history. These quantities are defined over interaction histories ${h}_k$ rather than abstract environment states, so they depend only on the observations and context tokens available to the model.

With terminal value ${V^{\pi}}({h}_{{H}})=0$, the sampled **temporal-difference residual** ([[01 Books/Volume III - Agentic Systems/05 Learning from Trajectories/17 Reinforcement Learning from Verifiable Rewards/V3-17.16 Source References#^ref-sutton1988learning|Sutton 1988]]) is

$$
{\delta}_k={r}_k+{V^{\pi}}({h}_{k+1})-{V^{\pi}}({h}_k).
$$

Its conditional expectation given ${h}_k$ and ${a}_k$ equals the true advantage when the value function is exact and subsequent observations and rewards follow the target environment distribution. A single observed residual is a noisy sample of that expectation, and substituting learned value estimates introduces approximation error on top of execution noise.[[01 Books/Volume III - Agentic Systems/05 Learning from Trajectories/17 Reinforcement Learning from Verifiable Rewards/V3-17.17 Footnotes#^fn5|⁵]] Dropping ${r}_k$ is valid only when that immediate step reward is zero. **[[05 Reference/Volume III - Agentic Systems/V3 Mathematical Foundations#^sec-vol3-appendix-math-rl|Generalized advantage estimation and KL divergence]]** develops the discounted and multi-step formulations ([[01 Books/Volume III - Agentic Systems/05 Learning from Trajectories/17 Reinforcement Learning from Verifiable Rewards/V3-17.16 Source References#^ref-schulman2016high|Schulman et al. 2016]]).

Estimating an existing return differs from injecting new reward terms into the training objective. A learned critic acts as an estimator: it approximates the expected continuation return under the current policy without altering the underlying task specification. A process reward model (PRM), by contrast, scores intermediate reasoning steps or tool invocations directly, augmenting the primary objective with synthetic feedback.

↩︎ Process reward models as step-level scorers that prune search at inference time are established in **[[01 Books/Volume III - Agentic Systems/01 The Model/03 Test-Time Compute/V3-03.05 Verifying Candidates#^sec-vol3-test-time-compute-outcome-step-evidence|Checking whole outputs vs. intermediate steps]]**.

While dense process rewards can guide search during inference ([[01 Books/Volume III - Agentic Systems/05 Learning from Trajectories/17 Reinforcement Learning from Verifiable Rewards/V3-17.16 Source References#^ref-lightman2023let|Lightman et al. 2024]]; [[01 Books/Volume III - Agentic Systems/05 Learning from Trajectories/17 Reinforcement Learning from Verifiable Rewards/V3-17.16 Source References#^ref-uesato2022solving|Uesato et al. 2022]]), training against them directly creates strong incentives for reward hacking. Because the policy generates the very tokens that the process verifier evaluates, systems engineers encounter three common failure modes:

1.  *Superficial compliance*: The agent generates formulaic boilerplate or ritualistic assertions (such as commenting “carefully verifying pointer bounds before dereferencing”) that satisfy the process model without performing any actual verification.
2.  *Locally consistent hallucinations*: The agent constructs fluent, step-by-step reasoning that appears rigorous to a local evaluator, even though the derivation rests on an unvalidated or false premise.
3.  *Pathological conservatism*: The agent avoids exploratory detours, speculative rollouts, or diagnostic failures that temporarily depress step scores, even when those intermediate investigations are necessary to diagnose complex software bugs.

Dense feedback reduces credit variance, but denser feedback is not necessarily correct feedback. Modifying the reward function changes what the policy optimizes, whereas value estimation estimates the performance of the policy under the true task metric. [[01 Books/Volume III - Agentic Systems/05 Learning from Trajectories/17 Reinforcement Learning from Verifiable Rewards/V3-17.05 Credit Across Turns#^tbl-vol3-credit-comparison|Table 2]] separates these design choices and their architectural commitments.

| Choice                 | Quantity supplied                             | Main obligation                                                             |
|:-----------------------|:----------------------------------------------|:----------------------------------------------------------------------------|
| Terminal check         | Observed trajectory outcome                   | Validate criterion, coverage, and protected assessment                      |
| Learned value estimate | Predicted return under a specified policy     | Assess prediction error and estimator bias                                  |
| Process reward         | Additional intermediate scoring criterion     | Justify the changed objective and assess its errors                         |
| Continuation sampling  | Empirical return at a declared fork condition | Restore compatible conditions and account for sampling uncertainty and cost |

Table 2: **Reward Definition and Return Estimation**: These choices can be combined. Predicting or sampling a declared return does not independently validate the check that defines it.

### Monte Carlo Value Estimates

^sec-vol3-rlvr-monte-carlo-value

Training a neural value critic on sparse multi-turn trajectories often fails when small prediction errors across tens of turns overwhelm the temporal-difference signal, while process reward models invite the policy to game intermediate step scores. **Continuation sampling** avoids both learned models by estimating state value directly from the environment. It rolls out ${K}$ independent trajectories (in this section ${K}$ counts continuations, not output tokens) from an intermediate execution boundary under a fixed continuation policy and averages their terminal returns. To accurately estimate ${V^{\pi}}({h})$, the starting state of these continuations must match the true conditional environment distribution given history ${h}$. In interactive environments with latent state (such as unobserved database locks, network traffic, or non-deterministic operating system processes), restoring a single concrete container snapshot conditions on that specific state rather than integrating over all hidden states consistent with ${h}$. A sound fork must therefore explicitly reconstruct the filesystem workspace, container network namespace, delivered context tokens, external mock services, continuation policy version, and verification oracle. Copying sandbox memory bytes or disk blocks alone does not guarantee environmental fidelity.

When individual continuations yield independent bounded returns ${R}^{({i})} \in [0, 1]$, the sample mean ${\hat{V}} = {K}^{-1}\sum_{i=1}^{K} {R}^{({i})}$ serves as an unbiased estimator of the expected continuation return. Because Bernoulli random variables achieve maximum variance at ${p}=0.5$ (${\sigma}^2 \le 1/4$), the estimator’s variance is bounded above by $1/(4{K})$, corresponding to a standard error of at most $1/(2\sqrt{{K}})$. Halving this uncertainty bound requires a fourfold increase in sampled rollouts. This error bound holds strictly for independently sampled branches; if rollouts share cached environment state or common prefixes, cross-sample correlation increases the variance. Furthermore, estimating action values ${Q^{\pi}}({h}, {a})$ requires conditioning explicitly on the chosen action ${a}$ before rolling out the continuation policy. Consequently, sampling values at selected turn boundaries estimates the net value delta across blocks of turns rather than isolating individual token decisions.

A hypothetical fork makes this estimate concrete. Out of 20 continuations launched from the initial bug report, 1 passes, giving a sampled value of 0.05. In the primary trajectory, the policy then runs a debugger, producing an observation that contains the failing line. Forking 20 continuations from this updated history yields 12 passes, corresponding to a sampled value of 0.60. The estimated value difference for the debugger turn is 0.55, even if the primary trajectory later fails on a subsequent edit. Each sample mean carries a standard error of at most 0.11. Because the two fork estimates are independent, their variances add, so the difference has a standard error of at most $\sqrt2/(2\sqrt {K})$, or 0.16. Treating a difference as resolved only when it is at least twice that bound, here 0.32, the observed 0.55 clears the threshold, so the gain is unlikely to be sampling noise. Resolution against noise does not make ${\Delta} {\hat{V}}$ an exact advantage, however; it remains an empirical estimate at two particular fork conditions. Of the three ways to connect the terminal failure to earlier turns ([[01 Books/Volume III - Agentic Systems/05 Learning from Trajectories/17 Reinforcement Learning from Verifiable Rewards/V3-17.05 Credit Across Turns#^fig-vol3-credit-assignment|figure 1]]), only the forks separate the debugger turn from the faulty edit on this trajectory. They do it by paying for extra rollouts rather than by adding a criterion the policy could learn to satisfy.

![[90 Assets/Volume III/17 Rlvr/Credit Assignment Topologies 9529dbb7aa.svg]]

Figure 1: **Three Ways to Connect a Terminal Outcome to Earlier Turns**: The same trajectory runs a debugger, makes a faulty edit, and fails the terminal check. (a) With only the terminal check, every remaining return equals the one outcome, so the check alone does not separate the useful turn from the faulty one. (b) Learned step scores supply intermediate feedback by adding a criterion to the objective, one the policy can learn to satisfy without progress on the task. (c) Continuation sampling restores each fork condition and averages the returns of its continuations; the difference between the two means estimates how much the debugger turn raised the chance of passing the declared check, with sampling error and without establishing cause.

^fig-vol3-credit-assignment

These continuation rollouts are expensive. Every branch consumes inference tokens, host memory, and sandbox execution time, and the total depends directly on how many intermediate boundaries the fork schedule samples, which makes the schedule the real design choice.

**Napkin Math 17.2: Continuation sampling in tokens**

**Problem**: An agent trajectory runs 30 turns of 512 generated tokens each. At each chosen intermediate boundary, the system samples 8 continuations, each running for the remainder of the episode. What generation cost results from sampling every intermediate boundary, compared with sampling only after turns 10 and 20?

**Variables**: ${H}$ is the trajectory horizon in turns, ${n_{\text{gen}}}$ the generated tokens per turn, ${K}$ the continuations per boundary, and ${\mathcal{B}_{\text{fork}}}\subseteq\{1,\ldots,{H}-1\}$ the index set of completed-turn fork points. Branches are nonrecursive.

**Math**:

$$
{N_{\text{base}}}={H}\,{n_{\text{gen}}},\qquad {N_{\text{total}}}({\mathcal{B}_{\text{fork}}})={H}\,{n_{\text{gen}}}+{K}\,{n_{\text{gen}}}\sum_{k\in{\mathcal{B}_{\text{fork}}}}({H}-{k}).
$$

When forking at every intermediate boundary, the remaining-turn sum is ${H}({H}-1)/2$, yielding ${N_{\text{total}}}/{N_{\text{base}}}=1+{K}({H}-1)/2$.

**Result**: The base trajectory generates 15,360 tokens. The multiple-of-base column in [[01 Books/Volume III - Agentic Systems/05 Learning from Trajectories/17 Reinforcement Learning from Verifiable Rewards/V3-17.05 Credit Across Turns#^tbl-vol3-mc-fork-inventory|table 3]] prices the gap: exhaustive intermediate sampling creates severe computational blowup compared with selective boundary evaluation.

| Fork schedule               | Continuation branches | Total generated tokens, including base | Multiple of base |
|:----------------------------|----------------------:|---------------------------------------:|-----------------:|
| Every intermediate boundary |                   232 |                              1,797,120 |              117 |
| After turns 10 and 20       |                    16 |                                138,240 |                9 |

Table 3: **Generated-Token Inventory for Two Fork Schedules**: The selected schedule estimates fewer intermediate conditions; the rows are not equal-accuracy alternatives.

^tbl-vol3-mc-fork-inventory

**Systems insight**: Exhaustive forking grows quadratically as ${K} {H}^2\,{n_{\text{gen}}}/2$, meaning that doubling the horizon quadruples the credit computation bill even though base trajectory generation only doubles. At this horizon of 30 turns, exhaustive forking multiplies token generation by 117. In contrast, sampling only two targeted boundaries costs 9 times the base trajectory. The engineering decision rule is to fork only at a handful of critical boundaries, spending the saved generation budget on exploring a broader diversity of tasks. This inventory tallies generated output tokens alone; context prompt processing, sandbox snapshot restoration, tool execution, and verification add overhead to both configurations, while variable-length continuations shift the exact totals.

Selective forking requires an effective policy for choosing boundaries. Two triggers suit this choice. A turn boundary qualifies when the policy’s action distribution has high token entropy (a next-token distribution spread across many candidates, measured in [[01 Books/Volume III - Agentic Systems/05 Learning from Trajectories/17 Reinforcement Learning from Verifiable Rewards/V3-17.09 Training Pathologies#^sec-vol3-rlvr-diversity-entropy|section 17.8.1]]), which indicates a decision among genuine alternatives. A turn also qualifies when it executes state-modifying tool calls such as file edits, database migrations, or network reconfiguration. Forking at two or three such junctures places the empirical credit estimates where trajectories actually diverge, at a small fraction of the exhaustive cost.

Credit assignment is a budget decision that trades computational cost against statistical fidelity. Fixed baselines incur zero token overhead but offer no step-level resolution. Learned value functions reduce variance at the expense of training a secondary critic. Continuation rollouts provide direct empirical value estimates at the price of substantial token generation. Group-relative policy optimization represents the minimalist end of this spectrum, comparing complete sampled returns within a task group without spending any additional tokens on intermediate branches.

**Checkpoint 17.1: Rewards and credit**

A reward is a measurement, and credit is an estimate built on it. Each has a price in errors or tokens.

**False-accept estimate**: The verifier from the false-accepts notebook keeps its error rate while the policy’s pass rate drops from 10 percent to 5 percent. Does the false share of rewarded rollouts rise or fall, and to roughly what value?

**Enforcement**: Why must runtime authorization remain in force when unauthorized proposals receive a reward penalty?

**Credit**: With terminal reward zero, when is the score estimator’s coefficient zero and when is it negative? Why does neither answer identify the faulty turn?

**Value sampling**: What conditions must continuation forks preserve, and why is a difference between two estimated values not generally an exact advantage?

**Credit budget**: A trajectory doubles from 30 to 60 turns. By what factor does exhaustive continuation sampling grow, and should the schedule fork at every boundary or at two chosen ones?
