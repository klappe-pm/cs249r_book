# V3-17.06 Group Relative Policy Optimization

[[Home|Home]] · [[01 Books/Volume III - Agentic Systems/Volume 3 Overview|Volume Overview]] · [[01 Books/Volume III - Agentic Systems/05 Learning from Trajectories/17 Reinforcement Learning from Verifiable Rewards/V3-17 Reinforcement Learning from Verifiable Rewards|V3-17 Reinforcement Learning from Verifiable Rewards]]

^sec-vol3-rlvr-grpo

Suppose three fresh rollouts attempt the connection-pool repair under the same fixture, budget, and assessment rule. Their completed terminal rewards are $[0,1,0]$. The successful rollout provides a direct comparison for the two failures without requiring an expert to label each intermediate edit, though this outcome comparison alone cannot identify which specific action caused success. **Group relative policy optimization (GRPO)** uses such within-task comparisons[[01 Books/Volume III - Agentic Systems/05 Learning from Trajectories/17 Reinforcement Learning from Verifiable Rewards/V3-17.17 Footnotes#^fn6|⁶]] to construct policy update coefficients, replacing a learned value baseline with groups of sampled outcomes ([[01 Books/Volume III - Agentic Systems/05 Learning from Trajectories/17 Reinforcement Learning from Verifiable Rewards/V3-17.16 Source References#^ref-shao2024deepseekmath|Shao et al. 2024]]).

The trade-off centers on baseline estimation rather than the reward source: **proximal policy optimization (PPO)** can operate with an external check, just as GRPO can evaluate rollouts against a learned reward model. PPO trains a value function, either sharing parameters with the policy or running in a separate critic network ([[01 Books/Volume III - Agentic Systems/05 Learning from Trajectories/17 Reinforcement Learning from Verifiable Rewards/V3-17.16 Source References#^ref-schulman2017proximal|Schulman et al. 2017]]). GRPO eliminates that critic alongside its memory and training overhead, paying for the omission with additional rollouts: it samples a group of ${G}$ attempts per task to estimate empirical group statistics. GRPO serves as our worked optimizer because its systems accounting is straightforward to track, though the execution isolation and trajectory recording requirements that follow apply equally to both approaches. Both optimizers also hold a reference policy, a frozen copy of the starting checkpoint used to penalize drift away from it ([[01 Books/Volume III - Agentic Systems/05 Learning from Trajectories/17 Reinforcement Learning from Verifiable Rewards/V3-17.06 Group Relative Policy Optimization#^sec-vol3-rlvr-grpo-clipped-objective|section 17.5.2]]). [[01 Books/Volume III - Agentic Systems/05 Learning from Trajectories/17 Reinforcement Learning from Verifiable Rewards/V3-17.06 Group Relative Policy Optimization#^tbl-vol3-ppo-vs-grpo-comparison|Table 4]] sets the two side by side.

| Dimension              | PPO                                                            | GRPO                                                            |
|:-----------------------|:---------------------------------------------------------------|:----------------------------------------------------------------|
| Models held            | Policy, value function, reference, and possibly a reward model | Policy and reference; the reward can be an external check       |
| Trained components     | Policy plus a value function, shared or separate               | Policy only                                                     |
| Baseline               | Learned value estimate ${V_{\phi}}({h}_k)$                     | Mean reward of the group for the same task                      |
| Credit granularity     | Per token or turn, through the value estimate                  | Per trajectory, shared by all its sampled tokens                |
| Rollouts per task      | Can be one                                                     | A group of ${G}$                                                |
| Characteristic failure | Value error on long, sparse-reward trajectories                | No reward-surrogate gradient when every rollout in a group ties |

Table 4: **PPO and GRPO Compared**: The two optimizers differ in the baseline they use, the granularity of their credit, and how they fail. Neither column implies a universal reduction in total memory or compute.

^tbl-vol3-ppo-vs-grpo-comparison

### Group-Relative Coefficients

^sec-vol3-rlvr-grpo-objective

Without a trained critic network, a single rollout’s terminal score gives the optimizer no reference level to decide whether the outcome beat expectations for that task. To construct a baseline without a critic, the system collects ${G}$ completed rollouts for the same task from a fixed **behavior policy** $\pi_{\text{old}}$, the specific model checkpoint, execution environment, and decoding configuration that generated the trajectories. Let ${R}_i$ denote the terminal reward assigned to rollout ${i}$. For outcome-only verification, the group statistics and advantage coefficients are:

$$
{\mu_{R}}=\frac{1}{{G}}\sum_{j=1}^{G} {R}_j,\qquad {\sigma_{R}}=\sqrt{\frac{1}{{G}}\sum_{j=1}^{G}({R}_j-{\mu_{R}})^2},\qquad {\hat{A}}_i=\frac{{R}_i-{\mu_{R}}}{{\sigma_{R}}+{\epsilon_{\text{stab}}}}. \tag{2}
$$

^eq-vol3-grpo-advantage

The positive stabilizer ${\epsilon_{\text{stab}}}$ keeps the expression well-defined when all rollouts receive identical rewards. The denominator uses the empirical population standard deviation with divisor ${G}$, rather than a Bessel-corrected sample standard deviation with divisor ${G}-1$. This convention is part of the objective: altering the divisor rescales every coefficient, and neither divisor accounts for unobserved task behaviors outside the sampled group. During policy updates, the rewards ${R}_i$ and coefficients ${\hat{A}}_i$ remain fixed constants; because this scalar is easily confused with the state-action advantage function of [[01 Books/Volume III - Agentic Systems/05 Learning from Trajectories/17 Reinforcement Learning from Verifiable Rewards/V3-17.05 Credit Across Turns#^sec-vol3-rlvr-credit|section 17.4]], the next definition keeps the two apart.

**Definition 17.3: Group-relative advantage**

***Group-relative advantage*** is the coefficient ${\hat{A}}_i$ that GRPO attaches to every sampled token of rollout ${i}$: the rollout’s reward minus the mean reward of its task group, divided by the group’s reward dispersion.

1.  **Significance**: It replaces a learned value function with ${G}$ rollouts per task. The group size therefore governs both the accuracy of the empirical baseline and the rollout compute budget; on a binary check, a lone pass in a group of ${G}$ receives a coefficient approaching $\sqrt{{G}-1}$.
2.  **Distinction**: The advantage ${A^{\pi}}={Q^{\pi}}-{V^{\pi}}$ is an expectation for one action at one history, whereas the group-relative coefficient is an empirical, standardized score broadcast uniformly across all tokens in an entire trajectory. Furthermore, because the group statistics include the rollout being scored, the action-independent baseline argument does not make the standardized estimator unbiased REINFORCE. Centering alone is harmless, since ${R}_i-{\mu_{R}}$ equals $({G}-1)/{G}$ times ${R}_i$ minus the mean of the other ${G}-1$ rewards, a valid leave-one-out baseline rescaled. The departure comes from dividing by ${\sigma_{R}}$, which every reward in the group helps set. It is a weighting choice recorded with the run rather than an error to be corrected.
3.  **Common pitfall**: Reading a large positive coefficient as evidence that the rollout was sound. The coefficient measures only how the rollout’s reward compared with its group; a false pass receives the exact same coefficient as a genuine success.

**Napkin Math 17.3: From three rewards to update coefficients**

**Problem**: Three hypothetical repair rollouts receive terminal rewards $[0,1,0]$, with no intermediate rewards and ${\epsilon_{\text{stab}}}=10^{-8}$. What coefficients enter the outcome-only GRPO update?

**Math**: The mean is ${\mu_{R}}=1/3$, or 0.333333. The squared deviations are $1/9$, $4/9$, and $1/9$, so ${\sigma_{R}}=\sqrt{2/9}$, or 0.471405. Dividing each centered reward by ${\sigma_{R}}$ gives $(-1/3)/\sqrt{2/9}=-1/\sqrt 2$ for each failure and $(2/3)/\sqrt{2/9}=\sqrt 2$ for the pass.

**Result**: Each failed rollout has coefficient -0.707107; the successful rollout has coefficient 1.414214. The three coefficients sum to zero.

**Systems insight**: The update sign emerges from relative group ranking rather than absolute reward values, and its magnitude scales up as successful trajectories become rarer. However, because every generated token within a rollout receives the identical scalar coefficient, crediting insightful diagnostic steps and mistaken proposals alike, GRPO trades fine-grained temporal credit assignment for substantial algorithmic and computational simplicity.

![[90 Assets/Volume III/17 Rlvr/Vol3 Rlvr Margin Lone Pass e2127c05e6.svg]]

*A rising concave curve of the lone-pass coefficient against group size from 2 to 16, with dots labeled G equals 3 at 1.41 and G equals 16 at 3.87.*

*The rarer the pass in its group, the larger the coefficient it receives.*

The group mean acts as an adaptive per-task baseline that absorbs intrinsic task difficulty. When a binary-evaluated group of size ${G}>1$ produces exactly one pass, the group mean is $1/{G}$ and the empirical standard deviation is $\sqrt{{G}-1}/{G}$. Under [[01 Books/Volume III - Agentic Systems/05 Learning from Trajectories/17 Reinforcement Learning from Verifiable Rewards/V3-17.06 Group Relative Policy Optimization#^eq-vol3-grpo-advantage|equation 2]], that lone successful rollout receives an advantage coefficient of

$$
{\hat{A}_{\text{pass}}}=\frac{1-1/{G}}{\sqrt{{G}-1}/{G}+{\epsilon_{\text{stab}}}} =\frac{{G}-1}{\sqrt{{G}-1}+{G}{\epsilon_{\text{stab}}}}, \tag{3}
$$

^eq-vol3-grpo-lone-pass

which evaluates to $\sqrt{{G}-1}$ when the stabilizer is zero and remains slightly below that bound for positive ${\epsilon_{\text{stab}}}$. On a difficult task where only one rollout in 16 succeeds, the group baseline drops to 0.0625. The solitary success receives an amplified coefficient of 3.87, while the fifteen failures receive small negative coefficients of 0.26 in magnitude. Conversely, on an easy task where only one rollout in 16 fails, the signs invert, heavily penalizing the isolated failure. In both scenarios, the update amplifies the contrast that separated success from failure on that specific problem instance. The group mean thereby fills the baseline role that a learned value function performs in PPO.

The same arithmetic explains why the false-accept share of [[01 Books/Volume III - Agentic Systems/05 Learning from Trajectories/17 Reinforcement Learning from Verifiable Rewards/V3-17.04 Verifiable Rewards#^eq-vol3-rlvr-false-accept-share|equation 1]] is not the false share of the gradient. False accepts occur on failing rollouts, which are most common on hard tasks, and on a hard task a false pass is often the group’s only pass, so it receives a coefficient near $\sqrt{{G}-1}$. A false pass on an easy task, one of many passes in its group, receives only a small positive coefficient, the mirror image of the small failure coefficients above. The same count of false accepts can therefore carry a larger or a smaller share of the update depending on which groups they land in. The hardest tasks, where the share of [[01 Books/Volume III - Agentic Systems/05 Learning from Trajectories/17 Reinforcement Learning from Verifiable Rewards/V3-17.04 Verifiable Rewards#^eq-vol3-rlvr-false-accept-share|equation 1]] is also highest, are where each one weighs most.

### Clipped Surrogate Objective

^sec-vol3-rlvr-grpo-clipped-objective

Taking multiple gradient steps on a collected batch of rollouts causes the updated policy ${\pi_\theta}$ to drift away from the behavior policy $\pi_{\text{old}}$ that sampled the actions, and an unclipped importance ratio can trigger destructive weight updates. Let ${w}_{i,t}$ denote the ${t}$-th sampled policy token in rollout ${i}$, ${x}_{i,t}$ its delivered context prefix, and ${N}_i>0$ the total number of sampled generation tokens. While tool outputs, environment responses, and prompt text appear within prefixes ${x}_{i,t}$, they are masked out of the policy loss and do not count toward ${N}_i$. At each generated token position, the **probability ratio** is:

$$
{\rho}_{i,t}({\theta})= \frac{{\pi_\theta}({w}_{i,t}\mid {x}_{i,t})} {\pi_{\text{old}}({w}_{i,t}\mid {x}_{i,t})}.
$$

Both probabilities must reflect the exact sampling convention used during generation. If generation applied temperature scaling or top-${k}$ / top-${p}$ truncation, the recorded behavior probability $\pi_{\text{old}}$ in the denominator must match the actual sampling distribution and remain strictly positive. Probability support eliminated by decoding filters cannot be restored by downstream loss ratios.

With clipping parameter $0<{\epsilon_{\text{clip}}}<1$ (kept distinct from the denominator stabilizer ${\epsilon_{\text{stab}}}$), the **clipped surrogate** objective is:

$$
{\mathcal{M}}_{i,t}({\theta})=\min\left\{ {\rho}_{i,t}({\theta}){\hat{A}}_i, \operatorname{clip}({\rho}_{i,t}({\theta}),1-{\epsilon_{\text{clip}}},1+{\epsilon_{\text{clip}}}){\hat{A}}_i \right\}.
$$

The outer minimum interacts with the sign of ${\hat{A}}_i$ so that clipping takes effect only when the update has already moved a token’s probability far enough in the favored direction. When ${\hat{A}}_i>0$, raising the token’s probability increases ${\rho}_{i,t}({\theta})$, and once the ratio exceeds $1+{\epsilon_{\text{clip}}}$ the clipped branch becomes the smaller of the two terms inside the minimum, flattening the surrogate and removing any gradient incentive to push the current probability higher. When ${\hat{A}}_i<0$, multiplying by the negative coefficient reverses the ordering of the two branches inside the minimum. Once the probability ratio drops below $1-{\epsilon_{\text{clip}}}$, the unclipped product is less negative than the clipped term, so the minimum again selects the flat clipped branch and avoids destructive runaway penalties. In contrast, when parameter changes move a token’s probability in the unfavorable direction, such as reducing the probability ratio below $1-{\epsilon_{\text{clip}}}$ when ${\hat{A}}_i>0$ or raising it above $1+{\epsilon_{\text{clip}}}$ when ${\hat{A}}_i<0$, the unclipped product is the smaller term in the minimum and continues to penalize the surrogate. Even where the flat branch is active, clipping caps only the direct gradient incentive on individual tokens rather than creating a true trust region that bounds overall policy shift. Parameter sharing across tokens and additional loss terms can still propel ${\rho}_{i,t}({\theta})$ well outside the clipping interval.[[01 Books/Volume III - Agentic Systems/05 Learning from Trajectories/17 Reinforcement Learning from Verifiable Rewards/V3-17.17 Footnotes#^fn7|⁷]]

A frozen **reference policy** $\pi_{\text{ref}}$, typically initialized from the pre-RL checkpoint, provides an auxiliary regularizer that penalizes excessive policy drift. In distributed pipelines, $\pi_{\text{ref}}$ maintains a distinct model identity from the behavior policy $\pi_{\text{old}}$ that generated the rollouts. Assuming strictly positive probabilities over shared token support, we define:

$$
{z}_{i,t}({\theta})=\frac{\pi_{\text{ref}}({w}_{i,t}\mid {x}_{i,t})} {{\pi_\theta}({w}_{i,t}\mid {x}_{i,t})},\qquad {\hat{\mathcal{D}}}_{i,t}({\theta})={z}_{i,t}({\theta})-\log {z}_{i,t}({\theta})-1.
$$

The penalty term ${\hat{\mathcal{D}}}_{i,t}({\theta})$ is strictly non-negative and vanishes if and only if the current and reference token probabilities coincide. Conditioned on prefix ${x}_{i,t}$, the expectation of ${\hat{\mathcal{D}}}_{i,t}({\theta})$ over tokens sampled from the *current* policy ${\pi_\theta}$ equals the Kullback–Leibler divergence ${\mathcal{D}_{\text{KL}}}({\pi_\theta}\parallel\pi_{\text{ref}})$, the expected log ratio of the two distributions under ${\pi_\theta}$, which is zero only when they agree. The identity is derived in **[[05 Reference/Volume III - Agentic Systems/V3 Mathematical Foundations#^sec-vol3-appendix-math-rl|Generalized advantage estimation and KL divergence]]**. The plain log ratio $-\log {z}_{i,t}$ has the same expectation, but a single sampled token can make it negative, so a per-token penalty built on it would sometimes reward drift. Each term ${z}-\log {z}-1$ is non-negative, so every token’s penalty pushes toward the reference. Because the tokens in the training batch were originally drawn from the behavior policy $\pi_{\text{old}}$, evaluating this sample average provides an approximation to the current-policy divergence that degrades as off-policy lag accumulates over multiple gradient steps.

Combining these components yields the empirical GRPO maximization objective:

$$
{\widehat{\mathcal{J}}_{\text{GRPO}}}({\theta})= \frac{1}{{G}}\sum_{i=1}^{G}\frac{1}{{N}_i}\sum_{t=1}^{{N}_i} \left[{\mathcal{M}}_{i,t}({\theta})-{\beta_{\text{KL}}} {\hat{\mathcal{D}}}_{i,t}({\theta})\right], \qquad{\beta_{\text{KL}}}\ge0. \tag{4}
$$

^eq-vol3-grpo-objective

Standard deep learning frameworks minimize the negative of ${\widehat{\mathcal{J}}_{\text{GRPO}}}({\theta})$ as a loss. The hyperparameter ${\beta_{\text{KL}}}$ scales the reference policy penalty. Because reward standardization, probability ratio clipping, and token length normalization collectively depart from the true expected return ${J}({\theta})$, production systems record these optimization hyperparameter choices alongside the trained model weights and validate performance through fresh online evaluation trials.

### Length Normalization

^sec-vol3-rlvr-grpo-length-normalization

Averaging token losses within each rollout by $1/{N}_i$ in [[01 Books/Volume III - Agentic Systems/05 Learning from Trajectories/17 Reinforcement Learning from Verifiable Rewards/V3-17.06 Group Relative Policy Optimization#^eq-vol3-grpo-objective|equation 4]] penalizes each token of a long failure far less than each token of a short failure, teaching the policy to stretch out failing attempts. At the start of an update, when the current policy matches the behavior policy and the probability ratios equal one, rollout ${i}$ contributes the following gradient to the reward surrogate:

$$
\nabla_{\theta}{\mathcal{L}}_i=\frac{{\hat{A}}_i}{{N}_i}\sum_{t=1}^{{N}_i}\nabla_{\theta}\log{\pi_\theta}({w}_{i,t}\mid {x}_{i,t}), \tag{5}
$$

^eq-vol3-grpo-length-coefficient

initially assigning every sampled token in trajectory ${i}$ an effective scalar weight of ${\hat{A}}_i/{N}_i$. This per-sequence divisor exerts asymmetric optimization pressure across different outcome classes ([[01 Books/Volume III - Agentic Systems/05 Learning from Trajectories/17 Reinforcement Learning from Verifiable Rewards/V3-17.16 Source References#^ref-liu2025understanding|Liu et al. 2025]]). For failing rollouts (${\hat{A}}_i<0$), extending generation length distributes the negative penalty across more tokens, diluting the per-token gradient penalty. For successful rollouts (${\hat{A}}_i>0$), producing fewer tokens concentrates the positive advantage on a compact sequence, amplifying the per-token reinforcement. The resulting optimization pressure, known as **length bias**, systematically favors concise successes and verbose failures.

Consider two failing rollouts within the same group that receive identical negative coefficients ${\hat{A}}_i$. If one fails after 128 tokens while the second exhausts the context budget at 4,096 tokens, the concise failure absorbs a per-token penalty 32× larger than that of the verbose failure. To eliminate this distortion, alternative implementations replace individual sequence lengths ${N}_i$ with a constant divisor shared across the group or microbatch, such as the mean trajectory length ${\bar{N}}$:

$$
{\widehat{\mathcal{J}}_{\text{const}}}({\theta})=\frac{1}{{G}\,{\bar{N}}}\sum_{i=1}^{G}\sum_{t=1}^{{N}_i}\left[{\mathcal{M}}_{i,t}({\theta})-{\beta_{\text{KL}}} {\hat{\mathcal{D}}}_{i,t}({\theta})\right].
$$

Under this constant-denominator objective, every token receives an update weight proportional to ${\hat{A}}_i$ alone, regardless of trajectory length. While these scalar weight comparisons illustrate the gradient mechanics, the actual trajectory length drift depends on how individual token gradient vectors align or cancel across sequences during training, an empirical dynamic examined in [[01 Books/Volume III - Agentic Systems/05 Learning from Trajectories/17 Reinforcement Learning from Verifiable Rewards/V3-17.09 Training Pathologies#^sec-vol3-rlvr-pathologies|section 17.8]].

Reward standardization introduces an analogous weighting trade-off across tasks. Dividing by ${\sigma_{R}}+{\epsilon_{\text{stab}}}$ normalizes the scale of centered rewards across groups. As ${\epsilon_{\text{stab}}}\to0$, every group without a tie has the same total squared coefficient, $\sum_i{\hat{A}}_i^2={G}$, whether it holds a single isolated success or an even split between passes and failures. The equality is between whole groups, not individual rollouts. Within the fixed total, a lone pass takes ${G}-1$ of the ${G}$ units and its failures share the remaining one, which is the amplification of [[01 Books/Volume III - Agentic Systems/05 Learning from Trajectories/17 Reinforcement Learning from Verifiable Rewards/V3-17.06 Group Relative Policy Optimization#^eq-vol3-grpo-lone-pass|equation 3]], whereas an even split spreads the total evenly. Omitting this dispersion divisor weights tasks strictly by their raw reward spread. Both normalizations are objective design choices that must be logged with the training run alongside the clipping threshold.

### Zero-Contrast Groups

^sec-vol3-rlvr-grpo-zero-variance

That constant group weight holds only when a group contains both passes and failures. When all ${G}$ repair attempts on a hard bug fail the acceptance check, or all ${G}$ attempts on a routine bug pass, every rollout in the completed group yields the identical reward, forming a **zero-contrast group**. In this scenario, every numerator in [[01 Books/Volume III - Agentic Systems/05 Learning from Trajectories/17 Reinforcement Learning from Verifiable Rewards/V3-17.06 Group Relative Policy Optimization#^eq-vol3-grpo-advantage|equation 2]] evaluates to zero, yielding advantage coefficients ${\hat{A}}_i=0$, surrogate terms ${\mathcal{M}}_{i,t}=0$, and policy gradient contributions $\nabla_{\theta}{\mathcal{M}}_{i,t}=0$. The reward-driven component of the policy gradient vanishes entirely, leaving only the reference policy penalty and any auxiliary regularizers active. Identical rewards do not imply identical behavior, because different reasoning paths or tool failures collapse into the same uninformative binary score.

Assuming independent rollout attempts on a task with fixed success probability ${p}$, a group of size ${G}$ contains at least one success and at least one failure with probability ${p_{\text{contrast}}}({G},{p})=1-{p}^{G}-(1-{p})^{G}$. This matches the informative-task fraction defined in **[[01 Books/Volume III - Agentic Systems/05 Learning from Trajectories/15 Trajectory Curation/V3-15.08 The Self-Improvement Loop#^eq-vol3-trajectory-curation-informative|equation 15.3]]** with ${k}={G}$, where the excluded outcomes represent the all-pass and all-fail edge cases. (This contrast probability differs from pass@${G}$, which requires only a single pass.)

Computational waste is greatest in the difficult task regimes where the learning signal is needed most. Sampling groups of 2 on a task with pass rate ${p}=$ 0.10 yields a contrast probability ${p_{\text{contrast}}}$ of only 0.18, leaving 82 percent of generated groups without any policy gradient signal. Even with larger groups of 8, a task solved on 5 percent of attempts produces zero reward gradient 66 percent of the time. Every uninformative group incurs the full systems cost of model inference, containerized tool execution, and reward verification. This binomial model assumes independent trials on a homogeneous task; evaluating a heterogeneous workload requires computing expected contrast per difficulty bucket, as aggregate fleet averages obscure the true zero-contrast rate.

![[90 Assets/Volume III/17 Rlvr/Vol3 Rlvr Margin Contrast d01e3874d2.svg]]

*An arch-shaped curve of the probability that a group of eight carries reward contrast against pass rate from 0 to 1, with a red dot labeled 0.34 at 5 percent and a green dot labeled 0.99 at 50 percent at the top of the arch.*

*Groups of eight carry a gradient reliably only at mid-range pass rates.*

Because binary outcome variance ${p}(1-{p})$ peaks at ${p}=0.5$, active task scheduling based on rolling pass rates maximizes the proportion of informative rollouts. Systems engineers can counter zero-contrast waste using two levers: increasing group size ${G}$ or filtering task queues by difficulty. The two levers carry different systems costs. Comparing them mirrors the cost-per-accepted-task framework introduced in **[[01 Books/Volume III - Agentic Systems/00 Introduction/01 Introduction/V3-01.07 Reliability and Cost Over a Horizon#^eq-vol3-cost-per-accepted-task|equation 1.2]]**: the total computational investment per task attempt divided by the probability of producing usable gradient information.

**Napkin Math 17.4: Rollouts per informative group**

**Problem**: A hypothetical update samples 8 rollouts for each of 512 tasks, with each rollout executing 30 interaction turns generating 512 tokens per turn. Under the baseline task distribution, the agent policy succeeds on 5 percent of attempts. What is the total generation cost per training step in tokens, and which scaling strategy purchases informative gradient signal more efficiently: doubling group size to 16, or filtering the dispatch queue for tasks where the agent succeeds roughly 50 percent of the time?

**Math**: Generating a single trajectory consumes 30 $\times$ 512 = 15,360 tokens, bringing the total generation burden across 4,096 rollouts to 62.9 million tokens per step. With binary outcome probability ${p_{\text{contrast}}}=1-{p}^{G}-(1-{p})^{G}$, the amortized rollout cost per informative group is ${G}/{p_{\text{contrast}}}$, as detailed in [[01 Books/Volume III - Agentic Systems/05 Learning from Trajectories/17 Reinforcement Learning from Verifiable Rewards/V3-17.06 Group Relative Policy Optimization#^tbl-vol3-rlvr-rollout-budget|table 5]].

| Lever             | Group size | Pass rate | ${p_{\text{contrast}}}$ | Rollouts per informative group |
|:------------------|:-----------|:----------|------------------------:|-------------------------------:|
| Current task mix  | 8          | 5%        |                    0.34 |                           23.8 |
| Double the group  | 16         | 5%        |                    0.56 |                           28.6 |
| Target difficulty | 8          | 50%       |                    0.99 |                            8.1 |

Table 5: **Cost of Reward Contrast**: Doubling the group raises contrast but spends more rollouts per informative group; targeting difficulty raises contrast without more rollouts per group, at the price of changing which tasks the policy trains on.

^tbl-vol3-rlvr-rollout-budget

**Result**: Curriculum difficulty targeting reduces rollout cost per informative gradient by 2.9× compared to the hard baseline mix, whereas doubling group size actually increases the rollout cost per usable gradient. The metric counts informative groups, not the information inside each one. A group of 16 estimates its baseline more precisely than a group of 8, so the verdict holds where the binding problem is obtaining any contrast at all. Targeting also shifts the task mixture, which the run must log ([[01 Books/Volume III - Agentic Systems/05 Learning from Trajectories/17 Reinforcement Learning from Verifiable Rewards/V3-17.10 Environments and Rollouts#^sec-vol3-rlvr-task-fixtures|section 17.9.1]]).

**Systems insight**: The metric ${G}/{p_{\text{contrast}}}$ quantifies the computational cost per informative gradient signal: the numerator represents rollout expenditure per group, while the denominator measures the probability that the group yields usable contrast. In regimes with low baseline pass rates, ${p_{\text{contrast}}}$ dominates system efficiency, and it responds far more dramatically to curated curriculum selection than to expanding group size. When obtaining any contrast is the binding constraint and the changed task mixture is acceptable, the demonstrated curriculum purchases informative groups more cheaply than the larger group. Baseline precision, required task coverage, and measured held-out learning progress can justify other group sizes; the run must record the task-selection distribution as well as the chosen group size.

While group-relative comparisons establish advantage coefficients and optimization weights, every term in the objective ultimately resolves to conditional token probabilities. These probabilities remain valid only if the training engine faithfully reconstructs the precise token contexts seen by the model during inference. In multi-turn agentic workflows, model generations interleave with tool executions, environment state mutations, and dynamic context compaction. Executing GRPO correctly in production therefore requires an exact, immutable log of the tokens passed to and sampled from each model invocation, rather than an unversioned end-of-episode transcript.
