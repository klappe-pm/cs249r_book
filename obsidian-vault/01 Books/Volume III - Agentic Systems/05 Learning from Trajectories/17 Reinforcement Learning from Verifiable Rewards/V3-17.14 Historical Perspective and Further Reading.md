# V3-17.14 Historical Perspective and Further Reading

[[Home|Home]] · [[01 Books/Volume III - Agentic Systems/Volume 3 Overview|Volume Overview]] · [[01 Books/Volume III - Agentic Systems/05 Learning from Trajectories/17 Reinforcement Learning from Verifiable Rewards/V3-17 Reinforcement Learning from Verifiable Rewards|V3-17 Reinforcement Learning from Verifiable Rewards]]

^sec-vol3-rlvr-history

The problem this chapter manages began as a question about games. Minsky ([[01 Books/Volume III - Agentic Systems/05 Learning from Trajectories/17 Reinforcement Learning from Verifiable Rewards/V3-17.16 Source References#^ref-minsky1961steps|1961]]) asked how a learning system that sees only whether a long strategy won or lost can distribute credit among the many decisions that produced the result, and he named it the credit-assignment problem. Two answers followed that still organize the field. Temporal-difference learning predicted later outcomes from earlier ones, so each step could be scored against the next prediction rather than the final result ([[01 Books/Volume III - Agentic Systems/05 Learning from Trajectories/17 Reinforcement Learning from Verifiable Rewards/V3-17.16 Source References#^ref-sutton1988learning|Sutton 1988]]). The REINFORCE family instead differentiated expected reward directly through the probabilities of sampled actions and subtracted a reinforcement baseline to tame the variance ([[01 Books/Volume III - Agentic Systems/05 Learning from Trajectories/17 Reinforcement Learning from Verifiable Rewards/V3-17.16 Source References#^ref-williams1992simple|Williams 1992]]). The policy gradient theorem showed that such estimates remain valid with function approximation ([[01 Books/Volume III - Agentic Systems/05 Learning from Trajectories/17 Reinforcement Learning from Verifiable Rewards/V3-17.16 Source References#^ref-sutton1999policy|Sutton et al. 1999]]), and the analysis of baselines as control variates explained why some reduce variance more than others ([[01 Books/Volume III - Agentic Systems/05 Learning from Trajectories/17 Reinforcement Learning from Verifiable Rewards/V3-17.16 Source References#^ref-greensmith2004variance|Greensmith et al. 2004]]).

Scaling policy gradients to neural networks exposed instability rather than bias. Trust region policy optimization bounded each update’s divergence from the policy that collected the data ([[01 Books/Volume III - Agentic Systems/05 Learning from Trajectories/17 Reinforcement Learning from Verifiable Rewards/V3-17.16 Source References#^ref-schulman2015trust|Schulman et al. 2015]]). Generalized advantage estimation made the bias-variance trade in the baseline explicit ([[01 Books/Volume III - Agentic Systems/05 Learning from Trajectories/17 Reinforcement Learning from Verifiable Rewards/V3-17.16 Source References#^ref-schulman2016high|Schulman et al. 2016]]). Proximal policy optimization replaced the trust region with the cheaper clipped objective that GRPO and many later methods inherit ([[01 Books/Volume III - Agentic Systems/05 Learning from Trajectories/17 Reinforcement Learning from Verifiable Rewards/V3-17.16 Source References#^ref-schulman2017proximal|Schulman et al. 2017]]). The systems problem arrived in the same years. Stale-synchronous parameter servers bounded how old a worker’s parameters could be ([[01 Books/Volume III - Agentic Systems/05 Learning from Trajectories/17 Reinforcement Learning from Verifiable Rewards/V3-17.16 Source References#^ref-ho2013more|Ho et al. 2013]]), asynchronous actor-learners kept every actor busy at the price of that lag ([[01 Books/Volume III - Agentic Systems/05 Learning from Trajectories/17 Reinforcement Learning from Verifiable Rewards/V3-17.16 Source References#^ref-mnih2016asynchronous|Mnih et al. 2016]]), and IMPALA separated acting from learning with an explicit off-policy correction ([[01 Books/Volume III - Agentic Systems/05 Learning from Trajectories/17 Reinforcement Learning from Verifiable Rewards/V3-17.16 Source References#^ref-espeholt2018impala|Espeholt et al. 2018]]), a split that agent rollout fleets resemble.

Language models then turned the reward itself into the hard part. Learning a reward model from human comparisons made preferences optimizable ([[01 Books/Volume III - Agentic Systems/05 Learning from Trajectories/17 Reinforcement Learning from Verifiable Rewards/V3-17.16 Source References#^ref-christiano2017deep|Christiano et al. 2017]]; [[01 Books/Volume III - Agentic Systems/05 Learning from Trajectories/17 Reinforcement Learning from Verifiable Rewards/V3-17.16 Source References#^ref-ouyang2022instructgpt|Ouyang et al. 2022]]), and measuring what happens when a policy is optimized against such a proxy showed a held-out gold score eventually declining while the proxy score keeps rising ([[01 Books/Volume III - Agentic Systems/05 Learning from Trajectories/17 Reinforcement Learning from Verifiable Rewards/V3-17.16 Source References#^ref-gao2023scaling|Gao et al. 2023]]). Executable checks offered a sturdier signal: unit tests became the reward for code generation ([[01 Books/Volume III - Agentic Systems/05 Learning from Trajectories/17 Reinforcement Learning from Verifiable Rewards/V3-17.16 Source References#^ref-le2022coderl|Le et al. 2022]]), outcome and process supervision were compared on checkable reasoning ([[01 Books/Volume III - Agentic Systems/05 Learning from Trajectories/17 Reinforcement Learning from Verifiable Rewards/V3-17.16 Source References#^ref-uesato2022solving|Uesato et al. 2022]]; [[01 Books/Volume III - Agentic Systems/05 Learning from Trajectories/17 Reinforcement Learning from Verifiable Rewards/V3-17.16 Source References#^ref-lightman2023let|Lightman et al. 2024]]), and the name reinforcement learning with verifiable rewards was attached to training against such checks ([[01 Books/Volume III - Agentic Systems/05 Learning from Trajectories/17 Reinforcement Learning from Verifiable Rewards/V3-17.16 Source References#^ref-lambert2024tulu|Lambert et al. 2024]]). On the optimizer side, sampled baselines that score each output against its siblings ([[01 Books/Volume III - Agentic Systems/05 Learning from Trajectories/17 Reinforcement Learning from Verifiable Rewards/V3-17.16 Source References#^ref-kool2019buy|Kool et al. 2019]]; [[01 Books/Volume III - Agentic Systems/05 Learning from Trajectories/17 Reinforcement Learning from Verifiable Rewards/V3-17.16 Source References#^ref-ahmadian2024back|Ahmadian et al. 2024]]) led to group-relative policy optimization ([[01 Books/Volume III - Agentic Systems/05 Learning from Trajectories/17 Reinforcement Learning from Verifiable Rewards/V3-17.16 Source References#^ref-shao2024deepseekmath|Shao et al. 2024]]). The same years documented the cost of an imperfect reward, from the early catalog of specification problems ([[01 Books/Volume III - Agentic Systems/05 Learning from Trajectories/17 Reinforcement Learning from Verifiable Rewards/V3-17.16 Source References#^ref-amodei2016concrete|Amodei et al. 2016]]; [[01 Books/Volume III - Agentic Systems/05 Learning from Trajectories/17 Reinforcement Learning from Verifiable Rewards/V3-17.16 Source References#^ref-krakovna2020specification|Krakovna et al. 2020]]) to a formal definition of reward hacking ([[01 Books/Volume III - Agentic Systems/05 Learning from Trajectories/17 Reinforcement Learning from Verifiable Rewards/V3-17.16 Source References#^ref-skalse2022defining|Skalse et al. 2022]]). The defense returned to the protection literature, where confinement ([[01 Books/Volume III - Agentic Systems/05 Learning from Trajectories/17 Reinforcement Learning from Verifiable Rewards/V3-17.16 Source References#^ref-lampson1973note|Lampson 1973]]) and complete mediation ([[01 Books/Volume III - Agentic Systems/05 Learning from Trajectories/17 Reinforcement Learning from Verifiable Rewards/V3-17.16 Source References#^ref-saltzer1975|Saltzer and Schroeder 1975]]) describe the verifier boundary in terms that predate learned policies entirely. For further reading, six works trace that lineage from classical learning theory to agent training:

- Sutton and Barto ([[01 Books/Volume III - Agentic Systems/05 Learning from Trajectories/17 Reinforcement Learning from Verifiable Rewards/V3-17.16 Source References#^ref-sutton2018reinforcement|2018]]) is the standard text on returns, value functions, temporal-difference learning, and policy gradients. It supplies the vocabulary every section of this chapter assumes.
- Williams ([[01 Books/Volume III - Agentic Systems/05 Learning from Trajectories/17 Reinforcement Learning from Verifiable Rewards/V3-17.16 Source References#^ref-williams1992simple|1992]]) introduces the REINFORCE estimator and the reinforcement baseline. Reading it shows how little the core update has changed since.
- Schulman et al. ([[01 Books/Volume III - Agentic Systems/05 Learning from Trajectories/17 Reinforcement Learning from Verifiable Rewards/V3-17.16 Source References#^ref-schulman2017proximal|2017]]) presents the clipped objective, read together with Schulman et al. ([[01 Books/Volume III - Agentic Systems/05 Learning from Trajectories/17 Reinforcement Learning from Verifiable Rewards/V3-17.16 Source References#^ref-schulman2016high|2016]]) for advantage estimation. The pair explains what GRPO keeps and what it removes.
- Espeholt et al. ([[01 Books/Volume III - Agentic Systems/05 Learning from Trajectories/17 Reinforcement Learning from Verifiable Rewards/V3-17.16 Source References#^ref-espeholt2018impala|2018]]) is the reference design for separating actors from learners with a bounded, corrected lag. Its staleness problem is the one agent rollouts make worse.
- Skalse et al. ([[01 Books/Volume III - Agentic Systems/05 Learning from Trajectories/17 Reinforcement Learning from Verifiable Rewards/V3-17.16 Source References#^ref-skalse2022defining|2022]]) gives a precise definition of when a proxy reward can be hacked. It turns the informal worry of this chapter into a property a reward designer can reason about.
- Saltzer and Schroeder ([[01 Books/Volume III - Agentic Systems/05 Learning from Trajectories/17 Reinforcement Learning from Verifiable Rewards/V3-17.16 Source References#^ref-saltzer1975|1975]]) states the protection principles that verifier isolation applies. Reading it shows why an instruction to the policy is never a substitute for mediation of every route to the verdict.
