# V3-17.15 Exercises

[[Home|Home]] · [[01 Books/Volume III - Agentic Systems/Volume 3 Overview|Volume Overview]] · [[01 Books/Volume III - Agentic Systems/05 Learning from Trajectories/17 Reinforcement Learning from Verifiable Rewards/V3-17 Reinforcement Learning from Verifiable Rewards|V3-17 Reinforcement Learning from Verifiable Rewards]]

^sec-vol3-rlvr-exercises

Every given below is a hypothetical problem input rather than a measurement of a real system. Each problem ends in a decision, and the calculation exists to justify it.

1.  **False accepts early and late in training (Basic).** A repair-task verifier accepts every criterion success (${u}=1$) and a fraction ${\epsilon_{\text{FA}}}=0.02$ of criterion failures. At the start of training the policy passes the reference criterion with probability ${p_{\text{valid}}}=0.04$; after several hundred updates it passes with ${p_{\text{valid}}}=0.40$.

    1.  Use [[01 Books/Volume III - Agentic Systems/05 Learning from Trajectories/17 Reinforcement Learning from Verifiable Rewards/V3-17.04 Verifiable Rewards#^eq-vol3-rlvr-false-accept-share|equation 1]] to compute the false-accept share of rewarded rollouts at both pass rates.
    2.  Find the largest ${\epsilon_{\text{FA}}}$ that keeps the share at or below $0.10$ when ${p_{\text{valid}}}=0.04$.
    3.  The team can afford one verifier-hardening effort, either before training starts or after the pass rate has risen. Decide when to spend it and justify the choice from parts (a) and (b).

2.  **Keeping the pass above the failure (Basic).** A proposed terminal score is ${R}={R_{\text{corr}}}+{\lambda_{\text{fmt}}}{\Phi}-{\lambda_{\text{len}}}\text{len}$ with ${\lambda_{\text{fmt}}}=0.4$, ${\lambda_{\text{len}}}=0.7$, and $\text{len}={N}/{N_{\text{cap}}}$ for an enforced cap ${N_{\text{cap}}}=8{,}000$ generated tokens.

    1.  Score a pass that uses $7{,}200$ tokens with ${\Phi}=0.5$, and a failure that uses $400$ tokens with ${\Phi}=1$. State whether this pair keeps the intended order.
    2.  Compute the lowest possible pass score and the highest possible failure score over the stated ranges, and state whether every observed pass outranks every observed failure.
    3.  Holding ${\lambda_{\text{fmt}}}=0.4$, find the range of ${\lambda_{\text{len}}}$ that satisfies the sufficient ordering condition, and explain why part (a) alone was not evidence that the original weights were safe.

3.  **Sizing an all-pass rule (Basic).** A timing-sensitive fixture passes a fixed incorrect patch with per-trial probability ${p_{b}}=0.30$ and a fixed correct patch with ${p_{g}}=0.95$. Trials are independent, acceptance requires ${n_{\text{trial}}}$ total passes, and verification stops at the first rejection.

    1.  Find the smallest ${n_{\text{trial}}}$ that brings an incorrect patch’s acceptance probability below $0.01$.
    2.  At that ${n_{\text{trial}}}$, compute the false-rejection probability for the correct patch and the expected number of trials for each patch.
    3.  A lone false pass on a hard task receives the largest group coefficient. Compare adding trials with repairing the fixture’s timing sensitivity, and state which the loop should do first.

4.  **A mixed task pool and its silent groups (Intermediate).** An update samples groups of ${G}=8$ over $256$ tasks. Half the tasks belong to a family the policy passes with ${p}=0.02$; the other half to a family it passes with ${p}=0.60$. Runs are independent and rewards are binary.

    1.  Compute ${p_{\text{contrast}}}$ for each family, the expected number of informative groups per update, and the rollouts spent per informative group across the whole pool.
    2.  Compute ${p_{\text{contrast}}}$ from the pool-average pass rate instead, and explain the size of the error.
    3.  For the hard family, compute the coefficient a lone pass receives with ${\epsilon_{\text{stab}}}\to0$. A colleague proposes dropping the hard family to cut the rollout bill. Critique the proposal, naming what it does to the training distribution, and propose an alternative that raises the hard family’s contrast without discarding it.

5.  **A continuation-sampling budget (Intermediate).** A repair trajectory runs ${H}=40$ turns of ${n_{\text{gen}}}=400$ generated tokens. The team may spend at most $30$ times the base trajectory’s generated tokens on credit estimation, including the base. Continuations are independent binary returns and are not recursively forked.

    1.  Compute the generation multiple for exhaustive forking with ${K}=16$ continuations per boundary, and for forks after turns $10$, $20$, and $30$ only.
    2.  Find the ${K}$ that brings the per-fork standard-error bound to $0.05$, and the multiple that ${K}$ costs at the three chosen forks.
    3.  Find the largest ${K}$ the budget allows at those three forks and its standard-error bound. Treat a difference between two fork values as resolved only if it is at least twice the bound on the standard error of the difference, $\sqrt2/(2\sqrt {K})$. Decide whether this schedule can resolve a value difference of $0.20$, and if it cannot, redesign the schedule within the same budget.

6.  **Reset for a short-horizon support agent (Intermediate, second-lighthouse transfer).** Apply the slot model of [[01 Books/Volume III - Agentic Systems/05 Learning from Trajectories/17 Reinforcement Learning from Verifiable Rewards/V3-17.10 Environments and Rollouts#^eq-vol3-rlvr-environment-cycle|equation 6]] to training the support agent on refund tasks, where a deterministic check compares the issued refund with the recorded policy. An episode has ${H}=4$ decisions with ${T_{\text{model}}}=0.6$ s of model service and ${T_{\text{tool}}}=0.1$ s of record lookup each, then ${T_{v}}=0.2$ s of assessment. Restoring the customer record and refund history from a cold fixture takes ${T_{r}}=6.0$ s; a snapshot restore takes $0.3$ s. The pool has $64$ slots, and the shared model service can sustain at most $40{,}000$ episodes per hour.

    1.  Compute the cycle time and the reset share of the cycle for both resets, and the idealized pool capacity in episodes per hour.
    2.  Compute the capacity gain that the shared model service actually permits, and decide whether the snapshot work or added model-service capacity should come next.
    3.  In the chapter’s repair-pool example, cold reset was under half of the cycle. Compare this lighthouse’s reset share, explain which term of [[01 Books/Volume III - Agentic Systems/05 Learning from Trajectories/17 Reinforcement Learning from Verifiable Rewards/V3-17.10 Environments and Rollouts#^eq-vol3-rlvr-environment-cycle|equation 6]] makes reset the lever here, and name the state a local snapshot does not restore if a refund reached an external payment service.

7.  **Staleness bounds and verifier provisioning (Advanced).** A repair-agent trainer steps every ${T_{\text{step}}}=40$ s and admits a record only if ${\Delta}\text{ver}\le2$. Of the rollouts, a fraction $0.70$ take $30$ s from first token to admission, $0.25$ take $90$ s, and $0.05$ take $200$ s. Verification receives $256$ completed trajectories per training step. Every trajectory needs up to ${n_{\text{trial}}}=3$ verification trials of ${T_{\text{trial}}}=10$ s, and the pool targets ${\rho_{\text{target}}}=0.8$.

    1.  Compute ${\Delta}\text{ver}$ for each duration class and the fraction of rollouts excluded. Explain why the excluded fraction understates the damage to hard tasks.
    2.  Find the step time above which the $200$ s rollouts are admitted, and the duration below which a rollout is admitted at the current step time. Compare slowing the trainer, capping rollout duration, and shortening ${\Delta} {T_{\text{rollout}}}$.
    3.  Size the verifier pool when all three trials execute. Then size it under stop-on-first-rejection when a fraction $0.60$ of candidates are incorrect with ${p_{b}}=0.10$ and the rest are correct with ${p_{g}}=0.97$.
    4.  Size the stop-on-first-rejection pool again by substituting the population’s mean pass probability into every power, and state the consequence of provisioning from that number.

8.  **Diagnosing a lengthening policy (Advanced, critique and redesign).** During GRPO training with per-rollout token averaging, the mean length of failing repair rollouts grows from $2{,}000$ to $6{,}000$ generated tokens while acceptance stays flat. The team proposes adding ${\lambda_{\text{len}}}\text{len}$ with ${\lambda_{\text{len}}}=0.4$ and ${\lambda_{\text{fmt}}}=0.2$, where $\text{len}={N}/4{,}000$ is normalized by a nominal cap the runtime does not enforce.

    1.  Using [[01 Books/Volume III - Agentic Systems/05 Learning from Trajectories/17 Reinforcement Learning from Verifiable Rewards/V3-17.06 Group Relative Policy Optimization#^eq-vol3-grpo-length-coefficient|equation 5]], compute the ratio of per-token penalties on a $2{,}000$-token failure and a $6{,}000$-token failure that share the same ${\hat{A}}_i$.
    2.  Score a $9{,}000$-token pass with ${\Phi}=0$ and a $500$-token failure with ${\Phi}=1$ under the proposal. Find the pass length beyond which a pass with ${\Phi}=0$ scores below the best-scoring failure.
    3.  Critique the proposal and redesign the response. The redesign should state which diagnostic runs before any penalty is added, what the runtime must enforce, and which condition any length term that survives must satisfy.

**What’s Next: From a measured single agent to coordination**

Splitting a task across several agents is justified only when the benefits of decomposition exceed its coordination costs. Every mechanism so far, from the harness to this chapter’s repair group, governs one trajectory, and the connection-pool race that group repaired fits in one controller’s context. The repair agent’s next assignment is wider: migrating an authentication module across a repository, a change that reaches every caller of its interface and tempts a split. Part V provides ways to curate learning material, update a policy, and test the result, but no system must complete all three stages before asking whether to delegate. **[[01 Books/Volume III - Agentic Systems/06 Agents at Scale/18 Multi-Agent Coordination/V3-18 Multi-Agent Coordination#^sec-vol3-multi-agent|Multi-Agent Coordination]]** compares a coordinated design with a capable single-agent baseline that holds the same parallel tools and a comparable resource budget. Delegation can separate work and context, but it also introduces shared-state conflicts, correlated mistakes, and obligations that cross agent boundaries, and the comparison must price those costs while preserving every runtime control already required for one agent.
