# V3-15.12 Fallacies and Pitfalls

[[Home|Home]] · [[01 Books/Volume III - Agentic Systems/Volume 3 Overview|Volume Overview]] · [[01 Books/Volume III - Agentic Systems/05 Learning from Trajectories/15 Trajectory Curation/V3-15 Trajectory Curation|V3-15 Trajectory Curation]]

^sec-vol3-trajectory-curation-fallacies

A data collection pipeline can preserve every execution record faithfully and still assemble a counterproductive training set. The mistakes below stem from confusing raw trace telemetry with the deliberate pedagogical choices required to train an agent, and each carries tangible computational or behavioral costs.

**Fallacy**: *A successful task makes every action in its trace a suitable demonstration.*

Task acceptance evaluates end-to-end task completion, whereas target selection decides which specific token sequences should receive gradient supervision. Consider an agent that successfully debugs a codebase after several stumbling attempts. Its transcript might include an initial malformed JSON tool call, a parser rejection from the runtime environment, and a subsequent correction. While that malformed call must remain in the transcript as conditioning context so the agent understands the environment’s feedback, it must never be included in the training loss as a target. In the recovery worksheet, a typical recovery trajectory contains 18,500 stored tokens, but only 2,000 of those tokens are designated as supervised targets ([[01 Books/Volume III - Agentic Systems/05 Learning from Trajectories/15 Trajectory Curation/V3-15.07 Recovery Demonstration Curation#^sec-vol3-flywheel-curation|section 15.6]]). Supervising the entire accepted trace forces the model to memorize and reproduce its own intermediate errors. In a sound curation pipeline, record validity (whether the telemetry accurately reflects what happened), task acceptance (whether the agent fulfilled its objective), and target eligibility (whether an action should be reinforced) are three distinct filtering decisions.

**Pitfall**: *Fine-tuning on every failure before probing its cause.*

When an agent fails, the easiest operational reaction is often to bundle the failed trajectories into a fine-tuning dataset. In the intervention ladder worksheet, systematic counterfactual probes demonstrate that 90 percent of observed failures originate in the supporting infrastructure below the model weights, such as malformed tool schemas, missing environment variables, or overly aggressive sandbox timeouts ([[01 Books/Volume III - Agentic Systems/05 Learning from Trajectories/15 Trajectory Curation/V3-15.03 Capability Gap Diagnosis#^sec-vol3-flywheel-intervention-order|section 15.2.2]]). Retraining the model across the entire failure corpus requires only \$58 of accelerator time, and that modest price creates a dangerous illusion of progress. Fine-tuning merely encourages the model to memorize ad hoc compensations for broken tooling. As soon as an external API changes or the brittle workaround misfires, the failure resurfaces. Systems engineers should run counterfactual probes first, fix defects in tools and runtime environments directly, and restrict fine-tuning to the remaining 300 genuine behavioral failures.

**Fallacy**: *A random split of collected traces holds out unseen tasks.*

Applying uniform random sampling across collected trajectories partitions individual attempts rather than underlying problem instances. When an evaluation harness collects 8 attempts per task and allocates a 80 percent fraction to training, 83.2 percent of the tasks end up present in both the training and test sets ([[01 Books/Volume III - Agentic Systems/05 Learning from Trajectories/15 Trajectory Curation/V3-15.05 Split Design#^sec-vol3-flywheel-evaluation|section 15.4]]). Under this configuration, test metrics reflect the agent’s ability to recall previously seen problem setups rather than its capability to generalize to novel tasks. Data splitting must occur at the granularity of task groups, with environment-seed variants inheriting their group’s assignment. Furthermore, any downstream derivatives (redacted transcripts, paraphrased prompts, or deduplicated variants) must inherit the assignment of their source task so that syntactic transformations do not accidentally leak problem state across the evaluation boundary.

**Pitfall**: *Cheapening the verifier cascade by dropping or loosening a predicate.*

When verification overhead begins to strain cluster budgets, engineers often attempt to reduce costs by removing expensive check predicates or loosening test assertions. This strategy alters the semantic definition of success rather than optimizing the system. In the cascade worksheet, reordering evaluation stages based on their computational cost per rejected candidate saved 16.9 percent of total verification work while maintaining the exact same candidate yield of 20.52 percent ([[01 Books/Volume III - Agentic Systems/05 Learning from Trajectories/15 Trajectory Curation/V3-15.06 Staged Verifier Cascades#^sec-vol3-verifier-economics|section 15.5.3]]). Dropping a verification check or silently discarding unresolved edge cases shifts the distribution of accepted trajectories, degrading the downstream training corpus. Performance optimizations should focus on scheduling predicates efficiently under fixed acceptance criteria. In addition, execution pipelines must isolate and report inconclusive runs separately rather than collapsing them into either rejections or unqualified admissions.

**Fallacy**: *Setting a recovery percentage by record count fixes the training mixture.*

Trajectory counts provide an unreliable unit of measure when balancing mixed training datasets. A curation system operates across three distinct metrics: raw trajectory records, total stored context tokens, and actively supervised target tokens. In the token budget worksheet, recovery trajectories make up 25 percent of total records, but they represent 44.97 percent of stored tokens and 39.06 percent of selected training targets ([[01 Books/Volume III - Agentic Systems/05 Learning from Trajectories/15 Trajectory Curation/V3-15.07 Recovery Demonstration Curation#^sec-vol3-flywheel-curation|section 15.6]]). Because recovery sequences involve multi-step error correction and verbose environment feedback, their token footprint differs substantially from standard trajectories. Specifying mixture proportions solely by record count miscalculates both the gradient signal allocated to recovery behaviors and the computational budget required for training. Engineers must define training mixtures in terms of supervised target tokens, and validate the resulting policy resilience against held-out task benchmarks under a declared compute budget.

**Pitfall**: *Sizing collection staging from average verifier utilization.*

Dimensioning staging storage against average verification throughput invites buffer exhaustion during outages. A verifier pool running at an average utilization of 0.75 appears to have substantial headroom during normal operation. However, automated verification runtimes suffer from bimodal service times. When a faulty test fixture, infrastructure timeout, or broken container environment forces every evaluation to run until its hard execution deadline, service throughput collapses while incoming agent trajectories continue to arrive. In the burst exhaustion worksheet, this prolonged tail latency exhausts the staging store in approximately 3.6 min ([[01 Books/Volume III - Agentic Systems/05 Learning from Trajectories/15 Trajectory Curation/V3-15.09 Collection Pipeline Architecture#^sec-vol3-flywheel-pipeline|section 15.8]]). Buffer capacity must be bounded by worst-case service latency rather than nominal arrival rates. Robust ingestion architectures use credit-based flow control to restrict outstanding verifier reservations, sizing storage watermarks to absorb both in-flight execution bursts and downstream controller response delays.
