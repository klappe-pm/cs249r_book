# V3-15.07 Recovery Demonstration Curation

[[Home|Home]] · [[01 Books/Volume III - Agentic Systems/Volume 3 Overview|Volume Overview]] · [[01 Books/Volume III - Agentic Systems/05 Learning from Trajectories/15 Trajectory Curation/V3-15 Trajectory Curation|V3-15 Trajectory Curation]]

^sec-vol3-flywheel-curation

When an agent issues a malformed tool call, receives a rejection from the environment runtime, and then inspects the schema to produce a passing patch, its trace records both a mistake and a valid repair. A naive curation pipeline might discard this trace because it contains a failed step, or edit out the error to make the agent appear infallible. Either choice discards the evidence of how to recover from an imperfect prefix. Admitting the successful repair while keeping the original misstep as conditioning context requires deciding which recovery trajectories to harvest, how to mask them safely, and how much training capacity to give them.

A training corpus composed solely of error-free trajectories teaches an agent how to act only when every prior step succeeded. In production, however, noisy tool outputs, transient network faults, and early mistakes push an agent off the nominal path. Once off that path, a model trained only on clean prefixes faces states it never saw during training, and over a long horizon a recoverable detour turns into a failed task. This distribution mismatch[[01 Books/Volume III - Agentic Systems/05 Learning from Trajectories/15 Trajectory Curation/V3-15.17 Footnotes#^fn7|⁷]] creates the need for recovery demonstrations, and the curation pipeline’s job is to capture, validate, and package those recovery episodes with their conditioning evidence intact.

↪ Exposure bias, the mismatch between training on recorded prefixes and running on prefixes the policy produced itself, and its horizon-dependent cost are developed in **[[01 Books/Volume III - Agentic Systems/05 Learning from Trajectories/16 Trajectory Fine-Tuning/V3-16.07 Exposure Bias#^sec-vol3-fine-tuning-exposure-bias|Exposure Bias]]**.

**Definition 15.2: Recovery demonstration**

A ***recovery demonstration*** is a curated trajectory record or isolated sequence segment that pairs the context of an operational disruption (a model mistake, environment fault, or partial observation) with an action assessed as correct under the task specification and runtime authority boundaries.

1.  **Significance**: Recovery demonstrations directly address long-horizon execution stability. Under horizon compounding (**[[01 Books/Volume III - Agentic Systems/00 Introduction/01 Introduction/V3-01.07 Reliability and Cost Over a Horizon#^eq-vol3-horizon-compounding|equation 1.1]]**), any step-level error that the policy can successfully remediate becomes a temporary detour rather than an irrecoverable task failure. Direct-success harvesting systematically starves the training distribution of these pivotal recovery branches.
2.  **Distinction**: An appropriate recovery action depends on context: it may diagnose an exception, retry with corrected parameters, back off, or hand off control to a human supervisor. It does not require the agent to overcome an outage it cannot repair or to exceed its authority to finish the task.
3.  **Common pitfall**: Treating every token in an eventually successful trajectory as a positive imitation target, thereby inadvertently teaching the model to repeat the initial mistake.

### Record Roles and Learning Uses

^sec-vol3-trajectory-curation-record-roles

A single extended trajectory may begin with clean progress, hit an unexpected environment error, execute a sound recovery, and later stall on an unrelated tool failure. Discarding that entire trajectory wastes genuine recovery signal, whereas training uncritically on all of its tokens teaches the model to replicate its bugs. Because a trace rarely fits a single outcome bucket, labels such as direct success, recovery, and negative describe the behavioral evidence a segment contains rather than granting blanket permission for training. The predicates of [[01 Books/Volume III - Agentic Systems/05 Learning from Trajectories/15 Trajectory Curation/V3-15.06 Staged Verifier Cascades#^tbl-vol3-verifier-cascade-stages|table 5]] already separate the two decisions. Record adequacy and permitted use are shared across routes, while the process, outcome, and target predicates are set by the consumer. A role label therefore indicates which consumer-specific checks a segment might pass ([[01 Books/Volume III - Agentic Systems/05 Learning from Trajectories/15 Trajectory Curation/V3-15.07 Recovery Demonstration Curation#^tbl-vol3-trajectory-taxonomy|table 8]]).

| Record role                  | Evidence worth preserving                                                                   | Learning-use decision                                                                                                                              |
|:-----------------------------|:--------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------|:---------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------|
| Direct-success demonstration | Grounded actions and adequate evidence for the declared result                              | Select useful responses under the demonstration policy; shortest length and absence of error codes are not sufficient criteria                     |
| Recovery demonstration       | The preceding context, observed disruption, response, and evidence for its assessed outcome | Select the justified response while retaining the mistake or fault as context where needed                                                         |
| Hard-negative candidate      | A plausible but inadequate response, with evidence identifying the relevant deficiency      | Consider diagnostic use, a separately assessed segment, or a compatible preference comparison; do not automatically imitate the deficient response |

Table 8: **Behavioral Roles and Learning Uses**: Record labels describe evidence. Each intended consumer still requires its own admission conditions.

^tbl-vol3-trajectory-taxonomy

A **hard negative** is an action or plan that looks plausible under surface checks, such as valid syntax, expected keywords, or a passing local smoke test, yet fails full functional verification. Pinpointing the exact step where an agentic trace went wrong is rarely simple. A failed assertion at the end of a run confirms that an error occurred without naming the earlier decision that caused it. Repeated tool calls that look like a loop may be legitimate polling against an asynchronous job. Curation systems therefore record the failure signal that implicates a candidate divergence step together with an explicit note when the first causal step is uncertain, rather than inventing an exact divergence point. Engineering teams also frequently assume that any recorded negative can be paired immediately with a success to train a preference objective, or replayed as a baseline for reinforcement learning against a verifiable reward. Both shortcuts fail in practice. A negative forms a valid comparison only under the shared-context condition of [[01 Books/Volume III - Agentic Systems/05 Learning from Trajectories/15 Trajectory Curation/V3-15.03 Capability Gap Diagnosis#^sec-vol3-trajectory-curation-learning-artifacts|section 15.2.3]], which a success generated under a different prompt, an extra hint, or updated tool outputs violates. A reinforcement learning baseline, in turn, needs a live, resettable fixture rather than a stored failure transcript.

**Systems Perspective 15.1: A recorded mistake is context, not a target**

A pervasive misconception in trajectory engineering is that an imitation training set must consist exclusively of pristine, error-free transcripts. In reality, a curated training dataset consists of complete conversational contexts coupled with explicit target masks, and these two components serve fundamentally different purposes.

A flawed model response should receive no direct positive imitation target loss. Nevertheless, that erroneous response must remain in the prompt history if subsequent recovery actions are to make any sense; a correction is meaningful only in the context of the error it resolves. Conversely, an execution trace that ultimately timed out or failed an integration test often contains early sub-tasks solved with exemplary precision. The curation pipeline marks which spans represent valid targets and which represent conditioning context; the loss-masking subsystem then omits their direct next-token loss terms while retaining them as conditioning during fine-tuning. Masking errors out of the loss while retaining them in the attention context teaches the model how to react to a failure without training it to generate one.

### Conditioning Evidence for a Correction

^sec-vol3-trajectory-curation-conditioning-evidence

In collection run `collection_run_r19_01`, target selection separates context from targets on task `pool_repair_01` inside test environment `pool_fixture_v1`. The run produces workspace candidate `ws_r20` and captures raw trace `trace_r19_01` (workspace numbers count candidate revisions after the evaluation trial’s `ws_r19`, while run and trace numbers count collection batches). End-to-end grading confirms that `ws_r20` satisfies `repair_accept_v1`, meeting all process constraints, resource ceilings, and cleanup duties.

The run belongs to a campaign whose declared learning use is recovery from harness refusals. Unlike the truncated observation that opened the chapter, this behavior is collected on the premise, recorded in the collection manifest, that the ladder’s probes left it with the model. Supplying the schema in context and validating proposals against a strict schema still left the policy emitting malformed edits that the runtime had to refuse. What remains to teach is how to respond after a refusal, and the recorded event sequence shows how that recovery unfolds:

- Event `e1` delivers the task prompt, tool definitions, and repository context.
- At event `e2`, the model proposes a file edit with a malformed argument. Because the tool runtime validates inputs locally before dispatch, it refuses the call immediately, and the trace records the refusal without logging an applied write.
- At event `e3`, the model reads the refusal, requests the API schema documentation, and inspects the returned specification.
- At event `e4`, the model issues a corrected, schema-compliant edit, and the tool enforces an atomic version check alongside the update rather than relying on a stale earlier read.
- At event `e5`, the harness runs the required checks on the edited workspace and returns their summary as an observation.
- At event `e6`, the model summarizes the validated changes, states the patch scope, and closes the session.

Under curation policy `repair_accept_v1`, an initial syntax refusal that the agent diagnoses and corrects promptly is eligible for recovery training. A target-selection manifest marks the actions worth imitating without editing the recorded sequence of events:

``` json
{
  "collection_run_id": "collection_run_r19_01",
  "source_trace_id": "trace_r19_01",
  "criteria_version": "repair_accept_v1",
  "target_selection_version": "recovery_targets_v1",
  "behavioral_role": "recovery",
  "context_only_events": ["e2", "e5"],
  "selected_response_events": ["e3", "e4", "e6"],
  "assessment_scope": "recorded correction and supported final report"
}
```

The source trace preserves the original task prompt, tool definitions, intermediate observations, and evaluation logs intact, while target selection marks only the policy tokens inside events `e3`, `e4`, and `e6`. The refused proposal (`e2`) and test summary (`e5`) stay in the sequence as conditioning context, supplying the history that makes the schema lookup and corrected edit make sense. Passing the overall task does not make every intermediate action a training target; each selected step must be a sound decision given what the agent knew at that turn. Placed beside the other two record roles, the recovery record sits between a direct success, whose supported responses can all be targets, and a hard negative, whose deficient response is never one ([[01 Books/Volume III - Agentic Systems/05 Learning from Trajectories/15 Trajectory Curation/V3-15.07 Recovery Demonstration Curation#^fig-vol3-trajectory-typology|figure 3]]).

![[90 Assets/Volume III/15 Trajectory Curation/Vol3 Trajectory Typology 0d859c3b98.svg]]

Figure 3: **Record Roles and Selected Targets**: Each role is drawn as a sequence of events, with selected targets distinguished from context. In the recovery demonstration, the refused malformed proposal at e2 and the check summary at e5 stay as context while e3, e4, and e6 become targets. A hard-negative candidate looks adequate under a targeted check and fails a required one. Its deficient response is never an imitation target, and where it went wrong is recorded with its basis rather than fixed at an exact step.

^fig-vol3-trajectory-typology

When only a local recovery window has verified evidence, the curation harness can admit that segment alone while leaving the broader task outcome marked as unverified. Likewise, if post-processing scrubs credentials or private paths to create `trace_r19_01_redacted_v1`, the pipeline must re-verify the target spans against the modified tokens. Deleting the malformed call, pasting future test outputs into an earlier prompt, or truncating intermediate observations changes the causal problem the model is being asked to learn. Preserving those causal links inside harvested traces is only half of recovery curation, however, because waiting passively for natural failures leaves rare fault modes unrepresented.

### Fault Injection

^sec-vol3-trajectory-curation-fault-injection

In production, routine execution occasionally exposes an agent to network drops, service timeouts, and tool errors, but waiting for spontaneous failures leaves wide gaps in the training corpus. Systematic **fault injection**[[01 Books/Volume III - Agentic Systems/05 Learning from Trajectories/15 Trajectory Curation/V3-15.17 Footnotes#^fn8|⁸]] fills those gaps deliberately. A collection protocol logs the injection version, the targeted tool call or observation stream, the simulated system state, and the expected recovery action. Fault injection stays strictly inside the runtime’s security boundaries: injecting a network timeout or file lock grants the agent no extra authority to install unapproved packages, widen filesystem permissions, or bypass human approval gates. Each condition in [[01 Books/Volume III - Agentic Systems/05 Learning from Trajectories/15 Trajectory Curation/V3-15.07 Recovery Demonstration Curation#^tbl-vol3-fault-injection-taxonomy|table 9]] names the causal distinction the fixture must preserve and the response a curator checks.

| Collection condition                               | Required causal distinction                                    | Candidate response to assess                                   |
|:---------------------------------------------------|:---------------------------------------------------------------|:---------------------------------------------------------------|
| Malformed proposal rejected locally                | No operation was dispatched                                    | Inspect the schema and correct the represented error           |
| Partial search result                              | Missing content is distinguished from an empty complete result | Request a valid continuation or report insufficient evidence   |
| Lost acknowledgment after a state-changing request | The endpoint may have acted despite the missing reply          | Reconcile the identified operation before a dependent retry    |
| Unavailable capability or exhausted allowance      | The original goal may be unreachable within the grant          | Stop or hand off with accurate scope and owned residual duties |

Table 9: **Fault Conditions for Recovery Collection**: Each condition preserves the relation between observations and possible effects. The criterion evaluates an appropriate response, not compulsory autonomous repair.

^tbl-vol3-fault-injection-taxonomy

Consider the lost-acknowledgment case in [[01 Books/Volume III - Agentic Systems/05 Learning from Trajectories/15 Trajectory Curation/V3-15.07 Recovery Demonstration Curation#^tbl-vol3-fault-injection-taxonomy|table 9]]. When a mock harness drops an acknowledgment after committing a state-changing remote call, the simulated environment must keep the side effects of that committed call in its state. If the mock returned a connection reset while silently rolling the server state back to a clean slate, it would misrepresent the distributed systems hazard and train the agent to retry blindly, causing duplicate writes in production. Replay determinism requires that every subsequent observation reflect the mutations that actually occurred.

Engineering teams must also separate tasks meant to teach autonomous repair from tasks meant to teach safe stopping. A recovery task provides an authorized, reachable fix inside the test environment; a safe-stopping task deliberately closes every repair path so the agent learns to halt cleanly and hand off to an operator. Because the two objectives require different grading rubrics, reliability coverage tracks unprompted agent mistakes and injected faults separately while recording any production failure modes that the fixture cannot reproduce. Harvesting these multi-turn recovery traces, however, changes the token geometry of the resulting corpus.

### Records, Stored Tokens, and Selected Targets

^sec-vol3-trajectory-curation-token-accounting

Because a recovery trajectory includes diagnostic cycles, failed attempts, and error observations, it is much longer than a direct-success run, yet only the corrected responses carry supervised loss. As a result, the share of recovery trajectories in a corpus matches neither their share of stored tokens on disk nor their share of active target tokens during training. Curation accounting starts from a retained pool of trajectories and draws subsets for specific training artifacts. One retained trace may supply conditioning context and target spans to an imitation corpus while also entering a preference pair, yet it remains a single task execution in the pool.

![[90 Assets/Volume III/15 Trajectory Curation/Vol3 Trajectory Curation Margin 003 018a2a9764.svg]]

*Three horizontal bars showing the recovery class's share of the hypothetical pool under three denominators: 25 percent of records, 45 percent of stored tokens, and 39 percent of selected target tokens.*

*One recovery class, three shares: records, stored tokens, targets.*

For a trajectory category ${j}$, let ${n}_j$ denote the number of retained records, ${\bar{S}}^{({j})}$ denote the average stored tokens per record under the active tokenizer, and ${\bar{w}}_j$ denote the average selected target tokens per record admitted into a specific imitation fine-tuning artifact. The total stored token volume ${V}_j$ and total target token volume ${W}_j$ are:

$$
{V}_j={n}_j{\bar{S}}^{({j})},\qquad {W}_j={n}_j{\bar{w}}_j.
$$

The stored token share measures ${V}_j$ against the total tokens in the repository, whereas the target token share measures ${W}_j$ against the active supervised tokens in the training dataset. Confusing the two breaks capacity planning. Processed tokens (context plus targets) drive attention compute and memory traffic during forward and backward passes. Selected target tokens, by contrast, decide which positions contribute direct loss terms (whose gradients still flow back through the conditioning context). Specifying a dataset mixture by record count while budgeting accelerators by target tokens, or vice versa, creates scheduling surprises, especially when multi-turn conversations are unrolled without prefix sharing.

**Napkin Math 15.3: One pool, three different token allocations**

**Problem**: Consider a hypothetical retained corpus containing 50,000 records categorized into disjoint roles. The demonstration policy admits direct successes and recovery trajectories; negative trajectories are reserved for diagnostic logging and preference modeling, providing zero imitation targets for this artifact. Using the empirical means listed in [[01 Books/Volume III - Agentic Systems/05 Learning from Trajectories/15 Trajectory Curation/V3-15.07 Recovery Demonstration Curation#^tbl-vol3-curation-token-allocation|table 10]], determine the fractional share that recovery trajectories occupy across three distinct denominators: total records, tokens (stored in the repository and processed by this artifact), and supervised target tokens.

| Retained class | Records | Mean stored tokens per record | Mean selected target tokens per record |
|:---------------|--------:|------------------------------:|---------------------------------------:|
| Direct         |  32,500 |                         7,200 |                                  1,200 |
| Recovery       |  12,500 |                        18,500 |                                  2,000 |
| Negative       |   5,000 |                         9,800 |        None in this imitation artifact |

Table 10: **Inputs to the Corpus Allocation Worksheet**: Counts and lengths are hypothetical, not an empirically recommended mixture. Selected targets refer to one presentation of each eligible record under a fixed tokenizer and selection policy.

^tbl-vol3-curation-token-allocation

**Math**: Total stored tokens follow ${V}_j = {n}_j{\bar{S}}^{({j})}$: direct successes account for 234,000,000, recovery traces account for 231,250,000, and negatives account for 49,000,000, totaling 514,250,000 across the repository and 465,250,000 across admitted demonstration records. Supervised target tokens follow ${W}_j = {n}_j{\bar{w}}_j$: direct demonstrations contribute 39,000,000 target tokens and recovery traces contribute 25,000,000 target tokens, totaling 64,000,000 supervisory tokens.

**Result**: Recovery accounts for 25 percent of total retained records, 44.97 percent of all stored tokens in the repository, 49.70 percent of the admitted demonstration tokens, and 39.06 percent of active target supervision tokens. The two token shares answer different questions. The repository share includes negatives that this artifact never trains on, so it prices storage; the admitted-demonstration share counts the tokens that training actually processes, so it prices compute.

**Systems insight**: Always formulate training mixtures in terms of target tokens while budgeting accelerator compute and memory in terms of processed context tokens. The operational multiplier separating these two metrics is the context-to-target ratio: 9.25 stored context tokens per target token for recovery traces, compared to 6 for direct successes. Increasing the recovery proportion inflates processed context volume far faster than supervised gradient signal. An engineering team that configures a mixture purely by record count will request a 25 percent recovery ratio but will end up committing 44.97 percent of repository storage and 49.70 percent of processed training tokens to recovery records. Naive multi-turn serialization that repeatedly unrolls conversational prefixes amplifies this computational overhead even further.

### Mixture Experiments

^sec-vol3-trajectory-curation-mixture-experiments

Token accounting prices the storage and compute footprint of a recovery-heavy corpus, but it cannot tell whether adding more recovery traces actually improves fault tolerance or crowds out direct-path competence on routine tasks. Answering that question requires an empirical mixture experiment that holds the evaluation protocol, base checkpoint, target-masking rules, and training schedule fixed while varying the recovery ratio across training cohorts. Curation engineers track deduplication and replacement in each cohort, because raising the recovery share under a fixed training budget reduces coverage of direct-path tasks.

Because context-to-target ratios differ by role, an experiment must state which denominator it holds constant and report results under both. Fixing total supervised target tokens forces the recovery-heavy cohort to process far more conditioning context tokens, raising training wall-clock time and FLOPs; fixing total processed tokens gives the recovery-heavy cohort fewer supervised target updates. When accelerator hours or training windows are hard limits, teams compare candidate mixtures at equal wall-clock time or compute cost. They screen those candidates on the development split and test the chosen configuration once on the held-out evaluation benchmark. To keep aggregate scores from hiding regressions, evaluation reports break results down across nominal tasks, injected fault conditions, and out-of-distribution transfer suites before computing any summary metric. They track task acceptance alongside hallucinated tool outputs, redundant steps, clean stops, and total token spend.

Once an effective mixture is chosen, its parameters are written into the **corpus manifest**, the machine-readable, versioned record that publishes a corpus and tells a trainer what each member is and how it may be used.[[01 Books/Volume III - Agentic Systems/05 Learning from Trajectories/15 Trajectory Curation/V3-15.17 Footnotes#^fn9|⁹]] Beyond the mixture weights, the manifest binds input trajectory pool identifiers, selection filter versions, token accounting denominators, test coverage matrices, and audit logs, whose per-record lineage and publication are developed in [[01 Books/Volume III - Agentic Systems/05 Learning from Trajectories/15 Trajectory Curation/V3-15.10 Trajectory Provenance Tracking#^sec-vol3-flywheel-provenance|section 15.9]] and [[01 Books/Volume III - Agentic Systems/05 Learning from Trajectories/15 Trajectory Curation/V3-15.11 Case Study Curating the Repair Record#^sec-vol3-trajectory-curation-manifest-publication|section 15.10.2]]. Deploying a model trained on this manifest changes the trajectories that subsequent collection runs produce. New failure modes and recoveries feed back into the harvesting pipeline, which closes a loop in which the policy being improved also generates the data that improves it.
