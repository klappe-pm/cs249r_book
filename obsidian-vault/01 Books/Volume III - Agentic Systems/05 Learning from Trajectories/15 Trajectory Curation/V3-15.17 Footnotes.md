# V3-15.17 Footnotes

[[Home|Home]] · [[01 Books/Volume III - Agentic Systems/Volume 3 Overview|Volume Overview]] · [[01 Books/Volume III - Agentic Systems/05 Learning from Trajectories/15 Trajectory Curation/V3-15 Trajectory Curation|V3-15 Trajectory Curation]]

^footnotes

<sup>1</sup> **Duplication in Code Corpora**: Code datasets are especially prone to leakage because repository forks, vendored libraries, and copy-pasted snippets reproduce the same defect across nominally distinct codebases. Evaluating code models on corpora that contain near-duplicates inflates reported accuracy by as much as a factor of two compared with deduplicated corpora ([[01 Books/Volume III - Agentic Systems/05 Learning from Trajectories/15 Trajectory Curation/V3-15.16 Source References#^ref-allamanis2019adverse|Allamanis 2019]]). An agent trajectory corpus inherits every duplicate in the underlying codebases and adds a second source of duplication: repeated execution rollouts on the same benchmark fixture. Grouping trajectories by their underlying task is therefore required for valid evaluation rather than an optional refinement.

^fn1

<sup>2</sup> **Benchmark Contamination**: When web-scale pretraining corpora grew to include public benchmarks, models were often trained directly on the test splits later used to grade them ([[01 Books/Volume III - Agentic Systems/05 Learning from Trajectories/15 Trajectory Curation/V3-15.16 Source References#^ref-sainz2023nlp|Sainz et al. 2023]]). Because base-model providers rarely publish token-level manifests of their pretraining data, downstream teams must rely on n-gram overlap probes and memorization checks to detect leakage. For an agent trajectory pipeline, the only reliable defense is structural: maintain private internal fixtures whose code and solutions have never appeared on the public web, and report separately which benchmark results come from those private evaluations.

^fn2

<sup>3</sup> **MinHash**: Minwise independent permutations were introduced to spot near-duplicate web pages at search-engine scale, where comparing every pair of documents directly is far too slow ([[01 Books/Volume III - Agentic Systems/05 Learning from Trajectories/15 Trajectory Curation/V3-15.16 Source References#^ref-broder1997resemblance|Broder 1997]]). Trajectory screening shares the same economics: signatures are cheap to compute and store, and the real cost lies in reviewing the candidate pairs they surface.

^fn3

<sup>4</sup> **Locality-Sensitive Hashing**: A hashing scheme in which the collision probability increases with item similarity, built for sublinear approximate nearest-neighbor search in high dimensions ([[01 Books/Volume III - Agentic Systems/05 Learning from Trajectories/15 Trajectory Curation/V3-15.16 Source References#^ref-indyk1998approximate|Indyk and Motwani 1998]]). Banding MinHash signatures adapts LSH to Jaccard similarity. Engineers choose ${b}$ and ${r}$ to match candidate volume to downstream review capacity, since large collision buckets can still make pair-review work grow rapidly with corpus size.

^fn4

<sup>5</sup> **Classifier Cascade**: An ordered sequence of tests that drops unpromising candidates early using cheap checks gained prominence in real-time object detection, where a cascade of boosted classifiers rejected background image patches after computing only a few features ([[01 Books/Volume III - Agentic Systems/05 Learning from Trajectories/15 Trajectory Curation/V3-15.16 Source References#^ref-viola2001|Viola and Jones 2001]]). A curation cascade shares the same compute economics, but its error tolerance is much stricter: whereas a visual detector can trade a small rise in false positives for faster throughput, loosening an admission check in a curation cascade pollutes the training corpus and degrades the resulting policy.

^fn5

<sup>6</sup> **Predicate Rank**: Relational database query optimizers faced the same scheduling problem when selection predicates began calling expensive user-defined functions. Ordering filters by selectivity alone ran heavy functions on rows that a fast check would have dropped. Sorting independent filters by rank, defined as per-row cost divided by rejection probability $(1 - {p^{\text{chk}}}_i)$, minimizes expected work, matching the exchange argument in [[01 Books/Volume III - Agentic Systems/05 Learning from Trajectories/15 Trajectory Curation/V3-15.06 Staged Verifier Cascades#^eq-vol3-trajectory-curation-order-rule|equation 2]]. In agent curation cascades, where checks range from sub-millisecond AST scans to multi-minute integration tests and human reviews, rank ordering decides where most of the verification budget goes.

^fn6

<sup>7</sup> **Compounding Imitation Error**: A policy trained only on states visited by an expert can turn a per-step error rate ${\epsilon}$ (the per-step error rate of horizon compounding, **[[01 Books/Volume III - Agentic Systems/00 Introduction/01 Introduction/V3-01.07 Reliability and Cost Over a Horizon#^eq-vol3-horizon-compounding|equation 1.1]]**) into as many as ${H}^2{\epsilon}$ mistakes over an ${H}$-step task, because an early error exposes the policy to states absent from its training distribution. Querying an expert interactively on learner-visited states, the core mechanism of DAgger, restores an error bound that grows linearly in ${H}$ ([[01 Books/Volume III - Agentic Systems/05 Learning from Trajectories/15 Trajectory Curation/V3-15.16 Source References#^ref-ross2011reduction|Ross et al. 2011]]). Recovery demonstrations are the offline curation counterpart: they supply supervised repair targets on the flawed state prefixes that direct-success collection omits.

^fn7

<sup>8</sup> **Fault Injection**: Introducing controlled failures to test resilience is a classic systems practice, popularized in distributed infrastructure as chaos engineering, which injects server, network, and disk faults into live clusters to check steady-state behavior ([[01 Books/Volume III - Agentic Systems/05 Learning from Trajectories/15 Trajectory Curation/V3-15.16 Source References#^ref-basiri2016|Basiri et al. 2016]]). Curation borrows the experimental discipline rather than the live production venue: faults are injected inside versioned, hermetic test fixtures where every fault is logged and environment state transitions stay deterministic, so demonstrations teach genuine recovery rather than quirks of the simulator.

^fn8

<sup>9</sup> **Dataset Documentation**: Documenting datasets with structured records that state how they were built, what they contain, and what uses they support was proposed by analogy to hardware datasheets in electronics ([[01 Books/Volume III - Agentic Systems/05 Learning from Trajectories/15 Trajectory Curation/V3-15.16 Source References#^ref-gebru2021|Gebru et al. 2021]]). A corpus manifest turns that documentation into a machine-checked contract for agent pipelines: its fields bind cryptographically to dataset versions, filter code, and verification logs so that training jobs can check corpus invariants automatically before starting a run.

^fn9

<sup>10</sup> **Expert Iteration**: The generate–select–train loop predates language models. In expert iteration, tree search guided by the current network finds stronger moves than the network alone, the network is trained to imitate them, and the updated network guides the next round of search ([[01 Books/Volume III - Agentic Systems/05 Learning from Trajectories/15 Trajectory Curation/V3-15.16 Source References#^ref-anthony2017thinking|Anthony et al. 2017]]). Rejection-sampling fine-tuning replaces tree search with repeated sampling and the expert with an automated check, which is why any blind spot in the check quickly becomes a habit of the loop.

^fn10

<sup>11</sup> **Credit-Based Flow Control**: The mechanism originates in network switches, where a transmitter may forward a cell only while holding a credit for a free downstream buffer slot, so buffers cannot overflow however bursty the traffic ([[01 Books/Volume III - Agentic Systems/05 Learning from Trajectories/15 Trajectory Curation/V3-15.16 Source References#^ref-kung1994credit|Kung et al. 1994]]). The receiver returns the credit only after draining the slot, the analog of returning a collection credit upon durable disposition.

^fn11

<sup>12</sup> **Why- and Where-Provenance**: Database research distinguished two questions about a derived datum: which source records justify its existence (why-provenance) and which source locations its values were copied from (where-provenance) ([[01 Books/Volume III - Agentic Systems/05 Learning from Trajectories/15 Trajectory Curation/V3-15.16 Source References#^ref-buneman2001why|Buneman et al. 2001]]). A trajectory record needs both. Where-provenance traces each retained field to the run, event, and transformation that produced it, and why-provenance traces its admission to the assessments that justified it, so a withdrawn source or a revised criterion can be followed to every affected corpus member.

^fn12

<sup>13</sup> **Merkle Tree**: A tree in which each internal node holds the hash of its children, introduced to build digital signatures from a conventional one-way function ([[01 Books/Volume III - Agentic Systems/05 Learning from Trajectories/15 Trajectory Curation/V3-15.16 Source References#^ref-merkle1988digital|Merkle 1988]]). Its useful property is that proving one leaf belongs under a trusted root needs only the sibling hashes along one path, logarithmic in the number of leaves. A corpus manifest can therefore commit to millions of record digests under one root and still verify a single member cheaply, but the root commits only to the leaves that were hashed.

^fn13
