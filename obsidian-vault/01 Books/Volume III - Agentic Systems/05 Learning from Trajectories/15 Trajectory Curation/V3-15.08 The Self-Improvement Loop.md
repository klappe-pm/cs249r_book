# V3-15.08 The Self-Improvement Loop

[[Home|Home]] · [[01 Books/Volume III - Agentic Systems/Volume 3 Overview|Volume Overview]] · [[01 Books/Volume III - Agentic Systems/05 Learning from Trajectories/15 Trajectory Curation/V3-15 Trajectory Curation|V3-15 Trajectory Curation]]

^sec-vol3-trajectory-curation-self-improvement

Suppose a curation round for the connection-pool repair agent retains only straight-line, clean executions while discarding messier traces where the agent recovers from misleading test diagnostics. Fine-tuning on that corpus strengthens straightforward repairs while leaving recovery unimproved, and in the next round a higher nominal pass rate hides that gap rather than closing it. Because corpus selection shapes the behaviors the next policy generation learns and samples, curators track three distinct signals in every round: what the current policy sampled, what the curation cascade kept, and how the resulting model scores under independent held-out evaluation.

### Rejection-Sampling Fine-Tuning

^sec-vol3-trajectory-curation-rejection-sampling

When a team lacks human demonstrations for new task fixtures, it can bootstrap training data from the current policy whenever verifying a candidate solution is easier than generating one, provided it logs attempted, rejected, and unverified runs alongside retained examples so a looser filter is never mistaken for a better model. In this generate–select–train loop, a versioned policy samples executions across a suite of task fixtures within a fixed compute budget. The admission cascade routes the resulting traces by role: clean demonstrations feed imitation buffers, branched pairs populate preference datasets, and unresolved traces enter diagnostic storage. Training on the admitted records produces a candidate checkpoint, which held-out evaluation tests to decide whether it becomes the generator for the next collection round (an internal development promotion that stays separate from production release).

Selecting sampled successes for imitation is **rejection-sampling fine-tuning**.[[01 Books/Volume III - Agentic Systems/05 Learning from Trajectories/15 Trajectory Curation/V3-15.17 Footnotes#^fn10|¹⁰]] The Self-Taught Reasoner (STaR) illustrates this loop in bootstrapped reasoning. The model generates candidate rationales, keeps those that reach correct answers, fine-tunes on them, and uses ground-truth answers to prompt new rationales on tasks it initially failed ([[01 Books/Volume III - Agentic Systems/05 Learning from Trajectories/15 Trajectory Curation/V3-15.16 Source References#^ref-zelikman2022star|Zelikman et al. 2022]]). In agentic tasks, an automated verifier checks that a run satisfies its acceptance criteria, while target review still checks that the intermediate tool calls and state transitions are sound enough to imitate.

Different learning objectives need different outcome mixes across attempts on a fixture. An all-success batch supplies valid imitation demonstrations showing clean paths through tools. An all-failure batch yields no end-to-end demonstrations under a success filter, yet still supplies localized recovery segments, negative candidates for preference pairs, and failure traces for new regression fixtures. Group-relative training objectives, by contrast, require outcome variation within the same fixture. They score each rollout by its reward minus the mean reward of the rollouts sampled for that fixture, so a batch in which every rollout gets the same reward produces zero advantage on every rollout and contributes no reward-surrogate gradient.

↪ Group relative policy optimization, which scores each rollout against the other rollouts sampled for the same task, is developed in **[[01 Books/Volume III - Agentic Systems/05 Learning from Trajectories/17 Reinforcement Learning from Verifiable Rewards/V3-17.06 Group Relative Policy Optimization#^sec-vol3-rlvr-grpo|Group Relative Policy Optimization]]**.

The sampling budget required to harvest these different data types follows from the per-attempt pass rate. Suppose the collection policy succeeds on a fixture with probability ${p}$ per attempt, with each of the ${k}$ attempts executed independently. A simple success filter extracts a demonstration from the fixture if at least one attempt succeeds, an event that occurs with probability $1-(1-{p})^{k}$. This expected yield sets the baseline compute cost per retained trajectory when the collector keeps at most one success from each full batch. For preference comparisons based on binary task acceptance and group-relative objectives with binary rewards, useful within-group contrast requires at least one success and at least one failure. Subtracting both the all-failure probability $(1-{p})^{k}$ and the all-success probability ${p}^{{k}}$ yields the **informative-task fraction**:

$$
{p_{\text{inform}}}({p},{k})=1-(1-{p})^{k}-{p}^{{k}}. \tag{3}
$$

^eq-vol3-trajectory-curation-informative

With 8 attempts, a fixture the policy solves 5 percent of the time yields a success with probability only 0.34, requiring an average of about 24 attempts to harvest a single successful trajectory. That same fixture forms a mixed group of successes and failures with probability 0.34. At 50 percent, where the task is moderately difficult, the mixed fraction reaches 0.99, providing abundant pairwise contrast. When the pass rate reaches 95 percent, nearly every fixture yields a success, but the mixed fraction drops back to 0.34 because batches rarely include a failing attempt. [[01 Books/Volume III - Agentic Systems/05 Learning from Trajectories/15 Trajectory Curation/V3-15.08 The Self-Improvement Loop#^eq-vol3-trajectory-curation-informative|Equation 3]] measures contrast rather than general utility. All-success groups remain valid demonstrations for imitation learning. By contrast, **[[01 Books/Volume III - Agentic Systems/05 Learning from Trajectories/17 Reinforcement Learning from Verifiable Rewards/V3-17.06 Group Relative Policy Optimization#^sec-vol3-rlvr-grpo-zero-variance|Zero-contrast groups]]** applies the ${p}^{{k}}$ and $(1-{p})^{k}$ terms to isolate groups whose uniform rewards offer zero reward-surrogate gradient in group-relative optimization.

A thin blue success-yield curve rises quickly toward one, tracing the thick purple informative-task curve below a pass rate of 0.5. *Easy fixtures yield successes most often; mid-difficulty fixtures yield contrast most often.*

Choosing fixture difficulty is therefore a resource allocation decision. Hard fixtures cost about 24 attempts per harvested success, but they force the agent into unfamiliar states and expose missing recovery skills. Easy fixtures cost almost nothing per success, serving as cheap regression anchors that preserve baseline competence. For rare or high-consequence task families, low harvest yields are a worthwhile price for keeping coverage intact. Recording the sampling and retention policies alongside the corpus makes these shifts visible across retraining rounds, where evolving interfaces can invalidate older records and repeated self-selection can narrow behavioral coverage.

### Staleness and Coverage Narrowing

^sec-vol3-trajectory-curation-coverage-narrowing

Across those retraining rounds, a trajectory recorded minutes ago is already unusable if it called a deprecated tool schema or passed a flawed grader, whereas a trace recorded months ago by an older policy remains a valid demonstration as long as the tools and environment semantics have not changed. In an evolving agent system, **staleness** therefore measures architectural incompatibility rather than chronological age. Trajectory stores prevent silent corruption by binding four provenance fields to every record: the generating policy, the environment fixture, the tool interface schema, and the grading criteria. When grading criteria change, the pipeline re-runs verification. When tool schemas change, existing records undergo schema migration and revalidation. As the policy improves, sampling shifts toward the harder edge cases the updated agent still fails.

Even when every schema and grader stays current, repeated self-training narrows behavioral coverage. Because strategies that cleared earlier filters appear more often in fine-tuning data, the updated policy reproduces those paths more often and samples them even more heavily in the next round. Left unchecked, training repeatedly on a model’s own filtered outputs shrinks distributional diversity, a form of model collapse ([[01 Books/Volume III - Agentic Systems/05 Learning from Trajectories/15 Trajectory Curation/V3-15.16 Source References#^ref-shumailov2024|Shumailov et al. 2024]]) driven by the hidden feedback loop between a model’s outputs and its future training set ([[01 Books/Volume III - Agentic Systems/05 Learning from Trajectories/15 Trajectory Curation/V3-15.16 Source References#^ref-sculley2015hidden|Sculley et al. 2015]]). Automated verifiers reject failures covered by their checks, but filtering alone cannot break that self-reinforcing loop; teams must measure and bound the drift directly.

Three telemetry signals warn when coverage is narrowing across rounds. Within each fixture, the number of distinct normalized action sequences falls as the policy repeats the same routines; across attempts on a fixture, pass@1 continues to rise while pass@${k}$ at large ${k}$ flattens out, showing that probability mass is concentrating on familiar paths rather than discovering alternative solutions; and across the benchmark, scores on held-out task families stall even as acceptance on training fixtures climbs. Because a drop in distinct action sequences can simply mean the agent is cutting out unneeded retries, engineers read action diversity alongside held-out transfer scores: when shrinking diversity coincides with flat held-out performance, the policy is over-specializing.

↩︎ pass@${k}$, the probability that at least one of ${k}$ sampled candidates passes, is established in **[[01 Books/Volume III - Agentic Systems/01 The Model/03 Test-Time Compute/V3-03.04 Selecting from a Candidate Pool#^sec-vol3-test-time-compute-pass-at-k|Candidate availability under pass@k]]**.

Countering this narrowing requires bounding how heavily any single fixture or synthetic round can shape the dataset. At the fixture level, per-task retention caps keep easily solved problems from flooding the corpus, and deduplication filters drop identical normalized action sequences unless they occurred under genuinely different observations. Across retraining rounds, the corpus pairs synthetic runs with a permanent anchor of verified external demonstrations (such as curated human traces) that self-play cannot overwrite. Early-stopping rules halt the loop as soon as action diversity and held-out scores plateau, even if training-fixture acceptance keeps rising.

External demonstrations and held-out evaluation suites play distinct roles here. Curated human or expert demonstrations anchor the training mixture because they come from outside the current policy’s habits. Protected evaluation tasks, by contrast, stay outside both training and automated selection under the development and final-test split discipline of [[01 Books/Volume III - Agentic Systems/05 Learning from Trajectories/15 Trajectory Curation/V3-15.05 Split Design#^sec-vol3-flywheel-evaluation|section 15.4]]. Because each promotion step in a self-improvement loop consults a holdout score, the benchmark that gates round-by-round promotion acts as a development set over multiple rounds, and a separate final-test suite must verify any final generalization claim. Periodic human audits across accepted, rejected, and borderline runs catch grader shortcuts and missed edge cases before promotion, while production telemetry supplies fresh task candidates from live traffic.

### Production Feedback Signals

^sec-vol3-trajectory-curation-production-feedback

Production sessions expose edge cases that offline fixtures never anticipated, but treating raw user actions as ground-truth labels corrupts a corpus. A user who accepts a patch without editing it may never have run the tests, and a user who abandons a session may have been interrupted. Extracting training signals from live telemetry requires treating each observed event as evidence of the user’s immediate action rather than a verdict on task correctness ([[01 Books/Volume III - Agentic Systems/05 Learning from Trajectories/15 Trajectory Curation/V3-15.08 The Self-Improvement Loop#^tbl-vol3-trajectory-curation-feedback-signals|table 11]]).

| **Signal**             | **Evidence Provided**                       | **Limitation**                                                             | **Possible Learning Use**                                                           |
|:-----------------------|:--------------------------------------------|:---------------------------------------------------------------------------|:------------------------------------------------------------------------------------|
| Accepted without edits | Recorded acceptance of the delivered output | The user may not have checked correctness                                  | Usability label under a declared criterion; candidate for task validation           |
| Edited or reverted     | A change to, or rejection of, the output    | May reflect correction, style, or changed requirements                     | Proposed target or comparison after checking the applicable task and context        |
| Mid-task correction    | New user guidance at a particular turn      | Does not establish the cause of the earlier behavior                       | Recovery context, intent clarification, or a candidate fixture                      |
| Retry or abandonment   | A retry or session-ending event             | Latency, cost, interruptions, and changed plans can produce the same event | Investigation or a label for the observed event, not assumed task failure           |
| Explicit rating        | The respondent’s recorded assessment        | Missing responses and response selection limit population claims           | Label for a defined satisfaction criterion, not automatic proof of task correctness |

Table 11: **Production Feedback Signals**: Preserve the observed event and its context before assigning a learning role. A signal may directly label its declared criterion without establishing unrelated properties.

^tbl-vol3-trajectory-curation-feedback-signals

Misreading these signals distorts the training set. When a user edits an agent’s output to add a new requirement, the edited text answers the revised request rather than the original prompt; training on that edited output without updating the prompt teaches the model to guess unstated constraints. Likewise, pairing an agent’s draft with a user’s edit as a preference comparison is valid only when the context and task goal stay identical across both versions. Telemetry pipelines therefore treat missing feedback as unobserved rather than positive, check user consent and data-use permissions before ingestion, and turn recurring production failures into graded regression fixtures before feeding them into training. Together with retention caps and external anchors, these safeguards turn self-improvement into a concrete collection plan with explicit sampling budgets, automated verification stages, and human review gates. Because rollout generation scales out easily across accelerators while sandboxed verification and human review have tight throughput ceilings, the collection pipeline must pace sampling against downstream checking capacity so unverified traces never swamp staging storage.
