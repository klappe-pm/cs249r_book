# V4-02.09 Which Physical Budget Binds First

[[Home|Home]] · [[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/Volume 4 Overview|Volume Overview]] · [[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/01 Machine Anatomy/02 The Physical Body/V4-02 The Physical Body|V4-02 The Physical Body]]

^sec-body-archetype-matrix

A single command from an action-chunking policy to accelerate a loaded mobile manipulator draws concurrently against all five physical budgets, which is why the simultaneous admission invariant of [[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/01 Machine Anatomy/02 The Physical Body/V4-02.02 The Five Physical Budgets#^sec-body-five-budgets|section 2.1]] admits it only when every margin is non-negative at once. The command executes against a camera frame that has already aged during neural inference, so that age enters the pre-brake delay. At the same time, the acceleration injects kinetic energy into the chassis, which friction brakes or regenerative drives must eventually dissipate. Generating that motion forces the arm’s geared joints to accelerate reflected rotor inertia, while the base’s direct-drive hubs add their rotating inertia to the stopping load. The phase currents that produce those torques dump resistive Joule heat into stator windings, which shed thermal energy only over minutes. The same currents pull transient current from the shared DC rail that powers the onboard compute host. Each of the five budgets pairs a governing physical law against the software assumption it invalidates, exposing a characteristic vulnerability by machine class and demanding a hardware-enforced runtime invariant ([[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/01 Machine Anatomy/02 The Physical Body/V4-02.09 Which Physical Budget Binds First#^tbl-02-cross-archetype-matrix|table 4]]).

### Which Budget Binds in Each Machine Class

^sec-body-binding-constraints

A mobile manipulator integrates two of the machine classes from **[[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/00 Introduction/01 The Causal Boundary/V4-01.06 Three Machine Classes#^sec-boundary-three-classes|Three Machine Classes]]** onto a single chassis: a Class 1 wheeled base governed primarily by chassis momentum and stopping distance, and a Class 2 articulated arm constrained by reflected rotor inertia, contact compliance, and static holding heat. While the classes identify the primary vulnerabilities, the instantaneous maneuver determines which budget binds first. For instance, holding a heavy payload in a cantilevered pose can drive joint windings toward their thermal dissipation limit over tens of seconds. Conversely, a sudden multi-axis acceleration burst coupled with full neural network execution can deplete electrical bus margins within milliseconds. The coupled calculations below compare the evidence behind the running machine’s limits and identify a stopping qualification priority. They do not establish an ordering of all five physical margins for an unspecified maneuver.

### Cross-Budget Coupling and Permission Rail Isolation

^sec-body-permission-rail-isolation

When an autonomous agent commands an aggressive multi-axis acceleration, the physical margins do not degrade in isolation. They deplete together. Drive inverters draw substantial current to overcome both chassis inertia and reflected rotor inertia. This transient current surge causes the shared DC bus voltage to sag, even as resistive losses (${P_{\text{loss}}} = {i_q}^2 {R_{\text{eff}}}$) steadily deposit thermal energy into motor windings. Because motor phase currents are pulse-width modulated by inverters and do not map directly to battery draw, systems architects must evaluate input current at the primary DC bus. This coincident load step establishes the true operating baseline across all subsystems.

On the 48 V drive bus, the sustained electrical screen passes under its stated assumptions. As shown in the coincident power step of notebook [2.7](https://mlsysbook.ai/vol4/02_body/02_body.html#nbk-body-dc-bus-power-integrity), a depleted battery pack at 42 V sags to 37.8 V under the coincident load step. Even under that sustained step, the rail keeps a 4.8 V buffer above the 33 V undervoltage lockout (UVLO) threshold of the 48-to-24 V converter, although the sustained screen does not establish the transient minimum.

The stopping dynamics expose a concrete qualification priority. Under emergency braking, the drive motors clear the required deceleration by only 16.6 percent, and only with phase current at the motor’s catalog peak. The chassis tip-over boundary has not been measured at all. For the arm, the optimistic stand-in thermal model gives a heat-soaked joint a threefold current burst of 43.1 s before its winding reaches the insulation rating. Additionally, the chassis stopping distance at the full 1.5 m/s travel limit leaves 357.5 mm of clearance to accommodate perception latency, communication jitter, and tracking variance. The whole stopping budget inherits the credible-deceleration premise, which rests on the peak current limit and the unmeasured center of gravity, so that premise requires qualification before relying on the stop. The transient rail minimum and installed arm thermal response remain unresolved too. Ranking the budgets requires a specified maneuver and duration, with comparably qualified limits for each.

Modern SoC architectures deploy dynamic voltage and frequency scaling (DVFS) algorithms to regulate die temperature and energy consumption. While DVFS protects silicon from thermal degradation, its timing penalties propagate directly into physical workspace clearance:

$$
\text{SoC thermal dissipation} \longrightarrow \text{DVFS clock throttling} \longrightarrow \text{vision latency stretch} \longrightarrow \text{observation age inflation} \longrightarrow \text{pre-brake travel expansion} \longrightarrow \text{clearance margin erosion}
$$

 When sustained motor currents and high-throughput perception pipelines elevate temperatures across the shared enclosure, the host kernel throttles the clock frequencies of onboard CPU and GPU accelerators. On the running machine, this thermal intervention stretches the navigation backbone that the stopping budget charges, not the grasp path of [[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/01 Machine Anatomy/02 The Physical Body/V4-02.03 Measurement Freshness#^tbl-02-latency-breakdown|table 1]], from 22 ms to 33 ms. This delay adds 11 ms directly to the end-to-end observation age ${t_{\text{age}}}$, inflating the pre-braking reaction time ${\tau_{\text{delay}}}$. When traveling at the maximum base velocity of 1.5 m/s, this algorithmic latency injects 16.5 mm of unmonitored chassis travel before emergency brakes can even begin mechanical engagement. In this manner, a standard operating-system power policy designed for digital silicon silently shrinks the robot’s stopping margin.

↪ Thermal clock throttling on the shared die, and the placement decisions that bound its effect on the safety path, are developed in **[[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/03 Running the Machine/13 Workload Placement/V4-13.05 Thermal and Timing Coupling#^sec-placement-thermal-timing-coupling|Thermal and Timing Coupling]]**.

Surviving a sustained bus voltage drop does not qualify the shared power rail to support safety-critical control channels. Transient dips during motor reversals remain difficult to bound completely, and electrical faults such as an open circuit breaker, a battery management system (BMS) overcurrent disconnect, or a blown DC-DC stage drop bus voltage to zero instantly. The running machine addresses these common supply faults with a **dual-rail architecture** that decouples motor dynamics from safety enforcement. In this design, the primary shared bus powers high-current traction inverters and steps down voltage for the primary application processor. An isolated, reverse-blocked permission rail separately powers the safety microcontroller, deterministic fieldbus transceivers, and holding-brake release coils. Under the drive-rail faults covered by its isolation and hold-up qualification, this separate supply preserves the permission loads. It does not make the detector, controller, or communication path infallible.

The permission rail incorporates local capacitive energy storage to preserve control availability during a loss of its upstream supply. For the stated constant 70 W permission load, ideal energy sizing gives an onboard 2.025 F supercapacitor bank 140 J of usable energy between its minimum nominal charge voltage (21.5 V) and regulator dropout (18 V), targeting 2 s of hold-up. Establishing a minimum available duration requires qualification of initial charge, capacitance tolerance and aging, conversion losses, equivalent series resistance, current demand, and regulator behavior. The 750 ms stop from the drive limit plus the 80 ms slow end of spring-brake engagement is a comparison for the powered stopping model only. Total main-battery failure does not establish that this model remains available. The qualified control interval must cover detection and decision, the duration of the response available under the fault, and brake engagement when those stages are sequential, or their actual sequence when they overlap. **[[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/03 Running the Machine/12 Safety Enforcement/V4-12.10 The Fallback Ladder#^sec-enforcement-fallback-ladder|The Fallback Ladder]]** checks the hold-up against the longest stop its fallback ladder commands.

Under the qualified rail and intact detector and controller assumptions, maintaining permission-rail power preserves the opportunity to detect power loss and initiate a response, but it does not supply the mechanical energy or high-current paths necessary for controlled regenerative braking. A controlled, powered stop requires intact inverter switching, sufficient bus voltage, and an operational energy dump, such as a dynamic brake chopper or a regenerative battery sink, to absorb kinetic deceleration energy. When a catastrophic power fault removes the supply or absorption path needed for a powered stop, that stopping model no longer applies. Under such conditions, stopping distances must be governed by the unpowered physical fallbacks analyzed in [[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/01 Machine Anatomy/02 The Physical Body/V4-02.07 Electrical Power Integrity#^sec-body-unpowered-states|section 2.6.2]]. A held-up permission rail does not change what the mechanical brakes deliver. Powering their release coils from that rail lets the permission logic choose when the spring brakes engage, but it does not give them the deceleration of a powered drive stop. Systems architects must treat stopping performance as conditional: isolate the safety supervisory rail, and then characterize the exact deceleration capabilities that remain active across every distinct electrical fault state. Downstream runtime enforcement and hardware qualification must preserve these underlying electrical premises.
