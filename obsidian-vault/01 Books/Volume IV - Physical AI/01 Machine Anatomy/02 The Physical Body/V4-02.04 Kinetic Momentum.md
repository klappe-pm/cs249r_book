# V4-02.04 Kinetic Momentum

[[Home|Home]] · [[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/Volume 4 Overview|Volume Overview]] · [[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/01 Machine Anatomy/02 The Physical Body/V4-02 The Physical Body|V4-02 The Physical Body]]

^sec-body-kinetic-momentum

When the loaded mobile manipulator drives down a warehouse aisle at its 1.5 m/s drive limit and a pedestrian steps out from behind a rack 1.10 m ahead, the capture-to-onset age from [[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/01 Machine Anatomy/02 The Physical Body/V4-02.03 Measurement Freshness#^sec-body-measurement-freshness|section 2.2]] becomes unguided travel toward the pedestrian. Even after the brake command arrives, halting the base is not instantaneous: the moving chassis carries linear momentum (${p} = {m} {v}$) and stores kinetic energy (${E_k} = \frac{1}{2}{m} {v}^2$) that must be dissipated across real space through mechanical work (${W} = \int {F} \, d{x}$). Stopping distance grows linearly with pre-brake delay but quadratically with velocity, so above the crossover speed of the elementary stopping budget, the braking term rather than the delay dominates the stop.

### Kinematic Stopping Distance and Pre-Brake Delay

^sec-body-stopping-distance-prebrake

As in the elementary stopping budget, a stop divides into two phases, pre-brake delay and active braking. During the pre-brake delay ${\tau_{\text{delay}}}$, the vehicle travels forward without any retarding force. This latency accounts for the age of the sensory observation when perception hands it downstream, followed by the communication transit, safety validation, and actuator-onset intervals required before mechanical braking begins. It excludes the dispatch-to-admission interval, in which the proposer and the permission path turn that observation into an admitted command, because **[[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/03 Running the Machine/12 Safety Enforcement/V4-12.04 Stopping Envelopes#^sec-enforcement-composite-budget|Sizing the composite stopping budget]]** charges that interval separately. An independent protective response need not wait for a motion planning proposal; safety enforcement can trigger immediately along a fast-path monitor. The safety budget must trace whichever concrete execution path initiates braking. Furthermore, if an obstacle can enter the path between sensing cycles, the budget must include an allowance for camera exposure and sampling intervals. The budget conservatively credits zero retarding force during this total pre-brake delay and assumes approximately constant forward speed ${v}$, which gives the blind travel distance

$$
{d_{\text{blind}}} = {v}\,{\tau_{\text{delay}}}
$$

↩︎ The two-phase stopping budget is established in **[[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/00 Introduction/01 The Causal Boundary/V4-01.05 Defining the Causal Boundary#^sec-boundary-kinematic-roofline|Delay consumes stopping clearance]]**.

Once the selected braking mechanism engages, the system enters the active braking phase. Mechanical work done by the retarding braking force ${F_{\text{brake}}}$ over the physical braking distance ${d_{\text{brake}}}$ must dissipate the machine’s stored kinetic energy, ${W} = {\Delta} {E_k}$. How that retarding force arises depends on the physical hardware. A powered motor stop requires functioning drive inverters, sufficient bus-voltage headroom, and a circuit capable of absorbing or dissipating returned electrical power. A mechanical brake, by contrast, operates independently of electrical power but carries distinct engagement latencies and thermal dissipation limits. In any safety certification, the credible deceleration must reflect the specific mechanism available during the fault scenario under analysis.

In an embodied machine, stored kinetic energy includes rotating powertrain components as well as the translating chassis. A component spinning at angular velocity ${\omega}$ stores rotational energy $\frac{1}{2} {J} {\omega}^2$, where its moment of inertia ${J}$ (in $\text{kg}\cdot\text{m}^2$) measures resistance to rotational acceleration. It plays the same role that mass plays in translational energy $\frac{1}{2} {m} {v}^2$. A transmission with **gear ratio** ${N_{\text{gear}}}$ turns the motor shaft ${N_{\text{gear}}}$ times for every revolution of the wheel, multiplying motor torque by ${N_{\text{gear}}}$ while dividing speed by ${N_{\text{gear}}}$. The machine’s total kinetic energy sums the translational energy of the chassis and payload (${E_{k,\text{trans}}} = \frac{1}{2} {m} {v}^2$) and the rotational energy of its wheels, gearboxes, and motor rotors (${E_{k,\text{rot}}} = \sum_i \frac{1}{2} {{J_{\text{rotor}}}}_i {{\omega_m}}_i^2$). Because a drive with gear ratio ${{N_{\text{gear}}}}_i$ spins its rotor at angular speed ${{\omega_m}}_i = {{N_{\text{gear}}}}_i \frac{{v}}{{r}}$, the rotor’s rotational kinetic energy reflects into the translational frame scaled by the square of the gear ratio:

$$
{E_{k,\text{total}}} = \frac{1}{2} {m} {v}^2 + \sum_i \frac{1}{2} {{J_{\text{rotor}}}}_i \left({{N_{\text{gear}}}}_i \frac{{v}}{{r}}\right)^2 = \frac{1}{2} \left({m} + \sum_i \frac{{{N_{\text{gear}}}}_i^2 {{J_{\text{rotor}}}}_i}{{r}^2}\right) {v}^2
$$

 This formulation reveals that the effective translational inertia of the machine is ${m_{\text{eff}}} = {m} + \sum_i \frac{{{N_{\text{gear}}}}_i^2 {{J_{\text{rotor}}}}_i}{{r}^2}$. For a platform decelerating to rest under sustained retarding force ${F_{\text{brake}}} = {m_{\text{eff}}} {a_{\text{brake}}}$, the work-energy theorem balances mechanical work against total kinetic energy:

$$
{m_{\text{eff}}} {a_{\text{brake}}} {d_{\text{brake}}} = \frac{1}{2} {m_{\text{eff}}} {v}^2
$$

 Dividing both sides by effective mass yields the kinematic braking travel:

$$
{d_{\text{brake}}} = \frac{{v}^2}{2 {a_{\text{brake}}}}
$$

 If an engineer evaluates braking capacity using bare chassis mass alone (${a_{\text{nominal}}} = {F_{\text{brake}}} / {m}$), they omit the kinetic energy stored in the spinning motor rotors. The result overestimates deceleration by the factor ${m_{\text{eff}}} / {m}$ and underestimates stopping travel by $\frac{{m_{\text{eff}}} - {m}}{{m}} = \sum_i \frac{{{N_{\text{gear}}}}_i^2 {{J_{\text{rotor}}}}_i}{{m} {r}^2}$. On the running machine, this correction remains modest. Its two driven wheels use direct-drive hub motors (${{N_{\text{gear}}}}_i = 1$), and because no portion of a hub motor lies outside its tire circumference, each rotor term ${{J_{\text{rotor}}}}_i/{r}^2$ cannot exceed the motor’s 9.5 kg total mass. The rotating parts add at most 5.2 percent to the 368 kg loaded mass. However, because the inertia reflection scales with ${{N_{\text{gear}}}}_i^2$, this rotational overhead becomes substantial on lightweight robot platforms driven through high-ratio planetary or harmonic gearboxes.

The parameter ${a_{\text{brake}}}$ is the credible sustained deceleration the physical plant can reliably exert against the environment, not an idealized rating copied from an actuator datasheet. The retarding force ${F_{\text{brake}}} = {m_{\text{eff}}} {a_{\text{brake}}}$ must fit beneath three independent physical ceilings. First, tire-floor traction imposes a strict friction ceiling. Only the braked wheels transmit retarding shear to the ground, and friction there can reach at most ${\mu}$ times the normal force they support, ${\phi_{\text{drive}}} {m} {g}$. This caps deceleration at ${a_{\text{brake}}} \le {\mu} {g} {\phi_{\text{drive}}}$, where ${\mu}$ is the tire-floor friction coefficient and ${\phi_{\text{drive}}}$ is the fraction of total vehicle weight carried by the driven wheels. Second, vehicle pitch stability limits deceleration through dynamic load transfer. While braking force acts at the floor contact patch, inertial force acts forward through the center of gravity at height ${h_{\text{cg}}}$ above it. Taking moments about the leading ground support, a horizontal distance ${d_{\text{lead}}}$ ahead of the center of gravity, the overturning inertial moment ${m} {a_{\text{brake}}} {h_{\text{cg}}}$ opposes the restoring gravitational moment ${m} {g} {d_{\text{lead}}}$. The platform stays on all wheels only so long as ${a_{\text{brake}}} \le {g}\,{d_{\text{lead}}}/{h_{\text{cg}}}$. Raising or extending a robotic arm alters both ${h_{\text{cg}}}$ and ${d_{\text{lead}}}$ in real time, making this pitch-stability boundary state-dependent. Third, the electromechanical actuators limit retarding force through the maximum torque their inverter current limit allows, a ceiling that [[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/01 Machine Anatomy/02 The Physical Body/V4-02.05 Actuator Transmission Limits#^sec-body-electrical-time-constants|section 2.4.2]] derives from the motor torque constant. If an inverter restricts phase current, or if the back-EMF of the spinning rotor leaves the inverter too little headroom to control current, the drive cannot deliver the required braking torque. Deceleration ${a_{\text{brake}}}$ degrades silently, expanding braking travel without software notification. The rotor’s rotational inertia is already counted in the effective mass ${m_{\text{eff}}}$ and must not be charged a second time. These three ceilings act simultaneously on the loaded chassis ([[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/01 Machine Anatomy/02 The Physical Body/V4-02.04 Kinetic Momentum#^fig-02-braking-fbd|figure 1]]), and notebook [2.3](https://mlsysbook.ai/vol4/02_body/02_body.html#nbk-body-braking-drive-limits) checks them against the running machine’s numbers once the next section derives the drive’s torque ceiling.

The constant-deceleration model requires a level surface and a qualified braking response. A conservative implementation charges the transient interval required to develop full retarding force into ${\tau_{\text{delay}}}$, then applies a certified lower bound ${a_{\text{brake}}}$. The actuator brake-onset allowance (${T_{\text{act}}}$) enters that delay once. Any subsequent calculation that models a finite torque ramp must reconcile its ramp travel with this allowance rather than add the same interval twice. Furthermore, empirical stopping trials across declared operating conditions establish the credible deceleration, its distribution, and its safety margin. A sample percentile alone does not constitute a certified lower bound.

In unstructured environments, an autonomous machine cannot allocate its entire clearance to nominal stopping dynamics. Obstacle tracking carries state estimation uncertainty, sensor calibration drifts over time, and localization algorithms introduce position error. The stopping distance ${d_{\text{stop}}}$ that the system defends must therefore include a spatial localization uncertainty bound ${\delta_{\text{loc}}}$ and a minimum physical clearance margin ${\delta_{\text{margin}}}$:[[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/01 Machine Anatomy/02 The Physical Body/V4-02.14 Footnotes#^fn6|⁶]]

$$
{d_{\text{stop}}} = {v}\,{\tau_{\text{delay}}} + \frac{{v}^2}{2 {a_{\text{brake}}}} + {\delta_{\text{loc}}} + {\delta_{\text{margin}}} \tag{2}
$$

 The sum ${\delta_{\text{loc}}} + {\delta_{\text{margin}}}$ is fixed spatial overhead that must remain unoccupied when the body comes to a complete rest.

^eq-body-stopping-distance

↪ The localization bound ${\delta_{\text{loc}}}$ and its dependence on map corrections are developed in **[[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/03 Running the Machine/09 Spatial Memory/V4-09.06 Coordinate Transform Expiry and Localization Drift#^sec-memory-localization-slam|Localization drift and map frames that jump]]**.

**Definition 2.2: Dynamic stopping distance**

***Dynamic stopping distance*** (${d_{\text{stop}}}$) is the minimum physical clearance required to bring a moving mass to a complete halt from its speed ${v}$ when the hazard is first imaged. It sums three parts. The first is the travel during the pre-brake delay ${\tau_{\text{delay}}}$, which spans the observation age of the frame that shows the hazard and the lease, tick, bus, and brake-onset intervals that follow until retarding force begins (the decomposition of **[[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/00 Introduction/01 The Causal Boundary/V4-01.05 Defining the Causal Boundary#^sec-boundary-kinematic-roofline|Delay consumes stopping clearance]]**), and which excludes the separately charged dispatch-to-admission interval. The second is the braking travel under the credible deceleration ${a_{\text{brake}}}$. The last is the localization bound ${\delta_{\text{loc}}}$ and protective clearance ${\delta_{\text{margin}}}$, as [[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/01 Machine Anatomy/02 The Physical Body/V4-02.04 Kinetic Momentum#^eq-body-stopping-distance|equation 2]] states.

1.  **Significance**: Quantifies the non-negotiable physical spatial margin that software proposals must clear before motion permission can be granted.
2.  **Distinction**: Unlike static geometric obstacle clearance in classical kinematics, dynamic stopping distance depends on the machine’s own speed and on every delay before braking, so each velocity setpoint and each delay term changes it.
3.  **Common pitfall**: Assuming an emergency stop command halts the machine instantaneously, ignoring that moving mass carries kinetic energy (${E_k} = \frac{1}{2}{m} {v}^2$) that requires finite mechanical work and braking distance to dissipate.

Three cases on the running machine show how the terms trade against one another ([[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/01 Machine Anatomy/02 The Physical Body/V4-02.04 Kinetic Momentum#^fig-02-stopping-distance|figure 2]]). With only the terms the body owns, the stop needs 742.5 mm. Charging the full 133.6 ms pre-brake delay of the elementary stopping budget (**[[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/00 Introduction/01 The Causal Boundary/V4-01.05 Defining the Causal Boundary#^sec-boundary-kinematic-roofline|Delay consumes stopping clearance]]**), whose terms later chapters measure one by one, gives that elementary stop plus the 150 mm fixed overhead, or 912.9 mm. That stop still fits the 1.10 m clear distance. Raising speed above the drive limit with that same delay pushes the stop to 1,417.2 mm, past the rack end, because speed enters the blind travel linearly and the braking travel quadratically. Notebook [2.2](https://mlsysbook.ai/vol4/02_body/02_body.html#nbk-body-stopping-distance-budgeting-high-tail-compute) works the first case term by term.

### Dynamic Velocity Ceilings and Clearance Budgeting

^sec-body-dynamic-velocity-ceilings

Because braking travel grows quadratically with speed while blind travel scales linearly with delay ([[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/01 Machine Anatomy/02 The Physical Body/V4-02.04 Kinetic Momentum#^fig-02-stopping-distance|figure 2]]), autonomous systems cannot rely on static, scene-independent speed limits. They require a dynamic velocity ceiling parameterized by instantaneous obstacle clearance. For a stationary obstacle detected at distance ${D_{\text{clear}}}$, such as a person standing at an aisle intersection or a dropped storage tote, safe operation demands that defended stopping distance remain strictly within the available clear path: ${d_{\text{stop}}} \le {D_{\text{clear}}}$. This constraint is how the first law, irreversibility (principle [[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/01 Machine Anatomy/V4 The Machine Anatomy#^pri-vol4-irreversibility|1]]), binds every motion cycle, with each parameter in ${d_{\text{stop}}}$ grounded in the machine’s verified physical limits. Using conservative bounds for actuation delay and retarding acceleration, the permission path solves this inequality to determine the maximum permissible command velocity ${v_{\max}}$. The symbol is the one that [[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/01 Machine Anatomy/02 The Physical Body/V4-02.03 Measurement Freshness#^eq-body-freshness-deadline|equation 1]] used for the speed of a tracked target, but here it bounds the machine’s own speed. Taking the positive physical root of the resulting quadratic relation[[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/01 Machine Anatomy/02 The Physical Body/V4-02.14 Footnotes#^fn7|⁷]] yields:

$$
{v_{\max}} = -{a_{\text{brake}}} {\tau_{\text{delay}}} + \sqrt{({a_{\text{brake}}} {\tau_{\text{delay}}})^2 + 2 {a_{\text{brake}}} ({D_{\text{clear}}} - {\delta_{\text{loc}}} - {\delta_{\text{margin}}})} \tag{3}
$$

 Whenever perceptual range degrades or an obstacle obstructs the trajectory, ${D_{\text{clear}}}$ contracts, continuously lowering the velocity ceiling. When available distance collapses to zero, the allowable forward velocity becomes zero. If the machine is already in motion, however, issuing a zero-velocity setpoint cannot instantaneously eliminate its accumulated kinetic energy. A negative available distance signals that the platform has already breached safe stopping margins, demanding immediate, full-authority emergency braking.

^eq-body-velocity-ceiling

**Napkin Math 2.2: The body's stopping distance**

**Problem**: The loaded warehouse mobile manipulator drives toward a rack end at its drive limit, and a person stands in the aisle at the clear distance. The body owns three terms of [[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/01 Machine Anatomy/02 The Physical Body/V4-02.04 Kinetic Momentum#^eq-body-stopping-distance|equation 2]]: braking travel, the fixed overhead, and the brake onset that enters ${\tau_{\text{delay}}}$. How much of the clear distance do these terms use, what speed would they alone permit, and how do speed and added delay change the answer?

**Variables**:

- Loaded mass at most 368 kg (derived upper bound, full tote rack) and drive limit ${v} =$ 1.5 m/s (illustrative)
- Clear distance ${D_{\text{clear}}} =$ 1.10 m (illustrative)
- Credible deceleration ${a_{\text{brake}}} =$ 2 m/s² (illustrative; loaded, dry floor)
- Localization bound ${\delta_{\text{loc}}} =$ 50 mm (illustrative) and protective clearance ${\delta_{\text{margin}}} =$ 100 mm (chosen)
- Brake onset ${T_{\text{act}}} =$ 20 ms (illustrative)

**Math**:

**1. Braking travel:** For the same credible deceleration, mass cancels from the work-energy balance. The loaded machine’s 414 J sets the energy the stopping system must absorb or dissipate, while the specified deceleration sets the braking distance. A different payload can change that deceleration and requires a new bound:

$$
d_{\text{brake}} = \frac{v^2}{2 a_{\text{brake}}} = \frac{(1.5 m/s)^2}{2 \times 2 m/s²} = 562.5 mm
$$

 The stopping distance so far is 562.5 mm.

**2. Fixed overhead:**

$$
\delta_{\text{loc}} + \delta_{\text{margin}} = 50 mm + 100 mm = 150 mm
$$

 The running total rises to 712.5 mm.

**3. Brake onset:** Between the brake command and the first retarding force, the base still moves at full speed:

$$
v\,T_{\text{act}} = (1.5 m/s) \times (20 ms) = 30 mm
$$

$$
d_{\text{stop}}\big|_{\text{body}} = 562.5 mm + 150 mm + 30 mm = 742.5 mm
$$

 The body’s own terms leave 357.5 mm of the 1.10 m clear distance for every delay the body does not own.

**4. The body-only ceiling:** Solving [[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/01 Machine Anatomy/02 The Physical Body/V4-02.04 Kinetic Momentum#^eq-body-stopping-distance|equation 2]] for speed with ${\tau_{\text{delay}}} = {T_{\text{act}}}$:

$$
v_{\max} = -a_{\text{brake}} T_{\text{act}} + \sqrt{(a_{\text{brake}} T_{\text{act}})^2 + 2 a_{\text{brake}} (D_{\text{clear}} - \delta_{\text{loc}} - \delta_{\text{margin}})} \approx 1.91 m/s
$$

 The body alone would permit 1.91 m/s, above the drive limit. The remaining delay terms, added in later chapters, spend part of this headroom.

**5. Speed vs. delay:** At a hypothetical 2 m/s, above the drive limit, the braking term alone grows to 1,000 mm, 77.8 percent more than at the drive limit. At the drive limit, each additional 100 ms of pre-brake delay adds 150 mm.

**Systems insight**: Platform velocity is the most sensitive variable in the stopping budget. Accelerating from 1.5 m/s to 2 m/s adds 437.5 mm in braking travel, as much distance as 291.7 ms of additional pre-brake delay at the drive limit. A velocity setpoint may therefore rise only after re-evaluating the clearance quadratic. Because physical braking distance scales with ${v}^2$, granting an aggressive velocity setpoint expands the defended stopping envelope and rapidly exhausts the clear distance margin defended by ${d_{\text{stop}}} \le {D_{\text{clear}}}$.

↪ The chunk lease, the time an admitted trajectory segment may keep executing without a replacement, is one of the delay terms that spend this headroom. Its sizing from this stopping distance is developed in **[[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/01 Machine Anatomy/04 The Nervous System/V4-04.05 Chunk Lease Sizing from the Stopping Budget#^sec-nervous-lease-sizing|Chunk Lease Sizing from the Stopping Budget]]**.

### Applying the Velocity Ceiling

^sec-body-velocity-governor-cbf

A dynamic speed ceiling protects the machine only when an independent check on the permission path tests each new velocity request against it before execution. The learned policy proposes an action. The safety check evaluates clearance, delay, and available deceleration to determine whether that request fits the stopping budget. Requesting a lower speed alters future trajectory points but cannot erase the distance required to stop from the current speed. Consequently, the check must evaluate the platform’s instantaneous state alongside candidate commands, keeping the machine inside the region from which it can still stop between control ticks under strict bounds on timing jitter, obstacle motion, and actuator response. That per-tick validation belongs to the permission path; **[[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/03 Running the Machine/12 Safety Enforcement/V4-12.04 Stopping Envelopes#^sec-enforcement-phase-portraits|Phase portraits and speed ceilings]]** formalizes it around the jerk-limited deceleration profiles executed on the physical platform. What the physical body contributes is the ceiling itself: a clearance-dependent boundary grounded in physical constraints that drops whenever traction degrades, actuator response slows, or localization uncertainty expands.

Enforcing this speed limit assumes the permission path can demand the deceleration ${a_{\text{brake}}}$ whenever safety requires it. Commanding rapid deceleration, however, requires sudden, large mechanical torque from the drive motors and gearboxes. If the permission path requests a torque step that exceeds what the actuator bandwidth budget allows, the mechanical body cannot deliver the required deceleration, invalidating the model-based stopping ceiling and risking collision. The same drives also shape what happens if the stop falls short. The reflected rotor energy counted in ${m_{\text{eff}}}$ then reaches the obstacle and the gear teeth through the transmission’s compliance, so the stopping calculation cannot substitute for a contact-load assessment.
