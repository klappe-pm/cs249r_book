# V4-02.05 Actuator Transmission Limits

[[Home|Home]] · [[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/Volume 4 Overview|Volume Overview]] · [[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/01 Machine Anatomy/02 The Physical Body/V4-02 The Physical Body|V4-02 The Physical Body]]

^sec-body-actuator-limits

When a policy writes a 50 N·m torque setpoint to a joint register or dispatches an emergency brake command over a Controller Area Network (CAN) bus or EtherCAT, the drive electronics may clamp the current at half that request while returning a clean acknowledgement packet in sub-millisecond time. In digital software, an execution target either accepts an instruction or raises a fault; in physical hardware, stator winding inductance, DC bus voltage headroom, magnetic saturation, and transmission elasticity bound what the joint actually delivers. The systems engineering challenge is to specify an actuator contract at the physical plant interface, written in delivered force, torque, slew-rate limits, response latency, and tracking error rather than digital setpoints, and to expose those limits directly to the runtime permission path before an unachievable command causes a collision.

### Actuation Mechanisms and the Actuator Contract

^sec-body-actuation-modalities

What that actuator contract must bound depends on how the joint converts stored energy into mechanical force. Electric drives dominate mobile manipulation and power the running machine of this book, where current in copper windings pulls on rotor magnets through a gear train. Other robots rely on pressurized fluid or calibrated mechanical springs, and each mechanism trades peak force density against bandwidth, compliance, and maintenance overhead.

![[90 Assets/Volume IV/02 Body/Spa Locator A caa6ae82a2.svg]]

*S·P·A locator triad with the Act node highlighted.*

*On the sense-plan-act (S·P·A) triad (**[[05 Reference/Volume IV - Physical AI/V4 The Sense–Plan–Act Paradigm#^sec-appendix-spa|The Sense–Plan–Act Paradigm]]**), the actuator contract sits at the Act node, where a command must become delivered force.*

Across the broader field, three non-rigid or fluid alternatives illustrate how the physical medium reshapes the contract:

- **Hydraulic servovalves**: For heavy machinery, construction robots, and high-load dynamic platforms, fluid power trades magnetic force for pressure. A cylinder’s force is supply pressure times piston area, ${F_{\text{cyl}}} = {p} {A}$, so raising the pressure lets a compact actuator deliver a large force. An electric motor’s force, by contrast, is bounded by the magnetic field its magnets and copper can sustain. The miniature servovalves on hydraulic legged robots respond fast, with bandwidths of hundreds of hertz ([[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/01 Machine Anatomy/02 The Physical Body/V4-02.13 Source References#^ref-scully2015hydraulic|Scully 2015]]), so the valve is rarely the slow element. Hydraulic control is bounded instead by fluid compressibility (oil bulk modulus ${\beta}$), nonlinear orifice flow, and the plumbing, pumps, and cooling needed to keep the fluid within its operating range. That maintenance burden is one reason Boston Dynamics retired its hydraulic Atlas humanoid in 2024 in favor of an all-electric successor ([[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/01 Machine Anatomy/02 The Physical Body/V4-02.13 Source References#^ref-bostondynamics2024electric|Boston Dynamics 2024]]).
- **Pneumatics and soft actuation**: Pneumatic artificial muscles and elastomeric chambers provide physical compliance that naturally cushions impacts. However, gas compressibility, valve flow restrictions, and acoustic transport delays limit closed-loop bandwidth to tens of hertz. Stored pneumatic energy can also discharge violently during rapid venting or line rupture. Safety validation requires qualifying the envelope of peak chamber pressure, flow rates, and measured contact dynamics.
- **Series elastic actuators (SEAs)**: By placing a calibrated spring directly between the gearbox and the output link (as in the ANYbotics ANYdrive), an SEA incorporates mechanical compliance into the drivetrain itself. The spring acts as a low-pass mechanical filter: it isolates delicate gear teeth from shock loads and decouples the large reflected rotor inertia (${N_{\text{gear}}}^2 {J_{\text{rotor}}}$) from the environment during impact. Its measured deflection also serves as a direct force sensor, since spring stiffness times deflection yields output torque ([[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/01 Machine Anatomy/02 The Physical Body/V4-02.13 Source References#^ref-pratt1995series|Pratt and Williamson 1995]]). Rather than dissipating energy, the spring stores elastic potential (${E} = \frac{1}{2} {k} {\Delta}{\theta_{\text{joint}}}^2$) at the joint and can return that energy into an obstacle. The engineering trade-off is reduced closed-loop position bandwidth and low-frequency structural resonance. The safety system and permission path must therefore monitor physical spring deflection directly instead of assuming rigid load tracking.

### Electrical Time Constants, Back-EMF, and Current Clamping

^sec-body-electrical-time-constants

The running machine’s wheels and arm joints are electric, so the electric drive is the plant whose limits the stopping budget depends on. To a policy, a torque command is a number written to a register; to the motor, it is a current that must be built up in a coil. Three results from introductory physics describe that conversion ([[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/01 Machine Anatomy/02 The Physical Body/V4-02.13 Source References#^ref-lynch2017modern|Lynch and Park 2017]]). First, a wire carrying current across a magnetic field feels a force proportional to the current. Wound around a rotor, those forces produce a torque proportional to current, ${\tau_{\text{motor}}} = {K_t} {I}$, where the **torque constant** ${K_t}$ (in $\text{N}\cdot\text{m/A}$) collects the magnet strength and the winding geometry. Second, the same wires, swept through the same field by the spinning rotor, have a voltage induced in them. A spinning motor is therefore also a generator, and its **back-EMF** (back electromotive force) ${e_{\text{bemf}}} = {K_e} {\omega_m}$ opposes the supply. Energy conservation ties the two constants together. The electrical power the motor converts, ${e_{\text{bemf}}}\,{I}$, equals the mechanical power it delivers, ${\tau_{\text{motor}}} {\omega_m}$, so ${K_e}$ and ${K_t}$ are the same number in SI units. A three-phase drive’s current and voltage conventions scale them by constant factors, which is why both must be identified under one convention. Third, the winding is a coil, and a coil opposes any change in its current, ${v} = {L_{\text{phase}}}\,d{I}/d{t}$ for inductance ${L_{\text{phase}}}$. Current, and therefore torque, cannot jump.

The budget needs two results from the motor controller: the torque delivered for a given current and the delay before that current appears. In a three-phase motor, field-oriented control (FOC) measures the winding currents and adjusts the inverter voltages so that the torque-producing current ${i_q}$ stays at right angles to the rotor’s magnetic field as the rotor turns. Torque is then ${\tau_{\text{motor}}} = {K_t} {i_q}$.[[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/01 Machine Anatomy/02 The Physical Body/V4-02.14 Footnotes#^fn8|⁸]] The controller splits the stator current into two components that turn with the rotor. The quadrature, or ${q}$-axis, current ${i_q}$ lies at right angles to the rotor field and produces torque. The direct, or ${d}$-axis, current ${i_d}$ lies along the field, produces none, and is normally held at zero. The coordinate transformations inside the drive appear in [[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/01 Machine Anatomy/02 The Physical Body/V4-02.05 Actuator Transmission Limits#^sec-body-drive-supporting-models|section 2.4.6]].

The delay between commanding a torque and delivering it begins in the stator windings. Adding the three effects around one winding circuit, the supply voltage must cover the resistive drop, the inductive voltage, and the back-EMF, which is Kirchhoff’s voltage law for the torque-producing current:

$$
{L_{\text{phase}}} \frac{d{i_q}({t})}{d{t}} + {R_{\text{phase}}} {i_q}({t}) = {v_q}({t}) - {e_{\text{bemf}}}({t})
$$

 where ${L_{\text{phase}}}$ is phase inductance, ${R_{\text{phase}}}$ is phase resistance, ${v_q}({t})$ is the applied quadrature voltage from the inverter bridge, ${i_q}({t})$ is the quadrature-axis (torque-producing) current, and ${e_{\text{bemf}}}({t}) = {K_e} {\omega_m}({t})$ is the back-EMF. The stator electrical time constant is:

$$
{\tau_e} = \frac{{L_{\text{phase}}}}{{R_{\text{phase}}}}
$$

If the inverter applied an uncontrolled voltage step, the current would approach its steady state exponentially along ${i_q}({t}) = {I_{\text{final}}}(1 - e^{-{t}/{\tau_e}})$, taking an entire time constant ${\tau_e}$ to reach 63 percent of the target. A closed-loop current controller drives the winding much faster. It applies full bus voltage until current reaches the setpoint, forcing a ramp rate of nearly $({V_{\text{bus}}} - {e_{\text{bemf}}})/{L_{\text{phase}}}$. At that point the current loop throttles the voltage to maintain regulation. On the running machine, this electrical rise is rapid. The hub motor windings (line inductance 1.885 mH, line resistance 0.7 Ω) yield ${\tau_e} =$ 2.7 ms. Starting from rest on a depleted 42 V pack, the initial current slope gives a resistance-free rise estimate of 1.1 ms, only 5.6 percent of the 20 ms brake onset budgeted for the platform. The resistive drop slows the ramp as current builds, so this estimate is optimistic and the full winding response must be measured. When braking from forward speed, the rotor continues spinning in the positive direction while the controller reverses the sign of the torque-producing current to oppose motion, so the positive back-EMF reinforces the inverter’s negative current drive, accelerates the current ramp further, and reduces the terminal voltage required to sustain peak braking current from ${e_{\text{bemf}}} + {I_{\max}} {R}$ to ${e_{\text{bemf}}} - {I_{\max}} {R}$. In this architecture, electrical rise time does not bottleneck emergency braking. Tire traction, maximum inverter current, and voltage headroom at high speed govern the stopping distance, as detailed in notebook [2.3](https://mlsysbook.ai/vol4/02_body/02_body.html#nbk-body-braking-drive-limits).

Torque tracks current directly, which means available voltage headroom also dictates the maximum torque slew rate:

$$
\frac{{d}{\tau_{\text{motor}}}}{d{t}} = {K_t} \frac{d{i_q}}{d{t}} \le \frac{{K_t} ({V_{\text{bus}}} - {K_e} {\omega_m})}{{L_{\text{phase}}}}
$$

 Inductance and voltage conventions must align consistently: line-to-line values reported on manufacturer datasheets pair with the full DC bus voltage. Per-phase values modeled under space-vector modulation scale by an amplitude of approximately ${V_{\text{bus}}}/\sqrt{3}$, the modulation voltage limit stated in **[[05 Reference/Volume IV - Physical AI/V4 Systems and Hardware#^sec-appendix-systems-actuators|Actuator Electrical and Thermal Dynamics]]**. At low wheel speeds, ample voltage headroom enables steep torque ramps. As rotational velocity climbs, back-EMF consumes that headroom, throttling the rate at which the inverter can inject current. An unconstrained policy setpoint requesting an instantaneous torque step is transformed by the motor hardware into a finite physical ramp; any demand exceeding this maximum slew rate produces severe tracking error. The safety stopping budget must account for this rate-limited response.

Voltage headroom bounds the rate of current rise; power electronics define the maximum current that can safely flow. Inverter bridges enforce a strict current ceiling ${I_{\max}}$ to protect the switching power transistors from thermal failure and prevent magnetic core saturation in the stator. For a motor coupled to a mechanical transmission with gear ratio ${N_{\text{gear}}}$ and mechanical efficiency ${\eta_{\text{gear}}}$, this ceiling caps joint output torque at:

$$
{\tau_{\text{out,max}}} = {\eta_{\text{gear}}} {N_{\text{gear}}} {K_t} {I_{\max}} \tag{4}
$$

^eq-body-torque-ceiling

This current limit settles a central safety requirement: whether the drives can sustain the deceleration demanded by the supervisory permission path. A motor drive may silently clamp current setpoints while continuing to acknowledge network commands; the safety path must interrogate measured output currents and status flags rather than inferring torque delivery from successful packet transmission. If emergency braking demands more torque than this ceiling allows, the assumed deceleration model breaks down. Any change to inverter current limits therefore invalidates the robot’s qualified stopping envelope and requires recomputing the stopping limit record ([[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/01 Machine Anatomy/02 The Physical Body/V4-02.08 Measuring a Machine’s Own Limits#^sec-body-measuring-limits|section 2.7]]).

**Napkin Math 2.3: Can the drives deliver the credible deceleration?**

The running machine brakes through two driven wheels, each a direct-drive 10-inch hub motor (the ZLTECH ZLLG10ASM800, with no gearbox, so ${N_{\text{gear}}} = 1$ and ${\eta_{\text{gear}}} \approx 1$) ([[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/01 Machine Anatomy/02 The Physical Body/V4-02.13 Source References#^ref-zltech2022zllg10asm800|Shenzhen Zhongling Technology 2022]]) with rolling radius ${r} =$ 133 mm. Its torque constant ${K_t} =$ 2.4 N·m/A is the datasheet’s peak torque divided by its peak current, and the drive’s current limit is set at that peak, ${I_{\max}} =$ 25 A. Casters carry the rest of the weight.

**Problem**: Does the loaded machine’s credible deceleration ${a_{\text{brake}}} =$ 2 m/s² fit under the traction, tip-over, and drive-torque ceilings, and does the drive keep control of its current at the 1.5 m/s drive limit?

**Math**:

1.  **Traction**: Only the driven wheels brake, so ${a_{\text{brake}}} \le {\mu} {g} {\phi_{\text{drive}}}$. With all the weight on them (${\phi_{\text{drive}}} = 1$), the floor must supply ${\mu} \ge {a_{\text{brake}}}/{g} =$ 0.204. The hub motors are rated to carry 300 kg per pair, so on the 368 kg machine ${\phi_{\text{drive}}} \le$ 0.815 and the floor must supply at least ${\mu} =$ 0.250. The dry floor (${\mu} =$ 0.60) meets that whenever the driven wheels carry at least a fraction 0.34 of the weight, and at the rated load share it supports ${\mu} {g} {\phi_{\text{drive}}} =$ 4.80 m/s². The inspected floor, a sealed low-traction surface whose friction a facility inspection certifies only down to ${\mu} =$ 0.12, supports only 1.18 m/s² even with every kilogram on the driven wheels.
2.  **Tip-over**: The machine stays on its wheels while ${a_{\text{brake}}} \le {g}\,{d_{\text{lead}}}/{h_{\text{cg}}}$, so the credible deceleration requires ${h_{\text{cg}}}/{d_{\text{lead}}} \le {g}/{a_{\text{brake}}} =$ 4.90. The machine’s specification gives neither the loaded center-of-gravity height nor its distance to the leading support, and extending the arm forward shortens ${d_{\text{lead}}}$. This ceiling cannot yet be checked.
3.  **Drive torque**: Each wheel delivers ${K_t} {I_{\max}} =$ 60 N·m, so the floor sees ${F} = 2 {K_t} {I_{\max}}/{r} =$ 902.3 N. With the rotating-mass bound of [[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/01 Machine Anatomy/02 The Physical Body/V4-02.04 Kinetic Momentum#^sec-body-stopping-distance-prebrake|section 2.3.1]], ${m_{\text{eff}}} \le$ 387 kg, and the torque ceiling is ${F}/{m_{\text{eff}}} \ge$ 2.33 m/s², at least 16.6 percent above the credible deceleration. With the current limit set at the rated 8.5 A instead, each wheel gives its rated 20 N·m and the ceiling falls to 0.78 m/s².
4.  **Voltage at the drive limit**: At 1.5 m/s the wheel turns at ${\omega_m} = {v_{\max}}/{r} =$ 107.7 rpm, and the line-to-line back-EMF is ${e_{\text{bemf}}} = {K_e} {\omega_m} =$ 28.2 V. Braking current flows with the back-EMF, so holding ${I_{\max}}$ through the 0.7 Ω line resistance requires only ${e_{\text{bemf}}} - {I_{\max}} {R} =$ 10.7 V at the terminals. Even a depleted 42 V pack sits 13.8 V above ${e_{\text{bemf}}}$. Driving at the same current would need ${e_{\text{bemf}}} + {I_{\max}} {R} =$ 45.7 V, more than the depleted pack supplies.

**Result**: The drives clear 2 m/s² by at least 16.6 percent, but only with the current limit at the motor’s peak. Traction holds on the dry floor if the driven wheels carry at least a fraction 0.34 of the weight, and it fails on the inspected floor. The tip-over ceiling remains unverified until the loaded center of gravity is measured, by weighing each wheel on corner scales or by a tilt test, with the arm stowed and extended.

**Systems insight**: The credible deceleration is a conjunction of three ceilings owned by different parts of the machine: the floor, the mass distribution that the arm changes, and the drive configuration. A current limit lowered to protect the windings cuts the torque ceiling below the stopping premise without any software change, so the configured current limit belongs in the limit record’s conditions.

Current regulation also incurs electrical and switching losses. Any stator current vector misaligned with the quadrature axis produces resistive heating without contributing shaft torque. Inverter pulse-width modulation and required protective dead times introduce further nonlinear voltage distortion.[[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/01 Machine Anatomy/02 The Physical Body/V4-02.14 Footnotes#^fn9|⁹]] Consequently, dynamic torque tracking must be verified through physical dynamometer measurements on the assembled actuator rather than inferred from current setpoints alone.

### Reflected Rotor Inertia and Optimal Inertia Matching

^sec-body-reflected-rotor-inertia

Once electromagnetic torque leaves the motor shaft, mechanical gearing introduces a dynamic asymmetry between torque and inertia. High-reduction joints employ gear ratios between $50{:}1$ and $160{:}1$ to multiply the output torque of compact, lightweight motors. While this reduces the motor torque required by a factor of ${N_{\text{gear}}}$, the motor rotor must spin ${N_{\text{gear}}}$ times faster than the actuated joint. When an external contact backdrives the joint, it must accelerate that rotor at ${N_{\text{gear}}}$ times the joint’s angular acceleration. Because rotational kinetic energy scales with the square of velocity ($\frac{1}{2} {J_{\text{rotor}}} {\omega_m}^2$), the rotor’s physical inertia ${J_{\text{rotor}}}$ resists load-side acceleration multiplied by the square of the gear ratio (definition [2.3](https://mlsysbook.ai/vol4/02_body/02_body.html#dfn-body-reflected-rotor-inertia)).

Through a 100:1 transmission, a small 50 g rotor imposes the apparent inertia of a heavy 11.2 kg flywheel with a 10 cm radius at the output shaft ([[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/01 Machine Anatomy/02 The Physical Body/V4-02.13 Source References#^ref-lynch2017modern|Lynch and Park 2017]]). When the end-effector collides with a rigid surface, the contact force must instantly decelerate this equivalent flywheel through the drivetrain, generating extreme peak stresses that can fracture gear teeth at their roots, as documented in [[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/01 Machine Anatomy/02 The Physical Body/V4-02.05 Actuator Transmission Limits#^fig-02-real-broken-gear-tooth|figure 4]].

**Definition 2.3: Reflected rotor inertia**

***Reflected rotor inertia*** (${J_{\text{ref}}}$) is the apparent rotational inertia of an actuator rotor at the load-side joint output, scaling with the square of the transmission gear ratio:

$$
{J_{\text{ref}}} = {N_{\text{gear}}}^2 {J_{\text{rotor}}}
$$

1.  **Significance**: In high-reduction transmissions (${N_{\text{gear}}} \ge 100$) it dominates the apparent load, capping joint acceleration ${\ddot{q}}$ and setting impact shock loads.
2.  **Distinction**: Unlike rotor inertia ${J_{\text{rotor}}}$ or link inertia ${J_{\text{load}}}$, it acts through transmission stiffness and backlash, so backdriving from the environment meets an ${N_{\text{gear}}}^2$ barrier.
3.  **Common pitfall**: Ignoring reflected rotor inertia during collision and contact modeling. Under sudden obstacle impact, the reflected kinetic energy of the spinning rotor concentrates stress directly on gear teeth before drive current can be interrupted.

The combined equation of motion for a geared joint driving load inertia ${J_{\text{load}}}$ with motor torque ${\tau_{\text{motor}}}$ is:

$$
{\ddot{q}} = \frac{{N_{\text{gear}}} {\tau_{\text{motor}}}}{{J_{\text{load}}} + {N_{\text{gear}}}^2 {J_{\text{rotor}}}}
$$

 Maximizing delivered load acceleration ${\ddot{q}}$ with respect to transmission ratio ${N_{\text{gear}}}$, by setting ${d}{\ddot{q}}/d{N_{\text{gear}}} = 0$, gives ${J_{\text{load}}} = {N_{\text{gear}}}^2 {J_{\text{rotor}}}$: acceleration peaks when the reflected rotor inertia equals the load inertia. Solving for ${N_{\text{gear}}}$ establishes the classical **inertia matching ratio**:[[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/01 Machine Anatomy/02 The Physical Body/V4-02.14 Footnotes#^fn10|¹⁰]]

$$
{N^*} = \sqrt{\frac{{J_{\text{load}}}}{{J_{\text{rotor}}}}} \tag{5}
$$

^eq-body-inertia-matching

Below ${N^*}$, joint acceleration is torque-limited: increasing ${N_{\text{gear}}}$ multiplies motor torque to accelerate the heavy load faster. Above ${N^*}$, more gearing *decreases* delivered joint acceleration, because the motor expends most of its electromagnetic torque accelerating its own spinning rotor (${N_{\text{gear}}}^2 {J_{\text{rotor}}}$) rather than the external link. For the 50 g rotor above driving an illustrative link of ${J_{\text{load}}} =$ 8.5 × 10⁻³ kg·m², the matched ratio is ${N^*} \approx$ 39. At 100:1 the same motor torque delivers only 67.7 percent of the matched-ratio acceleration, because ${\ddot{q}}({N_{\text{gear}}})/{\ddot{q}}({N^*}) = 2{\tilde{N}}/(1+{\tilde{N}}^2)$ with ${\tilde{N}} = {N_{\text{gear}}}/{N^*}$. The extra gearing has cost 32.3 percent of the joint’s acceleration.

The gear ratio also magnifies the mechanical resistance an external contact meets when attempting to move the link. This resistance to imposed motion constitutes the joint’s mechanical impedance. Because link inertia, joint damping, torsional compliance, and seal friction all shape this response, the gear ratio alone does not establish contact forces or structural fracture limits. Transmission selection must balance delivered joint acceleration against the impedance presented to accidental impacts.

Abrupt changes in commanded torque excite the torsional elasticity of geared joints before the physical link responds. Bounding joint acceleration and jerk moderates this excitation, but software trajectory smoothing cannot override mechanical resonance. Trajectory generators formulate control profiles that respect these physical boundaries ([[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/01 Machine Anatomy/02 The Physical Body/V4-02.13 Source References#^ref-lynch2017modern|Lynch and Park 2017]]). The hardware platform must provide the empirical response rates and contact stiffness limits required to tune them.

### Transmission Compliance, Contact Impedance, and Tooth Fracture

^sec-body-transmission-compliance

Mechanical gearboxes introduce backlash and elasticity that decouple the motor from the driven link. Upon direction reversal, the motor shaft traverses mechanical clearance before the gear teeth engage the opposing flank. Concurrently, as illustrated by the disassembled strain wave gear set in [[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/01 Machine Anatomy/02 The Physical Body/V4-02.05 Actuator Transmission Limits#^fig-02-real-strain-wave-gear|figure 3]], torsional compliance in gear teeth and flexible elements[[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/01 Machine Anatomy/02 The Physical Body/V4-02.14 Footnotes#^fn11|¹¹]] transforms the rigid link into an elastic system ([[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/01 Machine Anatomy/02 The Physical Body/V4-02.13 Source References#^ref-spong1987modeling|Spong 1987]]). Its dynamics follow the flexible-joint formulation given in **[[05 Reference/Volume IV - Physical AI/V4 Control and Dynamics#^sec-appendix-control-flexible-joints|Flexible joint dynamics and singular perturbations]]**. Step changes in torque excite this structural spring, driving the drivetrain into limit-cycle[[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/01 Machine Anatomy/02 The Physical Body/V4-02.14 Footnotes#^fn12|¹²]] chatter. Motor-side encoders conceal this deadband completely. The motor rotates smoothly while the output shaft remains pinned, causing link position ${q_{\text{out}}}$ to diverge from ${q_{\text{motor}}} / {N_{\text{gear}}}$ by a load-dependent deflection ${\delta_{\text{lost}}}$. The motor controller observes flawless setpoint tracking while the end-effector stalls, eventually experiencing an impulsive impact when the gear teeth make contact. Repeated shock cycles culminate in the root fatigue failure shown in [[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/01 Machine Anatomy/02 The Physical Body/V4-02.05 Actuator Transmission Limits#^fig-02-real-broken-gear-tooth|figure 4]].

Such failures show why dynamic impact loads, rather than static payload ratings alone, must govern actuator and transmission selection: repeated torque spikes and unmodeled contact shocks concentrate destructive stresses directly at the tooth root fillet. While smooth motion plans mitigate drivetrain vibration, safe physical interaction depends on measuring output torque and enforcing strict mechanical load boundaries.

**Systems Perspective 2.3: Impedance control for contact safety**

When a position-controlled robot encounters a rigid surface (contact stiffness ${k_{\text{contact}}} \ge 10^5\text{ N/m}$), a minor setpoint tracking error of ${\Delta} {x} = 1\text{ mm}$ escalates the contact force to ${F_{\text{contact}}} = {k_{\text{contact}}} {\Delta} {x} = 100\text{ N}$ within milliseconds as the inverter commands maximum current against the obstacle. On the mobile manipulator, the gripper contacting the spring-latched door strikes a steel plate with stiffness 4.0 × 10⁵ N/m. Once impact occurs, contact force ramps at ${F_{\text{contact}}} = {k_{\text{contact}}} {v_{\text{approach}}} {t}$: 12 N/ms under a guarded approach velocity of 0.03 m/s, and 40 N/ms at an unconstrained 0.10 m/s, rapidly exceeding the latch’s 100 N structural limit.

Impedance control ([[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/01 Machine Anatomy/02 The Physical Body/V4-02.13 Source References#^ref-hogan1985impedance|Hogan 1985]]) reformulates contact interactions away from pure position or force control. An impedance takes motion in and returns force; an admittance takes force in and returns motion. Because a rigid environment behaves as an admittance, yielding motion only when pushed, the manipulator is made to behave as an impedance, which answers a deviation in position and velocity with a restoring force (${F_{\text{contact}}} = {Z}({\dot{x}})$, force as a function of the imposed motion). The joint drive controller realizes this relationship by emulating a **virtual spring-damper system**:

$$
{F_{\text{contact}}} = {K_p} ({x_{\text{des}}} - {x}) + {K_d} ({\dot{x}_{\text{des}}} - {\dot{x}})
$$

 The stiffness ${K_p}$ multiplies the position error and the damping ${K_d}$ the velocity error, the roles a proportional and a derivative gain play in a feedback controller. Tuning them in software causes the arm to yield compliantly under tracking errors, shedding peak impact loads. However, closed-loop impedance behavior is physically bounded by current-loop update rates, inverter voltage headroom, and unmodeled joint elasticity. Algebraic control laws cannot alter the unyielding inertia of the rotor during the first milliseconds of an impact. The runtime permission path must therefore enforce approach speed limits derived from physical contact qualification rather than relying on software compliance alone. The impedance command interface, through which a learned policy proposes these gains and the permission path validates them before admission, is defined in **[[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/01 Machine Anatomy/04 The Nervous System/V4-04.07 Actuation Authority#^sec-nervous-command-interface|What a proposal may command]]**.

### Actuation Regimes and Gear Reduction Trade-Offs

^sec-body-actuation-regimes

The scaling disparity underlying inertia matching, in which output torque scales linearly with ratio (${\tau_{\text{out}}} = {\eta_{\text{gear}}} {N_{\text{gear}}} {\tau_{\text{motor}}}$) while reflected inertia scales quadratically (${N_{\text{gear}}}^2$), divides robotic actuation into three primary regimes. These regimes trade continuous torque density against mechanical backdrivability, distinguished by gear design, stage count, and reduction ratio ([[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/01 Machine Anatomy/02 The Physical Body/V4-02.05 Actuator Transmission Limits#^tbl-02-mechanical-drives|table 2]]): at the lowest ratios, **quasi-direct drive (QDD)** pairs a high-torque motor with a single compact planetary stage; at intermediate ratios, multistage planetary gearboxes reduce motor size at the cost of moderate reflected inertia and backlash; and at the highest ratios, strain-wave gears pack large torque multiplication into a compact joint at the cost of extreme reflected inertia and torsional compliance.

| Transmission                                    | Gear Ratio (${N_{\text{gear}}}$)                     | Reflected Inertia (${N_{\text{gear}}}^2$)                    | Implication for Learned Policies                                                                                                 |
|:------------------------------------------------|:-----------------------------------------------------|:-------------------------------------------------------------|:---------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------|
| **Quasi-Direct Drive (single-stage planetary)** | $3:1\text{--}10:1$ (${N_{\text{gear}}} \le 10$)      | ${N_{\text{gear}}}^2 \le 100$ (Low)                          | Lower reflected inertia supports backdriving and current-based torque estimation; contact response still requires qualification. |
| **Multistage Planetary**                        | $15:1\text{--}50:1$ (${N_{\text{gear}}} \approx 30$) | ${N_{\text{gear}}}^2 \approx 225\text{--}2{,}500$ (Moderate) | Semi-backdrivable; a smaller motor than QDD for the same output torque; gear backlash requires load-side joint sensing.          |
| **Strain Wave (Harmonic Drive)**                | $50:1\text{--}160:1$ (${N_{\text{gear}}} \ge 50$)    | ${N_{\text{gear}}}^2 \ge 2{,}500\text{--}25{,}600$ (Extreme) | High reflected inertia; zero initial backlash; torsional compliance and impact loading require qualification.                    |

Table 2: **Actuator transmission trade-off**: Reduction ratio (${N_{\text{gear}}}$), reflected rotor inertia scaling (${N_{\text{gear}}}^2$), and the consequence for learned control policies across quasi-direct-drive (a single low-ratio planetary stage), multistage planetary, and strain wave transmissions. The strain wave range is that of one catalog series ([[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/01 Machine Anatomy/02 The Physical Body/V4-02.13 Source References#^ref-harmonicdrive2014csg2uh|Harmonic Drive AG 2014]]).

^tbl-02-mechanical-drives

Actuator selection must satisfy continuous torque targets and permissible contact dynamics simultaneously. Direct-drive configurations eliminate gear backlash entirely, but require large, heavy motors to produce holding torque ([[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/01 Machine Anatomy/02 The Physical Body/V4-02.13 Source References#^ref-asada1983design|Asada and Kanade 1983]]). Increasing the gear ratio permits a much smaller motor to produce equivalent output torque, but introduces reflected inertia, friction, and transmission compliance.

Motor thermal limits and torque requirements intersect in an electromagnetic figure of merit. Stator resistive losses follow ${P_{\text{loss}}} = {i_q}^2 {R_{\text{eff}}}$, where ${R_{\text{eff}}}$ is the winding’s effective loss resistance, while electromagnetic torque follows ${\tau_{\text{motor}}} = {K_t} {i_q}$. Combining these expressions reveals that resistive dissipation scales with the square of motor torque: ${P_{\text{loss}}} = ({\tau_{\text{motor}}} / {K_m})^2$. Here the **motor constant** ${K_m} = {K_t} / \sqrt{{R_{\text{eff}}}}$ (in $\text{N}\cdot\text{m}/\sqrt{\text{W}}$) captures the motor’s torque-producing capacity per square root of dissipated heat. Motor datasheets specify this parameter directly ([[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/01 Machine Anatomy/02 The Physical Body/V4-02.13 Source References#^ref-tq2014ilm|TQ-Systems 2014]]). [[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/01 Machine Anatomy/02 The Physical Body/V4-02.06 Thermal Duty Cycles#^sec-body-thermal-capacity|Section 2.5.1]] relates ${R_{\text{eff}}}$ to the phase and line-to-line resistances of [[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/01 Machine Anatomy/02 The Physical Body/V4-02.05 Actuator Transmission Limits#^sec-body-electrical-time-constants|section 2.4.2]]. When driving a mechanical transmission of ratio ${N_{\text{gear}}}$ and efficiency ${\eta_{\text{gear}}}$, the motor must supply an internal torque of ${\tau_{\text{out}}}/({\eta_{\text{gear}}} {N_{\text{gear}}})$, resulting in:

$$
{P_{\text{loss}}} = \left(\frac{{\tau_{\text{out}}}}{{\eta_{\text{gear}}} {N_{\text{gear}}} {K_m}}\right)^2, \qquad {J_{\text{ref,in}}} = {N_{\text{gear}}}^2 {J_{\text{in}}}
$$

 where ${J_{\text{in}}}$ represents total input inertia (the rotor plus the transmission input stage), and ${J_{\text{ref,in}}}$ is that combined inertia reflected to the joint output. Increasing the reduction ratio ${N_{\text{gear}}}$ reduces winding heat by ${N_{\text{gear}}}^2$ while multiplying reflected inertia by ${N_{\text{gear}}}^2$. Consequently, for a selected motor and gearbox geometry, their product is invariant to the gear ratio:

$$
{P_{\text{loss}}}\,{J_{\text{ref,in}}} = \frac{{\tau_{\text{out}}}^2\,{J_{\text{in}}}}{{\eta_{\text{gear}}}^2 {K_m}^2} \tag{6}
$$

 Adjusting ${N_{\text{gear}}}$ relocates the actuator along a fixed trade-off curve between thermal dissipation and reflected inertia. Improving both characteristics concurrently requires selecting a motor with a superior ratio of ${K_m}^2/{J_{\text{in}}}$. Notebook [2.4](https://mlsysbook.ai/vol4/02_body/02_body.html#nbk-body-gear-ratio-cost) evaluates this trade-off for the mobile manipulator’s arm joints.

^eq-body-heat-inertia-product

**Napkin Math 2.4: What does the gear ratio cost?**

The running machine’s arm is a seven-joint, torque-sensing manipulator in the class of the Franka Emika Panda ([[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/01 Machine Anatomy/02 The Physical Body/V4-02.13 Source References#^ref-franka2018panda|Franka Emika GmbH 2018]]), whose joints use strain-wave gears (**[[05 Reference/Volume IV - Physical AI/V4 Systems and Hardware#^sec-appendix-systems-machine-data-sheet|The machine data sheet]]**). Franka does not publish the motors in those joints, so this example, like the thermal examples of [[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/01 Machine Anatomy/02 The Physical Body/V4-02.06 Thermal Duty Cycles#^sec-body-thermal-duty-cycles|section 2.5]], uses a sourced stand-in. The stand-in is a TQ-RoboDrive ILM 70x18 frameless motor ([[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/01 Machine Anatomy/02 The Physical Body/V4-02.13 Source References#^ref-tq2014ilm|TQ-Systems 2014]]) with ${K_t} =$ 0.187 N·m/A and effective loss resistance ${R_{\text{eff}}} =$ 0.495 Ω, driving a CSG-25 strain-wave gear ([[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/01 Machine Anatomy/02 The Physical Body/V4-02.13 Source References#^ref-harmonicdrive2014csg2uh|Harmonic Drive AG 2014]]), whose catalog lists one input inertia for all of that size’s ratios. Rotor plus gear input give ${J_{\text{in}}} =$ 7.34 × 10⁻⁵ kg·m². The joint must hold the arm’s maximum joint torque ${\tau_{\text{out}}} =$ 87 N·m through a gear of efficiency ${\eta_{\text{gear}}} =$ 0.85 (illustrative).

**Problem**: How do the copper loss and the reflected inertia change if the same motor drives the joint through 50:1, 100:1, or a quasi-direct-drive ratio of 10:1?

**Math**:

1.  **Motor constant**: ${K_m} = {K_t}/\sqrt{{R_{\text{eff}}}} =$ 0.266 N·m/√W, the value the motor’s data sheet publishes.
2.  **At 50:1**: The motor supplies ${\tau_{\text{out}}}/({\eta_{\text{gear}}} {N_{\text{gear}}}) =$ 2.05 N·m, dissipating $({\tau_{\text{motor}}}/{K_m})^2 =$ 59.3 W, and the joint reflects ${N_{\text{gear}}}^2 {J_{\text{in}}} =$ 0.183 kg·m².
3.  **At 100:1**: The motor supplies 1.02 N·m, the loss falls fourfold to 14.8 W, and the reflected inertia rises fourfold to 0.734 kg·m². The product ${P_{\text{loss}}} {J_{\text{ref,in}}}$ is unchanged.
4.  **At 10:1**: The motor would have to supply 10.2 N·m, 2.5× its 4.05 N·m peak torque. No current can make this motor hold the load.

**Result**: Doubling the ratio buys a fourfold cut in holding heat with a fourfold rise in the inertia that an impact must accelerate, and lowering the ratio toward quasi-direct drive fails outright with this motor. The gear ratio moves the joint along a fixed trade; only the motor sets the trade itself.

**Systems insight**: A low-ratio, backdrivable joint requires a motor with a much larger torque per square root of watt, the route quasi-direct drive takes. A high-ratio arm joint instead accepts the reflected inertia and recovers contact sensitivity by measuring torque at its output.

Mechanical backdrivability measures the ease with which an external load applied at the joint output rotates the motor shaft backward through the transmission. The external torque required to backdrive a geared joint is:

$$
{\tau_{\text{ext}}} = {N_{\text{gear}}}^2 {J_{\text{rotor}}} {\ddot{q}} + {\tau_{\text{fric}}}
$$

 where ${\tau_{\text{fric}}}$ denotes total joint friction referred to the output link. This friction varies dynamically with operating state ([[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/01 Machine Anatomy/02 The Physical Body/V4-02.13 Source References#^ref-lynch2017modern|Lynch and Park 2017]]): breakaway static friction dominates at rest, Coulomb friction opposes motion at constant levels, and viscous damping grows with velocity. The Stribeck effect, a dip in friction just after sliding begins, shapes the response at near-zero speeds. Furthermore, gear tooth mesh friction scales with transmitted load, ambient temperature, and lubrication aging. At low velocities, friction dominates the resistance of high-reduction gearboxes; during sudden impacts, reflected rotor inertia dominates. As a result, backdrivability must be verified through empirical load testing on the assembled joint rather than inferred from the reduction ratio alone.

Quasi-direct drive actuation takes the low-ratio route of notebook [2.4](https://mlsysbook.ai/vol4/02_body/02_body.html#nbk-body-gear-ratio-cost), using large-diameter, high-torque motors so a single modest reduction stage suffices ([[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/01 Machine Anatomy/02 The Physical Body/V4-02.13 Source References#^ref-seok2015design|Seok et al. 2015]]; [[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/01 Machine Anatomy/02 The Physical Body/V4-02.13 Source References#^ref-wensing2017proprioceptive|Wensing et al. 2017]]; [[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/01 Machine Anatomy/02 The Physical Body/V4-02.13 Source References#^ref-katz2019mini|Katz et al. 2019]]). Minimizing reflected inertia and internal friction maximizes mechanical transparency, the fidelity with which external interaction forces translate into measurable motor motion and winding currents. The penalty is a substantially heavier motor demand for any given output torque.

Mechanical transparency also dictates what motor currents reveal about contact states. In high-friction gearboxes, motor current primarily overcomes internal transmission losses rather than external loads. Conversely, when transmission friction and inertial resistance remain small, output torque simplifies to:

$$
{\tau_{\text{out}}} \approx {\eta_{\text{gear}}} {N_{\text{gear}}} {K_t} {i_q}
$$

 This relationship enables **proprioceptive sensing**, allowing the controller to estimate interaction forces directly from motor current without specialized external sensors. However, accurate estimation requires rigorous calibration across operating temperatures and speeds; current setpoints measure motor electromagnetic effort, portions of which are absorbed by acceleration and drivetrain losses. While backdrivability allows external collisions to displace the joint without shearing gear teeth, low gearing and motor current sensing do not guarantee safe contact forces. High link velocities can still impart severe impulsive shock before the current controller can respond. Safe interaction mandates qualifying the total closed-loop contact response.

High-reduction joints overcome transmission friction by sensing torque directly at the joint output. Strain gauge torque sensors mounted on the output side of strain wave gearboxes measure delivered torque directly, allowing closed-loop current controllers to cancel gearbox friction and cogging torque ([[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/01 Machine Anatomy/02 The Physical Body/V4-02.13 Source References#^ref-lynch2017modern|Lynch and Park 2017]]). The running machine’s arm adopts this approach, integrating torque sensors within each of its seven joints ([[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/01 Machine Anatomy/02 The Physical Body/V4-02.13 Source References#^ref-franka2018panda|Franka Emika GmbH 2018]]) to achieve transparency through measurement rather than low mechanical reduction. The engineering cost includes increased joint complexity, elevated mass, and the torsional compliance of the strain wave flexsplines, which complicates high-bandwidth motion tracking.

Whether mechanical transparency originates by construction (quasi-direct drive) or by active measurement (output torque sensing), contact operations require qualifying joint compliance, closed-loop torque response, and dynamic sensing fidelity against the operating environment. Dual-encoder architectures and load-side torque sensors expose internal transmission windup and mechanical backlash that motor-side encoders conceal. When load-side deflection departs from the qualified dynamic envelope, the runtime safety system can immediately intercede and reject subsequent motion commands.

### Supporting Drive and Impedance Models

^sec-body-drive-supporting-models

Deliverable motor torque and contact shock isolation rest on two compact models of how the drive and transmission transform effort and motion: the rotating-frame torque law behind ${\tau_{\text{motor}}} = {K_t} {i_q}$ ([[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/01 Machine Anatomy/02 The Physical Body/V4-02.05 Actuator Transmission Limits#^sec-body-electrical-time-constants|section 2.4.2]]), and the series-spring impedance that isolates reflected rotor inertia during a fast impact ([[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/01 Machine Anatomy/02 The Physical Body/V4-02.05 Actuator Transmission Limits#^sec-body-transmission-compliance|section 2.4.4]]). Inside the physical drive, FOC holds the ${d}$-axis current ${i_d}$ of [[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/01 Machine Anatomy/02 The Physical Body/V4-02.05 Actuator Transmission Limits#^sec-body-electrical-time-constants|section 2.4.2]] at zero, which minimizes stator losses and makes output torque proportional to the ${q}$-axis current alone (**[[05 Reference/Volume IV - Physical AI/V4 Systems and Hardware#^sec-appendix-systems-actuators|Actuator Electrical and Thermal Dynamics]]** states the voltage equations that govern both currents in the rotating frame):

$$
{\tau_{\text{motor}}} = \frac{3}{2} {N_{\text{poles}}} {\lambda_{\text{pm}}} {i_q} = {K_t} {i_q}
$$

 where ${N_{\text{poles}}}$ is the number of magnetic pole pairs on the rotor and ${\lambda_{\text{pm}}}$ is the permanent-magnet flux linkage, the magnetic flux from the rotor magnets that threads each winding. The $\frac{3}{2}$ scaling factor arises from the amplitude-invariant transformation convention adopted in this chapter. Motor drive firmware must enforce a transformation convention consistent with the identified torque constant ${K_t}$.

For frequency-domain dynamic analysis, mechanical impedance quantifies the joint’s physical resistance to imposed environmental motion. The Laplace variable ${s}$ stands for differentiation in time, so that for a sinusoidal motion of angular frequency ${\omega}$ it can be read as ${s} = {j}{\omega}$. An impedance term ${s} {J}$ grows with frequency like an inertia, and a term ${K}/{s}$ falls with frequency like a spring. The apparent mechanical impedance ${Z_{\text{out}}}({s}) = {\tau_{\text{ext}}}({s}) / {\dot{q}}({s})$ evaluated at the joint output flange couples the load dynamics directly to the reflected rotor dynamics through the drivetrain’s torsional stiffness ${K_{\text{joint}}}$. An external torque applied at the joint flange splits between two paths that share the same output velocity ${\dot{q}}({s})$, one accelerating the output link inertia ${J_{\text{load}}}$ directly and the other twisting the transmission spring against the reflected rotor. Along that second path, the transmission spring and the reflected rotor act in series so both stages bear equal transmitted torque while their relative angular velocities sum, causing the spring impedance ${K_{\text{joint}}}/{s}$ and reflected rotor impedance to combine through their reciprocal sum before adding to the output link impedance ([[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/01 Machine Anatomy/02 The Physical Body/V4-02.13 Source References#^ref-spong1987modeling|Spong 1987]]):

$$
{Z_{\text{out}}}({s}) = {s} {J_{\text{load}}} + {D_{\text{joint}}} + \left[\frac{1}{{s} {N_{\text{gear}}}^2 {J_{\text{rotor}}} + {N_{\text{gear}}}^2 {D_{\text{motor}}}} + \frac{{s}}{{K_{\text{joint}}}}\right]^{-1}
$$

 The coefficients ${D_{\text{joint}}}$ and ${D_{\text{motor}}}$ account for viscous damping in the output bearings and motor shaft. At low interaction frequencies, the series spring behaves rigidly relative to the rotor, and the environment experiences the aggregate inertia ${J_{\text{load}}} + {N_{\text{gear}}}^2 {J_{\text{rotor}}}$ assumed in rigid-body kinematic models. Above the transmission’s natural resonance frequency, the bracketed impedance term simplifies toward ${K_{\text{joint}}}/{s}$: external collision forces compress the joint compliance against the link inertia ${J_{\text{load}}}$, temporarily isolating the rotor’s reflected inertia. This series compliance cushions sudden impacts in flexible drivetrains and series elastic actuators, reinforcing why the flexible-joint dynamic model is necessary to calculate contact loads during manipulation.

**Checkpoint 2.1: Actuator limits**

The drives decide whether the stopping premise can be delivered, and the transmission decides what that delivery costs in heat and inertia.

**Torque ceiling**: Can you compute the deceleration ceiling of the two hub drives when their current limit is set at the motor’s rated current rather than its peak, and explain why a current limit lowered to protect the windings breaks the stopping premise without any software change?

**Current rise**: Can you estimate how long the hub-motor current takes to reach its limit from rest on a depleted pack, and explain why back-EMF at speed helps a braking current but can starve a driving one?

**Inertia matching**: Can you explain why the gear ratio that maximizes link acceleration makes reflected rotor inertia equal load inertia (${N_{\text{gear}}}^2 {J_{\text{rotor}}} = {J_{\text{load}}}$), and why oversizing the gearbox for static torque lowers dynamic joint acceleration?

**Heat vs. reflected inertia**: Can you explain why, for a given motor and gear, the ratio cannot reduce holding heat and reflected inertia together, and what must change to improve both?

Operating near these electrical and mechanical ceilings exacts an inevitable thermodynamic toll. Every ampere driving the motor against reflected rotor inertia or sustaining peak clamping torque dissipates resistive heat (${P_{\text{loss}}} = {i_q}^2 {R_{\text{eff}}}$) in the stator windings. That thermal energy accumulates directly in the motor’s thermal mass, bounding the operational duration of peak torque before winding insulation suffers accelerated thermal degradation.
