# V4-02.07 Electrical Power Integrity

[[Home|Home]] · [[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/Volume 4 Overview|Volume Overview]] · [[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/01 Machine Anatomy/02 The Physical Body/V4-02 The Physical Body|V4-02 The Physical Body]]

^sec-body-power-integrity

When the mobile manipulator swerves hard to avoid a collision, every drive motor, joint inverter, and accelerator demands peak current at once from one direct-current distribution bus. In simulation, current flows from an idealized source. On hardware, delivery is bounded by finite capacity, source impedance, and transient voltage stability. A machine that browns out mid-motion fails physically; the electrical supply is a shared resource.

Rail sag, regenerative overvoltage, and thermal accumulation are manifestations of one bounded energy network on different time scales. Of the three, voltage moves fastest, within microseconds to milliseconds whenever current demand exceeds what the source can deliver at that instant. Compute, sensing, and motor inverters draw from a common bus, so when a policy commands coordinated accelerations across several joints, the sum of their currents changes the voltage delivered to every node on the machine.

### Power Distribution Impedance, Transient Droop, and Brownout Resets

^sec-body-power-distribution-impedance

The mobile machine’s battery, harnesses, connectors, and protection switches all contribute source impedance.[[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/01 Machine Anatomy/02 The Physical Body/V4-02.14 Footnotes#^fn16|¹⁶]] Local capacitors supply part of a sudden load increase, so current drawn by the loads need not equal current arriving through the harness. For a source feeding one bus node through resistance and inductance,

$$
{V_{\text{source}}}({t})-{V_{\text{bus}}}({t})={R_{\text{bus}}}{}{I_{\text{source}}}({t})+{L_{\text{bus}}}\frac{d{I_{\text{source}}}}{d{t}} \tag{9}
$$

 where ${R_{\text{bus}}}$ is the lumped source resistance, ${L_{\text{bus}}}$ is the loop inductance, and ${I_{\text{source}}}$ is the current through that source path. The local capacitor bank ${C_{\text{bus}}}$ supplies the difference between load current ${I_{\text{load}}}$ and source current. In the ideal-capacitor model, charge removed from that bank lowers the same bus voltage:

$$
{V_{\text{bus}}}(0)-{V_{\text{bus}}}({t})=\frac{1}{{C_{\text{bus}}}}\int_0^{t}\left[{I_{\text{load}}}({s})-{I_{\text{source}}}({s})\right]d{s}.
$$

 These equations jointly determine the voltage response; the capacitor’s voltage change is not an additional drop to add to [[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/01 Machine Anatomy/02 The Physical Body/V4-02.07 Electrical Power Integrity#^eq-body-droop|equation 9]].

^eq-body-droop

↪ Sizing the bus capacitance against that droop, and the undervoltage lockout it must avoid, is developed in **[[05 Reference/Volume IV - Physical AI/V4 Systems and Hardware#^sec-appendix-systems-power|DC Bus Power Distribution and Energy Absorption]]**.

Compute and motion must be budgeted together because they share this path. An obstacle response can require fresh inference at the same time as a large drive-current change, so testing each load separately misses the combined step that the supply must support. Harness inductance limits how quickly source current can rise. Local capacitors supply the initial current deficit, while harness resistance continues to lower the rail after source current catches up with a sustained load. Once the currents settle, the inductive voltage is zero and the sustained drop is ${I_{\text{source}}}{R_{\text{bus}}}$. The worked example below screens that sustained condition. It does not establish the minimum voltage during the transient. If the actual voltage crosses a regulator’s undervoltage threshold, computation or communication may stop or reset. Power qualification must establish the voltage at the supplied devices during coincident load and the physical response if those devices lose power.

### Unpowered States and Unmanaged Fallback Dynamics

^sec-body-unpowered-states

A brownout reset turns the electrical fault into a mechanical one. Voltage supervisors pull the reset line on microcontrollers and gate drivers, clocks halt, register configurations vanish, and communication buses float. An acceleration command issued just before the reset stays physically active until the gate-driver stage collapses. If the driver then floats, phase current ceases, torque falls to zero, and the body coasts under gravity and inertia into workpieces or hardstops. If it instead shorts the low-side switches for dynamic braking, back-EMF (${e_{\text{bemf}}} = {K_e} {\omega_m}$) produces an immediate, abrupt counter-torque that jerks the mechanics at the joint limits.

Power removal is an unmanaged state transition, not an automatic return to safety. Cutting power leaves an actuator in one of three unpowered states, namely free coasting, phase-short dynamic braking, or spring-engaged mechanical braking, and each enforces a distinct mechanical behavior. Free coasting simply allows gravity to accelerate unconstrained joints without any resistance. An electromechanical friction brake requires 30 ms to 80 ms for its mechanical spring to overcome magnetic coil decay and engage its friction pads, during which gravity accelerates an overhead arm downward.

Phase-short dynamic braking generates a counter-torque that at low speed grows in proportion to angular velocity (${\tau_{\text{motor}}} \approx \frac{{K_t} {K_e}}{{R_{\text{phase}}}} {\omega_m}$) and gives zero holding torque at zero velocity. The proportionality does not last. As speed rises, the winding inductance limits the short-circuit current. The shorted winding is a resistance and an inductance driven by the back-EMF ${K_e}{\omega_m}$, which alternates at the electrical speed ${N_{\text{poles}}}{\omega_m}$. An inductance opposes an alternating current in proportion to its frequency, with reactance ${N_{\text{poles}}}{\omega_m} {L_{\text{phase}}}$, and that opposition adds to the resistance in quadrature. The current is therefore ${K_e}{\omega_m}/\sqrt{{R_{\text{phase}}}^2 + ({N_{\text{poles}}}{\omega_m} {L_{\text{phase}}})^2}$. The braking torque, the power dissipated in ${R_{\text{phase}}}$ divided by ${\omega_m}$, is ${K_t} {K_e} {\omega_m} {R_{\text{phase}}}/({R_{\text{phase}}}^2 + ({N_{\text{poles}}}{\omega_m} {L_{\text{phase}}})^2)$. That torque peaks where the electrical speed ${N_{\text{poles}}} {\omega_m}$ equals ${R_{\text{phase}}}/{L_{\text{phase}}}$ and falls beyond it, so above that speed a phase short brakes less the faster the joint turns. For the base’s hub motors that peak lies at 177.3 rpm of the wheel, above the 107.7 rpm of the drive limit, so braking torque still rises across the base’s speed range, though not strictly in proportion to speed. A fast, high-ratio joint motor has no such guarantee.

Industrial machine stop categories and drive safety functions sequence these states. The machinery standard defines three stop categories ([[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/01 Machine Anatomy/02 The Physical Body/V4-02.13 Source References#^ref-iec60204|International Electrotechnical Commission 2016a]]): remove power at once, stop under control and then remove power, or stop under control and keep power applied. Each category still requires specific physical evidence before a safety case can credit it (**[[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/03 Running the Machine/12 Safety Enforcement/V4-12.10 The Fallback Ladder#^tbl-12-iec60204-stopping-categories|Stop functions and physical response]]**). Beneath every category lies one physical fact. Once torque is removed, a joint enters one of the three unpowered states above. A drive’s Safe Torque Off (STO) therefore leaves a moving joint in free coast until a brake engages. The trajectory a machine follows after an electrical cutoff must be measured under full mechanical payload, not deduced from circuit diagrams. The choice of which safe fallback state the machine should enter belongs to system safety architecture, but the physical dynamics of an unpowered joint are unalterable mechanical constraints of the body.

↪ The placement of Safe Torque Off in the machine’s hardwired interlock is developed in **[[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/01 Machine Anatomy/04 The Nervous System/V4-04.07 Actuation Authority#^sec-nervous-actuation-authority|Actuation Authority]]**.

### Regenerative Overvoltage Surges and Dynamic Brake Choppers

^sec-body-regenerative-surges

Regenerative deceleration drives the bus toward its opposite boundary, overvoltage, as part of the machine’s kinetic energy returns electrically. Returned braking energy needs an absorption or dissipation path, and a battery cannot be assumed to accept it under every state of charge, temperature, or protection state. If the battery management system disconnects charge input, the remaining bus components must handle the regenerated energy without exceeding their voltage ratings.

Under the conservative assumption that the battery management system has disconnected and the local bus capacitance ${C_{\text{bus}}}$ must absorb the entire returned kinetic energy ${E_{\text{regen}}}$ without dissipation, the terminal DC bus voltage rises to:

$$
{V_{\text{final}}} = \sqrt{{V_{\text{initial}}}^2 + \frac{2 {E_{\text{regen}}}}{{C_{\text{bus}}}}} \tag{10}
$$

 where ${V_{\text{initial}}}$ is the bus voltage when deceleration begins (**[[05 Reference/Volume IV - Physical AI/V4 Systems and Hardware#^sec-appendix-systems-power|DC Bus Power Distribution and Energy Absorption]]** derives this energy balance). The worst case is a full pack, the state in which the battery management system refuses further charge.

^eq-body-regen-voltage

The capacitor-only endpoint is a screening calculation: if it exceeds a component rating, the stop requires another energy path. A dynamic brake chopper switches an absorption resistor across the bus when voltage reaches a chosen clamp threshold. Its resistor must handle both the peak regenerated power and the pulse energy, with enough recovery between stops. The worked bus calculation below sizes the peak-power requirement for the warehouse machine.

Power integrity is an architectural constraint. The design sets coordinated power budgets and current slew-rate limits across accelerators and drives. The permission path arbitrates simultaneous multi-axis accelerations so that their $d{I}/d{t}$ transients do not compound. Brake choppers protect the inverters from regenerative feedback during emergency stops. Notebook [2.7](https://mlsysbook.ai/vol4/02_body/02_body.html#nbk-body-dc-bus-power-integrity) works both halves of that budget, the bus’s sag and the stop’s regenerative surge, on the mobile manipulator’s 48 V drive bus.

**Napkin Math 2.7: DC bus voltage sag and regenerative surge**

The mobile manipulator’s lithium-ion pack feeds a 47.7 V nominal drive bus, with the voltage envelope of a commercial 48 V-class mobile robot’s pack ([[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/01 Machine Anatomy/02 The Physical Body/V4-02.13 Source References#^ref-mir2025mir250|Mobile Industrial Robots 2025]]). The bus carries the wheel drives, the arm’s joint drives, and a 48-to-24 V converter that supplies the application processor and control electronics. The converter shuts off when its input falls to 33 V ([[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/01 Machine Anatomy/02 The Physical Body/V4-02.13 Source References#^ref-meanwell2026ddr240|MEAN WELL 2026]]).

**Problem**: Under the coincident current step of an inference burst and the drive motors, and then a stop from the drive limit, does the bus remain within the operating limits of the converter and the motor inverters?

**Math**:

1.  **Parameters**:
    - Machine mass, travel speed, and credible deceleration: ${m} =$ 368 kg (upper bound, full tote rack), ${v} =$ 1.5 m/s, ${a_{\text{brake}}} =$ 2 m/s²
    - Bus characteristics: pack voltage from 54 V at full charge to 42 V depleted, source resistance ${R_{\text{bus}}} =$ 60 mΩ (illustrative), bulk link capacitance ${C_{\text{bus}}} =$ 22,000 μF
    - Operating limits: converter lockout ${V_{\text{uvlo}}} =$ 33 V, converter continuous input maximum 67.2 V, inverter switch rating ${V_{\text{breakdown}}} =$ 100 V
2.  **Sustained-load screen**:
    - As an illustrative coincident load, the two drive motors and the arm’s joint drives add 68.6 A while the application processor, drawing its 60 W thermal design power (TDP) through the converter at 91 percent efficiency, adds 1.4 A. Their aggregate increase is ${\Delta} {I} =$ 70 A. Assume the stated pack voltage is the unloaded source voltage and this coincident demand is the total load in the screen.
    - After source current settles to the increased load, the additional resistive drop is:

$$
\Delta V_{\text{sustained}} = \Delta I R_{\text{bus}} = 4.2 V
$$

    - The resulting sustained bus voltage at full charge and when depleted is:

$$
V_{\text{bus, sustained}} = 54 V - 4.2 V = 49.8 V \text{ (full)}, \quad 42 V - 4.2 V = 37.8 V \text{ (depleted)}
$$

    - Even from the depleted pack, the bus stays 4.8 V above the converter’s lockout; it would take a 150 A step to spend the 9 V between the depleted pack and the lockout. This screen does not establish the transient minimum, which inductance and capacitor depletion push lower.
3.  **Regenerative surge**:
    - During a stop from the drive limit, the loaded machine’s kinetic energy must be dissipated:

$$
E_k = \tfrac{1}{2} m v^2 = \tfrac{1}{2}\,(368 kg)(1.5 m/s)^2 = 414 J
$$

    - A full pack is the worst case, because that is when the battery management system refuses charge. If it disconnects charge input and an illustrative 75 percent of kinetic energy returns to the bus (${E_{\text{regen}}} =$ 310.5 J), an unprotected, lossless capacitor-only energy balance would end at:

$$
V_{\text{final}} = \sqrt{V_{\text{full}}^2 + \frac{2 E_{\text{regen}}}{C_{\text{bus}}}} \approx 176.5 V
$$

    - *Failure mechanism*: Raising the capacitor from full charge to the 60 V clamp absorbs only 7.5 J of the 310.5 J, so long before the bus reached that endpoint it would cross the converter’s input maximum and then the 100 V switch rating.
4.  **Dynamic braking clamp**:
    - Under constant deceleration over ${T_{\text{stop}}} =$ 750 ms, speed falls linearly, so the regenerated power, force times speed, falls linearly from its peak to zero. The peak is twice the average ${E_{\text{regen}}}/{T_{\text{stop}}}$:

$$
P_{\text{regen, peak}} \approx \frac{2 E_{\text{regen}}}{t_{\text{stop}}} = 828 W
$$

    - The clamp ${V_{\text{clamp}}} =$ 60 V sits above the full-charge voltage, so it never fires while charging, and within the converter’s input range. To hold it there, the brake chopper must divert this peak power through an absorption resistor. A resistor across the bus at the clamp voltage dissipates ${V_{\text{clamp}}}^2/{R_{\text{brake}}}$, which must be at least the peak power:

$$
R_{\text{brake}} \le \frac{V_{\text{clamp}}^2}{P_{\text{regen, peak}}} \approx 4.35 Ω
$$

**Result**: The sustained-load screen leaves the bus at 49.8 V from a full pack and 37.8 V from a depleted one, 4.8 V above the converter lockout. Unbuffered regenerative braking would drive the bus past the converter’s input maximum and the 100 V switch rating, which calls for a dynamic brake chopper whose resistor is no larger than 4.35 Ω and absorbs an 828 W peak (satisfied by an illustrative 3.9 Ω, 1 kW component).

**Systems insight**: Mechanical motion and electrical power are bidirectionally coupled. Acceleration requires a supply that sustains the coincident current demand; deceleration requires an adequate absorption or dissipation path. On this 48 V bus the depleted pack still sits 9 V above the converter lockout, more than twice this step’s resistive drop, so the sustained screen passes. The transient minimum, pulse energy, repetition, cooling, and component tolerances still require qualification.

**Checkpoint 2.2: Freshness, stopping, heat, and supply**

The actuator budget was checked in checkpoint [2.1](https://mlsysbook.ai/vol4/02_body/02_body.html#chk-body-actuator-limits). Before measuring these limits on an assembled machine, verify your understanding of the other four:

**Freshness deadline**: Can you calculate the maximum allowable measurement age for the mug riding the pick-station conveyor at 0.20 m/s, with a 15 mm grasp tolerance of which the initial estimate already spends 3 mm? Can you explain why that age is counted from exposure on the sensor rather than from the start of inference, and why the base’s stop for a standing person instead charges age as blind travel?

**Velocity ceiling**: Can you solve ${d_{\text{stop}}} \le {D_{\text{clear}}}$ for the admissible speed on the running machine’s numbers, and explain why doubling the pre-brake delay expands the stopping distance linearly while doubling travel speed expands the braking term quadratically?

**Thermal horizons and runaway**: Can you explain why an actuator can produce three times its rated torque for seconds but overheats its winding insulation if held there for minutes, and distinguish a sustained stall that reaches an excessive finite equilibrium from one in which copper’s resistance feedback causes thermal runaway?

**Rail sag and regeneration**: Can you explain how a coincident compute and drive current step sags a shared rail through resistance, inductance, and capacitor depletion, and why a stop from speed pumps energy back into the bus that a brake chopper must divert?

Preserving power integrity ensures that transient acceleration spikes do not trigger brownout resets and that regenerative braking does not overvoltage the bus, completing the analytical formulation of the five physical budgets. Calculating these envelopes from datasheets and circuit models, however, cannot guarantee operational safety on a physical robot. In deployment, source impedances rise as batteries age, contact friction varies across facility floors, and enclosed packaging degrades thermal dissipation. Defending these budgets in production requires measuring what the integrated hardware actually delivers under live operational stress.
