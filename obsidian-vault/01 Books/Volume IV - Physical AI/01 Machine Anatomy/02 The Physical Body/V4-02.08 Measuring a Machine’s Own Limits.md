# V4-02.08 Measuring a Machine’s Own Limits

[[Home|Home]] · [[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/Volume 4 Overview|Volume Overview]] · [[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/01 Machine Anatomy/02 The Physical Body/V4-02 The Physical Body|V4-02 The Physical Body]]

^sec-body-measuring-limits

A brake that stops an unloaded base on a cool laboratory floor has not established how the loaded mobile manipulator stops after repeated maneuvers. Characterization must connect each calculation to measurements under the conditions in which the machine will use it. A dynamometer, a test stand that applies a controlled mechanical load, isolates actuator behavior, just as the instrumented propulsion test stand in [[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/01 Machine Anatomy/02 The Physical Body/V4-02.08 Measuring a Machine’s Own Limits#^fig-02-real-motor-dyno-bench|figure 7]] evaluates an assembled electromechanical powertrain under live aerodynamic and structural loads. Integrated trials then establish what changes when the actuator shares a chassis, cooling path, and power supply with the rest of the machine. A defensible limit states what was measured, how uncertain the result is, and which conditions must remain true for a later decision to rely on it.

### Dynamometer Testing and Instrument Intrusion

^sec-body-dynamometer-characterization

On a dynamometer, a controlled load machine resists the actuator while torque, speed, current, and voltage are measured on a shared timebase. Four tests connect the earlier models to measurable parameters:

- **Torque and back-EMF transduction (${K_t}$ and ${K_e}$)**: By commanding the inverter’s Field-Oriented Control (FOC) current loop across a sweep of quadrature phase currents ${i_q}$ while the dynamometer absorbs torque at constant speed, engineers measure electromechanical torque transduction:

$$
{\tau_{\text{motor}}} = {K_t} {i_q}
$$

 Driving the unpowered motor at known speed and measuring open-circuit voltage supplies the corresponding back-EMF constant ${K_e} = {e_{\text{bemf}}} / {\omega_m}$. These tests establish the conversion factors used in [[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/01 Machine Anatomy/02 The Physical Body/V4-02.05 Actuator Transmission Limits#^sec-body-electrical-time-constants|section 2.4.2]].

- **Electrical rise time and inductive bandwidth (${\tau_e} = {L_{\text{phase}}}/{R_{\text{phase}}}$)**: With the rotor held so that the test current produces no torque, the inverter applies a small voltage step ${V_{\text{step}}}$ across the winding. The current response:

$$
{i_{\text{phase}}}({t}) = \frac{{V_{\text{step}}}}{{R_{\text{phase}}}}\left(1 - e^{-{t}/{\tau_e}}\right)
$$

 reveals the electrical time constant. The step must stay small, because its steady current ${V_{\text{step}}}/{R_{\text{phase}}}$ flows through a winding of low resistance: the full 42 V of a depleted pack across a hub motor’s 0.7 Ω would drive 60 A, 2.4× the motor’s peak current. The current probe and acquisition path must resolve the rise; otherwise the instrument smooths the response being measured and hides the actuator limit.

- **Enclosed thermal resistance (${R_{\text{th}}}$) and continuous torque ceilings**: A heating trial under the installed cooling conditions measures the winding temperature rise and loss power, establishing ${R_{\text{th}}} = {\Delta T} / {P_{\text{loss}}}$. The continuous torque ceiling from [[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/01 Machine Anatomy/02 The Physical Body/V4-02.06 Thermal Duty Cycles#^sec-body-thermal-capacity|section 2.5.1]] is then:

$$
{\tau_{\text{cont}}} = {K_t} \sqrt{\frac{{T_{\text{limit}}} - {T_{\text{amb}}}}{{R}({T_{\text{limit}}}) {R_{\text{th}}}}}
$$

 The test must reproduce the enclosure and neighboring heat loads under which that ceiling will be used. An open-bench rating overstates continuous capacity whenever the installed shroud restricts airflow or adjacent actuators warm the mounting frame.

- **Backdrive friction and impact dynamics**: By driving the actuator output shaft with the dynamometer load motor, the testbed measures the torque needed to backdrive the joint across speed, load, and temperature:

$$
{\tau_{\text{ext}}} = {N_{\text{gear}}}^2 {J_{\text{rotor}}} {\ddot{q}} + {\tau_{\text{fric}}}
$$

 The measured response establishes whether the transmission can yield under the intended external load and maps the friction term that [[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/01 Machine Anatomy/02 The Physical Body/V4-02.05 Actuator Transmission Limits#^sec-body-actuation-regimes|section 2.4.5]] shows is not a constant. Contact qualification must additionally exercise the relevant compliance and impact conditions. The backdrive model alone does not predict tooth failure.

Measurement can change the plant. A series current shunt lowers the supplied voltage, and an embedded temperature probe changes a local heat-flow path. These effects belong in the uncertainty budget along with sensor resolution, bandwidth, and clock alignment.

The sensor mechanisms in [[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/01 Machine Anatomy/02 The Physical Body/V4-02.03 Measurement Freshness#^sec-body-sensor-transduction|section 2.2.1]] also affect characterization. Velocity estimated from encoder samples by backward differencing trades resolution against latency, and that trade-off must enter the reported uncertainty and response time. Vibration above the sensor’s sampled bandwidth can likewise appear as slower motion:

$$
{f_{\text{alias}}} = |{f_{\text{vib}}} - {k} {f_s}|
$$

 The integer ${k}$ identifies the sampling-frequency multiple that folds the vibration into the observed band. Qualification must distinguish that measurement artifact from the motion used to establish the limit.

A limit means nothing without the context of its test. Every recorded limit states the payload, ambient temperature, supply voltage, enclosure state, firmware version, and concurrent computational workload of the run. A thermal time constant measured on an open bench does not transfer to the same actuator in a sealed fairing in sunlight, because enclosing the motor raises ${R_{\text{th}}}$ and with it ${\tau_{\text{th}}} = {R_{\text{th}}} {C_{\text{th}}}$. The hotter ambient also shrinks the margin below the insulation rating. A rail characterization that omits whether vision-language model inference ([[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/01 Machine Anatomy/02 The Physical Body/V4-02.13 Source References#^ref-brohan2023rt1|Brohan, Brown, Carbajal, Chebotar, Dabis, et al. 2023]]; [[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/01 Machine Anatomy/02 The Physical Body/V4-02.13 Source References#^ref-brohan2023rt2|Brohan, Brown, Carbajal, Chebotar, Chen, et al. 2023]]) was running during motor stalls describes an idle board rather than the combined draw of full compute and peak torque.

### Zero-Failure Bounds and Wear Over Life

^sec-body-statistical-bounds

The absence of a failure during testing bounds the failure rate statistically; it does not establish safety. If the mobile manipulator completes ${n} =$ 3,000 loaded stops without a brownout or an overrun, the data support only a per-stop failure rate below roughly $3/{n}$, the rule of three, which is about one stop in 1,000 at 95 percent confidence. The bound holds only if the trials are independent and stationary, so that one trial does not influence another and the failure probability stays the same from trial to trial.

↪ The rule of three, and the trial campaigns it sizes, is developed in **[[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/02 Teaching the Machine/07 Closed-Loop Evaluation/V4-07.07 Confidence from a Finite Campaign#^sec-evaluation-closed-loop-survival|Confidence from a Finite Campaign]]**.

Thermal history and wear can change the conditions between trials:

- **Heating across consecutive trials**: Repeated stops heat the windings, changing resistance and the available torque response. Trials taken at different starting temperatures do not characterize the same operating condition.
- **Rising battery resistance over life**: Battery aging increases source resistance, reducing the voltage margin under a current step.
- **Growing gearbox backlash**: Transmission wear changes reversal response and tracking error, invalidating a limit measured on a less worn assembly.

These changes make characterization a standing obligation. **Lifecycle degradation** changes the conditions behind a limit, so its record must identify when recalibration or a reduced operating envelope is required.

### The Six Elements of a Limit Record

^sec-body-limit-record-schema

The stopping and power calculations become usable by other layers only when their assumptions travel with their values. A **limit record** carries those assumptions together with the evidence that supports the limit. The learned proposer uses the limits to constrain candidate actions, while the independent permission path checks them before actuation. Recording a value does not certify it: the illustrative entries below still require measurements and uncertainty estimates before they can support operation. The six elements in [[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/01 Machine Anatomy/02 The Physical Body/V4-02.08 Measuring a Machine’s Own Limits#^tbl-02-limit-record|table 3]] make that missing evidence visible for the mobile manipulator’s credible deceleration, the drive torque ceiling that must support it, and the drive bus’s undervoltage lockout.

↪ The handoff record that carries each limit record to the permission path is developed in **[[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/01 Machine Anatomy/04 The Nervous System/V4-04.10 The Handoffs Matrix#^sec-nervous-handoffs-matrix|The Handoffs Matrix]]**.

Two of the elements are easily conflated. Uncertainty describes what is not known about the measurement (observation noise, instrument tolerance, sensor bandwidth, and calibration drift). Margin is the physical clearance the machine defends against what is not known. The provenance element then says how far the value itself can be trusted, by naming its evidence category. Every number that governs a machine falls in one of five categories: a direct measurement, a value derived from measurements through a stated model, a datasheet rating, an illustrative example, or a budget the designer chose. Only the first two can support a safety margin or a timeout. A value computed from datasheet inputs, such as the drive torque ceiling below, keeps the datasheet’s weaker standing until measurements replace its inputs. A chosen budget, such as the protective clearance ${\delta_{\text{margin}}}$, binds only once it is checked against the measured number it must fit.

The detector element matters most once the machine leaves the test stand, because operation can carry a limit outside the conditions under which it was established. The saturation duration against which the Schiaparelli lander’s inertial unit had been accepted was such a limit. The lander’s loss in 2016 shows what follows when a limit’s conditions are exceeded in operation and nothing downstream checks the result.

**War Story 2.1: ExoMars Schiaparelli IMU saturation (2016)**

**Context**: On October 19, 2016, the European Space Agency’s ExoMars Schiaparelli lander executed its atmospheric entry and descent toward the Martian surface, relying on an onboard Guidance, Navigation, and Control (GNC) computer to fuse Inertial Measurement Unit (IMU) gyroscopes with a Doppler radar altimeter ([[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/01 Machine Anatomy/02 The Physical Body/V4-02.13 Source References#^ref-esa2017schiaparelli|ESA Investigation Board 2017]]).

**Mechanism**: About three minutes after atmospheric entry, the parachute inflated and set the lander oscillating. The IMU measured a pitch rate larger than expected and raised a saturation flag, which persisted longer than the 15 ms assumed when the unit was accepted. While the flag was set, the GNC software integrated the saturation threshold rate as though it were the true rate, although the lander was in reality oscillating, and its attitude estimate drifted by about $165^{\circ}$, nearly upside down. When the radar altimeter later returned valid slant ranges, the software projected them onto that attitude and computed a negative altitude. No onboard check tested that altitude for plausibility.

**Impact**: Taking the negative altitude as a valid estimate, the guidance logic released the parachute and backshell, fired the braking thrusters for $3\text{ seconds}$ instead of the planned $30\text{ seconds}$, and activated its on-ground systems as if it had landed. The lander was still about $3.7\text{ km}$ above the surface. It fell freely for about half a minute and struck the ground at about $150\text{ m/s}$ ($540\text{ km/h}$).

**Response**: The ESA inquiry board recommended robust sanity checks in the onboard software, among them that altitude cannot be negative and cannot change from $3.7\text{ km}$ to a negative value in one second, and a design that handles IMU saturation explicitly, for example by inhibiting IMU inputs during parachute deployment.

**Systems lesson**: A physical body cannot transcend the saturation ranges of its sensory transducers. When a sensor’s physical limit (a gyroscope’s rate ceiling or an accelerometer’s dynamic range) is exceeded, a state estimator that integrates the saturated measurement as if it were valid drifts away from the physical state. The saturation duration against which the IMU had been accepted was a limit with conditions, and once descent exceeded them, no detector checked the estimate before it commanded an irreversible action.

**Checkpoint 2.3: Limit records**

A limit governs a decision only together with the conditions, evidence, and detector that travel with it.

**Uncertainty vs. margin**: Can you distinguish the uncertainty of a measured deceleration from the margin the machine defends, and explain why only a measured value, or one derived from measurements, can support that margin?

**Zero-failure bound**: Can you estimate how many failure-free loaded stops would bound the per-stop failure rate below one in 10,000 at 95 percent confidence, and state the two assumptions about the trials that the bound requires?

**Record conditions**: Can you name the condition of the credible-deceleration record that a lowered drive current limit, the inspected floor, and an arm extended forward would each violate?

**Detector**: Can you explain why the Schiaparelli lander’s accepted saturation duration failed as a limit in flight, and which detector in [[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/01 Machine Anatomy/02 The Physical Body/V4-02.08 Measuring a Machine’s Own Limits#^tbl-02-limit-record|table 3]] plays the missing role for the credible deceleration?

Measured and recorded one at a time, the five limits describe the body budget by budget, each under its own conditions. In operation, however, a single maneuver draws on all five budgets at once, and a change in one budget’s state can erode the margin of another. Which limit binds first therefore depends on both the machine’s physical class and the specific command under evaluation.
