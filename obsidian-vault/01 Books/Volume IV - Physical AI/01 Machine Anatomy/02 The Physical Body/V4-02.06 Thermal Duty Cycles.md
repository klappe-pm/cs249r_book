# V4-02.06 Thermal Duty Cycles

[[Home|Home]] · [[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/Volume 4 Overview|Volume Overview]] · [[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/01 Machine Anatomy/02 The Physical Body/V4-02 The Physical Body|V4-02 The Physical Body]]

^sec-body-thermal-duty-cycles

Holding a load motionless at full reach places deceptive demands on an articulated arm such as the mobile manipulator’s. Maintaining a static load against gravity demands continuous phase current through the stator coils, actively converting electrical energy into accumulating heat. Heat arrives in seconds and leaves in minutes, making duty cycle a primary design variable.

### Thermal Capacity and Insulation Breakdown Thresholds

^sec-body-thermal-capacity

Although a stationary joint performs zero mechanical work (${W} = \int {F} \cdot d{x} = 0$), sustaining its holding torque against gravity requires continuous phase current to produce magnetic Lorentz force (${\tau_{\text{motor}}} = {K_t} {i_q}$). The lumped thermal model uses ${i_q}$ in the same convention as its torque constant ${K_t}$. Here ${R_{\text{eff}}}$ denotes the effective aggregate loss resistance for that current, so total winding copper loss is:

$$
{P_{\text{loss}}} = {i_q}^2 {R_{\text{eff}}} = \frac{{\tau_{\text{motor}}}^2}{{K_t}^2} {R_{\text{eff}}}
$$

 For the amplitude-invariant drive convention above, with balanced phase currents and ${i_d}=0$, this effective resistance is ${R_{\text{eff}}}=\frac{3}{2}{R_{\text{phase}}}$, where ${R_{\text{phase}}}$ is resistance per phase. Three resistances of one winding therefore appear in this chapter. The phase resistance ${R_{\text{phase}}}$ belongs in the Kirchhoff equation of [[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/01 Machine Anatomy/02 The Physical Body/V4-02.05 Actuator Transmission Limits#^sec-body-electrical-time-constants|section 2.4.2]]. Datasheets quote the line-to-line value measured between two terminals, which for a star-connected winding spans two phases, $2{R_{\text{phase}}}$. The hub-motor current-rise example used that line value with the line inductance, whose ratio gives the same time constant. The thermal calculations use ${R_{\text{eff}}} = \frac{3}{2}{R_{\text{phase}}}$, three quarters of the line-to-line value, and every winding resistance in them, including ${R}({T_{\text{winding}}})$, is this effective resistance. The following illustrative thermal examples stipulate it and the matching current convention.

The copper-loss equation ties torque to current and current to heat. Commanded torque scales linearly with current (${\tau_{\text{motor}}} = {K_t} {i_q}$), but stator copper loss scales quadratically with torque (${\tau_{\text{motor}}}^2$). Demanding twice the torque requires four times the thermal dissipation. Because heat has no fast exit, thermal accumulation limits how long an arm can maintain static poses or execute dynamic bursts without mechanical brakes.

Reducing motor current to shed heat also reduces holding torque. A loaded arm therefore needs a response that preserves support, such as transferring the load to a mechanical brake, rather than assuming that thermal derating leaves the task unchanged. Excess winding temperature can damage electrical insulation, while excess rotor temperature can permanently reduce magnet strength. Those material limits bound the allowable temperature trajectory.

The winding and housing respond on different timescales. Copper heats rapidly under current, while the larger core and housing absorb and reject heat more slowly. A case temperature measurement can therefore lag the winding hotspot during a torque burst. The stator in [[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/01 Machine Anatomy/02 The Physical Body/V4-02.06 Thermal Duty Cycles#^fig-02-real-burnt-motor-stator|figure 5]] failed from overheating, and its damage sits where this model puts the heat: in the copper windings and the insulation between them.

A thermal model follows the same energy accounting as the stopping calculation: energy accumulates where the rate entering exceeds the rate leaving. Here the winding receives electrical losses and transfers heat to the core; the core transfers heat to the surroundings. Thermal capacitance measures how much energy raises a component’s temperature, while thermal resistance measures how strongly the heat-flow path resists that transfer. Representing the winding and core separately gives the two-node lumped-parameter thermal ordinary differential equation (ODE) system:

$$
\begin{aligned} {C_{\text{wind}}} \frac{d{T_{\text{winding}}}({t})}{d{t}} &= {P_{\text{loss}}}({t}) - \frac{{T_{\text{winding}}}({t}) - {T_{\text{core}}}({t})}{{R_{\text{th,wc}}}} \\ {C_{\text{core}}} \frac{d{T_{\text{core}}}({t})}{d{t}} &= \frac{{T_{\text{winding}}}({t}) - {T_{\text{core}}}({t})}{{R_{\text{th,wc}}}} - \frac{{T_{\text{core}}}({t}) - {T_{\text{amb}}}}{{R_{\text{th,ca}}}} \end{aligned}
$$

 where ${P_{\text{loss}}}({t}) = {i_q}^2({t}) {R}({T_{\text{winding}}}) = \frac{{\tau_{\text{motor}}}^2({t})}{{K_t}^2} {R}({T_{\text{winding}}})$ is the Joule dissipation source deposited directly into the copper coils, ${C_{\text{wind}}}$ is the small thermal capacitance of the winding, ${C_{\text{core}}}$ is the large thermal capacitance of the laminated stator core and housing, ${R_{\text{th,wc}}}$ is the conductive thermal resistance from winding to core, and ${R_{\text{th,ca}}}$ is the convective thermal resistance from core to ambient air.

This separation of timescales creates a dangerous observability gap. Because ${C_{\text{wind}}} \ll {C_{\text{core}}}$, under a sudden peak torque burst or sustained stall against gravity, the stator core temperature ${T_{\text{core}}}$ remains virtually clamped over the initial seconds by its large thermal inertia. The internal copper winding temperature surges at the initial adiabatic rate:

$$
\left.\frac{d{T_{\text{winding}}}}{d{t}}\right|_{t=0} \approx \frac{{P_{\text{loss}}}}{{C_{\text{wind}}}}
$$

 Under sustained peak torque, the internal copper hotspot ${T_{\text{winding}}}$ can climb past the 155 °C Class F rating within tens of seconds (notebook [2.5](https://mlsysbook.ai/vol4/02_body/02_body.html#nbk-body-thermal-burst-limits)) while an external thermistor bonded to the motor casing still reads far below the hotspot. The class temperature is not a cliff at which the insulation fails. IEC 60085 assigns it as the hot-spot temperature at which an insulation system reaches its rated life ([[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/01 Machine Anatomy/02 The Physical Body/V4-02.13 Source References#^ref-iec60085-2007|International Electrotechnical Commission 2007]]), and insulation aging[[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/01 Machine Anatomy/02 The Physical Body/V4-02.14 Footnotes#^fn13|¹³]] proceeds as a chemical reaction whose rate grows exponentially with temperature ([[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/01 Machine Anatomy/02 The Physical Body/V4-02.13 Source References#^ref-dakin1948electrical|Dakin 1948]]). Every excursion above the rating therefore spends insulation life faster than the rating assumes, and the accumulated aging culminates in the insulation charring and turn-to-turn electrical failures documented in [[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/01 Machine Anatomy/02 The Physical Body/V4-02.06 Thermal Duty Cycles#^fig-02-real-burnt-motor-stator|figure 5]]. A thermal budget must count accumulated exposure as well as the instantaneous peak.

A first-order approximation can summarize heating over a characterized operating range, with an effective winding temperature ${T_{\text{winding}}}$ and fitted thermal parameters:

$$
{C_{\text{th}}} \frac{d{T_{\text{winding}}}({t})}{d{t}} = {P_{\text{loss}}}({t}) - \frac{{T_{\text{winding}}}({t}) - {T_{\text{amb}}}}{{R_{\text{th}}}}
$$

 where ${R_{\text{th}}} = {R_{\text{th,wc}}} + {R_{\text{th,ca}}}$ represents the winding-to-ambient steady-state thermal resistance. The effective capacitance ${C_{\text{th}}}$ sets the fitted time constant ${\tau_{\text{th}}} = {R_{\text{th}}} {C_{\text{th}}}$. This approximation does not resolve a fast winding hotspot separately from the housing. Its transient predictions require validation before they can bound a burst.

At thermal steady state ($d{T_{\text{winding}}}/d{t} = 0$), heat generation balances heat dissipation to ambient:

$$
{P_{\text{loss,ss}}} =\frac{{T_{\text{limit}}} - {T_{\text{amb}}}}{{R_{\text{th}}}}
$$

 Substituting the copper loss identity ${P_{\text{loss}}} = \frac{{\tau_{\text{motor}}}^2}{{K_t}^2} {R}({T_{\text{limit}}})$ reveals the **continuous thermal torque ceiling**:

$$
{\tau_{\text{cont}}} = {K_t} \sqrt{\frac{{T_{\text{limit}}} - {T_{\text{amb}}}}{{R}({T_{\text{limit}}}) {R_{\text{th}}}}} \tag{7}
$$

 Continuous torque is limited by thermal dissipation, not by the peak current ratings of the drive electronics or the battery. The arm’s stand-in motor, for example, is rated for 4.05 N·m peak but only 1.25 N·m continuous, a ratio of 3.2 ([[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/01 Machine Anatomy/02 The Physical Body/V4-02.13 Source References#^ref-tq2014ilm|TQ-Systems 2014]]). A brief torque burst of several seconds causes a rapid transient temperature rise that stays within the insulation rating (${T_{\text{limit}}} =$ 155 °C) if its peak does. However, sustained holding torque or high-frequency cycling drives the winding toward an asymptotic thermal equilibrium ${T_\infty} = {T_{\text{amb}}} + {\bar{P}_{\text{loss}}} {R_{\text{th}}}$. If that equilibrium exceeds the rating, continuous operation ages the winding insulation far faster than its rated life allows and ends in phase-to-phase short circuits.

^eq-body-continuous-torque

The loss term must also change with temperature. Copper’s positive temperature coefficient (${\alpha_{\text{Cu}}} \approx 0.00393\text{ K}^{-1}$) increases the effective winding resistance by 47.2 percent from 20 °C to 140 °C, according to ${R}({T_{\text{winding}}}) = {R_0} [1 + {\alpha_{\text{Cu}}}({T_{\text{winding}}} - {T_0})]$. That upper temperature is the **engineering ceiling** ${T_{\text{ceil}}}$, a working limit chosen below the 155 °C insulation rating ${T_{\text{limit}}}$ to leave room for the error of the thermal model. The burst horizons and the stall derating below are checked against the rating itself. A schedule of repeated bursts is held to the ceiling, as is the installed continuous rating that the datasheet pitfall of [[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/01 Machine Anatomy/02 The Physical Body/V4-02.10 Fallacies and Pitfalls#^sec-body-fallacies-pitfalls|section 2.9]] obtains by evaluating [[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/01 Machine Anatomy/02 The Physical Body/V4-02.06 Thermal Duty Cycles#^eq-body-continuous-torque|equation 7]] at ${T_{\text{ceil}}}$ in place of ${T_{\text{limit}}}$. Holding the same torque at the ceiling therefore dissipates 47.2 percent more winding power than at the reference. A fixed-resistance calculation omits this feedback. The burst example below uses that approximation explicitly, and the subsequent stall calculation restores it.

Datasheet thermal ratings are optimistic because they are measured on an unconstrained motor mounted to a large heat sink in free air at $20^\circ\text{C}$ or $25^\circ\text{C}$. Installed in a sealed chassis beside power electronics, battery packs, and other joints, the motor loses convective airflow, which raises ${R_{\text{th}}}$. Neighboring loads raise its local ambient above the test ambient before its own coils carry current. Thermal margins must therefore be budgeted from heating and cooling time constants measured with the enclosure sealed and adjacent loads at their $\text{p99}$ power draw.

### Transient Thermal Bursts and Periodic Duty Cycles

^sec-body-transient-thermal-bursts

Because thermal capacity integrates power over time, a peak torque[[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/01 Machine Anatomy/02 The Physical Body/V4-02.14 Footnotes#^fn14|¹⁴]] or peak current rating is meaningless without four parameters: the initial winding temperature ${T_{\text{winding}}}(0)$, the allowed duration ${t_{\text{on}}}$, the ambient ${T_{\text{amb}}}$, and the recovery time ${t_{\text{off}}}$. An actuator that can produce three times its continuous torque sustains it only while the integral of copper loss (${P_{\text{loss}}} = {i_q}^2 {R_{\text{eff}}}$) stays within the margin below the insulation limit. From a cold winding, that can last tens of seconds, and from a heat-soaked one a fraction of that (notebook [2.5](https://mlsysbook.ai/vol4/02_body/02_body.html#nbk-body-thermal-burst-limits)).

**Napkin Math 2.5: Thermal accumulation across duty cycles**

This example uses the arm’s sourced stand-in joint of notebook [2.4](https://mlsysbook.ai/vol4/02_body/02_body.html#nbk-body-gear-ratio-cost): a CSG-25-50 strain-wave gear ([[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/01 Machine Anatomy/02 The Physical Body/V4-02.13 Source References#^ref-harmonicdrive2014csg2uh|Harmonic Drive AG 2014]]) driven by a TQ-RoboDrive ILM 70x18 frameless motor ([[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/01 Machine Anatomy/02 The Physical Body/V4-02.13 Source References#^ref-tq2014ilm|TQ-Systems 2014]]), whose rated current is ${I_{\text{cont}}} =$ 6.7 A. The motor has effective aggregate loss resistance ${R_{\text{eff}}} =$ 0.495 Ω, lumped thermal capacitance ${C_{\text{th}}} =$ 120 J/K, and installed thermal resistance ${R_{\text{th}}} =$ 2.5 K/W (both thermal values illustrative, because the motor’s data sheet leaves them to the installation). It runs in an enclosed ambient ${T_{\text{amb}}} =$ 40 °C with Class F insulation rated to 155 °C. The thermal time constant is ${\tau_{\text{th}}} = {R_{\text{th}}} {C_{\text{th}}} =$ 300 s.

**Problem**: How does the initial winding temperature change how long a threefold rated-current burst can last before the insulation limit?

**Math**:

1.  **Continuous equilibrium**: At ${I_{\text{cont}}} =$ 6.7 A the winding settles at ${T_{\text{eq}}} = {T_{\text{amb}}} + {I_{\text{cont}}}^2 {R_{\text{eff}}}\,{R_{\text{th}}} =$ 95.6 °C, below the limit.
2.  **Peak burst**: At ${I_{\text{peak}}} =$ 20.1 A the dissipation rises ninefold to ${P_{\text{peak}}} =$ 200.0 W, whose asymptote ${T_\infty} = {T_{\text{amb}}} + {P_{\text{peak}}} {R_{\text{th}}} =$ 540.0 °C lies far above the limit.
3.  **Survival horizons**: With ${T_{\text{winding}}}({t}) = {T_\infty} - ({T_\infty} - {T_{\text{winding}}}(0))\,e^{-{t}/{\tau_{\text{th}}}}$, the time to reach the limit is

$$
{t_{\text{limit}}} = {\tau_{\text{th}}} \ln\frac{{T_\infty} - {T_{\text{winding}}}(0)}{{T_\infty} - {T_{\text{limit}}}}
$$

 which gives 78.4 s from a cold start at ambient and 43.1 s from a heat-soaked start at ${T_{\text{eq}}}$.

**Result**: The heat-soaked joint holds the burst 45.1 percent less time than the cold one, so peak torque cannot be budgeted without tracking thermal history.

**Systems insight**: Peak torque is a stock of heat margin that thermal history spends, not a fixed rating. The admission check ${\hat{T}_{\text{wind}}} \le {T_{\text{limit}}}$ must use the current estimate of the winding temperature, and these first-order horizons are optimistic, because the winding hotspot of the two-node model runs ahead of the lumped temperature.

For repeated cycles of period ${t_{\text{cycle}}} = {t_{\text{on}}} + {t_{\text{off}}}$, the engineering ceiling ${T_{\text{ceil}}} =$ 140 °C sets a cycle-average power ceiling ${\bar{P}_{\max}} = ({T_{\text{ceil}}} - {T_{\text{amb}}})/{R_{\text{th}}} =$ 40 W. With peak dissipation during ${t_{\text{on}}}$ and none during ${t_{\text{off}}}$, this average-power screen bounds the duty cycle ${D_{\text{cycle}}} = {t_{\text{on}}}/{t_{\text{cycle}}}$ by

$$
{D_{\text{cycle}}} \le \frac{{T_{\text{ceil}}} - {T_{\text{amb}}}}{{P_{\text{peak}}} {R_{\text{th}}}} \tag{8}
$$

 which here is 20.0 percent, so a 3 s burst calls for a recovery of ${t_{\text{off}}} = {t_{\text{on}}}(1 - {D_{\text{cycle}}})/{D_{\text{cycle}}} \approx$ 12 s. The average does not bound the hottest instant. Because heat arrives in seconds during bursts but dissipates over minutes during recovery, the peak transient hotspot temperature during periodic pulsing strictly exceeds the time-averaged calculation,[[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/01 Machine Anatomy/02 The Physical Body/V4-02.14 Footnotes#^fn15|¹⁵]] reaching 142.0 °C (above the ceiling) before the rise of ${R}({T_{\text{winding}}})$ adds more. Average-power screening is optimistic, and a burst schedule is certified only against the transient hotspot trajectory.

### Torque-Speed Operating Envelopes and Back-EMF Roll-Off

^sec-body-torque-speed-envelopes

The burst calculation limits duration, while the back-EMF relation in [[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/01 Machine Anatomy/02 The Physical Body/V4-02.05 Actuator Transmission Limits#^sec-body-electrical-time-constants|section 2.4.2]] limits torque at speed. A feasible maneuver must satisfy both. At low speed, the drive can supply the current needed for a burst, but the winding temperature limits how long it may do so. At higher speed, back-EMF consumes voltage headroom and can prevent that current from appearing at all. On the arm’s stand-in joint motor, voltage headroom and thermal history bound the usable torque-speed region simultaneously ([[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/01 Machine Anatomy/02 The Physical Body/V4-02.06 Thermal Duty Cycles#^fig-02-motor-torque-speed-envelope|figure 6]]). Because steady-state operation at full supply voltage splits the bus voltage between the resistive drop ${i_q} {R_{\text{phase}}}$ and the back-EMF ${K_e} {\omega_m}$, rising torque demands more current, consumes a larger resistive voltage drop, and pulls the motor’s voltage-limited speed linearly down from 2,110 rpm at no load to 1,570 rpm at peak torque, while the burst that a cold winding sustains for 78.4 s reaches the insulation class in only 43.1 s from continuous-duty equilibrium.

^fig-02-motor-torque-speed-envelope

The continuous-duty region (${S}_1$) permits sustained operation under the declared cooling conditions. The larger intermittent region (${S}_3$) permits periodic bursts only with the starting temperature, duration, and recovery interval established above. Both regions narrow when speed consumes the drive’s voltage headroom. Their intersection with the current thermal state, rather than the catalog peak, gives the available torque for the next maneuver.

A simulator that uses only a fixed torque clamp can therefore teach a policy an unavailable action: maximum torque while the rotor is already near its voltage-limited speed, or a repeated burst without recovery. The actuator model and permission limits must include speed and thermal history. Temperature-dependent winding resistance further shrinks the available region during a sustained hold.

The standard remedy in legged-robot sim-to-real transfer replaces the clamp with an actuator model identified on the hardware. For the series elastic actuators of the ANYmal quadruped, Hwangbo et al. ([[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/01 Machine Anatomy/02 The Physical Body/V4-02.13 Source References#^ref-hwangbo2019learning|2019]]) trained a small network on a few minutes of logged joint data to map a short history of position errors and velocities to the torque the actuator actually delivered. The network then ran inside the simulator in place of an ideal actuator. The ideal model, which assumes any commanded torque appears instantly, failed to predict the measured torque. The same structure carries the envelope of this section into training: an actuator model that also takes speed, bus voltage, and winding temperature as inputs lets the simulator deny the torque the hardware cannot deliver.

### Copper Resistance Feedback and Stall Thermal Runaway

^sec-body-copper-resistance-feedback

The copper-resistance rise calculated in [[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/01 Machine Anatomy/02 The Physical Body/V4-02.06 Thermal Duty Cycles#^sec-body-thermal-capacity|section 2.5.1]] must enter the runtime estimate as well as the design calculation. An external thermistor can lag the winding hotspot, so a thermal observer combines measured current with the characterized heating and cooling model. A discrete update of the lumped energy balance is:

$$
{{\hat{T}_{\text{wind}}}{}}_{k+1} = {{\hat{T}_{\text{wind}}}{}}_{k} + \frac{{\Delta} {t}}{{C_{\text{th}}}} \left[ {{i_q}}_{k}^2 {R}({{\hat{T}_{\text{wind}}}{}}_{k}) - \frac{{{\hat{T}_{\text{wind}}}{}}_{k} - {T_{\text{amb}}}}{{R_{\text{th}}}} \right]
$$

 where ${R}({{\hat{T}_{\text{wind}}}{}}_{k}) = {R_0} [1 + {\alpha_{\text{Cu}}}({{\hat{T}_{\text{wind}}}{}}_{k} - {T_0})]$. The update interval, model error, and temperature uncertainty must fit the thermal margin. As the estimate approaches its ceiling, the permission path reduces admissible effort or transfers the load to a qualified support mechanism. Removing holding current without securing a gravity-loaded arm would exchange a thermal fault for uncontrolled motion. A static hold shows how copper’s feedback changes the sustainable torque (notebook [2.6](https://mlsysbook.ai/vol4/02_body/02_body.html#nbk-body-thermal-stall-derating)).

**Napkin Math 2.6: Static holding torque and stall derating**

The arm’s elbow, the same Panda-class stand-in joint as notebook [2.5](https://mlsysbook.ai/vol4/02_body/02_body.html#nbk-body-thermal-burst-limits), is asked to hold the arm’s maximum joint torque ${\tau_{\text{hold}}} =$ 87 N·m at stall (${\omega_m} = 0$) through its ${N_{\text{gear}}} =$ 50:1 strain-wave gear (${\eta_{\text{gear}}} =$ 0.85, illustrative). Its motor has ${K_t} =$ 0.187 N·m/A and cold effective aggregate loss resistance ${R_0} =$ 0.495 Ω at ${T_0} =$ 20 °C. The illustrative installed thermal resistance is ${R_{\text{th}}} =$ 1.80 K/W, lower than the 2.5 K/W of the burst example and so a better-cooled installation. The Class F limit is 155 °C, and the limb ambient is ${T_{\text{amb}}} =$ 40 °C.

**Problem**: With copper’s resistance rising with temperature, where does the stalled winding settle, and what holding torque is thermally sustainable?

**Math**:

1.  **Stall current**: ${\tau_{\text{motor}}} = {\tau_{\text{hold}}}/({\eta_{\text{gear}}} {N_{\text{gear}}}) =$ 2.05 N·m, so ${i_q} = {\tau_{\text{motor}}}/{K_t} =$ 10.9 A and the cold dissipation is ${P_0} = {i_q}^2 {R_0} =$ 59.3 W.

2.  **Runaway**: With ${R}({T_{\text{winding}}}) = {R_0}[1 + {\alpha_{\text{Cu}}}({T_{\text{winding}}} - {T_0})]$, the steady rise satisfies

$$
{\Delta T} = {i_q}^2 {R_0} \left[1 + {\alpha_{\text{Cu}}}({T_{\text{amb}}} - {T_0} + {\Delta T})\right] {R_{\text{th}}}
$$

 Rearranging terms yields ${\Delta T} = \frac{{i_q}^2 {R_0} [1 + {\alpha_{\text{Cu}}}({T_{\text{amb}}} - {T_0})] {R_{\text{th}}}}{1 - {i_q}^2 {R_0} {R_{\text{th}}} {\alpha_{\text{Cu}}}}$. The denominator establishes the **thermal runaway criterion**: if the positive feedback loop gain satisfies ${i_q}^2 {R_0} {R_{\text{th}}} {\alpha_{\text{Cu}}} \ge 1$, this fixed-current, linear-resistance model cannot settle to a stable thermal equilibrium. Above one, heat generation increases with temperature faster than heat removal, producing exponential growth. At exactly one, the temperature-dependent terms cancel and the remaining positive heating causes linear growth. In this stall scenario, the feedback factor is 0.4196 $< 1$, so temperature settles at a finite (yet fatal) equilibrium given by ${\Delta T} =$ 115.16 $+$ 0.4196 ${\Delta T}$. This yields ${\Delta T} =$ 198.4 K and drives the winding to 238.4 °C, far above its rating.

3.  **Derating**: At the limit the allowable rise is 115 K, so the sustainable dissipation is ${P_{\text{loss,ss}}} = {\Delta} {T_{\text{limit}}}/{R_{\text{th}}} =$ 63.89 W. At the hot resistance ${R}({T_{\text{limit}}}) \approx$ 0.758 Ω, that allows ${I_{\text{cont}}} = \sqrt{{P_{\text{loss,ss}}}/{R}({T_{\text{limit}}})} \approx$ 9.18 A and a hold of ${\tau_{\text{hold,max}}} = {\eta_{\text{gear}}} {N_{\text{gear}}} {K_t} {I_{\text{cont}}} \approx$ 73.0 N·m.

**Result**: Held without control, the stall drives the winding toward 238.4 °C, and staying within the insulation rating requires a 16.1 percent derating, from 87 N·m to 73.0 N·m.

**Systems insight**: At stall the motor does no mechanical work, so every watt it draws becomes heat, and copper’s rising resistance makes that heat grow with temperature. Continuous gravity holding above the thermally sustainable torque therefore needs another support mechanism, such as a friction brake or counterweight, in the premise the permission path admits; holding within that torque can remain electrically sustained under the stated thermal conditions.

Thermal capacity governs how long an actuator can sustain torque before winding insulation degrades or thermal runaway occurs, bounding continuous holds and transient bursts by the plant’s ability to reject heat. Motor windings, however, do not generate those thermal loads in isolation. Delivering the current pulses that drive these torque trajectories simultaneously stresses the upstream power distribution network, shifting the engineering constraint from heat dissipation in copper to transient voltage stability on the shared direct-current bus.
