# V4-03.05 The Cost of a Fresh Evaluation

[[Home|Home]] · [[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/Volume 4 Overview|Volume Overview]] · [[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/01 Machine Anatomy/03 The Cognitive Brain/V4-03 The Cognitive Brain|V4-03 The Cognitive Brain]]

^sec-brain-cognitive-pipeline

At the pick station the mobile manipulator’s arm consumes a new setpoint every few tens of milliseconds, while the mug keeps moving along the conveyor. Whether one large model could supply those setpoints depends on how long one fresh evaluation of it takes on the application processor, and that time depends on which resource the evaluation exhausts first, the memory bus or the arithmetic units. The intent model’s evaluation is priced first, together with what quantization can buy back; [[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/01 Machine Anatomy/03 The Cognitive Brain/V4-03.06 Chunk Supply and Evidence Validity#^sec-brain-chunk-supply|section 3.5]] then checks the motion buffer that the selected split relies on against the target’s drift. The single large-model cases below are alternatives used to expose the trade-off, not changes to the selected machine.

After ingestion (the tokenization of [[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/01 Machine Anatomy/03 The Cognitive Brain/V4-03.04 Learned Representations#^sec-brain-learned-representations|section 3.3]]), four learned stages turn those tokens into action proposals, and each hands the next a record that ages ([[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/01 Machine Anatomy/03 The Cognitive Brain/V4-03.05 The Cost of a Fresh Evaluation#^fig-cognitive-pipeline|figure 8]]). Across the proposal boundary, an isolated safety microcontroller (MCU) running the permission path on a 1 kHz tick decides whether candidate setpoints become admitted drive commands. These stages describe what the machine must compute, not how its networks are divided; the three learned stages of [[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/01 Machine Anatomy/03 The Cognitive Brain/V4-03.04 Learned Representations#^sec-brain-learned-representations|section 3.3]] describe the inside of one transformer. On the running machine, the intent model recognizes the mug and names the target (perception and intent), a tracker carries the mug’s estimate from frame to frame (memory), and the chunk policy, reading its own camera frames, turns the target into setpoints (planning). *Memory* here means the belief the machine keeps about the world, not DRAM. The chapters of Part III, beginning with **[[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/03 Running the Machine/08 Sensor Perception/V4-08 Sensor Perception#^sec-perception|Sensor Perception]]**, develop each stage in depth; here they matter only for what each hands downstream and how quickly that record goes stale.

Perception grounds what it recognizes in metric coordinates relative to the base, so that the mug has a 3D position and orientation (an ${SE(3)}$ pose)[[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/01 Machine Anatomy/03 The Cognitive Brain/V4-03.12 Footnotes#^fn6|⁶]] and a graspable handle rather than an abstract semantic label; its output ages from the moment of capture. Memory carries those estimates through occlusion and must let each one expire when the evidence behind it does. Intent decomposes a task such as “take the ceramic mug off the conveyor and hand it to the coworker at the packing station” into subgoals, and because the mug moves while the intent model deliberates, it hands the planner a target that expires, an intent lease in the vocabulary of Part III. Planning converts that target into candidate setpoints for the base and the arm, the proposals the permission path will judge.

↪ The intent lease and the deadlines that end it are developed in **[[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/03 Running the Machine/10 Grounded Intent/V4-10.09 The Intent Lease Specification and Contract#^sec-intent-lease|The Intent Lease Specification and Contract]]**.

These stages, and the ingestion before them, all run on the application processor and draw on the same unified memory bus, which is where the machine’s cadence is decided. On an edge robotics system-on-chip (SoC), memory bandwidth, arithmetic rate, and power envelope are fixed allocations, and model weights, sensor transfers, and host computation share that bandwidth. A policy cannot budget the full bus for itself while assuming that new camera frames arrive without interference, and scaling the model up only draws harder against the same ceiling. The transfer bound below is therefore a lower bound on evaluation time. The worked comparison later uses an assumed sustained fraction of peak bandwidth. **[[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/03 Running the Machine/13 Workload Placement/V4-13.04 Contention for Shared Resources#^sec-placement-resource-contention|Contention for Shared Resources]]** models how weight fetches, camera transfers, and telemetry writes queue for the shared memory controller.

↩︎ That every added layer of onboard compute draws against fixed memory bandwidth, electrical power, and thermal dissipation is established in **[[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/00 Introduction/01 The Causal Boundary/V4-01.11 The Four Bedrock Laws#^sec-boundary-bedrock-laws|The Four Bedrock Laws]]**.

Published measurements on the machine’s own module show how far apart those costs sit before any of that arithmetic. The MLPerf Inference edge suite times one query at a time, its single-stream scenario, for a fixed set of reference models ([[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/01 Machine Anatomy/03 The Cognitive Brain/V4-03.11 Source References#^ref-reddi2020mlperf|Reddi et al. 2019]]). Vendor submissions for the 64 GB Jetson AGX Orin place those reference latencies directly against the three cadences of the running machine ([[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/01 Machine Anatomy/03 The Cognitive Brain/V4-03.05 The Cost of a Fresh Evaluation#^fig-03-measured-orin-latency|figure 9]]).

Only three of the seven reference models answer inside the 50 ms chunk period: classification in 0.64 ms, question answering, and detection in 11.7 ms. A six-billion-parameter language model takes 4.2 s to produce one summary, 8× the 500 ms intent period, and the span from fastest to slowest is about 16,000×. The figure is a measured version of the two-speed argument. A language-model proposer on this module can revise intent only at a fraction of the rate the arm consumes setpoints. The same submission also shows that the cost is not fixed by the silicon alone. The language model’s latency was 10.2 s one round earlier on identical hardware, so the software stack sets the cost as much as the silicon does. Which bytes that software moves per evaluation is what the memory-wall analysis below quantifies.

### The Memory Wall

^sec-brain-memory-wall

A multi-billion-parameter model takes hundreds of milliseconds on a processor rated at tens of tera-operations per second. Its weights do not fit in on-chip static RAM (SRAM) or cache, so every fresh evaluation must stream them from off-chip low-power DRAM (LPDDR5) across a narrower shared bus. On-chip SRAM and caches can reuse weights and intermediate state without another external transfer only while the working set fits the memory allocated to that workload. Nominal chip-wide capacity does not guarantee that even a small action head stays resident when competing tasks share the processor. When operands cannot reach the arithmetic units as fast as those units can multiply them, memory bandwidth, not arithmetic speed, limits the update rate. That ceiling is the memory wall, and on a moving robot its cost is a delayed proposal while the body keeps traveling.

Consider a robot that generates continuous motor actions one discrete step at a time through an autoregressive foundation model, such as a multi-billion-parameter VLA. Its weights are *nonresident*, too large to stay in on-chip memory, so each token step streams them across the external DRAM bus. In single-token decoding with FP16 weights and request batch size ${B} = 1$, the dense weight projections become matrix-vector operations (${\mathbf{y}} = {\mathbf{W}}{\mathbf{x}}$), in which each fetched weight takes part in a single multiply-add before it is discarded. The bus, not the arithmetic, then sets the pace. The sustained memory bandwidth ${\text{BW}_{\text{mem}}}$ and model weight footprint ${M_{\text{weights}}}$ bound the maximum fresh-evaluation frequency, as [[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/01 Machine Anatomy/03 The Cognitive Brain/V4-03.05 The Cost of a Fresh Evaluation#^eq-brain-memory-wall|equation 2]] states:

$$
{f} \le \frac{{\text{BW}_{\text{mem}}}}{{M_{\text{weights}}}} \tag{2}
$$

 This bound must use the bandwidth actually available to the policy and the weights it transfers at the selected precision. It prices only the weight transfer. A fresh evaluation first reads its input tokens (image patches and the instruction) through the backbone in one pass, the **prefill**. That pass reuses every fetched weight across all of those tokens, so arithmetic rather than bandwidth can bind it. Notebook [3.1](https://mlsysbook.ai/vol4/03_brain/03_brain.html#nbk-brain-intent-model-latency) prices both terms for the mobile manipulator’s intent model. When a decoder emits one token per action dimension, each action dimension requires another token; if each token revisits off-chip parameters, that decode latency scales linearly with degrees of freedom.

^eq-brain-memory-wall

Single-token decoding at batch size 1 Servers can combine independent requests so that a weight matrix multiplies several input vectors at once, reusing fetched weights. A robot waiting for its next action token often has only one such request. Waiting for future observations to form a batch would add delay, and sequentially dependent action tokens cannot all be evaluated at once by an autoregressive decoder. This limits reuse in those projections. It does not make every operation in an embodied model a matrix-vector product. Image patches, multiple camera views, prompt tokens, and parallel action decoders can expose matrix-matrix work within one robot’s request. The one-use-per-fetch estimate therefore describes the single-token weight sweep, not the complete perception-and-action workload.

Whether a phase of the evaluation meets the memory wall or the arithmetic limit is decided by how much work it does per byte it moves, its **arithmetic intensity** (${I}$), defined in [[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/01 Machine Anatomy/03 The Cognitive Brain/V4-03.05 The Cost of a Fresh Evaluation#^eq-brain-arithmetic-intensity|equation 3]] as:

$$
{I} = \frac{\text{FLOPs}}{\text{Bytes transferred from off-chip memory}} \tag{3}
$$

 For the dense weight projections of single-token decoding at batch size 1, each a matrix-vector product ${\mathbf{y}} = {\mathbf{W}}{\mathbf{x}}$, counting weight traffic alone gives an optimistic estimate, because activation and context traffic add further bytes. In FP16 each weight occupies 2 bytes and participates in exactly one multiply-accumulate operation (2 FLOPs), so

$$
{I_{\text{GEMV, 16-bit}}} = \frac{2\,\text{FLOPs}}{2\,\text{Bytes}} = 1.0\,\text{FLOP/byte}
$$

 The roofline model ([[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/01 Machine Anatomy/03 The Cognitive Brain/V4-03.11 Source References#^ref-williams2009roofline|Williams et al. 2009]]) turns that ratio into a bound ([[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/01 Machine Anatomy/03 The Cognitive Brain/V4-03.05 The Cost of a Fresh Evaluation#^fig-patterson-roofline|figure 10]]). It plots attainable throughput against arithmetic intensity as two roofs. Below a crossover, the ridge point, throughput is capped by a sloped roof equal to bandwidth times the intensity; above it, by a flat roof equal to the processor’s peak arithmetic rate. It is read the same way as the stopping-budget plot of **[[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/00 Introduction/01 The Causal Boundary/V4-01.05 Defining the Causal Boundary#^sec-boundary-kinematic-roofline|Delay consumes stopping clearance]]**, where delay travel and braking travel trade dominance at a crossover speed. Both plots show two regimes and the point at which one gives way to the other.

The mobile manipulator’s application processor is a Jetson AGX Orin module (**[[05 Reference/Volume IV - Physical AI/V4 Systems and Hardware#^sec-appendix-systems-machine-data-sheet|The machine data sheet]]**). In its 60 W maximum-performance power mode, its dense FP16 GPU Tensor Core ceiling is 43 TFLOP/s; the aggregate sparse INT8 rating cannot be used for this FP16 workload. Using the registry’s rounded peak memory bandwidth of 204 GB/s, the hardware ridge point occurs at:

$$
I_{\text{ridge}} = \frac{R_{\text{peak}}}{\text{BW}} = \frac{43 TFLOP/s}{204 GB/s} \approx 210.8 FLOP/byte
$$

 For this weight-streaming example, ${I_{\text{GEMV, 16-bit}}} = 1.0\,\text{FLOP/byte}$ is far below 210.8 FLOP/byte, so memory bandwidth sets the roofline ceiling. Attainable throughput is bounded by the peak bandwidth times ${I_{\text{GEMV, 16-bit}}}$, or 0.204 TFLOP/s, about 0.47 percent of the dense FP16 peak; the sustained bandwidth that the notebook below uses lowers it further. That ratio is a workload throughput bound, not a measurement of how many cores are idle. Both roofs also assume the maximum-performance mode. A battery-powered base often runs a lower power mode, down to 15 W on this module, where lower clocks drop both the compute and the bandwidth roof; the chassis thermal budget sets the mode the machine can sustain.

**Definition 3.1: Memory wall**

***Memory wall*** is the limit on a workload’s rate that arises when its operands cannot reach the arithmetic units as fast as those units could consume them, so that the bytes moved from off-chip memory, not the operations performed, set the time per evaluation.

1.  **Significance**: A proposer whose weights do not stay on chip must stream them at least once per fresh evaluation, which gives [[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/01 Machine Anatomy/03 The Cognitive Brain/V4-03.05 The Cost of a Fresh Evaluation#^eq-brain-memory-wall|equation 2]]. On the machine’s application processor, the intent model’s 14 GB of FP16 weights at 142.8 GB/s sustained allow at most 10.2 Hz before any vision or decoding work, two orders of magnitude below the 1 kHz permission tick.
2.  **Distinction**: A compute bound yields to faster arithmetic; the memory wall yields only to fewer bytes per evaluation (fewer or narrower weights, or weights that stay resident on chip) or to more bandwidth. It is also distinct from memory capacity: a model can fit in DRAM and still be too slow to stream.
3.  **Common pitfall**: A frequent misconception is that a processor’s peak arithmetic rating predicts its batch-1 inference rate. Single-token weight streaming on this processor reaches only about 0.47 percent of its dense FP16 peak, so the rating overstates the attainable rate by orders of magnitude.

On the mobile manipulator’s application processor, then, the memory wall[[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/01 Machine Anatomy/03 The Cognitive Brain/V4-03.12 Footnotes#^fn7|⁷]] caps the intent model’s fresh-evaluation rate, before any vision or decoding work, far below the 100–1000 Hz at which stabilization, joint impedance control, and reflex-level collision avoidance run (notebook [3.1](https://mlsysbook.ai/vol4/03_brain/03_brain.html#nbk-brain-intent-model-latency)). Each of those loop rates is set by the physical time constant of the motion its loop must stabilize, a derivation carried out in **[[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/01 Machine Anatomy/04 The Nervous System/V4-04.04 Multi-Rate Cadences#^sec-nervous-cadences|Multi-Rate Cadences]]**. A model renewed that slowly can say where the arm should go, but it cannot close the loop on moving mass, so it proposes waypoints and a faster loop below it tracks them. That conclusion is about timing only. The separate requirement that learned outputs remain proposals, even when they arrive in time, comes from the failures of [[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/01 Machine Anatomy/03 The Cognitive Brain/V4-03.07 Cognitive Vulnerabilities#^sec-brain-cognitive-vulnerabilities|section 3.6]].

Repeated off-chip transfers also consume memory-system energy. Their power cost depends on the DRAM, controller, access pattern, and transfer rate, so a bandwidth calculation alone does not establish battery drain or actuator heating. Whatever the processor dissipates is heat released inside the same sealed chassis as the joint motors, and it raises the ambient temperature against which **[[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/01 Machine Anatomy/02 The Physical Body/V4-02.06 Thermal Duty Cycles#^sec-body-thermal-capacity|Thermal capacity and insulation breakdown thresholds]]** rates each motor’s continuous torque.

A fresh evaluation is not a single point on the roofline. Its prefill reuses each fetched weight across all of its input tokens and lands past the ridge, while its decode returns to the memory-bound point, one full weight sweep per token. For a decoder that emits ${S_{\text{out}}}$ tokens one after another, [[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/01 Machine Anatomy/03 The Cognitive Brain/V4-03.05 The Cost of a Fresh Evaluation#^eq-brain-eval-latency|equation 4]] adds the two phases:

$$
{t_{\text{infer}}} \approx \max\!\left(\frac{{O_{\text{prefill}}}}{{\eta_{\text{hw}}}\,{R_{\text{peak}}}},\ \frac{{M_{\text{weights}}}}{{\text{BW}_{\text{mem}}}}\right) + ({S_{\text{out}}}-1)\,\frac{{M_{\text{weights}}}}{{\text{BW}_{\text{mem}}}} \tag{4}
$$

 Here ${O_{\text{prefill}}}$ counts the prefill’s operations, ${R_{\text{peak}}}$ is the processor’s peak arithmetic rate at the activation precision, and ${\eta_{\text{hw}}}$ is the fraction of that rate the prefill achieves. The prefill cannot finish before its own weight sweep, hence the maximum; the prefill emits the first token, and each of the other ${S_{\text{out}}} - 1$ tokens costs one more sweep. Notebook [3.1](https://mlsysbook.ai/vol4/03_brain/03_brain.html#nbk-brain-intent-model-latency) prices both phases for the machine’s intent model.

^eq-brain-eval-latency

Prefill creates KV state that decode extends within one evaluation. [[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/01 Machine Anatomy/03 The Cognitive Brain/V4-03.05 The Cost of a Fresh Evaluation#^fig-03-request-local-prefill-decode|Figure 11]] follows a single observation through prefill, first-token sampling, and subsequent decode passes, then marks the boundary at which the running machine rebuilds its context.

**Napkin Math 3.1: Pricing one intent-model evaluation**

**Problem**: The mobile manipulator’s intent model is a 7B-parameter vision-language model sized after OpenVLA ([[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/01 Machine Anatomy/03 The Cognitive Brain/V4-03.11 Source References#^ref-kim2024openvla|Kim et al. 2024]]), running on the application processor. Where OpenVLA decodes a step of end-effector motion, the machine’s model decodes the same number of tokens to name a target pose ([[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/01 Machine Anatomy/03 The Cognitive Brain/V4-03.06 Chunk Supply and Evidence Validity#^sec-brain-proposer-interface|section 3.5.3]]). How long does one fresh evaluation take, which resource binds each part of it, and what renewal rate can the machine therefore state?

**Variables**:

- Parameters ${P} =$ 7B in ${N_L} =$ 32 layers of width ${d} =$ 4096 (Datasheet). The decoder emits ${S_{\text{out}}} =$ 7 tokens, one per output dimension, one after another (Datasheet).
- Input tokens ${S} =$ 288: 256 patches from one $224{\times}224$ view (Derived) plus 32 instruction tokens (Illustrative).
- Dense FP16 peak ${R_{\text{peak}}} =$ 43 TFLOP/s (Datasheet), of which the prefill achieves ${\eta_{\text{hw}}} =$ 50 percent (Illustrative).
- Peak bandwidth 204 GB/s (Datasheet), of which 70 percent is sustained (Illustrative), so ${\text{BW}_{\text{mem}}} =$ 142.8 GB/s.
- Stored weights: FP16 (2 bytes per parameter) or INT4 (half a byte), with activations kept in 16 bits (Chosen).

**Math**:

*The memory wall.* FP16 weights occupy 14 GB. If every evaluation streams them once, the bound above gives

$$
t_{\text{sweep}} = \frac{M_{\text{weights}}}{\text{BW}_{\text{mem}}} = \frac{14 GB}{142.8 GB/s} \approx 98.0 ms, \qquad f \le \frac{1}{t_{\text{sweep}}} \approx 10.2 Hz
$$

*The prefill binds on arithmetic.* All ${S}$ input tokens pass through the backbone together, costing two FLOPs per parameter per token (each weight multiplies one input value and adds the product to a running sum) plus a small attention term:

$$
O_{\text{prefill}} \approx 2 N S + 2 N_{L} d S^2 = 4.03 \times 10^{12} + 2.17 \times 10^{10} \approx 4.05 \times 10^{12}\ \text{FLOP}
$$

 Each fetched weight now serves ${S}$ tokens, so the intensity is about ${S} \approx$ 288 FLOP/byte, beyond the 210.8 FLOP/byte ridge of [[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/01 Machine Anatomy/03 The Cognitive Brain/V4-03.05 The Cost of a Fresh Evaluation#^fig-patterson-roofline|figure 10]]. Arithmetic binds, and the weight sweep runs underneath it:

$$
t_{\text{prefill}} = \frac{O_{\text{prefill}}}{\eta_{\text{hw}}\,R_{\text{peak}}} = \frac{4.05 \times 10^{12}\ \text{FLOP}}{0.5 \times 43 TFLOP/s} \approx 188.5 ms
$$

*The decode binds on bandwidth.* The prefill emits the first action token. Each of the remaining ${S_{\text{out}}} - 1 =$ 6 tokens is a matrix-vector pass that rereads every weight:

$$
t_{\text{FP16}} = t_{\text{prefill}} + (S_{\text{out}}-1)\,t_{\text{sweep}} \approx 189 ms + 6 \times 98.0 ms \approx 189 ms + 588 ms = 777 ms
$$

 That is about 1.3 Hz.

*INT4 weights shrink only the bandwidth term.* Storing the weights in four bits cuts them to 3.5 GB and each sweep to 24.5 ms, but the prefill still multiplies 16-bit activations at the FP16 rate:

$$
t_{\text{INT4}} \approx 189 ms + 6 \times 24.5 ms \approx 189 ms + 147 ms = 336 ms, \qquad f \le 2.98 Hz
$$

*Sensitivity.* Parallel decoding ([[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/01 Machine Anatomy/03 The Cognitive Brain/V4-03.11 Source References#^ref-kim2025openvlaoft|Kim et al. 2025]]) moves the action tokens into the prefill (${S} + {S_{\text{out}}} =$ 295) and removes the decode sweeps, giving 194 ms, only 2.9 percent inside a 200 ms period with the processor busy for all of it. Three camera views (${S} \approx$ 800) raise the INT4 total to 676 ms.

**Result**: At INT4 one evaluation takes about 336 ms. A published measurement of the same model and quantization on this processor reports 336 ms per frame ([[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/01 Machine Anatomy/03 The Cognitive Brain/V4-03.11 Source References#^ref-franklin2024openvla|Franklin 2024]]), a gap of 0.1 percent. That agreement is closer than two illustrative factors deserve. It shows that the model has the right terms, not that ${\eta_{\text{hw}}}$ and the sustained fraction are exactly right. A 3 Hz rate would need a 333 ms period, so the machine runs its intent model at 2 Hz, one evaluation per 500 ms.

**Systems insight**: The binding resource depends on the phase. Prefill reuses weights across tokens and is compute-bound; decode rereads them per token and is bandwidth-bound ([[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/01 Machine Anatomy/03 The Cognitive Brain/V4-03.11 Source References#^ref-pope2023efficiently|Pope et al. 2023]]). Weight-only quantization attacks only the second, so after INT4 the compute-bound prefill is most of the latency, and the next gain must come from fewer input tokens, lower-precision arithmetic that the processor’s kernels support, or a faster compute roof. [[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/01 Machine Anatomy/03 The Cognitive Brain/V4-03.05 The Cost of a Fresh Evaluation#^sec-brain-quantization|Section 3.4.2]] develops that choice and its accuracy cost.

### Quantizing the Intent Model

^sec-brain-quantization

The memory wall leaves two levers on the weight term, fewer weights or fewer bytes per weight. A smaller model may lose task recognition capability, which requires task evidence, so the selected design keeps the 7B-parameter model and pulls the second lever. **Quantization** stores each weight as a low-precision integer plus a shared scale, and reconstructs an approximate weight when the kernel needs it. In the common symmetric form, the weights are divided into groups of ${b_{\text{block}}}$, each group keeps one 16-bit scale ${s}$, and each weight ${w}_i$ becomes a ${b}$-bit integer ${q}_i$ ([[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/01 Machine Anatomy/03 The Cognitive Brain/V4-03.11 Source References#^ref-jacob2018quantization|Jacob et al. 2018]]; [[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/01 Machine Anatomy/03 The Cognitive Brain/V4-03.11 Source References#^ref-lin2024awq|Lin et al. 2024]]):

$$
{s} = \frac{\max_{i \in \text{group}} |{w}_i|}{2^{{b}-1} - 1}, \qquad {q}_i = \operatorname{clamp}\!\left(\operatorname{round}\!\left(\frac{{w}_i}{{s}}\right),\, -2^{{b}-1},\, 2^{{b}-1} - 1\right), \qquad {\hat{w}}_i = {s}\, {q}_i
$$

 Rounding moves each reconstructed weight ${\hat{w}}_i$ at most ${s}/2$ from ${w}_i$, so one large weight in a group coarsens the step for all the others; choosing which weights to protect from that error is where quantization methods differ. The stored size is ${b}/8$ bytes per weight plus the scales’ share. At ${b} = 4$ with the 128-weight groups that activation-aware weight quantization (AWQ) uses ([[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/01 Machine Anatomy/03 The Cognitive Brain/V4-03.11 Source References#^ref-lin2024awq|Lin et al. 2024]]), the scales add 3.1 percent to the four-bit payload, which the half-byte-per-parameter figure used here leaves out.

Two families of quantization act on different terms of the latency. *Weight-only* quantization, the machine’s choice, restores each weight to 16 bits inside the kernel just before the multiply. It shrinks the bytes every decode sweep rereads, but the arithmetic still runs at the 16-bit rate, so the compute-bound prefill does not move. *Weight-and-activation* quantization makes both operands integers, so the multiplications themselves run on integer units and can shorten the prefill as well. It pays off only when three conditions hold: the processor has fast integer units for that precision, the runtime has kernels for the model’s layer shapes, and the activations tolerate the coarser grid. A format without a kernel is only a smaller file. Evaluating the intent model at three weight precisions with the prefill-plus-decode model of notebook [3.1](https://mlsysbook.ai/vol4/03_brain/03_brain.html#nbk-brain-intent-model-latency) shows how closely the two-term estimate tracks measurements of the same model on the same module ([[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/01 Machine Anatomy/03 The Cognitive Brain/V4-03.05 The Cost of a Fresh Evaluation#^tbl-03-intent-quantization|table 1]]) ([[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/01 Machine Anatomy/03 The Cognitive Brain/V4-03.11 Source References#^ref-franklin2024openvla|Franklin 2024]]).

The computed and measured columns agree within 2.5 percent for the 8-bit format and 0.1 percent for INT4. The model underestimates the FP16 run by 7.5 percent, consistent with that run’s slower runtime, so the comparison supports the model’s two terms rather than its exact constants. The table also shows where weight-only quantization stops paying. Each halving of the bytes halves the sweep, but the 188.5 ms prefill is untouched, and at INT4 it is already 56 percent of the latency. Even weights that cost no bytes at all would leave the prefill, so no weight-only format can lift the intent model above 5.3 Hz. Beyond that ceiling, the gain must come from integer arithmetic in the prefill, fewer input tokens, or a processor with a higher compute roof.

The last column carries the cost. On this module the INT4 build’s action score fell from 95.3 percent to 90.1 percent ([[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/01 Machine Anatomy/03 The Cognitive Brain/V4-03.11 Source References#^ref-franklin2024openvla|Franklin 2024]]). OpenVLA’s authors, measuring task success on a real robot with a desktop GPU, found a different pattern. INT4 weights kept 71.9 percent success against 71.3 percent for bfloat16, while INT8 weights through a general-purpose quantization library fell to 58.1 percent. The cause was time, not rounding. The library’s added quantization operations cost more than the saved bytes, so the INT8 policy ran at 1.2 Hz on the evaluation GPU, against the 5 Hz control rate of its training data. The robot therefore met each observation later than the demonstrations had ([[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/01 Machine Anatomy/03 The Cognitive Brain/V4-03.11 Source References#^ref-kim2024openvla|Kim et al. 2024]]). On the module an 8-bit format was nearly free and INT4 reduced the offline action score; on the desktop GPU the 8-bit build lost success through its slower loop and INT4 was nearly free. The two 8-bit results used different software and kernels, and the two studies scored different tasks, so the pair shows that a bit width alone does not predict the outcome; the quantization method, the kernels, the control rate they allow, and the task together do.

A quantized checkpoint is therefore a different model. It must be evaluated on the machine’s own contact tasks at the deployed precision, carry its own calibration, and be named as such by the `model_id` of the proposal payload ([[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/01 Machine Anatomy/03 The Cognitive Brain/V4-03.06 Chunk Supply and Evidence Validity#^sec-brain-proposer-information|section 3.5.4]]). The footprint saving also has a systems value. Dropping from 14 GB to 3.5 GB of weights returns memory that retained context and perception also need, though how much of the computed latency saving survives when the cameras and the chunk policy share the bus must be measured under load. Even with that saving, quantization leaves the machine’s intent model at 2 Hz, one fresh proposal every 500 ms, while the arm below it consumes setpoints tens of times faster. No lever on the weight term closes a gap that large, and the gap raises two questions the weights cannot answer: how the arm stays supplied between evaluations, and how long whatever supplies it stays true.
