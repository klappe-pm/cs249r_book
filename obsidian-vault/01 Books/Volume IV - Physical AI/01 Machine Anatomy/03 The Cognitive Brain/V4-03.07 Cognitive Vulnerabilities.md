# V4-03.07 Cognitive Vulnerabilities

[[Home|Home]] · [[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/Volume 4 Overview|Volume Overview]] · [[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/01 Machine Anatomy/03 The Cognitive Brain/V4-03 The Cognitive Brain|V4-03 The Cognitive Brain]]

^sec-brain-cognitive-vulnerabilities

A chunk proposed by a mobile manipulator’s policy can arrive within its 40 ms execution budget, with every joint setpoint smooth and within kinematic limits, and yet still collide the gripper into the rim of the mug. The trajectory meets its real-time deadline, but its physical content is wrong, and the most consequential failures of learned physical AI systems sit in that gap. Learned foundation models expand task coverage across open-world environments, but no general proof of their integrated, closed-loop behavior across unconstrained state spaces is available. Engineering practice must instead analyze verified components within explicitly bounded operating regions.

Without a verified input domain and sensitivity bound, small sensing changes to a learned proposer can yield unexpectedly large changes in proposed motion, a sensitivity demonstrated early by imperceptible image perturbations that flipped image classification outputs ([[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/01 Machine Anatomy/03 The Cognitive Brain/V4-03.11 Source References#^ref-szegedy2013intriguing|Szegedy et al. 2013]]). Benchmark success does not establish closed-loop stability, timing bounds, or physical safety under unseen operating conditions; those guarantees require independent evidence across the model, the software runtime, and the physical plant.[[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/01 Machine Anatomy/03 The Cognitive Brain/V4-03.12 Footnotes#^fn9|⁹]] Even when a valid sensitivity bound exists, it characterizes only the relationship between input perturbations and output deviations over a specified domain. It provides no proof that the nominal proposed action is collision-free or physically admissible, meaning analytical bounds can inform, but never replace, an independent permission decision.

A numerical interface easily traps malformed tensors, NaN values, and out-of-range floating-point setpoints, but it cannot detect a wrong grasp plan. Both classical pipelines and learned models can emit well-formed yet wrong commands; learning, however, adds failures rooted in high-dimensional latent features, out-of-distribution hallucinations, and uncalibrated confidence scores. Independent safety monitors can reject proposals that contradict measured geometry or joint limits, but they cannot infer true task intent. Safe operation in the physical world requires examining each of these failure modes and stating the evidence checks needed beyond packet formatting.

### Semantic Hallucinations

^sec-brain-semantic-hallucinations

Consider a mobile manipulator clearing items near a conveyor. A discarded shipping carton carries a high-resolution printed photograph of a ceramic mug. The robot’s vision backbone attends to the visual texture, and the policy proposes an end-effector trajectory that closes the parallel jaws around the printed handle. The trajectory tensor is numerically flawless: smooth, within joint velocity and acceleration bounds, and clear of modeled obstacles. The handle it targets, however, is flat ink on cardboard. In web-scale vision models, attention maps track statistical correlations across 2D pixel arrays rather than measured 3D geometry.

Independent geometric evidence exposes this hallucination directly. A runtime validator compares the proposed grasp pose against calibrated depth returns to check whether physical relief supports the handle. If the depth sensor shows a flat cardboard surface within measurement resolution, the runtime rejects the proposal; if sensor dropouts or occlusion leave the region uncertain, the grasp remains unverified and must not be admitted. In neither case can a 2D attention map stand in for a 3D depth measurement. The proposer must therefore supply both its target coordinate frame and the supporting observation timestamps so that downstream checks can separate a physically grounded motion plan from an unverified visual guess.

↪ How depth error is bounded and qualified before a spatial claim can be trusted is developed in **[[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/03 Running the Machine/08 Sensor Perception/V4-08.07 Spatial Error and Clearance Bounds#^sec-perception-error-clearance|Spatial Error and Clearance Bounds]]**.

### The Softmax Illusion and Out-Of-Distribution Overconfidence

^sec-brain-softmax-illusion

A natural temptation is to filter out hallucinations like the cardboard mug, or frames degraded by intense specular glare, dynamic shadows, and water droplets on camera lenses, by rejecting proposals whose reported confidence falls below a threshold. That defense fails when deployment interfaces conflate mathematical normalization with the physical probability of task success. In discrete classification, classification-based tokenization, or categorical action heads, raw output logits are transformed via the softmax operator:[[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/01 Machine Anatomy/03 The Cognitive Brain/V4-03.12 Footnotes#^fn10|¹⁰]]

$$
{\sigma}({\mathbf{z}})_i = \frac{e^{{z}_i}}{\sum_{j=1}^{K} e^{{z}_j}}
$$

 whose outputs sum to one purely by algebraic construction. When an embodied machine encounters those out-of-distribution (OOD) observations or novel visual textures, the network can still emit confidence scores near one for physically disastrous actions. Modern deep networks are often overconfident even on in-distribution inputs ([[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/01 Machine Anatomy/03 The Cognitive Brain/V4-03.11 Source References#^ref-guo2017calibration|Guo et al. 2017]]), and networks constructed from rectified linear units provably approach maximum confidence on inputs far from their training distribution ([[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/01 Machine Anatomy/03 The Cognitive Brain/V4-03.11 Source References#^ref-hein2019relu|Hein et al. 2019]]).

Calibration evaluates how closely a model’s predicted confidence aligns with observed empirical success rates under specified conditions. A confidence score is useful for selecting or abstaining from a proposal only to the extent that this relationship holds for the deployed task. **[[05 Reference/Volume IV - Physical AI/V4 Machine Learning Background#^sec-appendix-ml-calibration|Softmax overconfidence and calibration error]]** formalizes binned expected calibration error and post-hoc temperature scaling. From a systems perspective, the execution runtime must bind calibration metadata directly to the model identity (`model_id`), preventing supervisory layers from mistaking uncalibrated algebraic certainty for physical permission.

In modern physical AI systems, populating the action payload’s `confidence` field is rarely as straightforward as extracting a softmax probability, because contemporary chunking policies do not operate as discrete classifiers. Action Chunking with Transformers (ACT), for example, decodes each multi-step chunk deterministically at inference time by clamping its latent style variable to the prior mean ([[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/01 Machine Anatomy/03 The Cognitive Brain/V4-03.11 Source References#^ref-zhao2023learning|Zhao et al. 2023]]). Similarly, diffusion and flow-matching policies iteratively denoise trajectories from Gaussian noise vectors. Neither architecture natively emits a normalized score. For these continuous decoders, confidence must be derived through an explicit, qualified estimation technique. Three primary architectures are used in practice:

- *Sample dispersion.* The system draws multiple trajectory chunks for the identical sensory observation by varying random diffusion noise seeds or sampling latent vectors. Measuring geometric dispersion across these trajectories describes the policy’s sampled output variability. Wide dispersion can reflect several valid motions as well as uncertainty; it does not by itself isolate uncertainty caused by missing knowledge.
- *Ensemble disagreement.* Multiple independently trained policy heads evaluate the same sensory input simultaneously. Downstream monitors measure the variance among their trajectory proposals, treating significant disagreement as an indicator of model uncertainty.
- *Conformal thresholds.* To convert heuristic dispersion or disagreement metrics into actionable statistical guarantees, the runtime applies inductive conformal prediction. Using a held-out calibration dataset, the system establishes an empirical threshold such that, under the assumption of exchangeability,[[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/01 Machine Anatomy/03 The Cognitive Brain/V4-03.12 Footnotes#^fn11|¹¹]] the predicted set covers the calibrated target with a chosen marginal coverage probability. Specifically, each candidate trajectory receives a nonconformity score measuring its residual error or disagreement, and the conformal set collects all candidates whose scores fall within the quantile threshold measured on the calibration trials, so high sensory ambiguity widens the set to include distinct trajectories while pairing the threshold with an admissibility filter can leave no candidate in the set at all. Physical validity still requires independent checks against current geometry and plant limits. If the conformal set is empty or contains mutually conflicting actions, the runtime refuses the candidate or abstains; if active motion is no longer admissible, it requires a physically qualified response or operator intervention ([[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/01 Machine Anatomy/03 The Cognitive Brain/V4-03.11 Source References#^ref-ren2023robots|Ren et al. 2023]]; [[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/01 Machine Anatomy/03 The Cognitive Brain/V4-03.11 Source References#^ref-angelopoulos2023gentle|Angelopoulos and Bates 2023]]).

Each uncertainty estimation strategy incurs distinct hardware and memory-bandwidth costs. Generating multiple samples from a single observation can reuse visual backbone features and batch each decoding iteration through the policy head, sharing a weight read across samples. Iterative decoders still revisit their weights across refinement steps, and the balance between arithmetic and memory traffic depends on the batch and kernels. In contrast, an ensemble of disparate models requires reading separate sets of weights across the memory bus for every head, multiplying memory bandwidth traffic by the number of heads. The action payload must therefore treat the confidence scalar ${\hat{p}}$ as meaningful only when accompanied by the identifier of the method that produced it and the corresponding calibration record, both resolved through `model_id`. A model lacking a qualified estimation pipeline must omit the confidence score entirely rather than emit an ungrounded default value.

The stipulated values in [[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/01 Machine Anatomy/03 The Cognitive Brain/V4-03.07 Cognitive Vulnerabilities#^tbl-03-calibration-degradation|table 4]] illustrate how environmental and hardware shifts can open a gap between confidence and success; they describe nominal illustrative regimes rather than a single deployed benchmark. The near-OOD row is a *covariate shift*, a change in the inputs the deployed model meets while the task stays the same. Across all shifted regimes, reported confidence significantly outstrips actual task success. A supervisory filter relying solely on an uncalibrated score threshold would mistakenly admit hazardous trajectories. Choosing an appropriate supervisory response depends on the integrity of the remaining sensor streams and the physical capabilities of the machine; a confidence discrepancy alone cannot dictate whether the robot should slow down, collect additional sensor frames, or execute an emergency stop.

### Perceptual Chattering and Covariate Shift

^sec-brain-perceptual-chattering

When sensory observations hover near a boundary between competing object classes or grasp modes, small frame-to-frame noise flips the winning label back and forth, a failure known as *perceptual chattering*. If a motion planner treats each label flip as an abrupt change in the physical world, it discards and resets its trajectory on every frame. In the fatal 2018 Tempe autonomous vehicle collision, the National Transportation Safety Board record documents this failure in the automated driving system: the perception module alternated between classifying a pedestrian pushing a bicycle as a vehicle, a bicycle, and “other.” After each reclassification the path predictor dropped the object’s earlier locations, preventing the motion planner from seeing that the obstacle was crossing the vehicle’s path ([[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/01 Machine Anatomy/03 The Cognitive Brain/V4-03.11 Source References#^ref-ntsb2019uber|National Transportation Safety Board 2019]]). Covariate shift, the near-OOD regime of [[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/01 Machine Anatomy/03 The Cognitive Brain/V4-03.07 Cognitive Vulnerabilities#^tbl-03-calibration-degradation|table 4]] in which the inputs change while the task stays the same, pushes more frames toward those boundaries; **[[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/02 Teaching the Machine/05 Physical Data/V4-05.03 Mechanics of Endogenous Covariate Shift#^sec-data-covariate-shift|Mechanics of Endogenous Covariate Shift]]** develops it.

↩︎ The tracking-history loss under classification changes in the 2018 Tempe collision is established in **[[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/00 Introduction/01 The Causal Boundary/V4-01.09 When the Responsibilities Fail Together#^sec-boundary-tracking-churn|Tracking history loss under classification churn]]**.

In robotic manipulation, perceptual chattering injects rapid direction reversals into joint trajectory setpoints. Over a discrete control interval ${\Delta} {t}$, a sharp step in commanded velocity demands an instantaneous acceleration ${\Delta} {\dot{\mathbf{q}}} / {\Delta} {t}$ beyond what the actuators can deliver. PD joint controllers react to the resulting velocity errors by commanding large opposing motor torques, the same derivative-gain torque step that the temporal-ensembling jerk trap of [[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/01 Machine Anatomy/03 The Cognitive Brain/V4-03.06 Chunk Supply and Evidence Validity#^sec-brain-action-chunking|section 3.5.1]] priced at a single chunk seam, now repeated at every flip. The resulting current spikes, tracking errors, and heat strain the power stage and gearbox, risking protective drive faults or structural vibration. Physical AI runtimes suppress perceptual chattering with three defenses:

1.  *Temporal hysteresis and debouncing.* Requiring a newly observed class or spatial mode to persist across ${N}$ consecutive perception frames before committing a state change, just like switch debouncing[[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/01 Machine Anatomy/03 The Cognitive Brain/V4-03.12 Footnotes#^fn12|¹²]] in embedded firmware.
2.  *Recursive state filtering.* Fusing successive observations into a running estimate so that a single noisy frame cannot overwrite an established world state.
3.  *Rate and jerk limiting.* Enforcing joint acceleration and jerk bounds in the real-time controller so that high-level label flicker cannot reach the motor shafts as torque shocks.

↪ Recursive state estimation, which accumulates evidence across frames instead of trusting the latest one, is developed in **[[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/03 Running the Machine/09 Spatial Memory/V4-09.03 Estimating State from Dated Measurements#^sec-perception-state-estimation|Estimating State from Dated Measurements]]**.

### When Intent Expires

^sec-brain-temporal-expiration-intent

Even when perception neither hallucinates nor chatters, the physical scene continues to move after a valid goal is issued. Notebook [3.3](https://mlsysbook.ai/vol4/03_brain/03_brain.html#nbk-brain-conveyor-evidence-horizon) already showed a chunk outliving its evidence, and the same failure applies one level up, to intent. Renewing a high-level goal identifier, or retransmitting a previous trajectory, does not refresh the observation that placed the mug. High-capacity intent models, which deliberate over long time horizons at low update rates, must therefore provide goals whose validity can be continuously audited against fresh, real-time telemetry while high-frequency reactive policies stream immediate motion.

The execution receiver must distinguish between rejecting a stale proposal and terminating an active, executing trajectory. Dropping an incoming proposal that failed its arrival deadline or validity check does not automatically invalidate the chunk currently running on the motors. The active chunk continues executing so long as its own chunk lease, the intent lease governing its target, the evidence behind it, its state bounds, and its safety margins all remain valid. If the active chunk lease or the governing intent lease expires, or the evidence behind the active chunk lapses, the supervisory permission path must end nominal tracking and engage the qualified fallback unless a valid successor restores the required authority. Because holding position, decelerating to a controlled stop, and removing torque require distinct physical preconditions, **[[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/03 Running the Machine/12 Safety Enforcement/V4-12.10 The Fallback Ladder#^sec-enforcement-fallback-ladder|The Fallback Ladder]]** develops the formal fallback ladder across these operational regimes.

Each of the four vulnerabilities ends the same way. Hallucination, overconfidence, chattering, and expiry cannot be reliably ruled out by the proposer’s own output and require checks against independently qualified evidence, which is why the opening comparison of classical and learned methods resolves into a partition rather than a choice. Physical AI architectures assign semantic interpretation to the learned proposer and execution authority to the permission path ([[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/01 Machine Anatomy/03 The Cognitive Brain/V4-03.07 Cognitive Vulnerabilities#^tbl-03-classical-vs-learned|table 5]]). Learned foundation models supply flexible task coverage across unstructured environments, but their lack of closed-loop formal guarantees requires treating their outputs as unprivileged proposals. Conversely, classical analytical checks enforce strict physical invariances and real-time bounds, yet their guarantees remain conditional on accurate kinematic models and fresh sensor data. Safety therefore rests on the interface, which must never let an unverified proposal bypass the checks of the permission path.

**Checkpoint 3.3: Proposals that arrive on time and are wrong**

A proposal can meet every deadline and still target the wrong thing, so the receiver needs evidence the proposer cannot supply about itself.

**Classical vs. learned**: For the welding cell and the conveyor mug pick, which approach would you use to propose motion in each, and which properties could you still prove or measure for each?

**Interface checkability**: Can you explain why the permission path can enforce this geometric intent lease on a target pose with a capture time but not on an ungrounded latent vector alone?

**Hallucination vs. staleness**: For the grasp on the carton photograph and for a smooth chunk that arrives after ${\tau_{\text{ev}}}$, which payload field or independent measurement lets the receiver refuse each?

**Threshold estimate**: Assuming every attempt in a regime receives its tabulated confidence score, if the receiver admitted every proposal scoring above the far-OOD confidence in [[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/01 Machine Anatomy/03 The Cognitive Brain/V4-03.07 Cognitive Vulnerabilities#^tbl-03-calibration-degradation|table 4]], which regimes would pass, and what stipulated fraction of near-OOD attempts would fail?
