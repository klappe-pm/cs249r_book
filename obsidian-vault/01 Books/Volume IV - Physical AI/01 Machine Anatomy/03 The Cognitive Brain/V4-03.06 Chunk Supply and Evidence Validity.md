# V4-03.06 Chunk Supply and Evidence Validity

[[Home|Home]] · [[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/Volume 4 Overview|Volume Overview]] · [[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/01 Machine Anatomy/03 The Cognitive Brain/V4-03 The Cognitive Brain|V4-03 The Cognitive Brain]]

^sec-brain-chunk-supply

One INT4 evaluation of the intent model takes 336 ms. Over that interval the mug keeps riding the conveyor, and an arm tracking a new setpoint every few tens of milliseconds would have nothing to track if each proposal named a single setpoint. Predicting a sequence of setpoints per evaluation solves the supply problem but raises a second one that the memory wall never touched. Every setpoint in such a sequence descends from one observation, so the sequence can outlast the evidence behind it. Sizing those sequences for the machine’s two proposers leads to three further questions: how long the supply stays true, what the slow proposer should hand the fast one, and what a proposal must carry for the permission path to refuse it.

### Action Chunking

^sec-brain-action-chunking

If a proposer emits only one future setpoint per evaluation and takes longer than one setpoint step to finish the next inference pass, the controller runs out of planned motion while the processor is still computing. To keep dense waypoints flowing despite slow full-model inference, modern physical AI policies employ **action chunking** (definition [3.2](https://mlsysbook.ai/vol4/03_brain/03_brain.html#dfn-brain-action-chunking)), predicting a multi-step trajectory segment in a single pass ([[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/01 Machine Anatomy/03 The Cognitive Brain/V4-03.11 Source References#^ref-zhao2023learning|Zhao et al. 2023]]; [[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/01 Machine Anatomy/03 The Cognitive Brain/V4-03.11 Source References#^ref-chi2024diffusionpolicy|Chi et al. 2025]]). Each chunk crosses the proposal boundary as one packet, reusing one evaluation across several outputs, though it does not make the next fresh camera observation arrive any sooner.

**Definition 3.2: Temporal action chunking**

***Temporal action chunking*** is the policy formulation that predicts an entire horizon of ${H}$ future continuous actions ${\mathbf{A}_{t:t+H-1}} = [{\mathbf{a}}_{t}, \dots, {\mathbf{a}}_{t+{H}-1}]$ in a single neural evaluation or diffusion rollout, amortizing parameter memory transfers across extended physical execution windows.

1.  **Significance**: Chunking divides the weight traffic per supplied setpoint by ${H}$, 16 on the mobile manipulator, but leaves the delay before a new observation can change the motion at one renewal period plus one inference, 90 ms for its chunk policy. It does not make fresh observations or policy decisions arrive at the waypoint rate; a downstream controller tracks the accepted trajectory between model updates.
2.  **Distinction**: Unlike single-step policies ${\mathbf{a}}_{t} \sim \pi(\cdot \mid {\mathbf{o}}_{t})$, which condition only on the current observation and re-evaluate the full neural network at every control step, action chunking deliberately generates multi-step feedforward trajectories. It executes only the start of each before the next replaces it (receding-horizon execution), and it blends across overlapping chunks to reconcile slow neural deliberation with fast closed-loop control.
3.  **Common pitfall**: Executing action chunks open-loop, without smoothing across chunk boundaries or a check by the permission path. Without smooth blending across overlapping chunk boundaries, switching between independently generated horizons injects acceleration jumps and torque shocks that excite mechanical gearbox resonances.

Predicting a horizon[[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/01 Machine Anatomy/03 The Cognitive Brain/V4-03.12 Footnotes#^fn8|⁸]] of ${H}$ actions per forward pass spreads the multi-gigabyte DRAM weight streaming cost over ${H} \times {\Delta t_{\text{step}}}$ seconds of physical execution. On the mobile manipulator’s application processor, a 7B FP16 model (14 GB) generating single-step actions at the 40 Hz setpoint rate would demand 40 $\times$ 14 GB $=$ 560 GB/s of memory bandwidth, 3.9 times the 142.8 GB/s it can sustain. A chunk of ${H} =$ 16 waypoints cuts that demand to 35 GB/s, 24.5 percent of the sustained bandwidth, if one sweep supplies each full chunk. More frequent replanning, decoder work, and competing memory traffic consume additional bandwidth. The saving is in weight traffic per supplied waypoint, not an equal gain in arithmetic intensity: predicting a chunk adds decoder work, and its position on the roofline depends on the operations performed and the bytes actually transferred. Predicting a sequence also changes how a learned policy uses recent actions, so this supply calculation establishes only the weight traffic for the stated schedule, not that the policy attends to the right evidence or recovers from an unfamiliar contact.

Supply rate vs. closed-loop reaction latency On the running machine, the smaller chunk policy follows its own illustrative schedule, in which three quantities separate the rate at which setpoints reach the servos from the delay before a new observation changes a proposal:

- *Setpoint supply cadence* (${f_{\text{exec}}} = 1/{\Delta t_{\text{step}}} =$ 40 Hz, ${\Delta t_{\text{step}}} =$ 25 ms): The rate at which the trajectory queue delivers discrete waypoints to the joint servo loop.
- *Policy replanning rate* (${f_{\text{replan}}} =$ 20 Hz, period ${T_{\text{chunk}}} =$ 50 ms): The cadence at which the smaller chunk policy begins an evaluation from fresh sensory frames. The period is chosen as a whole number of setpoint steps, here 2, so that every renewal begins on a setpoint boundary.
- *Closed-loop reaction latency* (${T_{\text{reaction}}} = {T_{\text{chunk}}} + {t_{\text{infer}}} =$ 50 ms $+$ 40 ms $=$ 90 ms): The worst sampling-phase wait plus the assumed inference time until a revised proposal is available, excluding further admission and physical response delay.

In this illustrative schedule, a disturbance immediately after capture can wait up to 90 ms for a revised learned proposal, before any additional admission and actuation delay. The independent permission path need not wait for that revision. Current qualified evidence, meaning measurements whose error bound and age have been established for this use (**[[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/03 Running the Machine/08 Sensor Perception/V4-08.07 Spatial Error and Clearance Bounds#^sec-perception-error-clearance|Spatial Error and Clearance Bounds]]** develops how), can trigger a protective response while the policy computes. Its ability to detect the relevant obstacle and complete a feasible response needs its own timing and sensing evidence. Chunking keeps candidate waypoints available; it establishes neither that response nor its deadline.

Pricing the decoder families The three decoder families of [[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/01 Machine Anatomy/03 The Cognitive Brain/V4-03.04 Learned Representations#^sec-brain-learned-representations|section 3.3]] differ in how they turn one observation into a chunk, and the machine’s processor prices that difference. Suppose the intent-sized backbone, with its deployed INT4 weights, produced one fresh chunk of ${H} =$ 16 setpoints of 7 values each. Every family first pays the same compute-bound prefill of the observation, about 189 ms, and then the families part ways:

- Binned autoregressive tokens need 112 sequential decode steps, each rereading the backbone weights, for about 2.9 s.
- FAST tokens cut the sequence to about 30 tokens, the count its authors report per arm for a one-second chunk and therefore generous for this shorter chunk, about 899 ms, still sequential.
- A parallel head folds every action into one longer prefill, about 264 ms.
- A $\pi_0$-sized action expert of 600 MB in 16-bit weights integrates 10 steps. Each step rereads the expert and the backbone’s 151 MB of cached keys and values rather than the backbone weights, about 5.3 ms per step on bandwidth, for about 241 ms in all. **[[05 Reference/Volume IV - Physical AI/V4 Machine Learning Background#^sec-appendix-ml-diffusion|Continuous trajectory diffusion and flow matching]]** derives the noise-prediction and flow-matching objectives these experts train on.

The decoder choice spans 12× in latency on the same backbone, the cost that parallel decoding and action experts were introduced to remove ([[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/01 Machine Anatomy/03 The Cognitive Brain/V4-03.11 Source References#^ref-kim2025openvlaoft|Kim et al. 2025]]; [[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/01 Machine Anatomy/03 The Cognitive Brain/V4-03.11 Source References#^ref-black2024pi0|Black et al. 2024]]). In the expert design, weights of hundreds of megabytes far exceed the processor’s on-chip caches, so each refinement step pays another off-chip transfer. A head small enough to stay resident would change that term. Reducing the number of refinement steps shortens the decoder provided the proposals keep the required task quality. Neither change touches the prefill of a fresh observation. None of the four therefore renews within the chunk policy’s 50 ms period, because the prefill alone exceeds it. A large backbone that reads each observation fresh cannot also be the fast renewer on this processor, whatever its decoder. Deployed dual systems reach the same conclusion by assigning renewal to a separate, smaller model, as [[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/01 Machine Anatomy/03 The Cognitive Brain/V4-03.06 Chunk Supply and Evidence Validity#^tbl-03-state-of-practice|table 2]] records, and the machine does the same with its ACT chunk policy.

Whatever the decoder, chunking creates one more problem, **chunk seam continuity**. Because each action chunk is generated independently from rolling sensory observations, the tail of chunk ${k}$ and the head of chunk ${k}+1$ may show slight positional and velocity discrepancies. If a controller concatenated these chunks directly, the resulting step-discontinuities in acceleration would inject large jerk ($\dddot{{\mathbf{q}}}$) into the physical linkages, inducing mechanical shock, acoustic vibration, gear-tooth wear, and motor overcurrent faults. To reduce discontinuities between overlapping chunks, systems can implement **temporal ensembling**, blending overlapping trajectory predictions via exponentially weighted averaging:

$$
{\mathbf{a}}_{t} = \frac{\sum_{k=0}^{{K}-1} {w}_k \, {\mathbf{a}}_{t}^{({k})}}{\sum_{k=0}^{{K}-1} {w}_k}, \quad {w}_k = \exp(-{m} \cdot {k})
$$

where ${\mathbf{a}}_{t}^{({k})}$ is the action for step ${t}$ predicted by the ${k}$-th oldest of the ${K}$ chunks that cover step ${t}$ (${k} = 0$ is the oldest), and ${m} > 0$ is a tunable weighting rate. Following ACT, the oldest prediction receives the largest weight, which favors smoothness over responsiveness; a smaller ${m}$ lets new observations enter the blend faster ([[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/01 Machine Anatomy/03 The Cognitive Brain/V4-03.11 Source References#^ref-zhao2023learning|Zhao et al. 2023]]). The formula assumes that the policy is queried at every control step, as ACT does, so that ${K} = \min({t} + 1, {H})$. The mobile manipulator’s chunk policy renews every 2 setpoints instead, and because its 50 ms renewal period is a whole number of 25 ms steps, each new chunk starts on a setpoint boundary. Because imitation-learned policies are trained on recorded demonstrations to predict motion starting from the instant the camera frame was captured, the waypoints use capture-relative times rather than starting when inference finishes, so elapsed entries are not newly executed when a chunk arrives. The first setpoint is dated at capture, and each following interval advances by 25 ms, so the last interval reaches the capture-relative horizon. By the time the 40 ms evaluation completes and the chunk arrives, the first 2 entries are already past; the next 2 setpoints precede replacement. The same formula then applies with ${k}$ counting renewals rather than steps. A 16-setpoint chunk renewed every 2 setpoints gives at most ${K} =$ 8 overlapping predictions at any step. A renewal period that fell between setpoints would leave each chunk’s first action off the grid, and the blend would first have to interpolate every chunk onto common setpoint times. Below the proposal boundary, the permission path interpolates the admitted waypoints into a setpoint for every bus cycle, and the drives close their own velocity and current loops on those setpoints.

Ensembling has two limits. It averages, and the average of two valid predictions need not be valid, the same mode averaging that defeats a regression head. If an older chunk passes left of an obstacle and a newer one passes right, the blend steers between them ([[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/01 Machine Anatomy/03 The Cognitive Brain/V4-03.11 Source References#^ref-black2025realtime|Black, Galliker, et al. 2025]]). It also ignores what executes while the policy computes. With the assumed 40 ms inference and 25 ms setpoints, the arm consumes 2 setpoints of the previous chunk before the new one exists, so no new prediction can change them. *Real-time chunking* treats those setpoints as committed. It freezes the part of the previous chunk that will execute during inference and has a flow or diffusion policy fill in the rest of the new chunk so that it agrees with that frozen prefix, rather than blending afterward ([[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/01 Machine Anatomy/03 The Cognitive Brain/V4-03.11 Source References#^ref-black2025realtime|Black, Galliker, et al. 2025]]). The method arrives from the learning side at this chapter’s distinction, in which the committed prefix is motion already admitted and the tail is a buffer that the next evaluation may still rewrite.

↪ The chunk-prediction objective and the modal commitment it buys in training are developed in **[[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/02 Teaching the Machine/06 Policy Training/V4-06.03 Policy Training Regimes#^sec-training-action-chunking|Action chunking and multi-step trajectory prediction]]**.

**Systems Perspective 3.1: The temporal ensembling jerk trap**

Exponentially weighted temporal ensembling averages overlapping predictions, but averaging sampled setpoints alone guarantees neither continuous position nor continuous velocity between samples or at chunk boundaries. The interpolator on the permission path determines those properties. If successive chunks disagree in slope, a controller can receive a step in commanded velocity even when position remains continuous. In joint proportional-derivative (PD) tracking (${\boldsymbol{\tau}} = {\mathbf{K}_{p}} {\mathbf{e}} + {\mathbf{K}_{d}} {\dot{\mathbf{e}}}$), that velocity jump produces a finite torque step. With an illustrative derivative gain for one joint, the diagonal entry ${K_d} = 40\,\text{N}\cdot\text{m}\cdot\text{s/rad}$ of ${\mathbf{K}_{d}}$, and an illustrative velocity discontinuity ${\Delta} {\dot{q}} = 0.5\,\text{rad/s}$, the derivative term changes by ${\Delta} {\tau} = {K_d} {\Delta} {\dot{q}} = 20\,\text{N}\cdot\text{m}$. Dividing by the servo period would instead describe a torque slew rate, not a torque. Abrupt torque changes can excite mechanical resonances or exceed drive limits. Interpolation must match position, velocity, and acceleration at adjoining segments, the property called ${C}^2$ continuity, while enforcing the machine’s velocity, acceleration, and torque limits. Smoothness alone does not establish that the requested motion is feasible.

^callout-perspective-3-1-d719c972

↪ The quintic spline that matches position, velocity, and acceleration across a chunk seam is developed in **[[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/03 Running the Machine/11 Trajectory Planning/V4-11.05 Kinodynamic Feasibility#^sec-planning-quintic-splines|Quintic splines and C^2 boundary matching]]**.

A chunked policy therefore has two rates that a single “action rate” conflates: the spacing of the waypoints it supplies and the interval between the fresh observations that change them. Supplying more waypoints from one sensory context does not refresh the observation on which they depend. A downstream controller can use current state measurements while tracking admitted waypoints between renewals, and its feedback corrects tracking errors within its capabilities, but a newly perceived obstacle still requires a timely checking or replanning response. The mobile manipulator’s own budget makes this distinction quantitative, first for supply and then for the time the evidence behind a chunk stays true.

### Two Proposers and the Evidence Horizon

^sec-brain-two-proposers

Chunking reduces the weight traffic per waypoint, but the decoder pricing showed that each fresh context still waits for the large backbone’s prefill, whatever the decoder. That prefill floor and the weight-streaming ceiling of [[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/01 Machine Anatomy/03 The Cognitive Brain/V4-03.02 The Embodied Brain#^fig-cadence-gap|figure 2]] explain why embodied models in the published literature split into two parameter regimes rather than converging on a single model size ([[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/01 Machine Anatomy/03 The Cognitive Brain/V4-03.06 Chunk Supply and Evidence Validity#^fig-03-parameter-by-domain|figure 12]]). Across 1,526 language and robotics models with disclosed parameter counts in the Epoch AI catalog between 2018 and 2026 (1,484 language and 42 robotics models) ([[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/01 Machine Anatomy/03 The Cognitive Brain/V4-03.11 Source References#^ref-epochai2026models|Epoch AI 2026]]), language models climbed continuously past hundreds of billions of parameters. Embodied vision-language-action backbones followed that curve upward, from RT-1 (35M) to GR00T N1 (2.2B), $\pi_0$ (3.3B), OpenVLA (7B), Helix System 2 (7B), RT-2 (55B), and PaLM-E (562B), to inherit web-scale semantic and visual breadth ([[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/01 Machine Anatomy/03 The Cognitive Brain/V4-03.11 Source References#^ref-brohan2023rt1|Brohan, Brown, Carbajal, Chebotar, Dabis, et al. 2023]]; [[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/01 Machine Anatomy/03 The Cognitive Brain/V4-03.11 Source References#^ref-bjorck2025gr00t|Bjorck et al. 2025]]; [[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/01 Machine Anatomy/03 The Cognitive Brain/V4-03.11 Source References#^ref-black2024pi0|Black et al. 2024]]; [[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/01 Machine Anatomy/03 The Cognitive Brain/V4-03.11 Source References#^ref-kim2024openvla|Kim et al. 2024]]; [[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/01 Machine Anatomy/03 The Cognitive Brain/V4-03.11 Source References#^ref-figureai2025helix|Figure AI 2025]]; [[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/01 Machine Anatomy/03 The Cognitive Brain/V4-03.11 Source References#^ref-brohan2023rt2|Brohan, Brown, Carbajal, Chebotar, Chen, et al. 2023]]; [[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/01 Machine Anatomy/03 The Cognitive Brain/V4-03.11 Source References#^ref-driess2023palme|Driess et al. 2023]]). This two-tier split, a slow intent model above a fast chunk policy, can now be budgeted on the warehouse mobile manipulator, and the chunks the fast policy supplies can be checked against how long they stay true. Budgeting that split also uses a term that the stopping budget of **[[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/00 Introduction/01 The Causal Boundary/V4-01.05 Defining the Causal Boundary#^sec-boundary-kinematic-roofline|Delay consumes stopping clearance]]** already charged, a lease, authority that lapses unless it is renewed. The motion the chunk policy proposes runs under a chunk lease, the longest an admitted chunk may keep driving the actuators without an admitted successor. **[[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/01 Machine Anatomy/04 The Nervous System/V4-04.05 Chunk Lease Sizing from the Stopping Budget#^sec-nervous-lease-sizing|Chunk Lease Sizing from the Stopping Budget]]** sizes that lease against the stopping budget, so that a late renewal is contained by that lease rather than by the length of the chunk. Notebook [3.2](https://mlsysbook.ai/vol4/03_brain/03_brain.html#nbk-brain-lpddr5-memory-wall-bottleneck-action-chunking) applies the memory bound to the split and checks whether chunking alone could give the larger model the required renewal cadence.

**Napkin Math 3.2: The renewal gap**

**Problem**: Chunking spreads one weight sweep across many setpoints. Can it let the intent model renew proposals within the chunk policy’s 50 ms period, or does the machine need a second, smaller proposer, and how much of the shared bus do the two proposers then take?

**Variables**:

- Intent model: 14 GB of FP16 or 3.5 GB of INT4 weights (Derived), an INT4 evaluation of 336 ms that decodes ${S_{\text{out}}} =$ 7 tokens (Derived, notebook [3.1](https://mlsysbook.ai/vol4/03_brain/03_brain.html#nbk-brain-intent-model-latency)), run at 2 Hz (Chosen).
- Chunk policy: 80M parameters, ACT’s size (Datasheet), 0.16 GB in FP16 (Derived), renewed at 20 Hz (Chosen).
- Chunk of ${H} =$ 16 setpoints at ${\Delta t_{\text{step}}} =$ 25 ms (Chosen); sustained bandwidth ${\text{BW}_{\text{mem}}} =$ 142.8 GB/s (Derived).

**Math**:

*Amortization.* Counting weight traffic only, as [[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/01 Machine Anatomy/03 The Cognitive Brain/V4-03.06 Chunk Supply and Evidence Validity#^sec-brain-action-chunking|section 3.5.1]] did, and setting aside the prefill’s arithmetic and the other decode sweeps, one FP16 sweep that emitted a whole chunk would be shared by ${H}$ setpoints:

$$
t_{\text{amort}} = \frac{t_{\text{stream}}}{H} = \frac{98.0 ms}{16\text{ setpoints}} \approx 6.1 ms\text{ per setpoint}
$$

*Renewal.* A fresh chunk still waits for a whole INT4 evaluation, prefill and decode together, while the chunk policy streams its own weights in about a millisecond; the rest of its inference target goes to vision encoding and the decoder’s arithmetic:

$$
\frac{t_{\text{INT4}}}{T_{\text{chunk}}} = \frac{336 ms}{50 ms} \approx 6.7\text{ renewal periods}, \qquad \frac{0.16 GB}{142.8 GB/s} \approx 1.12 ms
$$

*Bus share.* The INT4 intent model streams its weights ${S_{\text{out}}}$ times per evaluation, once under the prefill and once per remaining token ([[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/01 Machine Anatomy/03 The Cognitive Brain/V4-03.05 The Cost of a Fresh Evaluation#^eq-brain-eval-latency|equation 4]]); the chunk policy streams its weights once per renewal:

$$
S_{\text{out}}\,M_{\text{INT4}}\,f_{\text{intent}} + M_{\text{chunk}}\,f_{\text{chunk}} = 7 \times 3.5 GB \times 2 Hz + 0.16 GB \times 20 Hz = 49 GB/s + 3.2 GB/s = 52.2 GB/s
$$

**Result**: Amortization cuts the weight time per setpoint to 6.1 ms, but each fresh intent-model chunk arrives 6.7 renewal periods after its capture. The chunk policy’s sweep takes 2.2 percent of its period. Together the two proposers average 52.2 GB/s of weight traffic, 37 percent of the sustained bandwidth.

**Systems insight**: Amortization is a supply lever, not a renewal lever, so the machine renews with a second, smaller proposer. The average hides the timing. For 147 ms of every 500 ms, the intent model’s decode sweeps are modeled at the full sustained bandwidth, so a chunk-policy evaluation that overlaps them competes for the same bus, and its latency target holds only if it is measured under that contention. A late renewal is not free: the arm keeps moving on the previous chunk, and the chunk lease bounds how far.

The selected design follows. The 80M-parameter chunk policy uses ACT as its size reference and is assumed to be adapted to the mobile manipulator. It proposes base velocity and arm joint targets together at 20 Hz (a 50 ms replanning period), emitting waypoints spaced at ${\Delta t_{\text{step}}} =$ 25 ms (a 40 Hz supply rate), within a 40 ms p99 inference target that includes vision. The permission path interpolates admitted waypoints into per-cycle setpoints and the drives close their velocity and current loops at their own faster rates. The intent model revises its target at 2 Hz. That is a candidate architecture, not a measured timing result. Loaded execution must establish the inference target, and a percentile target still permits overruns; the chunk lease contains a missed renewal.

What retained history would cost The intent model as deployed reads one frame and keeps nothing between evaluations, so the budget above is complete for it. A variant that conditioned on several seconds of history would add memory pressure that the weights alone do not show, and the cost is worth knowing before anyone proposes one. The per-token charge from [[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/01 Machine Anatomy/03 The Cognitive Brain/V4-03.04 Learned Representations#^sec-brain-learned-representations|section 3.3]], 0.52 MB of full-width FP16 keys and values across all 32 layers through [equation 1](https://mlsysbook.ai/vol4/03_brain/03_brain.html#eq-brain-kv-cache), now scales with history. One synchronized set of 3 $224{\times}224$ frames (3 $\times$ 256 $=$ 768 patch tokens) adds 402.7 MB. This is a standard multi-head attention estimate for a hypothetical multi-camera extension, not a measured cache footprint for the published OpenVLA checkpoint. Counting language and proprioception tokens along with the visual patches raises each step to ${S} \approx$ 800 tokens, so each observation step adds 419.4 MB of KV cache. Retaining an unpruned 5 s historical context window at 10 Hz would yield 21.0 GB of KV cache. This hypothetical history exceeds the published OpenVLA checkpoint’s configured context window; the calculation exposes the storage cost of such an extension, rather than claiming that the checkpoint supports it. Combined with the intent model’s 14 GB of weights, this example claims 35.0 GB, about half of the application processor’s 68.7 GB (the module sold as 64 GB counts in binary gigabytes, $64 \times 2^{30}$ bytes) before runtime overhead. Perception, mapping, and the chunk policy also need that memory. Even where memory capacity permits, reading a large cache is further traffic on that same bus. Bounding retained history therefore serves both capacity and timely renewal. Spatial pooling or cross-attention can reduce context traffic; fixed-horizon action output does not by itself bound the observation history retained by the backbone.

The split fixes how often each proposer renews. The moving target fixes how long each renewal stays true. At the pick station the arm takes the mug from a moving conveyor, and a chunk is useful only while the mug stays within the grasp tolerance ${r_{\text{tol}}}$ of where the chunk’s evidence placed it, starting from the handle estimate’s initial error ${e_0}$. Untracked, the belt’s steady speed ${v_{\text{conv}}}$ uses up the margin ${r_{\text{tol}}} - {e_0}$ within the freshness deadline ${e_{\max}}/{v_{\max}}$ of **[[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/01 Machine Anatomy/02 The Physical Body/V4-02.03 Measurement Freshness#^sec-body-measurement-freshness|Measurement Freshness]]**, here with ${e_{\max}} = {r_{\text{tol}}} - {e_0}$ and ${v_{\max}} = {v_{\text{conv}}}$. A tracker removes that steady motion. It is the memory stage of the pipeline, re-estimating the mug’s position and velocity from each new frame, and a chunk computed from its estimate aims at where the mug will be if the belt keeps its speed. What that extrapolation cannot foresee is a change of speed after capture, the mug slipping on the belt at a residual acceleration ${a_{\text{slip}}}$. The chunk’s setpoints were fixed at its capture, so later frames do not correct them, and extrapolation over an evidence age ${T}$ drifts by $\tfrac{1}{2} {a_{\text{slip}}} {T}^2$. Setting that drift equal to the margin gives the **evidence horizon** of the tracked mug, the longest time the evidence behind a chunk keeps the mug within tolerance, in [[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/01 Machine Anatomy/03 The Cognitive Brain/V4-03.06 Chunk Supply and Evidence Validity#^eq-brain-evidence-horizon|equation 5]]:

$$
{\tau_{\text{ev}}} = \sqrt{\frac{2\,({r_{\text{tol}}} - {e_0})}{{a_{\text{slip}}}}} \tag{5}
$$

 When inference meets the stated target and no further admission delay is charged, a chunk is replaced one renewal period plus one inference after the capture it was computed from, the reaction latency ${T_{\text{chunk}}} + {t_{\text{infer}}}$ of [[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/01 Machine Anatomy/03 The Cognitive Brain/V4-03.06 Chunk Supply and Evidence Validity#^sec-brain-action-chunking|section 3.5.1]]. That span must fit inside ${\tau_{\text{ev}}}$, and the chunk must carry enough setpoints to bridge it, as [[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/01 Machine Anatomy/03 The Cognitive Brain/V4-03.06 Chunk Supply and Evidence Validity#^eq-brain-min-supply|equation 6]] requires:

$$
{h_{\min}} = \left\lceil \frac{{T_{\text{chunk}}} + {t_{\text{infer}}}}{{\Delta t_{\text{step}}}} \right\rceil \tag{6}
$$

 Together, [[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/01 Machine Anatomy/03 The Cognitive Brain/V4-03.06 Chunk Supply and Evidence Validity#^eq-brain-evidence-horizon|equation 5]] and [[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/01 Machine Anatomy/03 The Cognitive Brain/V4-03.06 Chunk Supply and Evidence Validity#^eq-brain-min-supply|equation 6]] distinguish the setpoint buffer a chunk carries from the duration its evidence remains valid. Notebook [3.3](https://mlsysbook.ai/vol4/03_brain/03_brain.html#nbk-brain-conveyor-evidence-horizon) applies both bounds to the machine’s schedule.

**Napkin Math 3.3: Buffer, not permission**

**Problem**: The chunk policy renews every 50 ms, is assumed to need 40 ms per evaluation, and emits 16 setpoints 25 ms apart. Does the schedule depend on tracking the mug, how many setpoints must a chunk carry, and how much of each chunk may the arm execute on the evidence behind it?

**Variables**:

- Conveyor speed ${v_{\text{conv}}} =$ 0.20 m/s and residual slip acceleration ${a_{\text{slip}}} =$ 0.50 m/s² (Illustrative).
- Grasp tolerance ${r_{\text{tol}}} =$ 15 mm (Chosen) and initial handle error ${e_0} =$ 3 mm (Illustrative), a margin of 12 mm.
- Renewal period ${T_{\text{chunk}}} =$ 50 ms, setpoint step ${\Delta t_{\text{step}}} =$ 25 ms, and ${H} =$ 16 (Chosen); inference ${t_{\text{infer}}} =$ 40 ms, a p99 target that includes vision (Illustrative).

**Math**:

*Untracked deadline.* The belt alone uses up the margin in

$$
\frac{r_{\text{tol}} - e_0}{v_{\text{conv}}} = \frac{12\ \text{mm}}{0.20\ \text{m/s}} = 60\ \text{ms}
$$

*Tracked horizon.* With the steady motion removed, [[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/01 Machine Anatomy/03 The Cognitive Brain/V4-03.06 Chunk Supply and Evidence Validity#^eq-brain-evidence-horizon|equation 5]] gives

$$
\tau_{\text{ev}} = \sqrt{\frac{2 \times 12\ \text{mm}}{0.50\ \text{m/s}^2}} \approx 219\ \text{ms}
$$

*Age at replacement and supply.* [[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/01 Machine Anatomy/03 The Cognitive Brain/V4-03.06 Chunk Supply and Evidence Validity#^eq-brain-min-supply|equation 6]] and the drift over the evidence age since capture give

$$
T_{\text{chunk}} + t_{\text{infer}} = 50 + 40 = 90\ \text{ms}, \quad \tfrac{1}{2} a_{\text{slip}} T^2 = \tfrac{1}{2} \times 0.50 \times (0.09\ \text{s})^2 \approx 2.0\ \text{mm}, \quad h_{\min} = \left\lceil \tfrac{90}{25} \right\rceil = 4
$$

*The whole chunk.* Executed open-loop to its end, one chunk would carry the mug to

$$
H\,\Delta t_{\text{step}} = 16 \times 25\ \text{ms} = 400\ \text{ms}, \quad e_0 + \tfrac{1}{2} \times 0.50 \times (0.40\ \text{s})^2 = 3 + 40 = 43\ \text{mm} > 15\ \text{mm}
$$

*Sensitivity.* Renewing at 10 Hz instead raises the age at replacement to 140 ms, still inside ${\tau_{\text{ev}}}$, and ${h_{\min}}$ to 6.

**Result**: Untracked, the mug leaves tolerance after 60 ms, before the first replacement at 90 ms, so this schedule depends on the tracker. Tracked, the evidence holds for about 219 ms: 4 setpoints bridge each renewal, and the 90 ms evidence age at replacement permits drift of only 2.0 mm. The 400 ms chunk outlasts its evidence by 181 ms.

**Systems insight**: The chunk is sized for supply, not for execution. Its tail beyond ${\tau_{\text{ev}}}$ keeps setpoints flowing if a renewal slips, but it is not permission to finish the motion, because the drift it would carry the arm through cannot be taken back once the gripper closes on the wrong place. The permission path therefore ages each chunk’s evidence against ${\tau_{\text{ev}}}$, and when renewal stops, the chunk lease, not the chunk length, bounds how far the arm may keep going.

On one clock, the schedule looks like [[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/01 Machine Anatomy/03 The Cognitive Brain/V4-03.06 Chunk Supply and Evidence Validity#^fig-03-chunk-supply-horizon|figure 13]]. With direct replacement, each chunk is in force for only 50 ms before its successor replaces it, so motion computed from that capture ends when the capture is 90 ms old, inside the 219 ms evidence horizon. Temporal ensembling instead retains contributions from older chunks. A blend must exclude contributions whose evidence has expired and carry the oldest capture time among the contributions it retains; the direct-replacement timing strip does not bound the age of that blend. If the chunk policy overruns its inference target, the notebook’s assumption no longer holds, and if renewal stops altogether, a further setpoint remains eligible only while both its evidence and the active chunk lease remain valid. If the implementation uses temporal ensembling, it must filter expired contributions before blending and date the result from the oldest retained evidence; seam continuity and feasibility still require independent checks.

Under the stated assumptions, the conveyor calculation supplies the quantities needed to connect cognition to physical permission. One chunk covers 400 ms of motion, a new chunk is assumed to meet its 40 ms inference target, and the evidence stays within the stated grasp tolerance for about 219 ms. The Nervous System must combine these proposer limits with the body’s measured limits to establish its handoff record. That requires more information than a trajectory tensor alone. Timely delivery and usable evidence bound how long a proposal can remain eligible; they do not establish that the proposed grasp was correct.

### Handing Intent to the Chunk Policy

^sec-brain-proposer-interface

Whether the permission path can audit the slow intent model’s goal alongside the fast chunk policy’s trajectory depends on what crosses the interface between the two tiers. That two-tier split is not peculiar to this machine. Helix runs a 7B-parameter vision-language model at 7 to 9 Hz above an 80M-parameter policy at 200 Hz ([[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/01 Machine Anatomy/03 The Cognitive Brain/V4-03.11 Source References#^ref-figureai2025helix|Figure AI 2025]]), and GR00T N1 runs its vision-language model at 10 Hz above a flow-matching transformer at 120 Hz ([[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/01 Machine Anatomy/03 The Cognitive Brain/V4-03.11 Source References#^ref-bjorck2025gr00t|Bjorck et al. 2025]]). Across these and other published designs ([[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/01 Machine Anatomy/03 The Cognitive Brain/V4-03.06 Chunk Supply and Evidence Validity#^tbl-03-state-of-practice|table 2]]), the reported rates bracket the machine’s own 2 Hz and 20 Hz. What separates them is less their rates than what the slow model hands the fast one, and that choice decides what the permission path can check.

Deployed systems use three kinds of interface between the two tiers. With a *latent vector*, Helix’s slow model passes a single continuous latent that conditions the fast policy ([[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/01 Machine Anatomy/03 The Cognitive Brain/V4-03.11 Source References#^ref-figureai2025helix|Figure AI 2025]]), and GR00T N1’s policy attends to token embeddings taken from an intermediate layer of its vision-language model ([[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/01 Machine Anatomy/03 The Cognitive Brain/V4-03.11 Source References#^ref-bjorck2025gr00t|Bjorck et al. 2025]]). Because a latent is trained end to end, it carries whatever the fast policy finds useful and costs no decoding. Other architectures pass a *language subtask*: $\pi_{0.5}$ first predicts the next subtask as text and then generates actions conditioned on it ([[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/01 Machine Anatomy/03 The Cognitive Brain/V4-03.11 Source References#^ref-black2025pi05|Black, Brown, et al. 2025]]), while the orchestrating model of Gemini Robotics 1.5 sends its action model natural-language step instructions ([[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/01 Machine Anatomy/03 The Cognitive Brain/V4-03.11 Source References#^ref-geminirobotics2025gemini15|Gemini Robotics Team 2025a]]). Still others emit an explicit *geometric target*, as in Gemini Robotics-ER, which predicts points, two- and three-dimensional boxes, trajectories, and grasps with coordinates in the scene ([[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/01 Machine Anatomy/03 The Cognitive Brain/V4-03.11 Source References#^ref-geminirobotics2025|Gemini Robotics Team 2025b]]).

The permission path can check only a claim it can compare with its own evidence, and the three interfaces differ exactly there. A latent vector means something only to the network that reads it. No independent checker can tell whether it refers to the mug or to the photograph of a mug on a carton ([[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/01 Machine Anatomy/03 The Cognitive Brain/V4-03.07 Cognitive Vulnerabilities#^sec-brain-semantic-hallucinations|section 3.6.1]]), and it carries no position that could age against the evidence horizon ${\tau_{\text{ev}}}$. A text subtask can be checked against the task predicates the permission path has admitted, but “pick up the mug” names no location, so it can be neither compared with measured depth nor aged against drift. A geometric target allows both checks. It can be compared with calibrated depth and keep-out geometry, and aged from the capture time of the frame it came from. This geometric interface lets the machine’s intent lease be enforced, because that lease names a grounded target, never a path, and expires at the earliest of the time its evidence stays valid and the deadlines the permission path sets. With a latent or a text interface, the permission path can still check each chunk’s motion under the chunk lease, but the target behind that motion lacks this independent grounded-position check.

The mobile manipulator therefore has its intent model emit a target: a position, an orientation, and a gripper command, the same seven values OpenVLA decodes as a step of motion, together with the capture time of the frame it read. The timing shows why that target cannot stand alone. One evaluation takes 336 ms, longer than the 219 ms evidence horizon of the tracked mug, so the pose the intent model read is stale before its target arrives. The intent model therefore contributes which object to take and approximately where, and the tracker of [[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/01 Machine Anatomy/03 The Cognitive Brain/V4-03.06 Chunk Supply and Evidence Validity#^sec-brain-two-proposers|section 3.5.2]] supplies where that object is now. The tracker binds the target to its own latest estimate of the mug, and the 80M-parameter chunk policy aims at that fresh pose. The two leases then age against different clocks. The intent lease ages from the capture time of the tracker’s latest estimate and is renewed each time the tracker confirms the same mug, so it lapses when tracking is lost or a permission-path deadline passes. Each chunk carries the capture time of the oldest observation behind it, normally the frame the chunk policy itself read, and its evidence ages from that time against ${\tau_{\text{ev}}}$.

The geometric bottleneck costs capability. A target pose cannot say how to fold a shirt or where to regrasp a cable, information a latent carries without a schema, so a checkable interface limits the tasks a single target can express. The machine accepts that limit in exchange for an intent the permission path can refuse. The same reasoning applies one level down to the chunk policy’s action space. Joint targets can be checked directly against joint limits and end-effector poses against workspace keep-out regions, which is why the proposal payload of [[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/01 Machine Anatomy/03 The Cognitive Brain/V4-03.06 Chunk Supply and Evidence Validity#^sec-brain-proposer-information|section 3.5.4]] carries both.

### Information the Proposer Must Supply

^sec-brain-proposer-information

The conveyor budget gives each payload field a physical purpose. The receiver needs the candidate motion, the capture time from which its evidence ages, and the conditions limiting its validity. Bundling that information with delivery metadata defines the proposal payload rather than a new evidence record in the book’s record chain. **[[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/01 Machine Anatomy/04 The Nervous System/V4-04.05 Chunk Lease Sizing from the Stopping Budget#^sec-nervous-supervisory-timers|Supervisory timers and proposal wire contracts]]** wraps this payload in the proposal header the permission path validates, and the Nervous System’s handoff record combines this supply with the body’s limits.

The **proposal payload** ${\mathcal{P}}$ is the typed candidate motion that the unprivileged Brain sends to the real-time Nervous System, packaged with the capture time of its evidence, the time after which it lapses, and the provenance the receiver needs to check it:

$$
{\mathcal{P}} = \langle {t_{\text{capture}}}, {t_{\text{valid}}}, {H}, {\Delta t_{\text{step}}}, {\mathbf{Q}_{1:H}}, {\dot{\mathbf{Q}}_{1:H}}, {\mathbf{X}_{1:H}}, {\mathbf{g}_{1:H}}, \text{model\_id}, \text{lease\_id}, {\hat{p}}, \text{crc} \rangle
$$

 The capture and validity times let the receiver compare the motion with freshness and calibration requirements it establishes independently; for the conveyor pick, ${t_{\text{valid}}}$ can lie no later than ${t_{\text{capture}}} + {\tau_{\text{ev}}}$. The payload requests motion. It is neither execution permission nor the handoff evidence record, and a payload that omits its observation time or validity horizon invites the receiver to execute a proposal computed from a scene that no longer exists.

The fields in [[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/01 Machine Anatomy/03 The Cognitive Brain/V4-03.06 Chunk Supply and Evidence Validity#^tbl-03-proposal-record-schema|table 3]] illustrate the information needed to interpret a candidate trajectory, rather than prescribing a second wire format. The proposer supplies trajectory and observation information. Validity claims require supporting evidence, while the receiver applies the separately established chunk lease. A `lease_id` associates the request with that permission process; it does not let the proposer grant itself authority. The header and handoff record specify delivery, admission, and expiration.

**Checkpoint 3.2: Chunk supply and evidence horizons**

Before analyzing runtime failure modes, check that you can separate what a chunk supplies from how long the evidence behind it holds.

**Amortization vs. renewal**: Can you explain why a chunk cuts the intent model’s weight time per setpoint to 6.1 ms yet leaves each fresh intent-model chunk several renewal periods late, and why that forces a second proposer?

**KV cache growth**: Can you use [equation 1](https://mlsysbook.ai/vol4/03_brain/03_brain.html#eq-brain-kv-cache) to size the cache for a retained history, and explain why reading that cache competes with current sensor transfers on the same bus?

**Supply vs. validity**: Can you explain why a chunk must carry at least ${h_{\min}}$ setpoints to bridge one renewal, why the evidence age of motion from one observation must stay inside ${\tau_{\text{ev}}}$, and why the remaining setpoints are a buffer rather than a plan?

**Slip estimate**: If residual slip on the conveyor quadrupled, by what factor would [[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/01 Machine Anatomy/03 The Cognitive Brain/V4-03.06 Chunk Supply and Evidence Validity#^eq-brain-evidence-horizon|equation 5]] shrink ${\tau_{\text{ev}}}$, and would the 90 ms age at replacement still fit inside it?
