# V4-04.04 Multi-Rate Cadences

[[Home|Home]] · [[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/Volume 4 Overview|Volume Overview]] · [[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/01 Machine Anatomy/04 The Nervous System/V4-04 The Nervous System|V4-04 The Nervous System]]

^sec-nervous-cadences

A motor drive cannot wait for the next camera-based proposal before regulating winding current. Conversely, forcing a learned policy to evaluate at current-loop rates wastes compute without generating fresher sensory observations. Decoupling these cadences allows each workload to meet its distinct physical or computational deadline, provided the boundary interface preserves data freshness and never blocks local real-time control.

### Physical Time Constants and Sampling Phase Lag

^sec-nervous-time-constants-phase-lag

The mobile manipulator’s motor inverters regulate winding current every 50 μs (20 kHz), while its chunk policy requires 40 ms at p99 latency to ingest camera frames and generate an action chunk. No single cadence can serve both the winding’s electromagnetic dynamics and the cost of deep visual inference. The four tiers named in [[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/01 Machine Anatomy/04 The Nervous System/V4-04.02 The Multi-Rate Bridge#^sec-nervous-multi-rate-bridge|section 4.1]] therefore each take their rate from a different time constant:

1.  **Inner Commutation and Current Loop (20 kHz, 50 μs cycle):** The PWM carrier frequency, selected to balance switching losses, current ripple, and acoustic noise, fixes the sampling instants. Phase currents are sampled once per carrier period at its midpoint, so a 20 kHz carrier yields ${T_s} =$ 50 μs. The stator winding’s electrical time constant establishes the plant pole that the current regulator must compensate. For the hub motors, ${\tau_e} = {L_{\text{phase}}}/{R_{\text{phase}}} =$ 2.7 ms (**[[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/01 Machine Anatomy/02 The Physical Body/V4-02.05 Actuator Transmission Limits#^sec-body-electrical-time-constants|Electrical time constants, back-EMF, and current clamping]]**) places that pole near 59 Hz. The closed-loop current bandwidth is tuned well above this pole and at least a factor of ten below the sampling rate, reaching at most about 2 kHz ([[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/01 Machine Anatomy/04 The Nervous System/V4-04.14 Source References#^ref-franklin1998digital|Franklin et al. 1998]]). This loop executes directly within drive silicon and commands electromagnetic torque through the motor controller.
2.  **Permission and Position-Supervision Tick (1 kHz, 1000 μs $\pm$ 10 μs cycle):** The rate here is set by distance rather than by a plant pole. Each tick of detection delay is base travel the stopping budget must absorb, and each tick must also supply the drives a fresh position setpoint. At one tick per millisecond both demands are met with the margins quantified below, while the drives close the subordinate velocity and current loops.
3.  **Action-Chunk Policy Stream (20 Hz, 50 ms cycle):** Bridging observation age and mechanical execution, the local policy emits receding-horizon action chunks (${H} =$ 16 waypoints spaced 25 ms apart). On this machine, the policy is a compact visuomotor network following the Action Chunking with Transformers (ACT) architecture, which decodes an entire chunk in a single forward pass. **[[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/01 Machine Anatomy/03 The Cognitive Brain/V4-03.06 Chunk Supply and Evidence Validity#^sec-brain-action-chunking|Action chunking]]** priced it against the iterative diffusion and flow-matching heads that trade more passes for expressiveness. Each pass must stream the model’s weights from memory on the application processor’s accelerator, which caps the pass rate near ${f} \le {\text{BW}_{\text{mem}}}/{M_{\text{weights}}}$. Because one pass yields all 16 setpoints, that sweep is paid once per chunk rather than once per setpoint, which is what lets the policy renew at this rate.
4.  **Multimodal Intent Planner (2 Hz, 500 ms cycle):** A high-capacity VLA model turns camera frames and a natural-language instruction into task goals and navigation targets. Its period is set by the prefill and weight sweeps of **[[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/01 Machine Anatomy/03 The Cognitive Brain/V4-03.05 The Cost of a Fresh Evaluation#^sec-brain-memory-wall|The memory wall]]**, not by any plant dynamics. It may therefore steer the chunk policy but never hold a chunk lease, the authority to keep driving the actuators that [[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/01 Machine Anatomy/04 The Nervous System/V4-04.05 Chunk Lease Sizing from the Stopping Budget#^sec-nervous-lease-sizing|section 4.4]] sizes in tens of milliseconds.

The two proposer cadences reflect mainstream action-chunking and VLA architectures as of 2026, and their frequencies will shift as models and accelerators evolve. The method developed below does not depend on them. It anchors the lease’s lower limit to what the proposer can supply and its upper limit to the physical stopping budget.

The current-loop rate follows directly from the winding’s electrical time constant ${L_{\text{phase}}}/{R_{\text{phase}}}$, and the loop closes within the drive’s local inverter. The harder timing question arises one level up, at the joint. There, inertia and structural compliance set the required closed-loop bandwidth, and every digital stage between sensor and actuator (sampling, computation, bus transport) exacts a penalty in phase lag. The crossover frequency ${\omega_c}$ marks the point where open-loop gain equals unity. At this frequency, the phase margin[[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/01 Machine Anatomy/04 The Nervous System/V4-04.15 Footnotes#^fn4|⁴]] defines the maximum additional phase lag the system can absorb before feedback transitions from stabilizing attenuation to self-reinforcing oscillation.

Excess phase lag causes corrective control torques to arrive out of phase with physical joint motion, eventually turning stabilizing negative feedback into unstable positive feedback ([[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/01 Machine Anatomy/04 The Nervous System/V4-04.14 Source References#^ref-nyquist1932regeneration|Nyquist 1932]]). Closing a feedback loop across the permission tick accumulates three distinct sources of latency. The discrete sample-and-hold introduces an effective transport delay equal to half the sample period (${T_s}/2$). The enforcer requires compute time (${T_{\text{comp}}}$) to evaluate constraints. The resulting command then incurs fieldbus transit latency (${T_{\text{bus}}}$) before reaching the drive. Because a pure delay ${T_d}$ removes ${\omega} {T_d}$ radians of phase across all frequencies, these stages collectively erode the phase margin at crossover according to [[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/01 Machine Anatomy/04 The Nervous System/V4-04.04 Multi-Rate Cadences#^eq-nervous-delay-phase|equation 1]], the total-delay relation given in **[[05 Reference/Volume IV - Physical AI/V4 Control and Dynamics#^sec-appendix-control-zoh|Discrete-time sampling and zero-order hold phase lag]]**:

$$
{\Delta \phi} = {\omega_c}\left(\frac{{T_s}}{2} + {T_{\text{comp}}} + {T_{\text{bus}}}\right) \tag{1}
$$

^eq-nervous-delay-phase

Consider an illustrative joint-loop crossover of 20 Hz and a design phase erosion allowance of 5$^\circ$. At a 20 Hz crossover, each 1 ms of pure delay along the loop spans that fraction of a full $360^\circ$ oscillation cycle and costs 7.2$^\circ$ of phase margin. A 1 ms tick’s sample-and-hold accounts for 3.6$^\circ$, which fits comfortably within the budget. However, the 1 ms fieldbus transit cycle contributes 7.2$^\circ$ on its own, and the enforcer’s declared worst-case execution time of 250 μs adds another 1.8$^\circ$. The combined delay of 1.75 ms imposes 12.6$^\circ$ of phase lag, 2.5× the allowance. If the controller delays output release until the subsequent tick boundary, total phase erosion escalates to 18$^\circ$ over 2.5 ms. Because the phase budget tolerates at most 0.69 ms of total delay, less than a single bus cycle, no increase in tick frequency can keep a 20 Hz loop closed across this communication bus within its erosion allowance. The loop might remain stable, but it would spend phase margin the design never allocated to delay. Given 1.75 ms of unavoidable transit and processing latency, a 1 ms tick stays within the chosen phase erosion allowance only at crossover frequencies no greater than 7.9 Hz. Reducing the tick rate to $500\text{ Hz}$ increases the sample-and-hold penalty alone to 7.2$^\circ$, whereas driving the tick frequency toward infinity still leaves the bus transport penalty untouched. Loop placement, rather than clock scaling, resolves this architectural constraint.

*The bus cycle alone spends more phase than the loop can spare.*

Consequently, the machine closes its high-rate joint velocity and current loops directly within the decentralized motor drives, where co-located encoder sampling bypasses the fieldbus entirely. The centralized 1 ms permission tick operates strictly as a supervisor and trajectory setpoint generator. This decouples the tick from joint-level phase margin constraints, replacing them with two distinct systems requirements. First, as a safety supervisor, each millisecond of detection delay incurs 1.5 mm of unbraked base travel at maximum drive velocity, directly consuming clearance from the stopping budget (notebook [4.1](https://mlsysbook.ai/vol4/04_nervous/04_nervous.html#nbk-nervous-watchdog-stopping-lease)). Second, as a setpoint source, the tick must evaluate the admitted trajectory frequently enough to supply smooth kinematic references to the drives. Transport delay along this reference path introduces tracking lag rather than eroding the drive loop’s phase margin, because the reference sits outside the feedback loop. Any outer supervisory corrections closed across the bus, such as cartesian compliance trims, must restrict their crossover bandwidth below 7.9 Hz.

Closing those loops in the drive is a hardware arrangement rather than merely a firmware setting. The fastest loop sits beside its sensor and its switch, so phase current is measured and switched next to the winding with no host processor in the loop. Dedicated PWM timers in the same silicon enforce the gap between complementary switch transitions that keeps the power bridge from shorting.[[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/01 Machine Anatomy/04 The Nervous System/V4-04.15 Footnotes#^fn5|⁵]] The running machine’s brushless hub drives follow this arrangement at a larger scale; the small stepper-motor driver of [[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/01 Machine Anatomy/04 The Nervous System/V4-04.04 Multi-Rate Cadences#^fig-real-motor-controller|figure 3]] shows it on one chip.

Perception pipelines feeding the policy stream execute at intermediate cadences constrained by optical physics, sensor exposure durations, and relative scene velocity. In a typical $300\text{ lux}$ warehouse lighting environment requiring a 15 ms exposure, the robot base traveling at its 1.5 m/s drive limit covers ${\Delta x} = {v}\,{t_{\text{exp}}} =$ 22.5 mm while the shutter is open. Resulting image-plane blur interacts with scene depth and optical geometry, setting physical limits on feature tracking. The achievable signal-to-noise ratio, spatial motion blur, and image preprocessing latency jointly establish the feasible sensing cadence for visual control, a trade-off **[[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/03 Running the Machine/08 Sensor Perception/V4-08 Sensor Perception#^sec-perception|Sensor Perception]]** develops. For the permission path the exposure matters as time. An observation is dated at its exposure midpoint, so half the exposure is already part of its age before any processing begins, and that age is the first interval the lease budget must charge ([[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/01 Machine Anatomy/04 The Nervous System/V4-04.05 Chunk Lease Sizing from the Stopping Budget#^tbl-04-event-timing|table 1]]).

The chunk-policy generation rate must satisfy the proposal-supply budget set in **[[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/01 Machine Anatomy/03 The Cognitive Brain/V4-03.06 Chunk Supply and Evidence Validity#^sec-brain-action-chunking|Action chunking]]**. For our mobile manipulator, a 20 Hz renewal cycle paired with an ${H} =$ 16 waypoint horizon ([[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/01 Machine Anatomy/04 The Nervous System/V4-04.14 Source References#^ref-zhao2023learning|Zhao et al. 2023]]) bridges visual perception and high-rate actuation. The 50 ms renewal period matches 2 discrete setpoint steps of 25 ms, so every scheduled renewal lands on a setpoint boundary, provided the handoff between the two clocks never makes the faster reader wait.

### Lock-Free and Wait-Free Mailbox Exchanges

^sec-nervous-mailbox-exchanges

If a 1 kHz safety loop blocks on a mutual exclusion lock held by a 20 Hz neural network runtime, one page fault or scheduling hiccup in the runtime becomes an unbounded priority inversion, and the hard real-time controller misses its cycle deadline. Because the tiers run on different processors at different rates, every exchange across a rate boundary must give the faster reader bounded, nonblocking access. Panel (b) of [[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/01 Machine Anatomy/04 The Nervous System/V4-04.04 Multi-Rate Cadences#^fig-04-multirate-cadence-buffer|figure 4]] follows the remedy developed here, the mailbox, through a clean exchange, a write preempted by the tick, and a stalled host, and in none of the three does the reader wait. Panel (a), which shows what the tick does when the proposer stops renewing altogether, returns in [[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/01 Machine Anatomy/04 The Nervous System/V4-04.05 Chunk Lease Sizing from the Stopping Budget#^sec-nervous-lease-sizing|section 4.4]] once the lease that governs it is defined.

Standard inter-process communication libraries rarely provide deterministic execution bounds out of the box. Message serialization, dynamic memory allocation, intermediate queueing, and thread scheduling can each introduce unpredictable delivery stalls. While shared memory eliminates buffer copying and kernel context switches, it requires concurrency controls to prevent the reader from ingesting partially written, inconsistent command payloads. The communication protocol must strictly bound the consumer’s execution time, even when the producer stalls at the most unfavorable point in its write cycle.

While a first-in, first-out (FIFO) queue guarantees sequential message delivery, message ordering is the wrong semantic model for physical motion control. If the control consumer falls behind the rate of production, queued motion plans represent progressively stale physical state estimates. Backpressure, the queue’s way of slowing a producer, cannot pause moving mass. The real-time enforcer requires the freshest complete proposal, paired with a strict temporal deadline. This principle does not prevent the controller from buffering the future waypoints of an already admitted trajectory; those waypoints follow an explicit execution schedule governed by a valid lease. Rather, it prevents treating the delayed consumption of historical commands as valid control inputs. Real-time distributed systems distinguish these two communication semantics. An *event message* is queued and consumed exactly once, where dropping a message loses state history. A *state message* represents current environmental state and updates in place, allowing new observations to overwrite older ones without queuing ([[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/01 Machine Anatomy/04 The Nervous System/V4-04.14 Source References#^ref-kopetz2011real|Kopetz 2011]]). The proposal mailbox implements state-message semantics via a single-slot seqlock, following the non-blocking write protocol of Kopetz and Reisinger ([[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/01 Machine Anatomy/04 The Nervous System/V4-04.14 Source References#^ref-kopetz1993nbw|1993]]) to ensure mutual independence between writer progress and reader execution.

^fig-04-multirate-cadence-buffer

A baseline nonblocking exchange uses a **single-producer single-consumer (SPSC) seqlock mailbox**[[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/01 Machine Anatomy/04 The Nervous System/V4-04.15 Footnotes#^fn6|⁶]] in the microcontroller’s own static memory. The proposal first crosses the board-level link as a framed packet, which a DMA transfer lands in a private receive buffer. A receive interrupt outside the tick checks the frame’s CRC and sequence number and copies the proposal into the mailbox slot. Both participants therefore execute on the microcontroller, the receive interrupt as writer and the permission tick as reader, and the application processor never touches mailbox memory.

↪ The boundary contract that keeps the application processor out of the microcontroller’s mailbox memory is developed in **[[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/03 Running the Machine/13 Workload Placement/V4-13.06 The Boundary Contract#^sec-placement-boundary-contract|The Boundary Contract]]**.

The writer increments the sequence counter to an odd value, copies the checked frame into the slot, and increments the counter to an even value using store-release memory ordering. The reader makes exactly one bounded read attempt per control cycle and rejects the snapshot if the counter begins odd or changes before the read finishes. A snapshot that passes becomes the active chunk only after its integrity, freshness, and physical-admissibility checks succeed, and the tick then interpolates it into setpoints ([[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/01 Machine Anatomy/04 The Nervous System/V4-04.05 Chunk Lease Sizing from the Stopping Budget#^sec-nervous-rate-interpolation|section 4.4.2]]). A rejected read leaves the previously admitted chunk in force, so the reader never blocks on the writer. Because the timer tick has higher priority than the receive interrupt, a tick that arrives mid-write sees the odd counter and discards the torn read in $\mathcal{O}(1)$ time without spinning. It keeps executing the active chunk for as long as its lease and evidence remain valid. A crashed or stalled application processor cannot leave the counter odd, because it never writes the slot. Its failure appears only as an absence of incoming frames, and the active chunk’s lease expires on schedule.

Formally, the reader validates the snapshot using two boundary counter observations:

$$
{c_{\text{start}}} \equiv {c_{\text{end}}} \equiv 0 \pmod 2 \quad \text{and} \quad {c_{\text{start}}} = {c_{\text{end}}},
$$

 where an odd count signals an active write and a value mismatch signals preemption during the copy. Unlike mutexes or semaphores, this protocol never suspends either participant. Because the reader never retries within a single tick, its computational overhead remains strictly $\mathcal{O}(1)$ regardless of writer activity. Correct execution requires that memory loads occur in strict programmatic order. Within a single core arbitrating between an interrupt handler and the periodic tick, compiler optimization barriers prevent instruction reordering. However, when a DMA engine or a second processor core writes the slot directly, hardware memory barriers become mandatory. On Arm Cortex-M profile cores, this requires the full-system data memory barrier instruction (`DMB`) ([[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/01 Machine Anatomy/04 The Nervous System/V4-04.14 Source References#^ref-arm2012an321|ARM Limited 2012]]). Omitting this barrier allows out-of-order execution pipelines to speculatively load payload fields before validating the sequence counter, silently allowing torn data into the control loop.

A **wait-free three-slot mailbox**[[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/01 Machine Anatomy/04 The Nervous System/V4-04.15 Footnotes#^fn7|⁷]] can avoid read/write collisions by assigning separate slots to the writer, the published proposal, and the reader. Atomic publication transfers slot ownership; it must never let the writer overwrite the reader’s active slot. Unlike the one-attempt seqlock, this design can provide a complete published snapshot without discarding a collided read. Its extra storage and ownership protocol must still be checked on the target hardware. Simpson’s four-slot mechanism reaches the same guarantee, a complete snapshot for a reader that never waits, using only single-bit control variables written with ordinary stores and no atomic read-modify-write instruction ([[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/01 Machine Anatomy/04 The Nervous System/V4-04.14 Source References#^ref-simpson1990four|Simpson 1990]]). Neither design makes an old proposal fresh or extends its lease.

Sequential diagnostic telemetry, by contrast, operates under event semantics. A single-producer single-consumer (SPSC) ring buffer preserves an ordered log of events until its bounded capacity fills, whereas the proposal mailbox exposes only the latest complete candidate state. The permission interface relies on these bounded-access guarantees to eliminate wait states; it intentionally discards historical command queues.

↪ The memory-ordering primitives, cache-line padding, and slot-ownership protocols that implement both exchanges are developed in **[[05 Reference/Volume IV - Physical AI/V4 Systems and Hardware#^sec-appendix-systems-ipc|Heterogeneous SoC Mailboxes, Memory Barriers, and Zero-Copy IPC]]**.

No robotics middleware setting can stand in for the chunk lease, because none derives its parameters from the physical stopping budget and none executes on the consumer’s own hardware clock at the point of actuation. Middleware can still approximate the mailbox’s state-message semantics, and the mapping shows how far configuration goes. In ROS 2, communication relies on the Data Distribution Service (DDS) or equivalent publish-subscribe transports ([[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/01 Machine Anatomy/04 The Nervous System/V4-04.14 Source References#^ref-macenski2022robot|Macenski et al. 2022]]), whose Quality-of-Service (QoS) policies reflect real-time contracts only partially ([[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/01 Machine Anatomy/04 The Nervous System/V4-04.14 Source References#^ref-omg2015dds|Object Management Group 2015]]). Configuring a *keep-last* history depth of one provides state-message semantics by retaining only the freshest sample. Selecting *best-effort* reliability drops lost packets rather than triggering retransmissions, avoiding the burst of stale samples that *reliable* delivery releases after network blips. A DDS *deadline* policy raises notification events when sample inter-arrival times exceed a threshold, acting as an alerting heartbeat without enforcing authority revocation. Similarly, a DDS *lifespan* policy purges samples whose age exceeds a configured threshold, but this mechanism relies on synchronized network clocks, defaults to arrival timestamps when synchronization degrades, and controls only message transport rather than granted actuation authority. Callback scheduling within middleware executors also introduces variable dispatch jitter, requiring dedicated response-time analyses across end-to-end processing chains ([[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/01 Machine Anatomy/04 The Nervous System/V4-04.14 Source References#^ref-casini2019response|Casini et al. 2019]]). At the direct hardware interface, the ros2_control framework implements a nonblocking realtime buffer using a single try-lock attempt. If the writer holds the mutex, the read aborts and the controller retains its prior command ([[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/01 Machine Anatomy/04 The Nervous System/V4-04.14 Source References#^ref-rostools2025realtime|ros2_control Development Team 2025]]), which mimics the one-attempt semantics of a seqlock. Regardless of middleware abstractions, the physical permission path must independently enforce its own freshness checks and lease bounds.

↪ The inverse failure, middleware that reports healthy delivery while transmitting stale payload data, is developed in **[[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/03 Running the Machine/09 Spatial Memory/V4-09.10 Spatial Belief Failure Modes and Map Corruption#^sec-memory-failure-modes|Spatial Belief Failure Modes and Map Corruption]]**.

Because the receive interrupt write routine is brief and resumes immediately after the tick completes, an access collision discards at most one read attempt without delaying the tick. However, if the upstream link halts completely, the mailbox slot remains unchanged across thousands of consecutive ticks. Bounded reader execution time does not guarantee bounded publication latency. The system therefore requires an explicit physical policy governing how long a previously admitted motion chunk may continue commanding actuators. Under the first law, irreversibility (principle [[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/01 Machine Anatomy/V4 The Machine Anatomy#^pri-vol4-irreversibility|1]]), every millisecond that a stalled proposal retains actuation authority produces physical displacement that cannot be retracted. That exposure has to be priced against the stopping budget, which makes the length of a proposal’s authority the next design decision.
