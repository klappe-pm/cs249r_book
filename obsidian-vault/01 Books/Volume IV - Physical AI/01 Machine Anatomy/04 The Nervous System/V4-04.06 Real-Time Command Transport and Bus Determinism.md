# V4-04.06 Real-Time Command Transport and Bus Determinism

[[Home|Home]] · [[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/Volume 4 Overview|Volume Overview]] · [[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/01 Machine Anatomy/04 The Nervous System/V4-04 The Nervous System|V4-04 The Nervous System]]

^sec-nervous-deterministic-fieldbuses

A profiling campaign can run the permission tick for days without a missed deadline and still not establish the tick’s worst case. An empirical percentile says how often sampled executions stayed under a threshold. It says nothing about executions that meet a combination of competing tasks, memory contention, or peripheral delay the campaign never produced. The lease budget needs the other kind of number: a response-time bound that accounts for every unit of permitted computation and queuing delay along the critical path under defined operating conditions. In an embodied system, timing determinism protects both stability and safety, because delayed feedback erodes the controller’s phase margin and delayed actuation consumes stopping clearance before the chassis can decelerate. Bounding the tick on the microcontroller comes first; bounding the drive network that carries its admitted setpoints to the motors follows the same logic ([[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/01 Machine Anatomy/04 The Nervous System/V4-04.14 Source References#^ref-kopetz2011real|Kopetz 2011]]).

### Response Time Analysis and Execution Variance

^sec-nervous-rta-variance

A permission tick whose own instructions finish in 250 μs can still miss its 400 μs deadline if a lower-priority link driver masks interrupts while re-arming a DMA descriptor or a burst of higher-priority timer interrupts preempts the tick mid-cycle. Response Time Analysis (RTA) bounds a task’s total response time by summing the three constituents introduced in [[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/01 Machine Anatomy/04 The Nervous System/V4-04.03 The Independence Requirement and the Two-Processor Hypothesis#^sec-nervous-silicon-partitioning|section 4.2]]: execution time, blocking delay, and preemption interference ([[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/01 Machine Anatomy/04 The Nervous System/V4-04.14 Source References#^ref-joseph1986finding|Joseph and Pandya 1986]]; [[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/01 Machine Anatomy/04 The Nervous System/V4-04.14 Source References#^ref-audsley1993applying|Audsley et al. 1993]]). To guarantee schedulability, each term requires an analytical upper bound under specified operating conditions:

$$
{R}_i = {C}_i + {B}_i + {I}_i \le {D}_i \tag{3}
$$

 where ${C}_i$ is the worst-case execution time (WCET) of task ${i}$, ${B}_i$ is the maximum blocking time spent waiting for lower-priority tasks to vacate shared critical sections, ${I}_i$ is the maximum interference from preempting higher-priority tasks, timer interrupts, and DMA transfers, and ${D}_i$ is the relative deadline.

^eq-nervous-response-budget

Each term demands distinct evidentiary backing. Formal static timing analysis against an accurate cycle-level processor model can establish a bound on the WCET ${C}_i$. High-watermark profiling under stress-bench conditions instead characterizes observed execution times; it can refute a declared WCET but cannot establish one. The blocking term ${B}_i$ represents the longest critical section of any lower-priority task that can lock a shared resource required by task ${i}$. The priority ceiling protocol establishes a provable bound on ${B}_i$. In this protocol, each synchronization lock carries a priority ceiling equal to the highest priority of any task that may acquire it. A task may acquire a lock only if its priority strictly exceeds the priority ceilings of all locks currently held by other tasks. Furthermore, any lock holder that blocks a higher-priority task temporarily inherits that task’s elevated priority. Under these constraints, a task is blocked by at most a single lower-priority critical section across its entire execution ([[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/01 Machine Anatomy/04 The Nervous System/V4-04.14 Source References#^ref-sha1990priority|Sha et al. 1990]]). An immediate priority ceiling protocol (IPCP), which instantly elevates a lock holder to the resource ceiling upon acquisition, guarantees the same blocking bound with lower runtime overhead. When a real-time task avoids shared software mutexes altogether through lock-free data structures, the blocking term ${B}_i$ reduces to the longest interval during which lower-priority driver code masks processor interrupts or holds a non-preemptible hardware peripheral transaction. Finally, the interference term ${I}_i$ aggregates the execution time of all higher-priority tasks and interrupt service routines released during the response window. Because a longer response time provides a wider window for higher-priority tasks to re-trigger, calculating ${I}_i$ requires solving a fixed-point recurrence relation. **[[05 Reference/Volume IV - Physical AI/V4 Systems and Hardware#^sec-appendix-systems-timers|Real-Time Timers, Watchdogs, and Schedulability]]** details this formulation. [[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/01 Machine Anatomy/04 The Nervous System/V4-04.06 Real-Time Command Transport and Bus Determinism#^eq-nervous-response-budget|Equation 3]] defines the overarching budget that the recurrence resolves, and notebook [4.2](https://mlsysbook.ai/vol4/04_nervous/04_nervous.html#nbk-nervous-response-margin) evaluates that budget for the permission tick.

**Napkin Math 4.2: The response-time margin**

**Problem**: The permission tick must finish within its deadline after every release. With its declared WCET and illustrative bounds on blocking and interference, does it meet the deadline, and what happens to the margin when one term loses its bound?

**Variables**:

- Declared WCET ${C}_i =$ 250 μs and deadline ${D}_i =$ 400 μs (both chosen).
- Blocking ${B}_i =$ 50 μs, the longest section in which lower-priority code masks the tick, such as the link driver re-arming its DMA descriptor (illustrative). The mailbox adds nothing to ${B}_i$, because the seqlock never makes the tick wait for the receive interrupt.
- Interference ${I}_i =$ 40 μs, the timer and bus interrupts that can preempt the tick within one response window (illustrative).

**Math**:

$$
R_i = C_i + B_i + I_i = 250 + 50 + 40 = 340\ \mu\text{s} \le D_i = 400\ \mu\text{s}, \quad D_i - R_i = 60\ \mu\text{s}
$$

**Systems insight**: The margin is only as dependable as its weakest constituent bound. For instance, moving the mailbox copy into a low-priority task guarded by a standard mutex without priority inheritance or priority ceilings leaves ${B}_i$ completely unbounded, because medium-priority work can preempt the lock holder indefinitely. This is the Mars Pathfinder pathology of war story [4.1](https://mlsysbook.ai/vol4/04_nervous/04_nervous.html#ws-nervous-1997-mars-pathfinder-priority-inversion). Similarly, permitting unscheduled DMA traffic on the tick’s memory path destroys the upper bound on ${I}_i$. If even a single term loses its bound, the tick lacks a defensible worst-case response time, leaving the stopping budget’s ${T_{\text{tick}}}$ parameter with no engineering foundation.

↪ How host memory traffic would inflate the permission tick’s interference if both paths shared one die is developed in **[[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/03 Running the Machine/13 Workload Placement/V4-13.04 Contention for Shared Resources#^sec-placement-resource-contention|Contention for Shared Resources]]**.

Neither a low average latency nor a modest processor utilization can substitute for that analytical bound,[[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/01 Machine Anatomy/04 The Nervous System/V4-04.15 Footnotes#^fn9|⁹]] because a single missed deadline degrades the machine across multiple architectural layers. When a deadline slips, distributed motor drives must hold their previous position setpoint for an entire additional bus cycle. Consequently, the actuator setpoint lags by a full period (1 ms) rather than the conventional half-period delay associated with a zero-order hold. Repeated deadline misses manifest as timing jitter, which Cervin et al. ([[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/01 Machine Anatomy/04 The Nervous System/V4-04.14 Source References#^ref-cervin2003control|2003]]) analyze as a direct erosion of phase margin in digital feedback loops closed through the tick. Within the safety subsystem, a delayed tick evaluates an expired lease or safety violation late; at the drive velocity limit, each 1 ms delay incurs an irreversible loss of 1.5 mm in physical stopping clearance.

Bounding these response terms requires categorizing every potential source of timing variability according to whether an analytical bound exists:

1.  **Bounded Sources:** Hardware mechanisms that admit analytical upper bounds from vendor architectural specifications. Examples include deterministic instruction pipelines, round-robin interconnect arbitration, zero-wait-state tightly coupled memory (TCM/SRAM), and deterministic vectored interrupt controllers on real-time microcontrollers.
2.  **Experimentally Characterized Sources:** Microarchitectural behaviors that cannot be proved strictly from first principles but can be enveloped empirically under frozen hardware configurations. Examples include bounded cache-line misses under hardware cache partitioning, write-buffer draining latencies, periodic DRAM auto-refresh pauses, and thermal throttling profiles stabilized by closed-loop active cooling.
3.  **Unbounded Sources:** Software and hardware mechanisms that have no finite mathematical worst-case bound and must be purged from the real-time critical path. Examples include operating-system page faults under demand paging, preemptive thread synchronization lacking priority inheritance, dynamic heap allocation over fragmented memory pools, and unscheduled peripheral bus contention.

A credible timing guarantee must therefore explicitly enumerate all permissible operations along the execution path. The hardware partitioning and core isolation strategies from [[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/01 Machine Anatomy/04 The Nervous System/V4-04.03 The Independence Requirement and the Two-Processor Hypothesis#^sec-nervous-silicon-partitioning|section 4.2]] eliminate broad classes of timing interference. A **zero-allocation static architecture** pre-allocates all buffers and queues at boot time to eliminate memory-management overhead. Neither strategy can eliminate contention for shared memory buses. Response-time analysis must account for every remaining shared-resource transaction, because a high task priority alone never guarantees real-time execution, as the Mars Pathfinder resets showed.

**War Story 4.1: The Mars Pathfinder priority inversion (1997)**

**Context**: In July 1997, shortly after landing on Mars, the Mars Pathfinder spacecraft’s avionics computer ran the VxWorks real-time operating system with preemptive priority-based scheduling across mission tasks ([[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/01 Machine Anatomy/04 The Nervous System/V4-04.14 Source References#^ref-reeves1997pathfinder|Reeves 1997]]).

**Mechanism**: A high-priority thread (`bc_dist`) distributed critical spacecraft bus telemetry, while a low-priority thread (`ASI/MET`) published meteorological sensor observations through a shared pipe. The mutex serializing access to this pipe was not explicitly declared in mission application code; rather, it was embedded deep within the operating system’s `select()` implementation to protect internal file-descriptor queues ([[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/01 Machine Anatomy/04 The Nervous System/V4-04.14 Source References#^ref-reeves1997pathfinder|Reeves 1997]]). When `ASI/MET` acquired this internal mutex, long-running medium-priority communications tasks preempted it. Because these medium-priority tasks outranked the meteorological thread, they starved it of CPU cycles, preventing it from releasing the mutex while `bc_dist` remained blocked, an unbounded priority inversion.

**Impact**: The blocked `bc_dist` thread missed its execution deadline. When the supervisory `bc_sched` watchdog initiated the subsequent bus cycle and detected that `bc_dist` had not completed, it flagged a critical timing fault and commanded a total system reset. Although telemetry collected prior to the reset survived in non-volatile storage, the resets recurred repeatedly during operational surface maneuvers.

**Response**: NASA engineers diagnosed the pathology on an identical ground testbed using VxWorks execution tracing, subsequently uploading an inflight software patch that enabled priority inheritance ([[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/01 Machine Anatomy/04 The Nervous System/V4-04.14 Source References#^ref-sha1990priority|Sha et al. 1990]]) on the underlying mutex semaphore.

**Systems lesson**: Preempting a low-priority lock holder with medium-priority work blocks high-priority consumers indefinitely. Priority inheritance resolves this vulnerability by temporarily elevating the lock holder’s priority to match that of the blocked high-priority task, allowing it to complete its critical section promptly. In robotic actuator and motor-control paths, engineers must either rigorously bound blocking duration (${B}_i$) using priority inheritance protocols or eliminate shared locks altogether through lock-free communication structures.

### Event-Triggered and Time-Triggered Bus Architectures

^sec-nervous-fieldbus-architectures

Once the permission tick finishes its check on the microcontroller, the admitted setpoint still has to cross a shared physical wire to reach the motor drives, where the same blocking and interference questions reappear. If a high-priority servo command arrives at a shared bus just after a lower-priority diagnostic frame has started transmitting, the command cannot preempt the bits already on the wire and must wait for the frame to finish. Physical buses handle shared medium access in one of two ways. On an event-triggered bus, nodes transmit whenever new data is ready and resolve collisions through dynamic bus arbitration. On a time-triggered bus, a fixed global schedule assigns dedicated transmission windows to each node in advance. Neither design guarantees bounded delivery by its name alone; establishing a worst-case bound requires pricing payload sizes, competing traffic, error-recovery overhead, and device controller delays.

The Controller Area Network (CAN) and its CAN FD variant resolve message collisions via bitwise priority arbitration. While a high-priority frame preempts lower-priority traffic competing for an idle bus, it must wait for any frame already in transmission to finish. Switching to CAN FD accelerates payload serialization by elevating the data-phase bitrate, yet it retains the rule that an in-progress frame cannot be preempted. The blocking duration depends on the longest eligible frame at its nominal and data-phase rates. A valid worst-case response budget must therefore combine both the frame’s own transmission time and the maximum duration of a lower-priority transmission already in progress.

EtherCAT adopts an on-the-fly time-triggered model: an Ethernet coordinator issues a single master frame that traverses all connected motor drives in a physical ring. Dedicated hardware controllers in each drive read incoming setpoints and write sensor feedback directly into designated bit offsets as the Ethernet frame streams through the physical layer. This architecture eliminates arbitration contention across the motor drives, while distributed clock synchronization synchronizes local PWM switching cycles. Time-Sensitive Networking (TSN) achieves comparable determinism over switched Ethernet through IEEE 802.1Qbv time-aware traffic shapers. A TSN delivery guarantee relies on synchronized network gates, bounded clock skew, and strict admission controls that gate best-effort traffic.

↪ The protocol engines and analytical response-time models of concrete fieldbus deployments are developed in **[[05 Reference/Volume IV - Physical AI/V4 Systems and Hardware#^sec-appendix-systems-fieldbus|Real-Time Fieldbuses and Lock-Free Synchronization]]**.

For an integrated mobile manipulator, the core architectural question is whether cyclic actuator commands and feedback fit within the allocated bus period while preserving sufficient margin for MCU control loop execution and motor drive processing. High-bandwidth perception streams (such as camera and LiDAR data) and asynchronous supervisory middleware require separate networks or prioritized channels to prevent unmodeled queuing delays from invading the motor command path. Industrial controllers build that separation into the hardware ([[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/01 Machine Anatomy/04 The Nervous System/V4-04.06 Real-Time Command Transport and Bus Determinism#^fig-industrial-ethercat-controller|figure 5]]), with ports that serve only the deterministic drive network and local I/O attached through terminals on the controller itself rather than through host software. The running machine does not use this controller; the photograph shows the pattern its own permission path follows.

Clock synchronization aligns the sampling and actuation instants across distributed drives;[[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/01 Machine Anatomy/04 The Nervous System/V4-04.15 Footnotes#^fn10|¹⁰]] however, phase alignment reveals nothing about the transport latency required for a newly computed setpoint to traverse the physical bus. Even if every drive clock fires simultaneously, the actuators will merely execute stale commands if transit delays stall delivery. A sound design therefore requires independent bounds for both command transport latency and multi-axis synchronization jitter, starting with whether the serialized frames fit inside the cycle at all.

### Comparing Bus Occupancy on CAN FD and EtherCAT

^sec-nervous-schedulability-analysis

If all nine servo axes of the mobile manipulator share a single CAN FD bus, arbitration headers and worst-case bit stuffing push the command and feedback frames alone past the 1 ms bus cycle, before any controller delay or bus contention is counted. To keep each link within its cycle budget, communication links on the mobile manipulator separate into three tiers by rate and scope:

1.  **Board-Level Peripheral Interconnects (SPI, Memory-Mapped I/O):** Synchronous SPI links and memory-mapped register transfers link local microcontrollers directly to joint position encoders, current-shunt analog-to-digital converters (ADCs), and inverter gate drivers with sub-microsecond latency ($< 1\,{\text{µ}}\text{s}$) along rigid, hardware-timed schedules.
2.  **Deterministic Drive Command Buses (EtherCAT, TSN Ethernet):** Deterministic industrial networks transport cyclic joint position setpoints and motor feedback across all distributed drive inverters within an allocated 1 ms bus cycle (${T_{\text{bus}}}$), maintaining sub-microsecond synchronization jitter (${\sigma_{\text{jitter}}} < 100\text{ ns}$).
3.  **Chassis and Auxiliary Diagnostic Buses (CAN FD):** Priority-arbitrated buses service lower-rate peripherals, such as battery management systems (BMS), thermal monitors, and chassis diagnostics, where arbitration delays remain acceptable under verified worst-case utilization bounds.

None of these bounds belongs to a protocol acronym alone. Each guarantee depends on the physical network topology, transceiver hardware, payload geometries, and cyclic schedules. The first check is whether every servo axis can exchange its command and feedback payloads within the permission path’s allocated bus cycle budget.

**Napkin Math 4.3: Fieldbus serialization before schedulability**

**Problem**: The mobile manipulator’s permission path exchanges position setpoints and feedback with its servo axes once per bus cycle. How much of that cycle do CAN FD frames, or one EtherCAT frame, occupy before controller and device delays are counted, and which buses pass this necessary check?

**Variables**:

- 9 servo axes, 7 arm joints and 2 drive wheels (datasheet), exchanged every 1 ms bus cycle (illustrative).
- An 8-byte record per axis in each direction, a 32-bit position and two 16-bit words (chosen).
- CAN FD at $1\text{ Mbps}$ nominal and $5\text{ Mbps}$ in the data phase (chosen). A CAN frame carries one message, so each axis needs a command frame and a feedback frame. The arbitration field and the trailer run at the nominal rate, 34 bits; the data-length code, payload, stuff count, and 17-bit CRC run at the data rate, 113 bits ([[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/01 Machine Anatomy/04 The Nervous System/V4-04.14 Source References#^ref-iso11898-1-2015|International Organization for Standardization 2015]]; [[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/01 Machine Anatomy/04 The Nervous System/V4-04.14 Source References#^ref-bosch2012canfd|Robert Bosch GmbH 2012]]). Both counts include the worst-case 21 dynamic stuff bits, because a bound must assume the bit pattern that lengthens the frame most.
- EtherCAT at $100\text{ Mbps}$ (datasheet), one frame carrying every axis’s command and feedback with a 64-byte header, and a forwarding delay of 0.6 μs per node, the shift-register pass-through of a slave controller that never buffers the frame (both illustrative).

**Math**: One CAN FD frame, then all frames on one bus and on an arm bus:

$$
t_{\text{frame}} = \frac{34\ \text{bits}}{1\ \text{Mbps}} + \frac{113\ \text{bits}}{5\ \text{Mbps}} = 34 + 22.6 = 56.6\ \mu\text{s}
$$

$$
18 \times 56.6 = 1018.8\ \mu\text{s} > 1000\ \mu\text{s}, \qquad 14 \times 56.6 = 792.4\ \mu\text{s}
$$

 The base’s 4 frames take 226.4 μs. For EtherCAT, serialization plus forwarding:

$$
\frac{(64 + 9 \times 16)\ \text{B} \times 8}{100\ \text{Mbps}} + 9 \times 0.6\ \mu\text{s} = 16.64 + 5.4 = 22.04\ \mu\text{s}
$$

**Result**: One CAN FD bus fills 101.9 percent of the cycle on serialization alone. A bus per limb passes, the arm bus at 79.2 percent with 207.6 μs left for blocking, competing frames, and retransmissions. EtherCAT occupies 2.2 percent and leaves 978.0 μs unallocated by this calculation.

**Systems insight**: CAN FD’s accelerated data phase cannot salvage a single shared bus topology. Every transmitted frame still incurs arbitration and trailer bit times at the lower nominal rate, and CAN requires distinct command and feedback frames for each joint axis. EtherCAT, in contrast, aggregates all axes into a single circulating telegram processed on the fly. Neither occupancy figure represents an end-to-end response bound. A rigorous CAN FD analysis must incorporate message arbitration, non-preemptive blocking, and retransmissions, whereas an EtherCAT budget must include coordinator cycle jitter, slave hardware propagation, and drive firmware latencies. Any unmodeled delay that creeps into the physical command path consumes stopping clearance at a rate of ${v}\,{\Delta t_{\text{bus}}}$.

### Bandwidth, Response Time, and Multi-Axis Synchronization Jitter

^sec-nervous-synchronization-frontier

A network link with plenty of raw bandwidth can still deliver a joint setpoint late if frames queue behind camera traffic. A bus that delivers setpoints quickly on average can still twist a coordinated multi-joint motion if the drives apply their updates at slightly different instants. Analyzing serialization occupancy separates three metrics that industrial datasheets often conflate: raw bandwidth, command response time, and multi-axis synchronization jitter. Bandwidth governs the total payload volume the link can carry per unit time. Command response time measures the complete latency, including queueing, arbitration, transmission, and drive input decoding, before an actuator applies a setpoint. Synchronization jitter bounds the time spread across multi-axis updates. A network design can improve any one of these metrics while leaving the others unconstrained.

Set against the cycle, the notebook’s occupancies separate into one failure and three passes ([[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/01 Machine Anatomy/04 The Nervous System/V4-04.06 Real-Time Command Transport and Bus Determinism#^fig-04-fieldbus-evolution|figure 6]]). Only the single shared CAN FD bus fails, needing 1018.8 μs before any contention or retransmission is counted. The per-limb buses (792.4 μs for the arm) and EtherCAT (22.0 μs) leave room for controller execution and clock synchronization. Staying within the cycle budget provides neither a synchronization-jitter bound nor a complete response bound. A defensible CAN FD analysis requires upper bounds on asynchronous message interference and non-preemptive blocking. An EtherCAT path demands explicit master cycle jitter, slave hardware propagation, and drive firmware latencies. A TSN schedule requires reserved transmission windows and bounded clock-synchronization errors.[[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/01 Machine Anatomy/04 The Nervous System/V4-04.15 Footnotes#^fn11|¹¹]] These factors are required inputs to the response-time equation, not inherent guarantees achieved by selecting a protocol standard.

Actuator commands also demand strict temporal freshness. Even when delivered comfortably within a deterministic transport window, a setpoint computed from stale perception is functionally hazardous. The evidence timestamp and lease expiry embedded in the command proposal remain safeguards even over nanosecond-synchronized fieldbuses. The transport budget must reserve adequate headroom for physical actuation to execute, and the real-time permission path must reject any setpoint whose authoritative validity window has expired.

**Checkpoint 4.1: Response bounds and command transport**

The lease budget counts the permission tick and the bus cycle as bounded terms. These items test whether you can supply the bounds it assumes.

**Percentile vs. bound**: Can you explain why a measured $\text{p99}$ response time, however long the test that produced it, may enter the handoff record only as a measured distribution and never as a proven bound?

**Response-time estimate**: If the longest tick-masking section in the link driver grew from 50 μs to 120 μs, with the tick’s WCET and interference unchanged, would the tick still meet its 400 μs deadline, and by how much would it pass or miss?

**Bus occupancy estimate**: With one command frame and one feedback frame per axis at 56.6 μs each, how many axes can one CAN FD bus serve within a 1 ms cycle before any blocking is counted?

**Synchronization vs. latency**: Can you explain why tightly synchronized drive clocks do not bound how long a newly issued command takes to reach the drives?

A fieldbus path qualified under declared traffic and fault conditions bounds command delivery within its allocated cycle, but on-time delivery says nothing about whether the command should have been sent. That depends on which node may issue setpoints at all, and on whether each setpoint is checked against the machine’s physical limits before it leaves.
