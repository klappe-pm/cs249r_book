# V4-04.10 The Handoffs Matrix

[[Home|Home]] · [[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/Volume 4 Overview|Volume Overview]] · [[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/01 Machine Anatomy/04 The Nervous System/V4-04 The Nervous System|V4-04 The Nervous System]]

^sec-nervous-handoffs-matrix

When a mobile manipulator overruns a stop, the diagnosis begins by locating the fault at one of five canonical handoffs between the photons collected by its cameras and the current delivered to its drives. A cloud service governs its interfaces with statistical service-level objectives (SLOs), such as a $\text{p99}$ request latency; when a tail event violates that target, the runtime absorbs the delay through retries, request rerouting, or graceful degradation. The five handoffs of an embodied machine define **hard cyber-physical service-level objectives** instead. Software cannot recall physical momentum or undo resistive heating in an actuator winding, so no retry mechanism can mask an unbudgeted interface violation.

Upstream, sensing hardware transfers raw transducer signals to perception pipelines. Perception yields intermediate geometric and semantic representations that fuse into an estimated world state. This state feeds the learned policy to produce an action plan, which in turn submits candidate trajectories to the safety enforcer. Finally, the approved command passes from the enforcer to the physical motor drives. Across each boundary, five interface limits govern feasibility: latency (${L_{\text{lat}}}$), bandwidth (${\text{BW}}$), energy and heat (${P_{\text{heat}}}$), determinism (${\sigma_{\text{jitter}}}$), and fault domain isolation (${\mathcal{F}}$). The five handoffs and five interface limits form a five-by-five matrix mapping every signal path to the physical budgets of the machine. These interface limits are distinct from the body’s five mechanical budgets, which enter the matrix primarily through its latency and energy columns. The contract that records these budgets, the handoff record defined below, joins the physical limit records of **[[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/01 Machine Anatomy/02 The Physical Body/V4-02.08 Measuring a Machine’s Own Limits#^sec-body-limit-record-schema|The six elements of a limit record]]** to the proposer limits of the Brain. Latency, renewal rate, and evidence age are therefore written in one schema.

↩︎ The body’s five mechanical budgets are established in **[[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/01 Machine Anatomy/02 The Physical Body/V4-02.02 The Five Physical Budgets#^sec-body-five-budgets|The Five Physical Budgets]]**.

The allocation in [[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/01 Machine Anatomy/04 The Nervous System/V4-04.10 The Handoffs Matrix#^tbl-04-synthesis-grid|table 3]] illustrates which limits are design-limiting at each handoff, using chosen design budgets rather than empirical measurements. The matrix gathers the physical budgets of the machine’s mechanical, cognitive, and sensory systems, together with the sensor capture that the five-level machine model places at the Boundary. In the Boundary row (${\mathcal{H}}_1$), where the physical world becomes digital measurement, bandwidth and latency bind first: raw multi-camera pixel streams saturate the serializer-deserializer (SerDes) lanes, and the exposure window imposes a strict floor on image age before serialization even begins. The Brain rows (${\mathcal{H}}_2$, ${\mathcal{H}}_3$) bind first on energy and latency, reflecting the heavy inference costs of the learned proposers. The Body row (${\mathcal{H}}_5$) binds first on determinism and heat, because the drives’ current loops must hold their timing jitter below $10\,{\text{µ}}\text{s}$, and every ampere delivered dissipates heat across the windings and power inverter stages.

The Nervous System row (${\mathcal{H}}_4$) forms the architectural bridge coupling the Brain and the Body. The proposal boundary between the learned planner and the enforcer carries an action proposal (${p}_t$), typically structured as a compact spline parameterization or waypoint chunk. For the compact payload assumed in this grid, the required throughput is modest (${\text{BW}} < 100\text{ kB/s}$), so bandwidth does not bind; instead, latency, determinism (${\sigma_{\text{jitter}}}$), and fault domain isolation (${\mathcal{F}}$) govern the interface. The permission path isolates stochastic model proposals using nonblocking mailboxes and expiring leases. On the mobile manipulator, the latency limit is the chunk lease, which consumes 60 ms of the 258.3 ms pre-brake ceiling available at the drive limit ([[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/01 Machine Anatomy/04 The Nervous System/V4-04.05 Chunk Lease Sizing from the Stopping Budget#^sec-nervous-lease-sizing|section 4.4]]), while the active command is rechecked within each 1 ms permission tick. Full trajectory admission has a separate computation and response budget and must complete before any candidate chunk activates; the tick interval does not imply that arbitrary occupancy queries or whole-trajectory collision checks fit within that brief slice. Fault isolation relies on a private peripheral bus, an independent oscillator, and the isolated, held-up power rail detailed in [[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/01 Machine Anatomy/04 The Nervous System/V4-04.08 Hardware Isolation and Fault Containment#^sec-nervous-hardware-isolation|section 4.7]].

From the causal boundary perspective, any interface failure localizes to the specific handoff and budget it violates. Semantic mistakes or flawed physical assumptions can still escape an otherwise timely interface; the matrix does not substitute for task-level evaluation or hazard analysis. When diagnosing an over-budget stop, trajectory overrun, or subsystem fault, engineers navigate [[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/01 Machine Anatomy/04 The Nervous System/V4-04.10 The Handoffs Matrix#^tbl-04-synthesis-grid|table 3]] through a three-step operational root-cause procedure:

1.  *Locate the boundary seam:* Identify which physical handoff (${\mathcal{H}}_1\text{--}{\mathcal{H}}_5$) recorded the initial timing excursion, sequence gap, torn payload, or thermal trip.
2.  *Check the binding dimension:* Query the specific physical limit (${L_{\text{lat}}}, {\text{BW}}, {P_{\text{heat}}}, {\sigma_{\text{jitter}}}, {\mathcal{F}}$) designated as *BINDING* for that handoff cell, identifying the primary resource bottleneck governing the interface.
3.  *Evaluate like-for-like evidence:* Compare runtime diagnostic traces directly against the recorded bound kind (`requirement`, `proven bound`, or `measured distribution`), verifying whether the failure stemmed from an unmodeled microarchitectural tail event or a direct physical budget violation.

A handoff diagnosis must strictly compare like evidence. For the ${\mathcal{H}}_2$ state-estimation path, suppose engineers allocate a $30.0\text{ ms}$ latency budget and measure $\text{p99} = 24.5\text{ ms}$ during a baseline hardware-in-the-loop run, yielding a sampled margin of $+5.5\text{ ms}$. Under thermal throttling, a subsequent run measuring $\text{p99} = 85.0\text{ ms}$ reveals a $-55.0\text{ ms}$ deficit against that design budget. The appropriate remedy is to reduce or reallocate computational workload while preserving independent safety enforcement during revalidation. A sampled $\text{p99}$ latency diagnoses a performance regression; it does not constitute a worst-case timing proof.

**Definition 4.3: Handoff record**

***Handoff record*** is the record that states one interface limit at one of the five handoffs between sensing and actuation: its value, the conditions under which it holds, its uncertainty and margin, the detector that catches an excursion, its provenance, and whether the value is a requirement, a proven bound, or a measured distribution.

1.  **Significance**: It is the second record in the record chain, after the limit record of the Body, and the first that ties a timing claim to the interface where the time is spent. On the running machine its ${\mathcal{H}}_4$ entry is the 60 ms chunk lease, recorded as a requirement whose margin depends on the intervals of [[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/01 Machine Anatomy/04 The Nervous System/V4-04.05 Chunk Lease Sizing from the Stopping Budget#^tbl-04-event-timing|table 1]].
2.  **Distinction**: Unlike a limit record, which describes a physical envelope of the plant such as credible deceleration, a handoff record describes an interface between two subsystems and names the clock on which its times are expressed. Unlike a latency in a profiling log, it states its bound kind, so a percentile cannot pass for a worst case.
3.  **Common pitfall**: Recording a bench $\text{p99}$ as a proven bound. The record then carries a number that the permission path trusts in conditions the bench never reached.

Every boundary condition in the grid is recorded this way, so the budgets survive changes of hardware, firmware, and model. Each entry carries the six elements of a limit record and adds the fields that locate the limit at a handoff and state what evidence supports it ([[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/01 Machine Anatomy/04 The Nervous System/V4-04.10 The Handoffs Matrix#^tbl-04-schema-notebook|table 4]]).

### Coupling the Distance, Lease, and Supply Budgets

^sec-nervous-coupled-budget

Shortening the chunk lease to save stopping distance, without checking how fast the proposer renews, causes the lease to expire before the next chunk arrives, so the robot stutters from stop to stop. Lengthening the lease to ride out slow renewals lets the robot overrun its rack-end clearance before braking begins. The machine’s physical, cognitive, and nervous subsystems therefore meet at a single shared budget, with the chunk lease at their point of convergence. In **[[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/01 Machine Anatomy/02 The Physical Body/V4-02.04 Kinetic Momentum#^sec-body-kinetic-momentum|Kinetic Momentum]]**, the loaded machine moving at its 1.5 m/s drive limit had to come to a complete stop within the 1.10 m clearance available at a rack end. Accounting for deceleration mechanics and fixed braking overheads left 258.3 ms for all computational and communication activities preceding brake onset. [[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/01 Machine Anatomy/04 The Nervous System/V4-04.05 Chunk Lease Sizing from the Stopping Budget#^sec-nervous-lease-sizing|Section 4.4]] then allocated a portion of this window: when a proposer stalls, it retains motion authority for one chunk lease before the emergency stop command reaches the motor drives one tick and one bus cycle later, bringing the running stopping budget to 835.5 mm.

Because the proposer needs enough time to renew that lease before it expires, **[[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/01 Machine Anatomy/03 The Cognitive Brain/V4-03.05 The Cost of a Fresh Evaluation#^sec-brain-cognitive-pipeline|The Cost of a Fresh Evaluation]]** sets the lower bound from what each learned model can supply. The intent model’s INT4 weights require 24.5 ms just to stream across the memory bus. A complete inference pass of compute-bound prefill followed by bandwidth-bound autoregressive decoding consumes 336 ms, so at 2 Hz the intent model cannot renew proposals on tens-of-milliseconds timescales. By contrast, the action chunking policy streams its parameters in 1.12 ms, and its target 20 Hz cadence supplies a fresh trajectory chunk every 50 ms. Its illustrative 40 ms $\text{p99}$ inference latency makes this cadence a feasible nominal supply target rather than a guaranteed worst-case bound, so intercepting late, dropped, or corrupt renewals remains the responsibility of the independent permission path.

These constraints dictate the **lease rule** for the machine. For continuous, uninterrupted trajectory tracking, the lease duration must exceed the proposer’s renewal interval plus an allowance for tail jitter. Simultaneously, to guarantee physical safety, the lease must fit within the pre-brake ceiling of [[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/01 Machine Anatomy/04 The Nervous System/V4-04.05 Chunk Lease Sizing from the Stopping Budget#^eq-nervous-prebrake|equation 2]] once detection latency, bus transport, actuator brake onset, and sensor evidence age at admission are deducted:

$$
{T_{\text{chunk}}} + {T_{\text{jitter}}} < {T_{\text{lease}}} \le \frac{{D_{\text{clear}}} - {\delta_{\text{loc}}} - {\delta_{\text{margin}}} - {v}^2/2{a_{\text{brake}}}}{{v}} - {T_{\text{tick}}} - {T_{\text{bus}}} - {T_{\text{act}}} - {t_{\text{age}}} \tag{4}
$$

 where ${T_{\text{chunk}}}$ is the chunk policy’s nominal renewal period, ${T_{\text{jitter}}}$ is the margin allocated for interarrival timing variations, and ${t_{\text{age}}}$ is charged as the evidence’s age at admission, rows 1 and 2 of [[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/01 Machine Anatomy/04 The Nervous System/V4-04.05 Chunk Lease Sizing from the Stopping Budget#^tbl-04-event-timing|table 1]] together, not its age at dispatch alone. This inequality captures the coupled cyber-physical budget in a single mathematical relation. The left side reflects compute supply, bounded by the neural proposer. The first term on the right reflects physical mechanics, governed by vehicle speed, workspace clearance, and braking deceleration. The remaining subtractive terms account for detection and communication delays, actuator brake onset, and the observation age accumulated across the perception pipeline. A faster vehicle, a less aggressive brake, or a tighter workspace lowers the upper bound; a slower or more jitter-prone neural network raises the lower bound. If the two bounds cross, no lease can preserve both uninterrupted proposal tracking and the stopping guarantee at that velocity; the permission path must interrupt tracking before authority expires.

^eq-nervous-lease-rule

On the mobile manipulator, the lower bound evaluates to the 55 ms renewal floor derived in notebook [4.1](https://mlsysbook.ai/vol4/04_nervous/04_nervous.html#nbk-nervous-watchdog-stopping-lease). The upper bound provides 236.3 ms prior to deducting observation age; subtracting the 51.6 ms capture-to-dispatch age allocated in row 1 of [[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/01 Machine Anatomy/04 The Nervous System/V4-04.05 Chunk Lease Sizing from the Stopping Budget#^tbl-04-event-timing|table 1]] leaves 184.7 ms. Because the observation must also traverse the dispatch-to-admission pipeline before execution, charging that interval’s budget from [[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/01 Machine Anatomy/04 The Nervous System/V4-04.05 Chunk Lease Sizing from the Stopping Budget#^tbl-04-event-timing|table 1]] increases total evidence age at admission to 114.9 ms, leaving an effective upper limit of 121.4 ms. This is the same subtraction as the notebook’s, taken in a different order. The notebook charged the lease first and left 176.3 ms under the ceiling for evidence age and the stop profile; the lease rule charges the age first and leaves this limit for the lease. The selected 60 ms lease sits just above the lower renewal bound, reflecting the physical reality that every additional millisecond of lease duration consumes 1.5 mm of stopping clearance at maximum drive velocity. This value remains an unverified allocation until the physical vehicle demonstrates that all constituent intervals fit within budget under load. Artificially inflating the lease duration to mask software latency spikes merely consumes stopping clearance, trading mechanical safety margin for compute slack.

Because each chunk packages base velocities and arm joint targets together, the architecture enforces a single shared chunk lease across both subsystems. When that lease expires, each physical body must execute a kinematically feasible, state-matched fallback maneuver. The base’s 258.3 ms pre-brake ceiling governs this allocation; it does not imply that the arm possesses surplus margin. The arm analysis in **[[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/03 Running the Machine/11 Trajectory Planning/V4-11.03 The Planning Handoff and the Trajectory Record#^sec-planning-handoff|The Planning Handoff and the Trajectory Record]]** evaluates stopping distance and response travel against its own localized workspace clearances. Validating the shared lease requires verifying timing, evidence age, dynamic state, and fallback limits simultaneously across both base and manipulator. The goal the intent model supplies is governed by a different timer, the intent lease, which limits how long a high-level goal remains active before it must be renewed and is sized in its own chapter, not from this stopping budget.

Before testing the lease on a humanoid and reviewing the engineering fallacies, verify your understanding of system-wide interface contracts:

**Signal handoff classification**: Can you identify the five canonical handoffs (${\mathcal{H}}_1\text{--}{\mathcal{H}}_5$) bridging physical plant mechanics to high-level deliberative reasoning?

**Binding physical constraints**: Can you explain why sensory handoffs bind first on bandwidth and exposure latency, whereas actuation handoffs bind first on determinism and heat?

**Handoff record provenance**: Can you describe why every numerical limit in an autonomous system must record its evidence category, operating conditions, bound kind, and verification status?

**Systems Perspective 4.2: A deadline set by a fall**

**Question**: Does the split among proposer, lease, and permission path still hold when the deadline comes from a fall rather than a stop?

An upright biped diverges from balance on the characteristic timescale ${\tau_{\text{fall}}} = \sqrt{{z_c}/{g}}$, where ${z_c}$ is its center-of-mass height. The biped is modeled in the sagittal plane as an inverted pendulum of point mass ${m}$ pivoting above a contact point at height ${z_c}$ (the linear inverted pendulum model that Kajita et al. ([[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/01 Machine Anatomy/04 The Nervous System/V4-04.14 Source References#^ref-kajita2001lipm|2001]]) introduced for walking-pattern generation). Newton’s second law for a small angular deflection ${\theta_{\text{tilt}}}$ from vertical then yields:

$$
{m} {z_c}^2 {\ddot{\theta}_{\text{tilt}}} = {m} {g} {z_c} \sin{\theta_{\text{tilt}}} \implies {\ddot{\theta}_{\text{tilt}}} \approx \frac{{g}}{{z_c}} {\theta_{\text{tilt}}}
$$

 The characteristic equation ${\ddot{\theta}_{\text{tilt}}} - {\omega_0}^2 {\theta_{\text{tilt}}} = 0$ yields natural divergence modes $e^{\pm {\omega_0} {t}}$ with natural frequency ${\omega_0} = \sqrt{{g}/{z_c}}$. The growing exponential mode governs how quickly an unrecovered perturbation tilts out of control, with characteristic divergence timescale:

$$
{\tau_{\text{fall}}} = \frac{1}{{\omega_0}} = \sqrt{\frac{{z_c}}{{g}}}
$$

 For an adult-scale humanoid with its center of mass near hip height (${z_c} =$ 0.9 m) and gravitational acceleration ${g} = 9.81\,\text{m/s}^2$, this evaluates to ${\tau_{\text{fall}}} = \sqrt{0.9 / 9.81} \approx 0.303\,\text{s} = 303\,\text{ms}$ (about 303 ms). This figure represents a characteristic divergence rate, not an absolute recovery deadline. The actual time available to recover depends on the biped’s initial angular deflection, its angular velocity, support polygon geometry, and available kinematic reach. Nonetheless, this timescale demonstrates why deliberative inference cannot be placed in the feedback loop governing local balance: an explicit, state-dependent recovery calculation must define the hard real-time deadline.

The architectural partition between proposer, lease, and permission path remains valid for bipeds, but the recovery deadline becomes dynamic and state-dependent. Rather than bringing drives to a standstill, balance recovery requires placing a foot at the dynamic capture point ([[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/01 Machine Anatomy/04 The Nervous System/V4-04.14 Source References#^ref-pratt2006capture|Pratt et al. 2006]]), a process demanding both finite swing time and peak joint torque. The interface lease must accommodate these physical demands:

- **The lease is sized from the dynamic capture-point deadline.** On a wheeled base, an expired lease triggers mechanical braking to bring the vehicle to rest (${v} \cdot {T_{\text{lease}}}$). On an underactuated biped, locking the joints mid-stride makes the fall worse, because engaging holding brakes while the center of mass moves forward causes the torso to pivot forward into an uncontrolled ground impact. Where reach, swing-time, torque, and energy limits permit recovery, the lease fallback action must therefore execute an **emergency capture-point step**. The chunk lease, permission tick, fieldbus transit, and observation age must all fit within the dynamic recovery budget while reserving sufficient time to swing and plant the foot. The characteristic fall timescale frames this constraint but cannot substitute for dynamic simulation.
- **The proposer must meet the recovery budget.** An inference renewal period that consumes a major fraction of the characteristic fall timescale cannot safely participate in posture stabilization. High-level intent models must remain decoupled from balance reflexes. Whether a mid-level chunk policy can safely control locomotion depends on whether its renewal interval and tail jitter leave adequate margin for fallback step execution.
- **Actuator thermal limits and supply sag constrain the recovery step.** Executing an emergency recovery step demands simultaneous peak torque across multiple leg joints. This surge draws a large transient current, inducing resistive heating in motor windings and risking DC bus voltage collapse (**[[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/01 Machine Anatomy/02 The Physical Body/V4-02.07 Electrical Power Integrity#^sec-body-power-distribution-impedance|Power distribution impedance, transient droop, and brownout resets]]**). Consequently, the permission path must evaluate candidate recovery trajectories against instantaneous thermal and electrical reserves before admission.

Whether the deadline comes from a warehouse rack end or a biped’s fall, the handoff matrix and the lease rule only protect the machine if the hardware and firmware underneath them honor the boundaries the equations assume. In practice, subtle implementation shortcuts in watchdog servicing, calibration ports, state feedback, and middleware configurations routinely bypass those boundaries without triggering a compile or bench error.
