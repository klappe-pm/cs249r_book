# V4-16.03 Physical Criteria for Release

[[Home|Home]] · [[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/Volume 4 Overview|Volume Overview]] · [[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/04 Governing the Machine/16 Deployment Release/V4-16 Deployment Release|V4-16 Deployment Release]]

^sec-release-question

A deployment review that signs off on aggregate benchmark scores or smooth demo videos leaves the machine with no testable rule for when it may move. At a rack end, the base must stop inside the available clearance; during handover, the arm must stay within the assessed contact-force limit; and both claims depend on the permission path finishing its check before its deadline. Together, those requirements give release authorization three testable physical criteria:

$$
{d_{\text{stop}}}({v}, {\tau_{\text{delay}}}, {a_{\text{brake}}}, {\delta_{\text{loc}}}, {\epsilon_{\text{track}}}) \le {D_{\text{clear}}}, \quad {F_{\text{contact}}}({v}, {m_{\text{eff}}}, {k_H}) \le {F_{\text{limit}}}, \quad {T_{\text{exec}}} \le {T_{\text{deadline}}}. \tag{1}
$$

^eq-release-authorization-criterion

The first term abbreviates the full stopping budget of **[[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/03 Running the Machine/12 Safety Enforcement/V4-12.04 Stopping Envelopes#^eq-enforce-stopping-envelope|equation 12.1]]**, whose protective margin is charged as well. In the third criterion, ${T_{\text{exec}}}$ is the worst-case execution time of one permission-path cycle and ${T_{\text{deadline}}}$ the deadline that cycle must meet inside its tick (**[[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/03 Running the Machine/12 Safety Enforcement/V4-12.08 The Minimal Intervention Principle and Optimization Filters#^sec-enforcement-loop-budget|Execution timing in the sub-millisecond loop]]**). A permission computed after its deadline cannot be credited for that cycle, and the qualified fallback must contain the resulting delay.

Because each criterion is a physical inequality, release authorization cannot rest on intuition, demonstrations, or aggregate benchmark scores. Authorizing deployment requires an auditable safety argument that links every inequality to measured observation ages, worst-case execution time bounds, empirical braking and contact trials, and a validated fallback. These physical bounds also depend on the individual machine’s calibration: its camera and lidar sensor transforms, joint encoder zero offsets, and motor torque constants. A lost or drifted calibration invalidates the certified stopping budget even when software binaries remain unchanged. A sensor mount that shifts in service erodes the localization allowance ${\delta_{\text{loc}}}$ ([[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/04 Governing the Machine/16 Deployment Release/V4-16.06 The Verdict’s Standing Conditions#^sec-release-calibration-drift|section 16.5.1]]), and a firmware update that overwrites measured sensor extrinsics and joint offsets invalidates the budget just as quickly. The deployment pipeline must therefore preserve and re-verify calibration records before restoring the operating permit ([[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/04 Governing the Machine/16 Deployment Release/V4-16.10 Maintaining Release Through Updates#^sec-release-modular-safety-cases|section 16.9.1]]).

For the warehouse mobile manipulator, the release question asks whether empirical and analytical evidence satisfies these criteria for each of its three tasks inside a scoped operating envelope. The release case defines the assessed physical bounds, the specified hardware fault model, and the validated permission and fallback ladder. This argument is the safety case, which [[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/04 Governing the Machine/16 Deployment Release/V4-16.04 Safety Cases and Claims#^sec-release-assurance-grammar|section 16.3.1]] gives a formal structure, and it justifies granting a revocable operating permit while those physical conditions hold. The permit is the signed authority under which the permission path may admit the policy’s tracking commands ([[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/04 Governing the Machine/16 Deployment Release/V4-16.05 The Three Verdicts#^sec-release-three-verdicts|section 16.4]]). Finite test runs cannot establish protection against uncharacterized or unmodeled hazards.

A release case is scoped by the operating envelope: the target operational design domain (ODD) paired with the machine’s internal hardware, thermal, and timing invariants. Deployment release authorizes a system configuration under standing operating conditions, complementing rather than replacing the short execution leases that bound individual action chunks. Site inspections, calibration checks, and per-tick permission checks operate at the distinct cadences dictated by their respective failure mechanisms. The envelope also fixes what each of the three possible verdicts, defined in [[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/04 Governing the Machine/16 Deployment Release/V4-16.05 The Three Verdicts#^sec-release-three-verdicts|section 16.4]], refers to. Unconditional operation (`UNCOND`) covers the full verified envelope. Conditional operation (`COND`) covers a separately evidenced subset with constrained kinematics or environmental fences. Refusal (`REFUSE`) covers none of it. For the mobile manipulator, the proposed nominal operating envelope specifies aisle travel up to 1.3 m/s (chosen; see the Reader Guide) with the tote rack loaded to at most 50 kg. It also specifies floors surveyed to a friction coefficient of at least 0.204 and travel past rack ends with at least 1.10 m of clear distance. The friction floor is an optimistic screen obtained by dividing the required braking deceleration by standard gravity under the assumption that all vehicle weight rests on the driven wheels. Because passive casters carry a share of the normal load without contributing braking force, the driven wheels must generate the total stopping force from a smaller normal load, so actual qualification must include loaded wheel forces, drive settings, and stopping traces. For manipulation, the proposed envelope specifies door approaches at 0.03 m/s and coworker handovers at 0.10 m/s tool center point (TCP) speed.

↩︎ The operational design domain and its known absences are established in **[[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/02 Teaching the Machine/05 Physical Data/V4-05.08 Collection Policy Coverage#^sec-data-curse-dimensionality-recoverability-geometry|Choosing a tractable coverage table]]**.

Inside these boundaries, the learned neural policy proposes actions across the nominal task envelope, but every proposed action requires independent, per-tick permission from an isolated safety enforcer. Mode-specific conditions ([[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/04 Governing the Machine/16 Deployment Release/V4-16.03 Physical Criteria for Release#^tbl-16-odd-invariants|table 1]]) narrow that envelope. The takeover analysis of **[[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/04 Governing the Machine/14 Supervisory Intervention/V4-14.03 Human Authority, Reaction Time, and Clearance#^sec-intervention-clearance-deadlines|Clearance-derived fallback deadlines]]** supplies candidate intervention speeds, screening an in-loop request at 0.75 m/s and an out-of-loop crawl at 0.15 m/s. Neither screen qualifies as a standalone release limit, because each depends on a human response time that the release case has not measured. The in-loop slow-down holds only for an operator answering an expected stimulus, and the out-of-loop crawl already fails the 99th-percentile response. The robust design therefore runs the base’s own local stop first and hands over control from standstill, where waiting costs time but no clearance. The restricted mode of a `COND` verdict narrows the envelope further. On a floor characterized only through the site inspection regime, the machine runs at 1 m/s, below the 1.23 m/s optimistic all-weight inspected-floor screen, while still charging dispatch-to-admission delay and maintaining a positive stopping reserve.

The rules in [[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/04 Governing the Machine/16 Deployment Release/V4-16.03 Physical Criteria for Release#^tbl-16-odd-invariants|table 1]] follow a strict priority ladder: refusal conditions override mode-specific restrictions, which in turn override nominal limits. Because speed ranges overlap across operational contexts, these thresholds define operating modes rather than disjoint numerical intervals. The payload row shows how a mode differs from an interval. If the tote-rack load estimate has gone stale since the last pick, the base may not transit, because its stopping budget assumes a known mass. The stationary arm may keep working under its own separately evidenced claim. An invalid estimate or a measured overload refuses both. A conditional state restricts admitted proposals through enforcer clamping. A refusal state revokes the operating permit, so the permission path admits no further tracking commands, and commands a validated fallback trajectory. Onboard wheel slip estimation cannot provide prospective clearance guarantees because it detects low friction only *after* traction has already been lost. A thin film of clear oil (${\mu} \approx$ 0.05) can be visually indistinguishable from dry concrete to the navigation cameras. By the time a loaded base moving at aisle speed (1.3 m/s) initiates braking on an oil patch and measures slip, it has already committed to a stopping distance that exceeds the clear distance. Consequently, the release case must justify how site surveys, contamination exclusion, and bounded inspection and response procedures preserve the assessed friction floor throughout the authorized interval. Periodic surveys alone cannot exclude a spill between inspections.

↩︎ The ladder of resident fallback responses that a refusal commands is established in **[[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/03 Running the Machine/12 Safety Enforcement/V4-12.10 The Fallback Ladder#^sec-enforcement-fallback-ladder|The Fallback Ladder]]**.

The safety case must also document machine behavior when operation encounters an envelope boundary. Event-triggered edge telemetry freezes circular buffer traces for post-incident review ([[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/04 Governing the Machine/16 Deployment Release/V4-16.10 Maintaining Release Through Updates#^sec-release-data-curation-flywheel|section 16.9.5]]), while the deterministic permission path enforces physical boundaries in real time. [[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/04 Governing the Machine/16 Deployment Release/V4-16.03 Physical Criteria for Release#^tbl-16-odd-invariants|Table 1]] summarizes the physical dimensions maintained by runtime monitors and administrative site controls. Internal environmental conditions, such as ambient temperature, DC bus rail voltage, and compute system-on-chip (SoC) utilization, enter the release case through fault injection and stress tests conducted across their qualified ranges.

↩︎ The internal qualification ranges for temperature, bus voltage, and compute load that the release case inherits are established in **[[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/04 Governing the Machine/15 Adversarial Verification/V4-15.03 The Operating Envelope#^sec-verification-operating-envelope|The Operating Envelope]]**.

The nominal latch row in [[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/04 Governing the Machine/16 Deployment Release/V4-16.03 Physical Criteria for Release#^tbl-16-odd-invariants|table 1]] scopes the baseline policy release case. Although the separately stipulated recovery campaign in **[[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/02 Teaching the Machine/07 Closed-Loop Evaluation/V4-07.09 The Evaluation Record#^sec-evaluation-recovery-update|Qualifying a recovery update]]** evaluates a changed candidate across nominal and displaced-plate conditions, that experimental provenance does not silently extend the contact-force or timing bounds established here. Deploying the recovery policy requires its own evaluation and safety case update.

A defensible release claim must specify its plant dynamics, assumed contact geometry, and modeled fault set. The release case for the mobile manipulator rests on three criteria. The base stops inside the 1.10 m clear distance before reaching a pedestrian who has stepped out at a rack end and stopped. The arm unlatches the door without exceeding the 100 N structural limit. An item handover does not exceed 50 N of peak contact force against the coworker. The base’s stopping claim assumes that the person, once in the path, stands still. A person who keeps walking toward the moving base closes the gap during the whole reaction and braking interval. As **[[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/03 Running the Machine/12 Safety Enforcement/V4-12.04 Stopping Envelopes#^sec-enforcement-composite-budget|Sizing the composite stopping budget]]** showed, sizing the budget for that approach would cut the aisle speed to the much lower walking-person ceiling. The site therefore controls aisle-end crossings procedurally, and that site crossing rule is itself a premise the release case must evidence. A complete release argument demonstrates which human contacts are excluded before impact, which detected contacts fall within the compliance model, and which single-point faults trigger the isolated fallback. Halting motion after impact occurs does not reduce the initial shock force.

The contact criteria arrive as premises from the enforcement record rather than as calculations derived during release review. The spring-mass impact peak that grows linearly with approach speed (**[[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/02 Teaching the Machine/06 Policy Training/V4-06.05 The Simulator as a System Component#^eq-training-impact-peak|equation 6.7]]**) was first met as the soft-spring exploit of the latch simulator. **[[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/03 Running the Machine/12 Safety Enforcement/V4-12.08 The Minimal Intervention Principle and Optimization Filters#^sec-enforcement-intentional-contact|Intentional contact and in-contact energy barriers]]** specializes it to an unbraked elastic contact with a coworker’s hand (**[[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/03 Running the Machine/12 Safety Enforcement/V4-12.08 The Minimal Intervention Principle and Optimization Filters#^eq-release-unbraked-force|equation 12.13]]**), using the arm’s effective mass and the ISO/TS 15066 biomechanical hand model.[[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/04 Governing the Machine/16 Deployment Release/V4-16.15 Footnotes#^fn1|¹]] The release case evaluates the operational consequences of those physical results. At the arm’s maximum free-space limit of 1 m/s, even the transient impact model predicts 207 N, exceeding the 50 N criterion by a factor of 4.1×. Consequently, unseparated coworker proximity cannot be permitted at full free-space speed without additional physical containment. For a free hand that recoils upon impact, the criterion admits approach speeds up to 0.24 m/s. At the chosen handover speed of 0.10 m/s, the model predicts a transient force of 20.7 N, below the case criterion. If the coworker’s hand is clamped against a rack upright or structural frame, however, the ceiling drops to 0.053 m/s. At that same 0.10 m/s approach speed, a clamped collision generates 94.9 N, which breaches the limit. The handover claim is therefore strictly a transient-contact claim, inheriting two premises: that the lumped-mass body model and effective mass accurately describe the collision, and that the handover geometry excludes pinching or clamping hazards. Because physical calculations alone cannot guarantee workspace clearance, the release case must establish both premises through empirical evidence and site inspection.

Unlike the arm’s instantaneous force criteria, the mobile base’s claim is an integrated distance budget. Validating this claim requires empirical evidence bounding the perception observation age, communication bus latencies, and execution lease durations. **[[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/03 Running the Machine/12 Safety Enforcement/V4-12.04 Stopping Envelopes#^sec-enforcement-composite-budget|Sizing the composite stopping budget]]** sized that budget term by term at aisle speed (**[[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/03 Running the Machine/12 Safety Enforcement/V4-12.04 Stopping Envelopes#^tbl-enforcement-stopping-budget|The warehouse mobile manipulator's stopping budget]]**), integrating **[[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/03 Running the Machine/12 Safety Enforcement/V4-12.04 Stopping Envelopes#^eq-enforce-stopping-envelope|equation 12.1]]** with the jerk-limited ${C}^2$ braking profile from **[[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/03 Running the Machine/11 Trajectory Planning/V4-11.06 Behavior at the Seam#^sec-planning-c2-stopping-suffix|The C^2 stopping suffix and jerk-limited braking]]**, and found the spare and the reserve that dispatch-to-admission travel leaves. Release inherits those terms as premises. In deployment authorization, the critical engineering metric is the margin of clear distance that remains unspent, and the question release adds is how large an error in a single input would spend it. Notebook [16.1](https://mlsysbook.ai/vol4/16_release/16_release.html#nbk-release-aisle-spare) recapitulates the enforcement budget and adds that question as step 4.

**Napkin Math 16.1: The aisle spare**

**Problem**: At the 1.3 m/s aisle speed, how much of the 1.10 m clear distance does the stopping budget leave unspent, what error in a single input would spend it, and how much remains once dispatch-to-admission work is charged?

**Variables**:

- Aisle speed ${v} =$ 1.3 m/s (Chosen); clear distance ${D_{\text{clear}}} =$ 1.10 m (Illustrative, standing in for a site survey)
- Pre-brake delay ${\tau_{\text{delay}}} =$ 133.6 ms (Derived from illustrative terms: 51.6 ms observation age plus 82 ms from the last valid renewal to brake onset)
- Credible deceleration ${a_{\text{brake}}} =$ 2 m/s² (Illustrative) and jerk bound ${j_{\max}} =$ 7.11 m/s³ (Derived, **[[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/03 Running the Machine/11 Trajectory Planning/V4-11.06 Behavior at the Seam#^sec-planning-c2-stopping-suffix|The C^2 stopping suffix and jerk-limited braking]]**)
- Allowances ${\delta_{\text{loc}}} =$ 50 mm and ${\epsilon_{\text{track}}} =$ 40 mm (Illustrative); margin ${\delta_{\text{margin}}} =$ 100 mm (Chosen)
- Dispatch-to-admission allocation ${\Delta_{\text{da}}} =$ 63.3 ms (Illustrative allocation, **[[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/03 Running the Machine/13 Workload Placement/V4-13.07 Silicon Isolation Decisions#^sec-placement-dispatch-admission|The board-link budget and the dispatch-to-admission interval]]**)

**Math**:

1.  **Reaction**: ${v}\,{\tau_{\text{delay}}} =$ 1.3 m/s $\times$ 133.6 ms $=$ 173.7 mm.
2.  **Braking**: Because ${v} \ge {a_{\text{brake}}}^2/{j_{\max}}$, the stop reaches full deceleration, and ${v}^2/(2{a_{\text{brake}}}) + {v}\,{a_{\text{brake}}}/(2{j_{\max}}) =$ 422.5 mm $+$ 182.8 mm $=$ 605.3 mm.
3.  **Spare**: 173.7 mm $+$ 605.3 mm $+$ 50 mm $+$ 40 mm $+$ 100 mm $=$ 969.0 mm, leaving 1,100 mm $-$ 969.0 mm $=$ 131.0 mm.
4.  **One input wrong**: As extra delay, 131.0 mm $/$ 1.3 m/s $=$ 100.8 ms. As weaker braking, setting step 2 equal to the 736.3 mm braking room gives ${a} =$ 1.39 m/s², a 31 percent shortfall.
5.  **Admission charged**: ${v}\,{\Delta_{\text{da}}} =$ 1.3 m/s $\times$ 63.3 ms $=$ 82.3 mm, leaving a reserve of 48.7 mm, or 37.5 ms at aisle speed.

**Result**: The budget spends all but 131.0 mm of the clear distance before admission work, and all but 48.7 mm after it.

**Systems insight**: The validity of the stopping guarantee ${d_{\text{stop}}} \le {D_{\text{clear}}}$ hinges on the narrow margin of unspent distance rather than the nominal distance consumed. Measured against the partial spare of step 3, a credible deceleration overstated by 31 percent spends the whole of it. The reserve that step 5 leaves after admission work is less than half that spare, so once ${\Delta_{\text{da}}}$ is charged a smaller braking error suffices.

This reserve must also absorb any unmodeled runtime uncertainty, so it cannot be treated as extra allowable latency or repurposed as a margin for control handovers. The reserve left after admission work, 37.5 ms at aisle speed, tolerates less unbudgeted delay or braking shortfall than the break-even values of step 4. Unless each component of the timing and kinematic chain is measured, nothing shows that the reserve survives the latencies and friction variation of a production floor. Each of the three criteria (stop, latch, and handover) therefore depends directly on premises about achievable deceleration, sensor-to-actuator latency, and contact stiffness that no calculation can certify. Because these physical properties can degrade over mechanical operating life, an auditable safety case must expose these runtime dependencies so that certification engineers can judge whether the empirical evidence supports deployment.
