# V4-16.04 Safety Cases and Claims

[[Home|Home]] · [[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/Volume 4 Overview|Volume Overview]] · [[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/04 Governing the Machine/16 Deployment Release/V4-16 Deployment Release|V4-16 Deployment Release]]

^sec-release-safety-cases-claims

A photograph of a clear aisle cannot establish that a loaded mobile base will stop before reaching an obstacle. A valid stopping claim depends on guaranteed physical clearance, actual braking capability, and end-to-end response timing, with each parameter supported by empirical evidence gathered under documented operating conditions. A safety case connects these physical premises to the release claim step by step, so an assessor can see which guarantees rest on measurements and which remain unverified assumptions.

### Claims, Arguments, and Warrants

^sec-release-assurance-grammar

A bundle of test logs and benchmark plots gives a release reviewer no way to tell which physical claims the data actually support or where an unstated assumption hides. A safety case is auditable only when its logic is written down in a form that another engineer can inspect and challenge link by link. Two notations are widely used in practice, and both capture the same structure. The **claim-argument-evidence (CAE)** framework divides the case into three parts. The *claim* is a statement about system behavior that is either true or false. The *argument* is the causal or inductive reasoning connecting the claim to retained support. The *evidence* consists of the calibrated measurements, justified analyses, and validated operating procedures ([[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/04 Governing the Machine/16 Deployment Release/V4-16.14 Source References#^ref-bishop1998methodology|Bishop and Bloomfield 1998]]; [[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/04 Governing the Machine/16 Deployment Release/V4-16.14 Source References#^ref-bloomfield2014building|Bloomfield and Netkachova 2014]]). **Goal Structuring Notation (GSN)** draws the same structure as a directed graph: top-level goals (claims) split through explicit strategies (arguments) into concrete solutions (evidence items). Context and assumption nodes state the operating envelope within which each link holds ([[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/04 Governing the Machine/16 Deployment Release/V4-16.14 Source References#^ref-kelly1998arguing|Kelly 1998]]; [[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/04 Governing the Machine/16 Deployment Release/V4-16.14 Source References#^ref-kelly2004goal|Kelly and Weaver 2004]]). The fault injection records enter this graph directly as solutions supporting each named sub-claim. Because release adjudication turns an assurance graph into a binding operational authorization, the safety case of definition [16.1](https://mlsysbook.ai/vol4/16_release/16_release.html#dfn-release-goal-structured-safety-case) evaluates directly to an enforceable release verdict.

↩︎ The fault-record set, in which every record names the one release claim it supports, is established in **[[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/04 Governing the Machine/15 Adversarial Verification/V4-15.12 Fault Records for Release#^sec-verification-contract-matrix|The fault-record set specification]]**.

This decomposition comes from Stephen Toulmin’s model of practical argumentation[[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/04 Governing the Machine/16 Deployment Release/V4-16.15 Footnotes#^fn2|²]] ([[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/04 Governing the Machine/16 Deployment Release/V4-16.14 Source References#^ref-toulmin1958uses|Toulmin 1958]]). In industrial practice, standards such as ANSI/UL 4600 require goal-based safety cases for autonomous physical products ([[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/04 Governing the Machine/16 Deployment Release/V4-16.14 Source References#^ref-ul4600|UL Standards & Engagement 2023]]; [[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/04 Governing the Machine/16 Deployment Release/V4-16.14 Source References#^ref-koopman2023ul4600|Koopman 2023]]). Production autonomous fleets use this same structure. For example, Waymo organizes the safety case for its driverless ride-hailing operations around layered, dynamic claims showing the absence of unreasonable risk, arguing separately that its risk acceptance criteria are complete and that their empirical evaluation is sound ([[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/04 Governing the Machine/16 Deployment Release/V4-16.14 Source References#^ref-favaro2023building|Favaro et al. 2023]]). For an embodied robot, the root claim ${C}_0$ states a testable physical invariant, such as a stopping distance inside the surveyed aisle clearance (${d_{\text{stop}}} \le {D_{\text{clear}}}$) or a bounded collaborative contact force (${F_{\text{contact}}} \le {F_{\text{limit}}}$). Supporting sub-claims name the required mechanical, electrical, and computational conditions, and retained measurements, derivations, or validated procedures back each leaf. Writing these relationships as a directed acyclic graph (DAG) exposes intermediate dependencies and rules out circular reasoning, so a reviewer can trace any operational claim down to its physical measurements and operating assumptions.

**Definition 16.1: Claim-argument-evidence safety case**

***Claim-argument-evidence safety case*** is a structured argument that a named machine, in a stated operating envelope and under a stated fault model, meets explicit physical criteria, written as claims that decompose through stated arguments into retained evidence.

1.  **Significance**: It makes a release decision checkable one link at a time. For the warehouse machine, the three inequalities of [[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/04 Governing the Machine/16 Deployment Release/V4-16.03 Physical Criteria for Release#^eq-release-authorization-criterion|equation 1]] become six claims whose leaves are individual premises. A reviewer can find the one premise, such as a braking deceleration no loaded trial has measured, on which the verdict turns.
2.  **Distinction**: A test report or benchmark score records what happened under the conditions sampled. A safety case states why that evidence supports a claim about conditions the machine has not yet met, and names the assumptions under which the reasoning stops holding.
3.  **Common pitfall**: A frequent misconception is that the case is paperwork assembled after development for a regulator. It is written with the design, and in this book’s architecture its verdict decides what the permission path lets the machine do.

The soundness of this argument DAG depends on keeping evidence distinct from demonstration. A **demonstration** records successful task execution across an uncharacterized test set, such as a string of clean door openings with the strike plate at its nominal position or a thousand collision-free warehouse runs. Demonstrations show nominal performance across observed runs, but they give no mathematical or statistical bounds on tail behavior, sensor faults, or corner cases outside those logged runs. Evidence, by contrast, consists of falsifiable, calibrated records with stated uncertainty bounds: an execution-time distribution measured on a hardware logic analyzer paired with worst-case execution time (WCET) bounds, loaded stopping trials across degraded friction surfaces, or hardware-in-the-loop (HIL) fault-injection traces at envelope corners. The evidence must match the scope of the claim. Nominal trials can support nominal capability claims, and calibration logs can validate sensor coordinate frames, but bounding an envelope limit or worst-case response requires systematic coverage, stated uncertainty margins, and an explicit warrant.

Connecting evidence to a claim requires a **warrant**, the reasoning principle that explains why the measured data support the claim. For a learned neural policy, teams often reach for an inductive warrant, arguing from thousands of failure-free test episodes to future operational reliability. The exposure wall analyzed in **[[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/02 Teaching the Machine/07 Closed-Loop Evaluation/V4-07.10 Verification Ceilings and the Exposure Wall#^sec-evaluation-limits|Verification Ceilings and the Exposure Wall]]** shows why purely inductive warrants cannot support catastrophic hazard claims. At 95 percent confidence the zero-failure exposure wall of **[[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/02 Teaching the Machine/07 Closed-Loop Evaluation/V4-07.10 Verification Ceilings and the Exposure Wall#^eq-eval-exposure-wall|equation 7.9]]** becomes ${T} \ge 3.0/{\lambda}$ (because $\ln 20 \approx 3.0$), where ${\lambda}$ is the failure rate per operating hour to be demonstrated. Statistically proving an ultra-low failure rate of $10^{-9}$ per operating hour therefore requires billions of operating hours without a single failure ([[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/04 Governing the Machine/16 Deployment Release/V4-16.14 Source References#^ref-butler1993infeasibility|Butler and Finelli 1993]]; [[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/04 Governing the Machine/16 Deployment Release/V4-16.14 Source References#^ref-kalra2016driving|Kalra and Paddock 2016]]). Deciding what target rate is acceptable in the first place is an engineering and societal judgment that empirical testing cannot make on its own ([[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/04 Governing the Machine/16 Deployment Release/V4-16.14 Source References#^ref-koopman2022how|Koopman 2022]]).

A sound safety case bridges this gap with deterministic architectural warrants. Rather than betting physical safety on policy generalization, the system grounds its top-level safety claims in an independent permission path. That path evaluates the control barrier functions of **[[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/03 Running the Machine/12 Safety Enforcement/V4-12.06 Safe Sets as Conditional Permission#^sec-enforcement-safe-sets-conditional-permission|Safe Sets as Conditional Permission]]** to clamp actuator commands, and commands an assessed safe stop whenever a learned proposal approaches the boundary of the viable set ${\mathcal{C}}$. The architectural warrant narrows the claim rather than escaping physical limits. The permission path protects only against monitored hazards that the actuators can arrest within the available stopping clearance. The learned proposer still needs its own operational argument to show task capability and handle unmonitored tasks. Recent standards formalize these complementary roles. ISO/PAS 8800:2024 adapts automotive safety lifecycles, data curation rules, and assurance arguments to AI components ([[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/04 Governing the Machine/16 Deployment Release/V4-16.14 Source References#^ref-iso8800|International Organization for Standardization 2024b]]). The Assurance of Machine Learning in Autonomous Systems (AMLAS) framework supplies safety-case patterns for machine learning pipelines across autonomous systems ([[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/04 Governing the Machine/16 Deployment Release/V4-16.14 Source References#^ref-hawkins2021amlas|Hawkins et al. 2021]]). In this book’s architecture, the permission path carries the safety argument for all monitored physical hazards. It anchors its formal claims to a rated watchdog and to the certified functions beneath it in the drives, the safety-rated laser scanner, and the arm’s safety controller ([[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/04 Governing the Machine/16 Deployment Release/V4-16.04 Safety Cases and Claims#^sec-release-standards-map|section 16.3.4]]). The learned proposer’s argument can then focus on operational availability and unmonitored mission tasks.

### Contextual Assumptions and the Weakest Link

^sec-release-contextual-assumptions

A stopping test passed on clean, dry concrete under bright warehouse lamps can fail the first time the robot enters a dim aisle if the safety case never stated the lighting assumption behind its camera exposure time. Every physical measurement rests on ambient conditions, and moving the machine into a dimly lit aisle can lengthen camera exposure and observation age even when the mechanical brakes and tires behave identically. An assurance argument must state these contextual assumptions explicitly so that measurements gathered under one set of conditions are never credited outside their verified operating envelope.

When an operating assumption is left unstated, safety depends entirely on whether a downstream check happens to catch the resulting deviation. In the mobile manipulator’s stopping budget, the warrant quietly assumes that warehouse illumination permits the navigation camera to operate at its nominal exposure. If dim lighting doubles that exposure time from 16 ms to 32 ms, the observation arrives 8 ms older than assumed (measuring exposure age from mid-frame). Left unchecked, this additional latency translates to 10.4 mm of extra travel at the robot’s 1.3 m/s aisle velocity, consuming roughly 8 percent of the budget’s 131.0 mm margin. On this machine the permission path’s frame admission age check, the freshness test of the observation contract (**[[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/03 Running the Machine/08 Sensor Perception/V4-08.09 The Observation Contract#^sec-perception-observation-contract|The Observation Contract]]**), rejects the delayed frames. Because an active chunk can execute only while its evidence and lease remain valid, the robot instead halts in an aisle where the design assumed it could run. The team meets the unstated lighting assumption as unexplained availability loss rather than as a collision, and what kept the blind spot from eroding clearance was a latency check that knows nothing about illumination. A design without that check would have spent the margin silently.

Assumptions that lack runtime monitors carry far greater danger. Mechanical drift in a camera mount can introduce steady-state calibration offsets while every frame passes freshness and timestamp validations. The safety case must explicitly link localization tolerances to calibration routines and physical inspection logs. Treating fresh telemetry as proof of accurate spatial calibration leaves that warrant unsupported.

↪ Premises that no sensor on the machine can observe, and the restrictions they force on a release claim, are developed in **[[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/04 Governing the Machine/18 Engineering Physical AI/V4-18.04 The Epistemic Frontier#^sec-frontier-method-limits|What the release claim rests on]]**.

A safety case is not an aggregate scorecard in which high performance in one branch compensates for a deficit in another. Benchmark accuracy on an object recognition dataset cannot offset an uncharacterized latency tail on a CAN FD (Controller Area Network with Flexible Data-Rate) bus, nor can millions of simulated training episodes compensate for missing brake-wear measurements. When every child claim is *required*, the argument is conjunctive, and it is no stronger than its weakest required premise. In practice it is usually weaker, because doubt across independent premises accumulates. For a stipulated probability model, let each event mean that one required premise is valid under its stated conditions, and let ${p}_1, \dots, {p}_n$ be the assigned event probabilities. These are not finite-trial confidence levels and estimate neither this robot’s measured hazard rate nor calibrated assurance confidence. Define the illustrative claim event ${C}$ exactly as the conjunction of these independent events; its probability is their product:

$$
\Pr({C}) = \prod_{i=1}^{{n}} {p}_i. \tag{2}
$$

^eq-release-conjunctive-confidence

If each of 10 required premises holds with assigned probability 0.99, the conjunction holds with only about 0.90, lower than any individual premise. The mobile base’s stopping claim is conjunctive in exactly this way. Surveyed clear distance, credible braking deceleration, brake onset latency, observation age, and localization and tracking tolerances all enter a single stopping budget. A flawed warrant in any single term invalidates the root claim, regardless of how thoroughly the other terms are documented. An assessor therefore looks to the least supported required premise and the number of premises sharing the claim, not to the gross volume of supporting documentation.

This conjunctive degradation has one principled engineering exception. An argument can support a claim through two *independent* legs, either of which suffices on its own, so that the claim fails only if both legs fail simultaneously ([[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/04 Governing the Machine/16 Deployment Release/V4-16.14 Source References#^ref-bloomfield2003multilegged|Bloomfield and Littlewood 2003]]). In a separate stipulated model, define ${C}$ exactly as the union of ${m}$ independent sufficient-leg events with assigned probabilities ${r}_1, \dots, {r}_{{m}}$. The event holds unless all legs fail:

$$
\Pr({C}) = 1 - \prod_{j=1}^{{m}} (1 - {r}_j). \tag{3}
$$

^eq-release-multilegged-confidence

If each of two independent legs holds with assigned probability 0.90, the combined claim holds with roughly 0.99, well above either leg alone. The equality describes the stipulated event model. If sufficient-leg events merely imply a broader physical claim, their union probability supplies a lower bound rather than automatically equaling the probability of that claim. The third law, *proposal is not permission*, instead supplies a conditional architectural argument: a policy error alone cannot breach a monitored stopping or contact invariant while the qualified enforcer, plant, sensing, timing, and fault premises hold. Policy validation and per-tick permission checks together do not justify assigning independent probabilities to their success.[[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/04 Governing the Machine/16 Deployment Release/V4-16.15 Footnotes#^fn3|³]] Claim C5 in [[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/04 Governing the Machine/16 Deployment Release/V4-16.04 Safety Cases and Claims#^tbl-16-cae-scorecard|table 2]] requires evidence that shared processors, memory buses, power rails, or sensor pipelines cannot compromise the permission path under the credited fault and interference model. Such isolation evidence addresses common causes within its qualified scope; it does not alone establish every statistical independence assumption. If the event independence is unsupported, the two-leg probability calculation is unavailable; shared causes must be represented explicitly in the argument. The enforcer leg is itself conjunctive across its own physical premises, because claims C1 through C4 must all hold for the enforcer to function. Diverse legs protect against policy failure; they cannot make up for unmeasured brake physics or missing timing margins, so the reviewer must check every leaf against the actual plant.

### Matching the Argument to the Machine

^sec-release-argument-plant-alignment

Flawless arithmetic on assumed braking and latency numbers proves only that the robot would stop in time if the numbers were true; it says nothing about whether the physical machine on the floor actually meets them. While analytical stopping models relate brake onset, deceleration, sensor age, and tracking error cleanly on paper, an equation is only as sound as its physical inputs. A release reviewer must check which parameters come from measurements on the production hardware and which remain unverified design assumptions.

For collaborative manipulation tasks, biomechanical thresholds govern the contact force argument once the claim explicitly details the target body region, contact classification (transient vs. quasi-static), pressure distribution, impact geometry, and measurement methodology ([[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/04 Governing the Machine/16 Deployment Release/V4-16.14 Source References#^ref-iso10218part2|International Organization for Standardization 2025b]]). The mobile base’s claims, which form the core of the release argument, derive directly from empirical stopping distance budgets and site survey logs. Two conceptual frameworks borrowed from automotive engineering organize the fault analysis. Functional safety mitigates hazards arising from hardware malfunctions or systematic software defects ([[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/04 Governing the Machine/16 Deployment Release/V4-16.14 Source References#^ref-iso26262|International Organization for Standardization 2018]]). In contrast, Safety of the Intended Functionality (SOTIF) addresses hazards stemming from performance insufficiencies or specification gaps in the absence of hardware faults, such as an obstacle detector failing to recognize a worker partially obscured by shrink-wrapped pallet staging ([[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/04 Governing the Machine/16 Deployment Release/V4-16.14 Source References#^ref-iso21448|International Organization for Standardization 2022]]). Every leaf of the release argument requires support appropriate to its premise: qualified plant and timing observations, justified analysis, or validated operating restrictions under explicit assumptions. The formal standards governing these subsystems are machinery safety standards, mapped in [[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/04 Governing the Machine/16 Deployment Release/V4-16.04 Safety Cases and Claims#^sec-release-standards-map|section 16.3.4]] to each component.

The claim-argument-evidence scorecard in [[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/04 Governing the Machine/16 Deployment Release/V4-16.04 Safety Cases and Claims#^tbl-16-cae-scorecard|table 2]] defines six core release claims, pairing each physical warrant with its adjudication against the evidence developed in this book. Claim C3 shows how a warrant fails. An unnoticed oil slick on a polished concrete floor destroys wheel traction and invalidates the nominal deceleration model. A physically realizable condition that invalidates an assurance warrant in this manner is a **defeater** ([[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/04 Governing the Machine/16 Deployment Release/V4-16.14 Source References#^ref-goodenough2013eliminative|Goodenough et al. 2013]]). Adversarial safety review hunts for these defeaters ([[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/04 Governing the Machine/16 Deployment Release/V4-16.08 Adversarial Review#^sec-release-adversarial-review|section 16.7]]). Similarly, Claim C6 depends strictly on test records validating the four supervisory authority handoff rules (A1–A4) established in **[[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/04 Governing the Machine/14 Supervisory Intervention/V4-14.09 The Authority Record#^sec-intervention-evidence-tuples|Per-tick evidence tuples and the fault injection suite]]**. Drawn as an assurance DAG ([[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/04 Governing the Machine/16 Deployment Release/V4-16.04 Safety Cases and Claims#^fig-16-argument-graph|figure 1]]), the argument splits C1 into its three physical scenarios (base stop, door latch, mug handover), ends every branch in a premise leaf whose outline records the maturity of its evidence, and attaches the oil-film defeater directly to C3. Because no leaf has been measured on the loaded production configuration, every row of the scorecard evaluates to `REFUSE`, the verdict that grants no operating permit and commands an assessed stop or hold ([[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/04 Governing the Machine/16 Deployment Release/V4-16.05 The Three Verdicts#^sec-release-three-verdicts|section 16.4]]). [[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/04 Governing the Machine/16 Deployment Release/V4-16.07 Adjudicating the Safety Case#^sec-release-case-worked-full|Section 16.6]] works through why, branch by branch.

**Checkpoint 16.1: Safety case decomposition and warrants**

Before presenting a safety case for adjudication, test its structure:

**Top-level claim falsifiability**: Can you state the top-level claim as a falsifiable physical proposition, such as a stop inside the clear distance, rather than an aspiration?

**Warrant validity**: Can you name the warrant connecting each test bench to the claim above it, and the premises under which that warrant holds?

**Defeater enumeration**: Can you show where a defeater such as lens fouling or bus arbitration collisions appears in the argument graph?

**Evidentiary provenance**: Can you trace each empirical evidence node to its signed test trace and current calibration, and each analytical or procedural node to its retained derivation, assumptions, or accountable procedure and validation?

The scorecard enforces a strict separation between physical safety requirements and the empirical records available to substantiate them. Even when every internal warrant is traced to a hardware measurement, however, an engineering team does not choose its own risk-reduction hierarchy or protective ratings in a vacuum. The release reviewer must keep this evidentiary discipline across the entire assurance tree while verifying which external machinery and regulatory standards govern each branch.

### The Standards Map for the Running Machine

^sec-release-standards-map

Certifying an industrial mobile manipulator against road-vehicle standards, or against fixed-arm standards that ignore a moving base, leaves the hazards of a moving arm on a moving base uncovered. Before authorizing deployment, an assessor must establish which formal standards and regulations govern each subsystem and verify where the corresponding evidence was gathered. Because this running machine is an industrial mobile manipulator rather than an autonomous road vehicle, its formal certification path follows industrial machinery directives rather than automotive standards. ISO 12100 sets the overall risk-management framework. The engineering team identifies hazards, evaluates risk, and applies a three-step reduction hierarchy, with inherently safe design first, engineered safeguards second, and user warnings and operating procedures last ([[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/04 Governing the Machine/16 Deployment Release/V4-16.14 Source References#^ref-iso12100|International Organization for Standardization 2010]]). Every standard in [[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/04 Governing the Machine/16 Deployment Release/V4-16.04 Safety Cases and Claims#^tbl-16-standards-map|table 3]] addresses a specific tier of this hierarchy. Autonomy and automotive standards enter as borrowed engineering methods, supplying vocabulary for learned policies, distribution shifts, and assurance cases that traditional industrial standards are only beginning to adopt.

Two standards in the map require a clear boundary because the running machine straddles mobile robotics and fixed manipulation. ISO 10218-2:2025 governs industrial robot systems and collaborative work cells, but it explicitly excludes the kinetic hazards introduced when a manipulator rides on a mobile platform ([[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/04 Governing the Machine/16 Deployment Release/V4-16.14 Source References#^ref-iso10218part2|International Organization for Standardization 2025b]]), which is this warehouse machine’s configuration. Conversely, ISO 3691-4 treats the mobile base as a driverless industrial truck. The standard that bridges this gap by treating the mobile manipulator as a single unit is ANSI/A3 R15.08. Mounting an active manipulator on a mobile platform classifies the machine as an Industrial Mobile Robot (IMR) Type C, and Part 2 sets the safety requirements for integrating that combined unit into an active facility ([[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/04 Governing the Machine/16 Deployment Release/V4-16.14 Source References#^ref-ansi1508part1|Association for Advancing Automation 2020]], [[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/04 Governing the Machine/16 Deployment Release/V4-16.14 Source References#^ref-ansi1508part2|2023]]). In jurisdictions where R15.08 lacks statutory standing, the combined hazards of mobility and manipulation fall back onto the machine’s primary ISO 12100 risk assessment.

A second boundary concerns which layers of the permission path carry formal safety ratings. As **[[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/03 Running the Machine/12 Safety Enforcement/V4-12.10 The Fallback Ladder#^sec-enforcement-certified-functions|Certified safety functions beneath the permission path]]** established, the only rated function within the permission path is the safety MCU’s lease-expiry and heartbeat watchdog, which asserts Safe Torque Off (STO) or Safe Stop 1 (SS1) on the motor drives when valid renewals cease. The admission checks that evaluate neural proposals carry no rating. The certified functions of the motor drives, the safety-rated laser scanner, and the arm’s safety controller sit beneath the permission path. For release, the formal protective claims for every monitored hazard therefore rest on that watchdog and that certified layer. The release argument still uses the software admission checks for operational utility and nominal hazard prevention, but any hazard claim that depends only on unrated software remains an empirical premise rather than a certified safety function.

Statutory compliance reaches physical AI through product-safety law rather than through standalone artificial intelligence rules, and its sharpest consequence for release concerns updates. Under the European Union’s Machinery Regulation ([[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/04 Governing the Machine/16 Deployment Release/V4-16.14 Source References#^ref-eu2023machinery|European Parliament and Council of the European Union 2023]]), a *substantial modification* is any physical or digital change made after deployment that the original manufacturer did not foresee and that introduces a new hazard or raises an existing risk enough to require additional safeguarding. An organization that pushes an unverified over-the-air model update can therefore become, in law, the manufacturer of the modified machine, with every obligation that role carries ([[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/04 Governing the Machine/16 Deployment Release/V4-16.10 Maintaining Release Through Updates#^sec-release-modular-safety-cases|section 16.9.1]]). The same regulation requires third-party conformity assessment[[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/04 Governing the Machine/16 Deployment Release/V4-16.15 Footnotes#^fn4|⁴]] for machinery whose safety components have fully or partially self-evolving behavior and use machine learning to execute safety functions, so a learned safety function cannot be self-certified. Its essential requirements also constrain learning directly. The limits of each safety function must be documented in the manufacturer’s risk assessment. No rule or parameter the machine generates, even during a learning phase, may change those limits where the change could create a hazardous situation (Annex III, section 1.2.1). Keeping the learned proposer outside the permission path meets that requirement only if the safety argument does not depend on the proposer’s learned behavior. Two neighboring acts reach the same machine. The EU AI Act, Regulation (EU) 2024/1689 ([[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/04 Governing the Machine/16 Deployment Release/V4-16.14 Source References#^ref-eu2024aiact|European Parliament and Council of the European Union 2024a]]), as amended in July 2026 by Regulation (EU) 2026/1744 ([[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/04 Governing the Machine/16 Deployment Release/V4-16.14 Source References#^ref-eu2026omnibus|European Parliament and Council of the European Union 2026]]), places the Machinery Regulation in Section B of its Annex I, so AI-specific safety rules for machines arrive through the machinery framework. The Commission must adopt delegated acts that insert those rules into the Machinery Regulation’s Annex III and that apply by 2 August 2028. Because the robot receives network updates, it is also a product with digital elements under the Cyber Resilience Act ([[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/04 Governing the Machine/16 Deployment Release/V4-16.14 Source References#^ref-eu2024cra|European Parliament and Council of the European Union 2024b]]), which requires vulnerability handling and secure updates throughout the manufacturer’s support period, at least five years unless the product is expected to be in use for less. Its duty to report actively exploited vulnerabilities applies from 11 September 2026, ahead of the rest of the act. [[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/04 Governing the Machine/16 Deployment Release/V4-16.04 Safety Cases and Claims#^tbl-16-standards-map|Table 3]] lists the dates from which the other obligations apply. These milestones do not alter the underlying physics or systems engineering; they set the calendar dates by which statutory authorities will demand an inspectable assurance case.

The standards map also marks the boundary between statutory obligations and the systems architecture developed in this book. Formal standards prescribe documented arguments, rated protective functions, and independent third-party assessments. The verdicts, signed capability descriptors, and release manifest developed in the rest of this chapter are one engineering architecture for carrying those obligations into the runtime system. The safety case accepted before deployment then stays binding on the machine long after the inspectors have left the floor.
