# V4-16.05 The Three Verdicts

[[Home|Home]] · [[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/Volume 4 Overview|Volume Overview]] · [[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/04 Governing the Machine/16 Deployment Release/V4-16 Deployment Release|V4-16 Deployment Release]]

^sec-release-three-verdicts

Treating a release sign-off as an informal paper memo forces teams either to ground a useful machine over an unmeasured corner case or to wave it onto the floor with unproven assumptions. Every row of [[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/04 Governing the Machine/16 Deployment Release/V4-16.04 Safety Cases and Claims#^tbl-16-cae-scorecard|table 2]] ends in an adjudication, and the release decision resolves the entire assertion DAG into one of three verdicts: **unconditional operation**, **conditional operation**, or **refusal to operate**. In this book’s architecture, these verdicts are cryptographically signed capability descriptors loaded into the real-time safety microcontroller before motor power stages are energized, not advisory certificates or administrative paperwork. A valid descriptor is the machine’s **operating permit**: while it holds, the permission path may admit the policy’s tracking commands inside the envelope the descriptor names, and when it is revoked or absent, the permission path admits none. Although safety standards do not mandate this specific mechanism ([[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/04 Governing the Machine/16 Deployment Release/V4-16.04 Safety Cases and Claims#^sec-release-standards-map|section 16.3.4]]), the qualified gate and permission path enforce the authenticated descriptor’s limits on physical operation. The first verdict, unconditional operation (`UNCOND`), authorizes tracking proposals across the full operating envelope specified in a signed release manifest, provided its standing conditions remain continuously verified. Each hazard claim in that safety case requires qualified measurements, justified analysis, or validated operating restrictions applicable to the specified physical plant, payload distribution, operational design domain, and fault set. Brake thermal capacity, stopping clearance, and tracking-error bounds must all be qualified for that envelope, and independent permission checks must stand wherever learned policy proposals can reach physical actuators. The verdict does not eliminate residual or newly emergent hazards; it authorizes operation solely under the assessed claims and requires detection and safe withdrawal within the qualified response budget whenever a physical premise fails.

**Definition 16.2: Release verdict**

***Release verdict*** is the signed decision that sets how much authority a named machine’s permission path may grant: `UNCOND` for the full assessed operating envelope, `COND` for a separately evidenced restricted envelope, or `REFUSE` when any required premise is missing, contradicted, or unknown, in each case only while its standing conditions hold.

1.  **Significance**: It turns the weakest premise of the argument into the machine’s operating limit on ${d_{\text{stop}}} \le {D_{\text{clear}}}$. On this book’s illustrative evidence every claim carries an unknown premise, so the warehouse machine’s verdict is `REFUSE`. A stipulated set of loaded stopping and floor records would support a `COND` at 1 m/s on inspected floors, below the 1.23 m/s optimistic all-weight partial ceiling.
2.  **Distinction**: A test result records the outcome of an individual trial, whereas a degraded mode is an autonomous runtime fallback triggered during execution. By contrast, a release verdict is established before deployment: it names the qualified envelope and the empirical evidence upon which it rests, while leaving the proposal boundary fixed, since the permission path continues to validate every proposed action individually.
3.  **Common pitfall**: A common misconception is that a conditional verdict (`COND`) can patch an incomplete safety case by ignoring an unproven claim. A restricted envelope instead demands its own dedicated evidence and analytical limits; an unresolved premise in the primary case leaves that operating mode firmly refused.

### Restricted Operation and Access Boundaries

^sec-release-verdicts-ssm

When a safety case lacks evidence for full-speed aisle travel or open-floor contact, letting the robot run under an informal operating note leaves the permission path enforcing the wrong limits. The second verdict, conditional operation (`COND`), applies when empirical evidence supports the stated hazard claims within a restricted subset of the envelope and binds the permission path to those narrower limits. In the running machine, `COND` selects a pre-validated safety configuration, much like industrial safety controllers and protective optical scanners switch between pre-validated field sets[[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/04 Governing the Machine/16 Deployment Release/V4-16.15 Footnotes#^fn5|⁵]] and speed-monitoring profiles. All restricted kinematic thresholds (${v_{\max}} \le {v_{\text{restricted}}}$, ${a_{\max}} \le {a_{\text{restricted}}}$) and spatial geofences are evaluated and validated as one configuration before release; the runtime verdict selects among these vetted profiles rather than inventing parameters on the fly. A conditional verdict (`COND`) is an offline, predeployment contract that restricts the machine’s operating envelope until re-adjudication. A **degraded mode** is an online runtime fallback, pre-assessed and named in the signed release, that the permission path invokes when an active subsystem loses telemetry or sensor redundancy ([[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/04 Governing the Machine/16 Deployment Release/V4-16.06 The Verdict’s Standing Conditions#^sec-release-blind-travel-limits|section 16.5.3]] sizes one for the base). Release then enforces physical, kinematic, or environmental boundaries that exclude unverified hazard states, such as de-rated joint speeds, a restricted illumination range, or a physical barrier that keeps humans outside the manipulator’s workspace. Where contact force cannot be bounded for unseparated co-presence, a restricted release case may require physical separation: [[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/04 Governing the Machine/16 Deployment Release/V4-16.05 The Three Verdicts#^fig-16-real-robot-safety-cell|figure 2]] illustrates yellow perimeter mesh fencing around an industrial workcell. Crediting that separation requires validated access-interlock and stopping evidence showing that the protective response completes before hazardous access.

Fencing excludes unseparated contact by keeping people out of the hazardous volume. The alternative that lets people share the floor, speed and separation monitoring (SSM, **[[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/03 Running the Machine/12 Safety Enforcement/V4-12.10 The Fallback Ladder#^sec-enforcement-certified-functions|Certified safety functions beneath the permission path]]**), needs a different access claim from the stationary-person stopping claim of C1. Its separation clearance must account for human approach velocity across both system reaction time and physical deceleration, robot displacement before brake onset, the executed braking profile, and measurement uncertainty. The warehouse case retains its site crossing rule; engineering teams cannot substitute a stationary-person stopping model into this context and claim to have achieved unseparated, open-floor human-robot collaboration.

Every quantitative condition attached to a release verdict names the operational limit the permission path enforces, maintaining explicit margin below the calculated physical ceiling. Item handover illustrates this margin requirement. The contract signs a TCP speed below the absolute ceiling that the contact criterion admits under **[[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/03 Running the Machine/12 Safety Enforcement/V4-12.08 The Minimal Intervention Principle and Optimization Filters#^eq-release-unbraked-force|equation 12.13]]**. The gap between these two speeds accounts for unobserved physical evolution between discrete sensor samples: velocity growth between sampling instants, encoder quantization noise, and drive lag during an enforcement clamp. A contract signed at the theoretical ceiling leaves zero margin for these physical latency terms, and the release case requires measured empirical bounds on each term before the signed operational speed can be accepted.

The inspected-floor condition follows the same discipline. Where the site inspection regime certifies a friction coefficient of only 0.12, the illustrative all-weight-on-driven-wheels screen uses the optimistic traction ceiling ${\mu} {g} =$ 0.12 $\times$ 9.81 m/s² $=$ 1.18 m/s² against the 2 m/s² that the dry-floor budget assumes. Actual loaded credible deceleration also depends on the driven-wheel load share, drive and geometry conditions, and qualified stopping traces. Under the all-weight screening premise, the partial budget that admits 1.44 m/s on surveyed floors admits only 1.23 m/s under this reduced traction. Visual inspection alone cannot establish this lower friction bound because a thin film of transparent oil readily escapes visual detection. The site must qualify both the friction assessment method and the operating procedures (cleaning schedules, spill isolation, and access restrictions) that preserve the assessed floor state between inspections. If that maintenance regime cannot guarantee the exclusion of contaminants over the authorized operating interval, reducing vehicle speed does not salvage the safety case; the aisle remains refused.

The third verdict, refusal to operate (`REFUSE`), is mandatory whenever the safety case has missing warrants, uncharacterized tail latencies, incomplete fault-injection coverage, or unmonitored failure modes. For systems where torque removal causes immediate physical harm (such as upright bipedal humanoids falling under gravity), `REFUSE` cannot simply de-energize motor phases to zero torque; it must execute an assessed, powered descent or seated hold. If the safety enforcer’s worst-case execution time under the designated computational placement has never been verified against its 400 μs deadline, or if the fault-injection campaign omitted common-cause failures identified in the deployment topology, the hazard remains unquantified and cannot support release. Refusal is likewise mandatory whenever physical measurements contradict the mathematical model behind a safety budget term, such as loaded stopping trials that yield decelerations below the 2 m/s² assumed by the braking model.

A refusal verdict is an actionable engineering result rather than an administrative dead end. It redirects schedule pressure toward evidence by pointing to the exact broken link in the assertion DAG. If release is refused because loaded stopping trials contradict the braking model, the engineering team does not debate risk tolerance. Developers instead repeat physical braking trials at maximum payload mass until the qualified trial campaign and its uncertainty analysis support a credible lower deceleration bound. They then re-derive the stopping budget from that lower bound and submit for re-adjudication only when the aisle budget closes on validated data. The refusal verdict names the exact empirical test that must pass before re-adjudication can proceed.

Accountability for the release decision must rest with a named **lead systems adjudicator** rather than being split across development teams. When a vision team signs off on perception metrics, an actuator supplier on torque ratings, and a machine learning team on policy convergence, each group can assume that another layer holds the safety margin. The lead adjudicator alone attests that the integrated, physical machine satisfies the safety case. This sign-off binds the release verdict to a specific set of technical records: cryptographic digests of verified policy weights, calibration logs for manipulator contact-force sensing, empirical brake-onset measurements paired with justified worst-case response bounds, and fully loaded stopping trials establishing credible deceleration limits. The adjudicator signs the release manifest with a private key held inside a hardware security module,[[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/04 Governing the Machine/16 Deployment Release/V4-16.15 Footnotes#^fn6|⁶]] binding the manifest to the organization’s signing key and sign-off record. The robot runtime checks this digital signature against a public key anchored in its hardware root of trust before enabling actuators.

↪ The adjudicator’s signature as one of several decisions behind each admitted action, each needing a named owner, is developed in **[[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/04 Governing the Machine/17 Responsible Physical AI/V4-17.03 Decisions Behind an Admitted Action#^sec-responsibility-many-hands|Decisions Behind an Admitted Action]]**.

The adjudicator’s signature records the internal acceptance of the engineering organization, whereas external certification requires independent evaluation. UL 4600 requires independent assessment of the autonomous safety case ([[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/04 Governing the Machine/16 Deployment Release/V4-16.14 Source References#^ref-ul4600|UL Standards & Engagement 2023]]), and machinery whose primary safety functions rely on machine learning falls under regulations, such as the European Union Machinery Regulation, that require third-party conformity assessment ([[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/04 Governing the Machine/16 Deployment Release/V4-16.04 Safety Cases and Claims#^sec-release-standards-map|section 16.3.4]]). An assessor who did not build the safety case brings an independent view to the assumptions that shaped its trade-offs. Organizational separation alone does not establish a probability of coincident error or the statistical independence required by [[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/04 Governing the Machine/16 Deployment Release/V4-16.04 Safety Cases and Claims#^eq-release-multilegged-confidence|equation 3]]. The release manifest must therefore retain the full body of raw physical evidence needed for an independent audit, not just the final verdict.

The adjudicator ultimately signs one of the three decisions summarized in [[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/04 Governing the Machine/16 Deployment Release/V4-16.05 The Three Verdicts#^tbl-16-verdicts-taxonomy|table 4]]. A conditional verdict requires its own independently evidenced restricted envelope; an unresolved gap in the nominal argument cannot be relabeled as an operational constraint. No verdict ever moves the proposal boundary. Under both `UNCOND` and `COND`, learned policies only propose candidate actions while the deterministic safety enforcer independently admits or blocks every control command, as principle [[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/03 Running the Machine/V4 Running the Machine#^pri-vol4-proposal-permission|3]] requires. The release verdict sets the physical envelope that the permission path enforces; it never grants learned policies unchecked authority.

| Signed verdict | Evidence required                                                                                                | Permission and runtime limits                                                                                             | Expiry or refusal trigger                                       |
|:---------------|:-----------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------|:--------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------|:----------------------------------------------------------------|
| `UNCOND`       | Full operating-envelope claim, measured plant and timing bounds, independently validated permission and fallback | Policy proposes across full *signed* task envelope; every action passes the enforcer                                      | Evidence, configuration, calibration, or standing premise fails |
| `COND`         | Restricted operating-envelope claim and its own evidence, limits, and fault coverage                             | Policy proposes only within signed reduced limits; every action passes the enforcer                                       | Any restricted premise becomes false or unknown                 |
| `REFUSE`       | Missing, contradictory, expired, or unauthenticated critical evidence                                            | No operating permit; execute the assessed stop or hold, the fallback-ladder rung the release case validates for the plant | Re-adjudication with repaired evidence                          |

Table 4: **Three signed release verdicts for the mobile manipulator**: Full operation and restricted mode both retain independent per-action permission. Refusal is an actionable engineering result.

^tbl-16-verdicts-taxonomy

Each verdict in [[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/04 Governing the Machine/16 Deployment Release/V4-16.05 The Three Verdicts#^tbl-16-verdicts-taxonomy|table 4]] is signed against the physical state of the machine at the moment of evaluation. The right-hand column of the table therefore pairs every operational grant with an explicit expiry or refusal trigger, because a brake lining or sensor mount that passed inspection before commissioning does not stay in its commissioning state. As the physical plant wears and drifts over time, a release verdict holds only while runtime checks, scheduled measurements, and enforced site procedures continue to verify its standing premises.
