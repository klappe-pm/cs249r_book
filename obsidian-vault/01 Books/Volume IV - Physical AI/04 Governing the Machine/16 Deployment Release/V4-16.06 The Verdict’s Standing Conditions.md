# V4-16.06 The Verdict’s Standing Conditions

[[Home|Home]] · [[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/Volume 4 Overview|Volume Overview]] · [[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/04 Governing the Machine/16 Deployment Release/V4-16 Deployment Release|V4-16 Deployment Release]]

^sec-release-standing-conditions

A signed release can lapse without any change to the underlying software. Brake linings wear down, camera brackets shift under continuous vibration, or warehouse traffic polishes concrete lanes until traction drops. When these physical premises drift, the stopping claim certified by the adjudicator no longer holds. Standing conditions bind the operational verdict to these physical baselines by defining an explicit observation cadence for each premise alongside deterministic fallback responses when a check fails. Because failure modes originate in hardware and environment, their observation schedules must follow physical degradation mechanisms rather than software sprint schedules. An assurance case maintained this way, its validity updated throughout operating life as monitored evidence shifts, is what the safety literature calls a *dynamic safety case* ([[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/04 Governing the Machine/16 Deployment Release/V4-16.14 Source References#^ref-denney2015dynamic|Denney et al. 2015]]).

### Degradation, Drift, and Observation Cadence

^sec-release-calibration-drift

None of the three verdicts is a permanent grant. Mechanical linkages develop backlash, friction surfaces wear, optical lenses collect dust, and operating environments change. Operating under either an unconditional (`UNCOND`) or conditional (`COND`) verdict therefore carries continuous obligations for the life of the deployment. Notebook [16.1](https://mlsysbook.ai/vol4/16_release/16_release.html#nbk-release-aisle-spare) shows how little wear the stopping claim tolerates. A credible deceleration 31 percent below the assumed 2 m/s² spends the whole partial spare, and a smaller loss spends the reserve that remains once admission work is charged, leaving no allowance for perception latency or traction variation. A lining that wears that far is discovered at a rack end as an overrun unless the degradation first trips a standing condition, such as a scheduled loaded deceleration trial or an automated pre-shift brake diagnostic, before the machine is cleared to enter the aisle. Every operational obligation pairs a physical premise with an automated detector and an immediate mitigation, establishing a standing condition tied to the release verdict.

Sensor mounting drift can silently invalidate localization while every camera frame stays sharp and fresh. Daily thermal cycling and machine vibration alter extrinsic sensor geometry, and software rollback cannot realign a physically displaced bracket. The safety case must therefore require qualified online drift detection or scheduled physical recalibrations, setting inspection intervals from the underlying mechanical wear rates. A cryptographic checksum confirms only that the stored calibration file matches its build artifact; it gives no evidence that the physical camera still sits at that modeled pose.

A release verdict depends on physical premises with widely differing observation schedules. For instance, the mobile manipulator’s optimistic all-weight traction screen requires a floor friction coefficient of at least 0.204; the braking claim additionally requires qualified loaded wheel forces, drive conditions, and stopping traces. Dry concrete exceeds this threshold by a wide margin, at roughly 0.60, but a baseline site survey conducted before commissioning cannot guarantee surface conditions against fluid spills or traffic polishing over time. The safety case must therefore specify routine floor maintenance schedules alongside strict exclusion rules whenever surface contamination occurs, supplemented by any onboard traction monitoring. Similarly, the arm’s contact-force sensors require periodic recalibration against reference loads. Every physical premise must be rechecked continuously or periodically at a cadence matched to how fast it degrades. This is the fourth law, *permission must be earned* (principle [[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/04 Governing the Machine/V4 Governing the Machine#^pri-vol4-evidence-bounds-authority|4]]), applied over time. The verdict grants permission contingent on operating conditions, and that permission endures only while the evidence supporting each premise remains valid.

Every runtime-monitored premise requires a dedicated detector and a pre-allocated fallback response; conversely, premises governed by facility maintenance require accountable operators and enforceable exclusion rules. If a safety case credits a navigation-camera dispatch age of 51.6 ms, the runtime monitor must measure frame arrival latency against hardware clocks, accounting for sensor readout and transport delays separately. Forwarding an unverified metadata timestamp does not confirm that the frame is fresh. Without hardware-synchronized event timestamps, unverified inputs quietly turn safety premises into ungrounded assumptions. Different premises demand different sampling frequencies. The tote-rack load, bounded by its 50 kg mechanical payload limit, can be checked at each load-changing event if access controls exclude unrecorded loading and the check completes before the changed load receives motion authority. Conversely, base velocity, position tracking, and action lease validity must be evaluated deterministically on every 1 ms safety-enforcer tick.

### The Envelope Trichotomy and Epistemic Gaps

^sec-release-envelope-trichotomy-sub

A naive binary health check assumes normal operation whenever no explicit error flag is asserted, mistaking a dead sensor or a diverged filter for safe clearance. Checking monitored premises at runtime instead exposes three states of **envelope membership**: known-true, known-false, and unknown. Membership is *known-true* when authenticated telemetry and hardware-referenced timestamps confirm that the plant’s bounded state-uncertainty set remains strictly inside certified envelope boundaries. Estimator convergence or small covariance matrices alone do not prove containment if model assumptions have broken down. When measured telemetry reveals a physical boundary violation, such as base tracking error exceeding the 40 mm bound reserved within the stopping envelope, the state is *known-false*. Finally, when an optical encoder loses serial communication, a camera frame fails its cyclic redundancy check (CRC), or an extended Kalman filter diverges, the system enters an *epistemic gap*. In this *unknown* state, the controller cannot tell whether the physical machine is safely positioned or breaching clearance margins.

When telemetry supporting active motion becomes unknown or known-false, the safety enforcer revokes trajectory tracking and executes the pre-certified fallback procedure for the plant’s dynamic state. This runtime intervention differs from rejecting an invalid future trajectory proposal, because an already active trajectory chunk can continue executing only while its supporting sensor evidence, physical bounds, and lease duration remain intact. The system’s flight recorder must log whether revocation stemmed from unobservable state telemetry or an active boundary breach, while the hardware supplies enough reserve energy and sensing capability to bring the plant to a controlled stop or hold.

**Definition 16.3: Envelope trichotomy**

![[90 Assets/Volume IV/16 Release/Dfn Envelope Trichotomy 3a2877b962.svg]]

*Three boxes left to right: known-true with a qualified uncertainty set inside the envelope and fresh verified evidence; unknown, reached when evidence is lost, from dropout, CRC mismatch, or estimator divergence; and known-false, reached over the top when a boundary breach is measured, such as tracking error above its bound. A bracket under known-true reads permit equals 1, tracking may continue. One bracket under unknown and known-false reads permit equals 0, validated stop or hold, logged causes stay distinct.*

***Envelope trichotomy*** is the three-state epistemic classification of cyber-physical system health relative to its signed operating envelope: *known-true* (qualified state-uncertainty set contained inside the supported envelope), *known-false* (verified boundary violation triggering deterministic hardware refusal), and *unknown* (state uncertainty caused by sensor dropout, frame corruption, or estimator divergence).

1.  **Significance**: An unknown state is not evidence of a boundary breach, yet neither condition permits continued tracking. The permission decision is refusal in both unknown and known-false states; the logged cause remains distinct and the response depends on the plant’s validated stop or hold capability.
2.  **Distinction**: Unlike a binary boolean status check (`is_safe == true`), the envelope trichotomy separates sensor blindness from active violations, ensuring telemetry loss is never mistaken for safe clearance.
3.  **Common pitfall**: Treating a stored command as indefinitely valid after communication fails. An active chunk may continue only within its own still-valid evidence and lease; otherwise the assessed fallback must take over.

The same three-state distinction applies to verification test records. A test trial marked `UNOBSERVABLE` or `INVALID` during acceptance testing (**[[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/04 Governing the Machine/15 Adversarial Verification/V4-15.05 Fault Oracles and Acceptance#^sec-verification-four-verdicts|Four verdicts and the invalid-test rule]]**) cannot establish the claim its required witnesses or test conditions failed to resolve. It leaves a gap in that coverage; other valid records remain evidence only for the conditions and claims they establish. Verified `PASS` records support the claims covered by their conditions and witnesses. A valid `FAIL` contradicts the claim tested under those conditions and withdraws any authorization that relies on it.

### Blind Travel, Kinetic Consequences, and Cycle Limits

^sec-release-blind-travel-limits

When telemetry fails or sensors black out, a machine traveling without sensory feedback moves blind; it must execute a deterministic fallback stop before its forward travel consumes the remaining spatial clearance. How fast that clearance vanishes depends directly on vehicle speed, contact geometry, and available actuator authority. Sizing the response therefore requires calculating the kinetic energy and blind travel accumulated during each millisecond of delay, and matching each sensor loss to a pre-certified fallback path.

How the system responds when a premise cannot be checked must follow from the physics of unmonitored motion rather than from arbitrary severity categories. When the mobile manipulator traverses an aisle at 1.3 m/s with its tote rack fully loaded, its 368 kg mass carries 311 J of kinetic energy. If its primary lidar stops updating, every millisecond of blind travel carries the base 1.3 mm closer to potential obstacles. The safety case pre-certifies three possible fallback paths, two of which apply only when the signed release names them in advance. The first is a degraded mode that permits continued motion while diverse redundant sensors keep state uncertainty bounded, for example throttling the aisle speed to 0.65 m/s (half the aisle speed, a chosen value) while secondary sensors watch the path. The second is a switch to a restricted limit set that a signed `COND` case pre-validated for that exact sensor loss, if the release includes one. The runtime only selects that profile; it does not create a new verdict. Dead reckoning and wheel odometry may retain qualified position bounds over a brief window, but cannot verify obstacle clearance on their own. The third option, the hardware STOP response, handles complete loss of observability by executing a configured drive and brake sequence. Stopping the vehicle within available clearance margins requires calibrated response timing, sufficient drive traction, and known brake deceleration.

Action leases bound how long an admitted trajectory chunk may run without renewal. Checking this lease on every controller tick does not require re-measuring every long-term site condition. Instead, the runtime permission path checks fresh local sensor telemetry alongside the signed validity windows of slower site inspections and maintenance records. If the proposer stalls or its communication drops, task throughput suffers, but safety holds as long as the enforcer and its fallback path remain decoupled, responsive, and intact.

Any standing condition governing physical motion requires an enforcement path with a measured detection and response budget. In this book’s architecture, an independent safety enforcer checks trajectory proposals and monitored premises before issuing actuator permissions, re-evaluating system state on every 1 ms control tick. A subsystem failure reaches this enforcer along one of two paths: a silent proposer that stops transmitting is caught only when its existing action lease expires, whereas an actively monitored hardware fault raises an immediate interrupt flag. Because detection delays differ between these paths, so does the distance the base travels blind (notebook [16.2](https://mlsysbook.ai/vol4/16_release/16_release.html#nbk-release-silent-proposer-window)).

*Two horizontal timelines in milliseconds. Silent proposer: a 60-millisecond lease, 2 milliseconds of tick and bus transfer, and 20 milliseconds of brake onset, 82 milliseconds in total. Monitored premise: the same 2 milliseconds and 20 milliseconds, 22 milliseconds in total.*

*The lease dominates: a silent proposer reaches brake onset in 82 ms, a flagged premise in 22 ms.*

**Napkin Math 16.2: The silent-proposer window**

**Problem**: The base runs the aisle at 1.3 m/s. How far does it travel before brake onset when the proposer falls silent, how far when a monitored premise fails and its flag reaches the enforcer at once, and how late may that flag arrive before monitoring stops the base no sooner than the lease would?

**Variables**:

- Aisle speed ${v} =$ 1.3 m/s (Chosen), so 1.3 mm of travel per millisecond
- Chunk lease 60 ms and permission tick 1 ms (Chosen); bus transfer 1 ms and brake onset 20 ms (Illustrative)
- Flag latency ${\delta_{\text{flag}}}$ from the physical failure to a flag the enforcer can read (Unknown; the detector’s qualification must bound it)

**Math**:

1.  **Silent proposer**: 60 ms $+$ 1 ms $+$ 1 ms $+$ 20 ms $=$ 82 ms, and 1.3 m/s $\times$ 82 ms $=$ 106.6 mm.
2.  **Monitored premise, immediate flag**: 1 ms $+$ 1 ms $+$ 20 ms $=$ 22 ms, and 1.3 m/s $\times$ 22 ms $=$ 28.6 mm.
3.  **Crossover**: ${\delta_{\text{flag}}} +$ 22 ms $=$ 82 ms at ${\delta_{\text{flag}}} =$ 60 ms, the length of the lease itself.

**Result**: Monitoring can save up to 78 mm of travel, but only for a flag that arrives within 60 ms of the failure; a slower flag stops the base no sooner than waiting out the lease.

**Systems insight**: The 22 ms local subtotal is not a complete detection-to-stop time; it reflects only controller cycle delay, communication transfer, and physical brake engagement. The decisive term governing whether an active monitor preserves physical clearance is ${\delta_{\text{flag}}}$, the hardware detection latency. If an architectural budget fails to bound ${\delta_{\text{flag}}}$, the safety case cannot claim proactive protection. Every millisecond spent detecting a fault carries the loaded vehicle another 1.3 mm toward whatever the failed premise was protecting against.

When an operating premise transitions to unknown or known-false, the enforcer revokes trajectory tracking and commands the appropriate rung of the fallback ladder, and the signed safety case must specify both this fallback selection and its measured timing. For the mobile base, the certified stopping sequence uses controlled, powered deceleration to bring the wheels to rest before applying electromechanical holding brakes. Because digital controller logic and brake-coil hold-up capacitors cannot deliver drive motor torque, the safety case must confirm drive energy, feedback sensing, and inverter continuity across every fault condition expected to use this active deceleration profile. By contrast, a raw torque-inhibit or free-wheeling cutoff is a different physical response; it cannot inherit stopping-distance guarantees calculated for controlled electrical braking.

Entering an unknown or known-false state also triggers local black-box logging. The onboard recorder must preserve the telemetry window leading up to refusal alongside the execution of the fallback trajectory, even if the initiating fault cuts external networking or primary bus power. These records are essential for root-cause investigation, but writing them to nonvolatile storage must never delay the hard real-time safety stop; [[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/04 Governing the Machine/16 Deployment Release/V4-16.10 Maintaining Release Through Updates#^sec-release-data-curation-flywheel|section 16.9.5]] sizes the capture path.

On longer timescales, even when every runtime heartbeat succeeds, the **release verdict** carries an operational cycle and inspection budget.[[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/04 Governing the Machine/16 Deployment Release/V4-16.15 Footnotes#^fn7|⁷]] Operating hours and mechanical fatigue wear components in ways that high-frequency telemetry cannot observe directly (such as surface pitting on gear teeth, elastomer seal stiffening, or fastener preload relaxation). Once this cycle budget expires, the release case must be re-adjudicated before autonomous motion can resume. Across every timescale, a deployment verdict remains valid only while its supporting standing conditions remain demonstrably known-true, checked at intervals matched to physical wear rates and backed by pre-certified fallback stops with measured latencies. Claims, verdicts, and revocation conditions are now defined. The six claims of the warehouse mobile manipulator can be adjudicated against the physical evidence assembled across this book.
