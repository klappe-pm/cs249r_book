# V4-16.02 The Decision to Deploy

[[Home|Home]] · [[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/Volume 4 Overview|Volume Overview]] · [[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/04 Governing the Machine/16 Deployment Release/V4-16 Deployment Release|V4-16 Deployment Release]]

^sec-release-decision-to-deploy

Verification left the machine with a signed set of fault records. Each pairs an observed trace under an injected fault (a stalled proposer, a stale frame, lost fieldbus frames, a sagging bus) with an oracle fixed before the trial, and the set names the operating regimes no trial reached. Beside it sit the records the earlier chapters produced: the authority record of arbitration and fallback outcomes, the placement record of compute scheduling and isolation, and the evaluation record of closed-loop task performance. Those records say how the machine behaved under the conditions tested. They do not say which operating claim the machine may now be granted, because a fault record proves nothing outside the envelope it sampled, and an untested mode is a gap rather than a pass. Offline validation accuracy and clean laboratory demonstrations sit in the same position. Release is the step that decides, claim by claim, what this evidence licenses across a named operational design domain.

↩︎ The empirical verification evidence backing release claims is established in **[[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/04 Governing the Machine/15 Adversarial Verification/V4-15.09 Fault Injection on Hardware#^sec-verification-hardware-fault-injection|Fault Injection on Hardware]]**.

That step is a threshold every machine operating among humans eventually crosses, the point where builders formally grant it authority to act outside the laboratory. In conventional software engineering, deployment is largely reversible; an operator can recall a defective build within minutes, bounding the cost of failure to the time required to roll back the binary. For physical systems, that release grant differs. Every action authorized by the runtime delivers energy across the causal boundary into the physical environment. When a release decision errs, the failure appears as an actuator driving past an obstacle clearance line or a gripper exerting uncalibrated force against human tissue, and no software rollback can recall kinetic energy already transferred. Aerospace, rail transit, and industrial robotics manage this risk through an explicit safety case, a structured and auditable argument stating what the machine is claimed to do, under what operating conditions, and on what evidence. Physical AI inherits this burden with an added difficulty. A learned policy cannot be proven safe by inspecting its weights, and finite field trials cannot surface every rare tail failure, which is where the worst consequences lie.

The warehouse mobile manipulator shows why past operational success cannot by itself license deployment. In an active logistics depot it must travel narrow aisles past protruding rack ends, unlatch a spring-latched cage door, and hand a ceramic mug to a human coworker. Each subtask turns on a distinct physical claim: a stopping distance inside the clear distance at maximum payload, a bounded peak force on the latch, and a limited contact force during the handover. A spotless test run shows only that the machine executed those motions under the exact conditions of that single trial. A sound release decision must instead bound the physical variations the machine will face in production and show that verified plant dynamics and low-level safety interlocks keep risk within limits across that entire envelope.

A deployed system must also do useful work inside its certified envelope. Closed-loop evaluation logs show whether an end-to-end policy completes its tasks, how often humans must intervene, and which environmental conditions cause the policy to break down. Engineers must weigh these task metrics against the speed limits, payload caps, and spatial restrictions required by the safety case. A mobile base that trips safety stops every few meters avoids collisions while failing its service target; conversely, an agile policy that finishes every nominal trial cannot be given actuator authority until deterministic guardrails enforce safe bounds during perception failures. The release decision brings these two requirements together into one operating profile.

↩︎ The closed-loop evaluation record of task success, intervention rates, and breakdown conditions is established in **[[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/02 Teaching the Machine/07 Closed-Loop Evaluation/V4-07.09 The Evaluation Record#^sec-evaluation-logs|The Evaluation Record]]**.

Because different facilities have different lighting, floor friction, and foot traffic, an initial release case applies to a single facility, drawing on its site clearance surveys, loaded braking trials, calibrated impact-force measurements, and cryptographically signed fault records. Rather than boiling deployment readiness down to a single benchmark score, the release decision is a bounded, revocable judgment that determines which operational claims the available evidence actually supports, under what physical conditions, and for how long. This chapter builds that decision in the order an adjudicator meets it: the claims are first written as testable physical inequalities over a bounded operating envelope, then organized into an argument whose every link names its evidence. The argument resolves into one of three verdicts (full operational authorization, restricted operation inside enforced limits, or refusal), and standing conditions define when a granted verdict expires. A worked adjudication applies all of this to the running machine, an adversarial review tries to break whatever case survives, and a signed release manifest binds the final decision to the executing machine so that every later software update, retrained checkpoint, or hardware modification passes through the same gate.

↩︎ The signed fault records a release case consumes are established in **[[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/04 Governing the Machine/15 Adversarial Verification/V4-15.12 Fault Records for Release#^sec-verification-fault-manifest|Fault Records for Release]]**.
