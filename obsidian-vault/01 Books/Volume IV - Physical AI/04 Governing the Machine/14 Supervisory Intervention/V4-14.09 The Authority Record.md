# V4-14.09 The Authority Record

[[Home|Home]] · [[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/Volume 4 Overview|Volume Overview]] · [[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/04 Governing the Machine/14 Supervisory Intervention/V4-14 Supervisory Intervention|V4-14 Supervisory Intervention]]

^sec-intervention-authority-log

When an incident investigator asks, weeks after an abrupt blocked-aisle takeover, which entity drove the mobile base as it neared the rack end, the machine’s recorded telemetry provides evidence bounded by its capture coverage, clock alignment, independent plant measurements, and trusted custody. The **authority record** bridges real-time supervisory arbitration with downstream verification and release claims. To establish this audit trail, the authority record consumes two upstream contracts. From the enforcement record of **[[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/03 Running the Machine/12 Safety Enforcement/V4-12.12 The Enforcement Record#^sec-enforcement-telemetry|The Enforcement Record]]**, it ingests the rungs of the fallback ladder so that every logged fallback explicitly identifies the rung executed by the permission path. From the placement record of **[[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/03 Running the Machine/13 Workload Placement/V4-13.09 The Placement Record#^sec-placement-hardware-allocation|The Placement Record]]**, it incorporates the permission path’s declared timing bounds and bus arbitration settings, which every operator command also crosses, together with the placement record’s loaded-test field, which stays empty until verification fills it. The record shows who held each channel at each tick; it does not show who decided the speed, the staffing, or the operating domain that put the machine there.

↪ The record of who decided that the machine could be in that situation at all, with a named owner for each decision, is developed in **[[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/04 Governing the Machine/17 Responsible Physical AI/V4-17.10 The Approval Record#^sec-responsibility-approval-record|The Approval Record]]**.

To these upstream contracts, the authority record adds two deliverables of its own. The static authority manifest translates the capability matrix of [[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/04 Governing the Machine/14 Supervisory Intervention/V4-14.03 Human Authority, Reaction Time, and Clearance#^sec-intervention-human-authority|section 14.2]] into a machine-checkable contract. It binds every actuator channel to its authorized actors, operational preconditions, preemption tiers, and transition deadlines, and the safety microcontroller verifies and seals this manifest before the actuator drivers energize. Complementing the manifest, the record’s per-tick log forms an independent stream that records, for every control cycle, which entity held kinetic authority alongside the commands proposed, admitted, and dispatched, paired with independently measured physical response. In this stream, a missing cycle registers as an explicit communication or capture gap rather than an implied pass. The static manifest establishes the legal rules of interaction, while the per-tick log supports checking recorded runtime adherence under the capture, synchronization, custody, and declared-gap assumptions of [[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/04 Governing the Machine/14 Supervisory Intervention/V4-14.08 Trustworthy Operational Logs#^sec-intervention-trustworthy-logs|section 14.7]].

### The Static Authority Manifest and Capability Binding

^sec-intervention-manifest-binding

If an operator’s permissions or a channel’s rate limits can be edited in software while the machine runs, a post-incident log cannot prove which rules the arbiter was supposed to enforce at the moment of a takeover. The static authority manifest fixes those rules before the actuators energize: loading and cryptographically sealing the manifest during system initialization binds every subsequent event to the permissions and physical limits in force at boot. It specifies every permitted interaction as a verifiable contract between the physical platform and potential command sources. Each entry delineates a distinct physical authority domain using a hardware actuator mask, an authorized entity class (such as a local physical operator, a remote teleoperation console, an autonomous trajectory policy, or an emergency stop loop), and an integer **preemption tier**. Before any command vector enters the actuation pipeline, the permission path verifies the sender’s cryptographic role credentials against this manifest. Over the network, an operator’s command arrives as an `OPERATOR` payload under the same proposal header, carrying the operator’s role, the requested channel mask, the command vector, a monotonically increasing nonce, and a keyed authentication tag.

In addition to credential validation, each manifest entry enforces four operational invariants on its assigned channel:

1.  *Single-writer mutual exclusion*: Hardware access controls guarantee that exactly one entity writes to an actuator channel at any given instant.
2.  *Execution lease duration (${T_{\text{lease,man}}}$)*: For human operators, the lease defines the maximum tolerable silence before the enforcer revokes authority and triggers a state-dependent fallback.
3.  *Command rate ceiling (${r}$)*: A hardware slew limit caps how rapidly a requested command vector may change between successive cycles, preventing abrupt step inputs.
4.  *Bumpless blending duration (${\tau_{\text{blend}}}$)*: A transition window enforces a ${C}^2$ continuous authority transfer ramp that preserves the kinodynamic feasibility bounds established in **[[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/03 Running the Machine/11 Trajectory Planning/V4-11.05 Kinodynamic Feasibility#^sec-planning-kinodynamic-feasibility|Kinodynamic Feasibility]]**, requiring a qualified executable profile and a feasible state-matched fallback at every transition state if communication severs. The fallback depends on surviving sensing, controller, and energy premises; duration and continuity alone do not establish it.

### Per-Tick Evidence Tuples and the Fault Injection Suite

^sec-intervention-evidence-tuples

A general telemetry tuple that records only a single machine-wide mode cannot show which channels were mid-blend, which fallback rung fired, or why a higher-tier source preempted a lower one. Every entry in the authority record’s per-tick log therefore expands the evidence tuple of [[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/04 Governing the Machine/14 Supervisory Intervention/V4-14.08 Trustworthy Operational Logs#^sec-intervention-trustworthy-logs|section 14.7]] with further fields. A field names the specific actuator channels governed by the arbitration decision. Another gives the ladder rung of any active `FALLBACK`. Others give the root cause of any preemption and the instantaneous blend factor ${\alpha}({t})$ of the enforcer-managed profile. The last is a cryptographic hash link to the preceding log block, authenticated through the platform’s protected anchoring scheme. The versioned static manifest supplies the decoding context, specifying physical units, field serialization order, clock synchronization mappings with their associated error bounds, credential validation rules, and hardware access control masks.

↪ **Byte layout:** The operator payload’s and the authority record’s fields, types, and offsets are given in **[[05 Reference/Volume IV - Physical AI/V4 Systems and Hardware#^sec-appendix-systems-wire-authority|Authority record and operator payload]]**.

Verification draws its test inputs from the static manifest and its evidence from the event schema. The manifest’s `authority_claims` field hands **[[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/04 Governing the Machine/15 Adversarial Verification/V4-15.07 Deriving the Fault List#^sec-verification-forward-analysis|Forward physical analysis and common-cause modes]]** four authority rules by identifier, each of which becomes a hardware-in-the-loop fault case on the machine:

- **A1.** Exactly one qualified enforcer output path writes each actuator channel. Source arbitration and hardware access controls prevent a second sender from bypassing that path while the policy and remote assistant both propose commands; successful bus arbitration alone does not satisfy this claim.
- **A2.** No lower-tier source bypasses an active higher-tier source or the independent permission check. An autonomous policy proposal during `MAN` is ignored, and an assistant command that opposes the enforcer’s admitted barrier limit is clamped or rejected.
- **A3.** An unacknowledged handover or stalled manual lease decays authority by ${T_{\text{timeout}}}$ of [[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/04 Governing the Machine/14 Supervisory Intervention/V4-14.03 Human Authority, Reaction Time, and Clearance#^sec-intervention-clearance-deadlines|section 14.2.4]], initiating controlled fallback by the earlier of the configured lease ceiling and the clearance-derived deadline of [[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/04 Governing the Machine/14 Supervisory Intervention/V4-14.03 Human Authority, Reaction Time, and Clearance#^eq-intervention-latest-fallback|equation 4]]. This clearance deadline has a partial-budget upper bound of 751.6 ms at the 0.75 m/s slow-down before an in-loop takeover, and 100.8 ms for a handover requested by the policy at the 1.3 m/s aisle speed. Both values represent additional-delay allocations in the capture-origin budget. The runtime deadline evaluates the actual event history and future delay budgets without double-charging for elapsed capture-to-admission transit.
- **A4.** The enforcer rejects replayed `veto_path`, `manual_drive`, or `accept_item` packets, and packets with invalid authentication tags or expired sequence counters, without modifying still-valid channel authority. It logs each rejection in the evidence tuple while allowing the active lease to age normally.

These authority rules turn the event log into a checkable contract. The same synchronized hardware timestamps also mark which controller generated each segment of an intervention trajectory, and that separation must exist before any segment can serve as a training target for imitation learning.

### Causal Masking and Recovery Eligibility

^sec-intervention-recovery-eligibility

If a training pipeline feeds an entire rescued episode into behavioral cloning without filtering, the policy learns to imitate the autonomous drift that caused the near-miss, the enforcer’s synthetic blend spline, and the operator’s initial startled oscillations. The curation rules of **[[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/02 Teaching the Machine/05 Physical Data/V4-05.09 Intervention and Recovery Data#^sec-data-intervention-recovery|Intervention and Recovery Data]]** prevent that contamination by deciding which logged samples may train a policy: drift commands are quarantined, drift states are relabeled only by an automated expert, and only stabilized, reviewed recovery is admitted. Each rule keys on a timestamp that the authority record supplies, so a logging system that records intervention episodes for downstream policy retraining must preserve controller ownership, target meaning, and declared sample eligibility. This chapter’s conservative direct-imitation policy applies **causal intervention masking** to that record. Its optimization objective over recorded trajectories is formulated as:

$$
{\mathcal{L}_{\text{imitation}}}({\theta}) = \mathbb{E}_{({\mathbf{x}}, {\mathbf{u}_{\text{target}}}, {\mathcal{M}}) \sim {\mathcal{D}_{\text{train}}}} \left[ \sum_{k=0}^{{S}} {\mathcal{M}}({k}) \cdot \ell\left({\pi_\theta}({\mathbf{x}}_k), {\mathbf{u}_{\text{target}}}({k})\right) \right] \tag{8}
$$

 where $\ell({\pi_\theta}({\mathbf{x}}_k), {\mathbf{u}_{\text{target}}}({k}))$ denotes the policy prediction loss (such as mean squared error across action chunks or score-matching denoising loss for diffusion policies), ${\mathcal{M}}({k}) \in \{0, 1\}$ is a binary causal mask, and ${\mathbf{u}_{\text{target}}}({k})$ is the supervised training target. Each sample’s mask and target are evaluated at that sample’s logged hardware timestamp. The target carries its own symbol because it is not the admitted command ${\mathbf{u}^*}$ of the evidence tuple; in each interval it is chosen from the logged fields, or supplied by an expert, as the interval list below states. Constructing the training dataset ${\mathcal{D}_{\text{train}}}$ requires selective transformation rather than wholesale ingestion of raw telemetry. While every logged state preserves its chronological position in the trajectory, its supervision target ${\mathbf{u}_{\text{target}}}({t})$ and mask ${\mathcal{M}}({t})$ depend on the physical regime of the recording interval. As a result, the command dispatched to the hardware is not necessarily the training target. Deterministic authority transitions stamped by the safety microcontroller establish controller ownership. Empirical stabilization criteria, task validity checks, and the availability of expert labels, in turn, determine which samples receive ${\mathcal{M}}({t}) = 1$.

A supervisory intervention does not occur as a single monolithic demonstration. Instead, it spans four distinct physical intervals along the execution timeline:

1.  **Compounding policy drift (${t_{\text{div}}} \le {t} < {t_{\text{commit}}}$)**: The autonomous policy ${\pi_\theta}$ drifts away from the expert state distribution, accumulating compounding errors while the human supervisor perceives the anomaly, orients, and decides to intervene (the pre-commit latency modeled in [[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/04 Governing the Machine/14 Supervisory Intervention/V4-14.03 Human Authority, Reaction Time, and Clearance#^eq-intervention-reaction-floor|equation 1]]).
    - *Masking policy*: The autonomous proposals ${\mathbf{u}_{\text{nom}}}({t})$ executed during this drift are quarantined from training targets. Without an external expert label, the system sets ${\mathcal{M}}({t}) = 0$.
    - *Relabeling*: The *learner-visited states* ${\mathbf{x}}({t})$ remain in the log for the corrective aggregation of **[[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/02 Teaching the Machine/05 Physical Data/V4-05.09 Intervention and Recovery Data#^sec-data-intervention-recovery|Intervention and Recovery Data]]**, whose relabeling rule accepts only an automated expert’s labels. A drift state therefore enters training with ${\mathcal{M}}({t}) = 1$ and target ${\mathbf{u}_{\text{target}}}({t}) = \pi^*({\mathbf{x}}_t)$ only when such an expert, for example an omniscient trajectory planner with privileged simulation state, supplies the correction.
2.  **Atomic handover and enforcer blend (${t_{\text{commit}}} \le {t} < {t_{\text{commit}}} + {\tau_{\text{blend}}}$)**: The safety microcontroller latches the channel endpoints and computes a ${C}^2$ continuous smoothstep trajectory between controllers.
    - *Masking policy*: The system sets ${\mathcal{M}}({t}) = 0$ unconditionally. Dispatched setpoints ${\mathbf{u}}({t})$ during this window are synthetic interpolation splines generated by the enforcer’s deterministic quintic polynomial profile. Because they reflect neither learned neural policy intent nor human cognitive demonstration, training on this interval would condition the network to emit open-loop kinematic ramps detached from environmental feedback.
3.  **Operator re-orientation and hunting (${t_{\text{commit}}} + {\tau_{\text{blend}}} \le {t} < {t_{\text{stabilize}}}$)**: Upon receiving the active manual lease (${T_{\text{lease,man}}}$), the operator experiences neuromuscular latency, visual parallax, and control re-orientation, producing high-jerk, oscillatory corrections while regaining situational awareness.
    - *Masking policy*: The system enforces ${\mathcal{M}}({t}) = 0$. Bainbridge documented this control hunting directly. An operator whose active manual skill has lapsed during passive monitoring makes abrupt step corrections, oscillating around the setpoint before settling into smooth tracking ([[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/04 Governing the Machine/14 Supervisory Intervention/V4-14.13 Source References#^ref-bainbridge1983ironies|Bainbridge 1983]]). Control stabilization often takes significantly longer than the physical handover itself. In driving simulator studies, operators resuming control from automated systems required approximately 35–40 s before their lateral steering stabilized ([[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/04 Governing the Machine/14 Supervisory Intervention/V4-14.13 Source References#^ref-merat2014transition|Merat et al. 2014]]), at least 7× the length of the machine’s 5 s pre-trigger window. Consequently, post-trigger masking cannot rely on a fixed time constant; logging systems must evaluate empirical stabilization criteria. Discarding these initial frames prevents hesitation and oscillatory hunting from corrupting the imitation buffer. This exclusion involves an engineering trade-off, because interval 3 also contains the operator’s initial corrective inputs, which certain human-gated methods retain ([[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/04 Governing the Machine/14 Supervisory Intervention/V4-14.09 The Authority Record#^tbl-14-intervention-learning|table 8]]). The enforcer marks the stabilization boundary ${t_{\text{stabilize}}}$ only after tracking error and command slew have remained within calibrated limits for a declared dwell interval. Later violations mask the affected samples again. The limits are:

$$
\lVert {\mathbf{e}_{\text{track}}}({t}) \rVert \le {\epsilon_{\text{stable}}} \quad \text{and} \quad \lVert {\dot{\mathbf{u}}_{\text{human}}}({t}) \rVert \le {j_{\text{stable}}}.
$$

 Here, ${\mathbf{e}_{\text{track}}}({t})$ is the difference between the admitted manual setpoint and the measured plant response, ${\dot{\mathbf{u}}_{\text{human}}}({t})$ is the time derivative of the operator’s command, and the parameter ${j_{\text{stable}}}$ denotes a channel-specific slew ceiling, representing an acceleration derivative (jerk) only when the underlying command vector represents acceleration. Both thresholds must hold continuously across the dwell window before the logger lifts the mask.
4.  **Stabilized expert recovery (${t} \ge {t_{\text{stabilize}}}$)**: The operator executes coherent, task-directed recovery maneuvers once measured control metrics stabilize. Smooth motion alone does not validate a recovery; the episode must also satisfy formal task-completion criteria before its samples qualify as expert targets.
    - *Masking policy*: The system sets ${\mathcal{M}}({t}) = 1$ with imitation target ${\mathbf{u}_{\text{target}}}({t}) = {\mathbf{u}_{\text{human}}}({t})$ exclusively for stabilized segments that pass task-validity review. All other telemetry frames remain masked from direct imitation, so this interval is the only post-takeover segment the curation rules admit as expert recovery data.

Feeding unsegmented telemetry directly into behavioral cloning or diffusion policies injects synthetic enforcer ramps and operator control transients into the training distribution. The causal mask ${\mathcal{M}}({t})$ and target vector ${\mathbf{u}_{\text{target}}}({t})$ use the commit and state timestamps recorded by the safety microcontroller to enforce controller ownership boundaries, bounded by hardware clock synchronization and sampling limits.

The binary causal mask represents the most conservative member of a broader algorithmic spectrum. Contemporary human-in-the-loop learning methods treat interventions in fundamentally different ways: as direct state labels, as importance weights over surrounding trajectories, or as negative reinforcement signals indicating policy failure. [[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/04 Governing the Machine/14 Supervisory Intervention/V4-14.09 The Authority Record#^tbl-14-intervention-learning|Table 8]] maps five representative paradigms onto these four operational intervals. For instance, Human-Gated DAgger (HG-DAgger) grants the human supervisor full authority upon takeover and aggregates only the human’s executed commands, leaving interval 1 unmasked but unlabeled ([[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/04 Governing the Machine/14 Supervisory Intervention/V4-14.13 Source References#^ref-kelly2019hgdagger|Kelly et al. 2019]]). Sirius generalizes ${\mathcal{M}}({t})$ by assigning categorical importance weights across sample classes; it assigns zero weight to a fixed pre-intervention window while upweighting human recovery trajectories ([[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/04 Governing the Machine/14 Supervisory Intervention/V4-14.13 Source References#^ref-liu2023sirius|Liu et al. 2023]]). Specifically, Sirius sizes its pre-intervention window to 15 robot actions (approximately 2 s), derived from observed human reaction times. While Sirius fixes this window a priori, the authority record timestamps the commit and supports reconstruction of the drift interval once ${t_{\text{div}}}$ is identified from the retained evidence, a duration that the out-of-loop takeover budget of [[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/04 Governing the Machine/14 Supervisory Intervention/V4-14.03 Human Authority, Reaction Time, and Clearance#^sec-intervention-reaction-drift|section 14.2.2]] shows can be 2.2× longer. Meanwhile, Reinforcement Learning from Intervention Feedback (RLIF) eliminates the assumption of human optimality altogether, treating the intervention event as a negative reward penalty within an off-policy reinforcement learning framework ([[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/04 Governing the Machine/14 Supervisory Intervention/V4-14.13 Source References#^ref-luo2024rlif|Luo et al. 2024]]). Similarly, Human-in-the-Loop Sample-Efficient Robot Learning (HIL-SERL) incorporates human interventions as targeted correction buffers for real-world reinforcement learning ([[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/04 Governing the Machine/14 Supervisory Intervention/V4-14.13 Source References#^ref-luo2025hilserl|Luo et al. 2025]]). Finally, RECAP, which trains the $\pi^{*}_{0.6}$ vision-language-action model, blends autonomous rollouts, teleoperated interventions, and demonstrations using advantage conditioning, explicitly labeling human takeover corrections as positive-advantage actions ([[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/04 Governing the Machine/14 Supervisory Intervention/V4-14.13 Source References#^ref-physicalintelligence2025recap|Physical Intelligence et al. 2025]]).

|:------------------------------|:-------------------------------------------------|:----------------------------------------------|:--------------------------------------|:---------------------------------------|
| Causal mask (this chapter)    | Boundary between owners                          | Excluded unless an automated expert labels it | Excluded until measured stabilization | Commit, blend end, stabilization times |
| HG-DAgger (2019)              | Label: human commands while in control           | Not labeled                                   | Kept with the human’s commands        | Owner per sample                       |

None trains on the enforcer’s blend as a demonstration. Entries summarize each method’s published design ([[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/04 Governing the Machine/14 Supervisory Intervention/V4-14.13 Source References#^ref-kelly2019hgdagger|Kelly et al. 2019]]; [[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/04 Governing the Machine/14 Supervisory Intervention/V4-14.13 Source References#^ref-liu2023sirius|Liu et al. 2023]]; [[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/04 Governing the Machine/14 Supervisory Intervention/V4-14.13 Source References#^ref-luo2024rlif|Luo et al. 2024]], [[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/04 Governing the Machine/14 Supervisory Intervention/V4-14.13 Source References#^ref-luo2025hilserl|2025]]; [[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/04 Governing the Machine/14 Supervisory Intervention/V4-14.13 Source References#^ref-physicalintelligence2025recap|Physical Intelligence et al. 2025]]); a later edition should refresh the list.

^tbl-14-intervention-learning

Individual learning algorithms change, but every one of them must answer the same three systems questions: who held write authority over each sample, whether the takeover serves as a supervised label, an importance weight, or a reward penalty, and which physical intervals enter the training set. Downstream training pipelines cannot recover those ownership boundaries from trajectory kinematics alone. Recording the commit, blend-end, and stabilization timestamps on the safety microcontroller’s clock grounds dataset curation and fleet-level intervention rates directly in hardware measurements.

When an engineering team assembles the safety case for release (**[[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/04 Governing the Machine/16 Deployment Release/V4-16.04 Safety Cases and Claims#^sec-release-argument-plant-alignment|Matching the argument to the machine]]**), the authority record also supplies the primary evidence that runtime handovers obey rules A1 through A4 (claim C6 in **[[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/04 Governing the Machine/16 Deployment Release/V4-16.04 Safety Cases and Claims#^tbl-16-cae-scorecard|Claim-argument-evidence review for the warehouse mobile manipulator]]**). That release dossier pairs the static authority manifest with the loaded stopping and blending measurements from [[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/04 Governing the Machine/14 Supervisory Intervention/V4-14.05 Transfer without Discontinuity#^sec-intervention-bumpless-transfer|section 14.4]] and hardware-in-the-loop traces that verify an unbroken audit chain. Because a handover that looks clean on the communication bus can still slip a wheel on a wet floor or jolt a loaded tote rack, the dossier always checks bus timestamps against independent plant sensors. It treats measured preemption times as empirical percentiles rather than analytical worst-case bounds.

↩︎ The distinction between an empirical latency percentile and a response-time bound is established in **[[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/01 Machine Anatomy/04 The Nervous System/V4-04.06 Real-Time Command Transport and Bus Determinism#^sec-nervous-deterministic-fieldbuses|Real-Time Command Transport and Bus Determinism]]**.

**Checkpoint 14.3: Authority evidence and causal masks**

The authority record is useful twice, once to an investigator and once to a learner, and both uses depend on the same ownership timestamps.

**Evidence tuple**: Can you name the incident question that becomes unanswerable when the log drops ${\mathbf{u}_{\text{nom}}}$, when it drops ${\mathbf{u}^*}$, and when it drops ${T_{\text{lease,rem}}}$?

**Interval masks**: For one takeover, can you assign ${\mathcal{M}}({t})$ and ${\mathbf{u}_{\text{target}}}({t})$ to each of the four intervals and name the logged event that bounds each?

**Sample count estimate**: An out-of-loop takeover commits 4.5 s after the divergence, and the arm then blends for 38 ms. At the 1 ms permission tick, can you estimate how many logged ticks fall in intervals 1 and 2, and how many of them may train an imitation policy when no automated expert is available?

**Fixed windows**: Can you explain what a fixed 2 s pre-intervention window misclassifies for a takeover that took the design out-of-loop time?
