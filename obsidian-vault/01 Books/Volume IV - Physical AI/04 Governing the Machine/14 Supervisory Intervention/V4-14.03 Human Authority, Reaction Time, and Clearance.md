# V4-14.03 Human Authority, Reaction Time, and Clearance

[[Home|Home]] · [[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/Volume 4 Overview|Volume Overview]] · [[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/04 Governing the Machine/14 Supervisory Intervention/V4-14 Supervisory Intervention|V4-14 Supervisory Intervention]]

^sec-intervention-human-authority

An assistant guiding the arm can move the chassis if its credential also permits base-drive commands. A command admitted under that permission performs mechanical work on the plant before software can revoke it. Authority must therefore identify the physical channels a person may affect, rather than grant unrestricted access through a generic operator role.

The remote assistant who clears a blocked aisle and the coworker who takes an item from the gripper act on the same machine, and neither should hold the other’s authority. Each grant therefore names an operation together with its timing budget, physical degrees of freedom, and preemption rule. The permission path keeps these grants in an explicit **capability matrix**, a permissions table mapping individual operations (such as an emergency power cutoff, a velocity trim adjustment, a trajectory path veto, or manual drive of the base and arm) to authenticated roles. The design reflects classic taxonomies of human supervisory control[[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/04 Governing the Machine/14 Supervisory Intervention/V4-14.14 Footnotes#^fn2|²]] ([[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/04 Governing the Machine/14 Supervisory Intervention/V4-14.13 Source References#^ref-sheridan1992telerobotics|Sheridan 1992]]) and multi-level automation ([[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/04 Governing the Machine/14 Supervisory Intervention/V4-14.13 Source References#^ref-parasuraman2000model|Parasuraman et al. 2000]]). Each entry in this matrix defines the exact actuator channels the operation governs, the preemption tier it carries over the learned policy, and the maximum propagation latency permitted from physical input to actuator response.

Sheridan also describes the supervisor’s work as five steps in sequence: plan the task off-line, teach the plan to the computer, monitor the automatic action, intervene when it fails or the goal changes, and learn from the episode ([[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/04 Governing the Machine/14 Supervisory Intervention/V4-14.13 Source References#^ref-sheridan2021supervisory|Sheridan 2021]]). Intervening in flight and learning from the episode require finer granularity than a single autonomy level assigned to the whole task. The mobile manipulator needs an authority assignment per channel and per instant: during a takeover, the arm can sit near the manual end of the scale while the base still runs at the automatic end, and the enforcer holds the final admission gate on every channel throughout because it checks the operator’s commands just as it checks the policy’s. Levels that differ across channels are what a supervisor can lose track of, the mode confusion of [[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/04 Governing the Machine/14 Supervisory Intervention/V4-14.07 Authority Handover Failure Modes#^sec-intervention-failure-modes|section 14.6]], so a channel left out of a transfer keeps its autonomous owner only while the console announces that it does, and otherwise holds. Each entry in the capability matrix therefore names the exact operation and channel it governs.

Naming the operations separates intervention from **safety enforcement**, two mechanisms that system architectures routinely conflate. Enforcement withholds permission from a candidate action proposed by a learned model, modifying, clamping, or projecting the setpoint while leaving the machine under autonomous control. Intervention transfers authority away from the model to an external entity, terminating autonomous operation and giving the supervisor the right to propose, while every command still passes the permission path. An **enforcement filter** sits in the fast inner control loop (1 kHz / $\le$ 1 ms) to prevent the policy from demanding an over-current maneuver or violating a barrier certificate ([[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/04 Governing the Machine/14 Supervisory Intervention/V4-14.13 Source References#^ref-ames2019control|Ames et al. 2019]]) during nominal tracking. An intervention mechanism strips the policy of its proposal token when an operational boundary is breached or an operator demands control. A system equipped with enforcement but lacking intervention cannot be redirected when human intent changes or external conditions fall outside the policy design envelope, leading to conservative deadlock when barrier constraints collapse the feasible control set. Conversely, a system equipped with intervention but lacking enforcement permits a lagging operator to drive the plant into structural failure during nominal manual operation. [[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/04 Governing the Machine/14 Supervisory Intervention/V4-14.03 Human Authority, Reaction Time, and Clearance#^tbl-14-enforcement-vs-intervention|Table 1]] sets the two mechanisms side by side, from trigger and authority holder to typical failure.

↩︎ The actuator current and thermal limits an enforcement filter must respect are established in **[[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/01 Machine Anatomy/02 The Physical Body/V4-02.02 The Five Physical Budgets#^sec-body-five-budgets|The Five Physical Budgets]]**.

| Dimension            | Enforcement filter                                                                        | Intervention state machine                                                                 |
|:---------------------|:------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------|:-------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------|
| Primary trigger      | Invariant boundary approach (${\dot{h}} + {\gamma_{\text{cbf}}} {h} < 0$) or torque limit | Human takeover assertion, monotonic lease timeout, or operational design domain (ODD) exit |
| Authority holder     | Autonomous policy retains proposal rights; enforcer modifies setpoints                    | Authority transferred to external entity or fallback controller                            |
| Execution loop       | Fast inner control loop (1 kHz / $\le$ 1 ms)                                              | Same permission tick; protocol exchange spans multiple ticks                               |
| Safety lease role    | Enforces continuous barrier satisfaction on admitted commands                             | Lease expiry invokes the state-dependent validated fallback                                |
| Typical failure mode | Feasible set collapse; conservative deadlock or oscillation                               | Opposing commands cancel, mode confusion, or late human reaction                           |

Table 1: **Architectural boundaries of safety enforcement and supervisory intervention**: Enforcement operates in-loop as an active constraint on proposed actions; intervention transitions authority between discrete proposers.

^tbl-14-enforcement-vs-intervention

**Definition 14.1: Supervisory intervention**

***Supervisory intervention*** is the transfer of the right to propose commands on named actuator channels between a learned policy and an authenticated person, committed on a control tick and bounded by a lease, while every command continues to pass the same permission check.

1.  **Significance**: The transfer is a delay on a moving machine, so it is priced in the stopping budget ${d_{\text{stop}}} \le {D_{\text{clear}}}$ rather than in the user interface, and the waiting before braking, not the braking itself, decides whether the machine stops in time.
2.  **Distinction**: Enforcement changes a proposal and leaves the proposer in place; intervention changes the proposer and leaves the check in place. A machine needs both, because neither can do the other’s job.
3.  **Common pitfall**: A frequent misconception is that a takeover hands the operator the actuators. The operator gains only the right to propose, and the machine still needs a local stop that does not wait for the operator to act.

### Capability Matrices and the Intervention-Enforcement Split

^sec-intervention-capability-split

On the warehouse mobile manipulator, a coworker taking a mug from the gripper at the packing station would be at risk if their station credential also allowed them to command the arm’s joints or drive the base. The capability matrix prevents that cross-channel hazard by letting the remote assistant redirect motion while denying any motion authority to the coworker accepting an item.

In that matrix, the remote assistant who supervises the mobile manipulator may hold a signed **trajectory veto** capability for the base’s planner channel: role `remote_assistant`, operation `veto_path`, channel `planner_proposal`, preemption tier above the learned planner but below the local enforcer, authenticated session and nonce, and a clearance-derived acknowledgment deadline. The enforcer responds to a valid veto with a checked local hold or stop. The same session carries a second entry, `manual_drive`, on the base-velocity and arm-joint channels, which becomes active only through a handover that commits within the clearance-derived deadline, and lapses with the manual lease of [[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/04 Governing the Machine/14 Supervisory Intervention/V4-14.04 Authority Transitions#^sec-intervention-authority-transitions|section 14.3]]. Every command it carries still passes the permission check. A wheel drive-torque packet from that credential is denied, because neither entry grants the base’s wheel drive-torque channel.

The coworker at the packing station holds a different entry, `accept_item`, which lets the coworker take from the gripper the mug the arm picked from the takeaway conveyor. That entry grants no motion authority. The item-handover channel of the enforcement record caps the arm’s approach to the coworker’s hand at 0.10 m/s, and no capability lifts that cap. The handover is a collaborative application in the sense of ISO 10218-2:2025 ([[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/04 Governing the Machine/14 Supervisory Intervention/V4-14.13 Source References#^ref-iso10218part2|International Organization for Standardization 2025b]]), and the body model of the hand that bounds it, with the contact force that model predicts, is set out once in **[[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/03 Running the Machine/12 Safety Enforcement/V4-12.08 The Minimal Intervention Principle and Optimization Filters#^sec-enforcement-intentional-contact|Intentional contact and in-contact energy barriers]]**. ISO 10218-2 leaves out the hazards of mounting the arm on a mobile platform, which ANSI/A3 R15.08-2 addresses for the running machine ([[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/04 Governing the Machine/14 Supervisory Intervention/V4-14.13 Source References#^ref-ansi1508part2|Association for Advancing Automation 2023]]).

The ceiling behind that cap comes from the contact-force model (**[[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/03 Running the Machine/12 Safety Enforcement/V4-12.08 The Minimal Intervention Principle and Optimization Filters#^eq-release-unbraked-force|equation 12.13]]**). For a free hand, which recoils, the selected 50 N criterion admits up to 0.24 m/s, and the cap sits under that ceiling. A hand clamped against a rack upright or the arm itself cannot recoil, and the same criterion then admits only 0.053 m/s, below the cap. The cap therefore holds only if the handover geometry excludes clamping, a premise the release case must evidence. The ceiling is conditional on these inputs and does not by itself establish a pressure or injury limit for a particular contact geometry; the site’s risk assessment qualifies the effective mass, the force criterion, and the contact geometry before the cap is qualified.

↪ The release criterion that holds the handover’s contact force under its assessed limit is developed in **[[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/04 Governing the Machine/16 Deployment Release/V4-16.03 Physical Criteria for Release#^sec-release-question|Physical Criteria for Release]]**.

Each capability entry binds actor, operation, channel, deadline, authentication, preemption tier, and fallback; a valid signature alone grants no motion authority. Physical clearance sets the tolerable deadline; the operator’s response time determines whether a human can meet it.

### Human Reaction Time and Travel Before Braking

^sec-intervention-reaction-drift

The remote assistant must notice the blocked aisle, decide how to recover, move the controls, and deliver a checked request. Summed in sequence, the total supervisory takeover latency ${T_{\Sigma}}$ is:

$$
{T_{\Sigma}} = {T_{\text{sensory}}} + {T_{\text{cognitive}}} + {T_{\text{motor}}} + {T_{\text{comm}}} \tag{1}
$$

 The first three terms belong to the person and depend on engagement and expectation; the last belongs to the console and the link. Human response times are distributions with long right tails, so a stopping budget needs a value that nearly every request meets, not the typical one. The calculation below compares a continuously engaged operator with an out-of-loop assistant. Its communication term includes transport and the protocol exchange, so that exchange must not be charged again.

^eq-intervention-reaction-floor

Waiting consumes distance before the selected stop begins. The finished stopping budget from **[[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/03 Running the Machine/12 Safety Enforcement/V4-12.04 Stopping Envelopes#^sec-enforcement-composite-budget|Sizing the composite stopping budget]]** supplies the machine delay, resident stop, and fixed allowances. Adding the human waiting interval exposes whether remote rescue can fit at the current speed; it does not authorize waiting when the local stop is already required.

The budgets that follow are illustrative design comparisons, not measured takeover performance. Each of them must also pay for the dispatch-to-admission interval ${\Delta_{\text{da}}}$, which **[[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/03 Running the Machine/13 Workload Placement/V4-13.07 Silicon Isolation Decisions#^sec-placement-dispatch-admission|The board-link budget and the dispatch-to-admission interval]]** allocated but has not yet measured. A takeover does not remove that interval. The policy must still infer on the frame that shows the blocked aisle before it can request help, and its request, like any proposal, reaches the arbiter only through the safety microcontroller, so the base keeps rolling through ${\Delta_{\text{da}}}$ before the human response even begins. This chapter calls a budget without the interval the *partial budget* and a budget with it the *complete budget*. The stops and overruns below use the complete budget. The speed ceilings and deadlines give the partial value first and then the charged one, because the gap between the two is itself a design lesson.

The stopping budget of **[[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/03 Running the Machine/12 Safety Enforcement/V4-12.04 Stopping Envelopes#^tbl-enforcement-stopping-budget|The warehouse mobile manipulator's stopping budget]]** shows what [[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/04 Governing the Machine/14 Supervisory Intervention/V4-14.03 Human Authority, Reaction Time, and Clearance#^eq-intervention-reaction-floor|equation 1]] costs on the mobile manipulator. At the 1.3 m/s aisle speed it leaves 131.0 mm of the 1.10 m rack-end clear distance before dispatch-to-admission travel, and 48.7 mm once the 82.3 mm of travel during ${\Delta_{\text{da}}}$ is charged. A takeover delay adds to the machine’s own pre-brake delay ${\tau_{\text{delay}}}$, and every millisecond of it becomes travel at the aisle speed:

1.  **Mode 1: Continuous in-loop teleoperation**: An attending operator with hands on the controls of a synchronized leader arm or a real-time console, responding to the stimulus the task has led them to expect, makes a simple reaction. The Model Human Processor[[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/04 Governing the Machine/14 Supervisory Intervention/V4-14.14 Footnotes#^fn3|³]] ([[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/04 Governing the Machine/14 Supervisory Intervention/V4-14.13 Source References#^ref-card1983psychology|Card et al. 1983]]) prices that reaction as one perceptual, one cognitive, and one motor cycle ([[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/04 Governing the Machine/14 Supervisory Intervention/V4-14.13 Source References#^ref-jastrzembski2007model|Jastrzembski and Charness 2007]]). At the slow end of each cycle’s range, ${T_{\text{sensory}}} =$ 200 ms, ${T_{\text{cognitive}}} =$ 170 ms, and ${T_{\text{motor}}} =$ 100 ms. The console adds ${T_{\text{comm}}} =$ 135 ms: the scene reaches the operator after a video glass-to-glass latency[[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/04 Governing the Machine/14 Supervisory Intervention/V4-14.14 Footnotes#^fn4|⁴]] of 104 ms, measured for a wired teleoperation link ([[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/04 Governing the Machine/14 Supervisory Intervention/V4-14.13 Source References#^ref-schimpe2022opensource|Schimpe et al. 2022]]), and the request returns through a 31 ms protocol exchange, the four-phase handshake that [[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/04 Governing the Machine/14 Supervisory Intervention/V4-14.04 Authority Transitions#^sec-intervention-handshake-protocol|section 14.3.3]] builds and budgets phase by phase. By [[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/04 Governing the Machine/14 Supervisory Intervention/V4-14.03 Human Authority, Reaction Time, and Clearance#^eq-intervention-reaction-floor|equation 1]], ${T_{\Sigma, 1}} =$ 605 ms. The machine travels 786.5 mm during that delay, several times the spare margin. Solving the complete budget for speed, with ${T_{\Sigma, 1}}$ and ${\Delta_{\text{da}}}$ added to ${\tau_{\text{delay}}}$, bounds the machine to 0.797 m/s. The value holds only for the expected stimulus. A driver who is waiting for a brake signal needs up to 0.75 s to detect it and reach the pedal, and a surprise raises that to about 1.5 s ([[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/04 Governing the Machine/14 Supervisory Intervention/V4-14.13 Source References#^ref-green2000stop|Green 2000]]). [[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/04 Governing the Machine/14 Supervisory Intervention/V4-14.03 Human Authority, Reaction Time, and Clearance#^sec-intervention-speed-ceilings|Section 14.2.3]] prices those cases.
2.  **Mode 2: Passive out-of-the-loop supervisory takeover**: An assistant who monitors the telemetry of several machines passively does not make a simple reaction, and its stages cannot be priced separately. Driver take-over studies measure the whole interval, from the take-over request to the first control input. Across 520 experimental conditions in 129 studies, the mean take-over time averaged 2.72 s, and the distribution of those condition means is skewed to the right ([[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/04 Governing the Machine/14 Supervisory Intervention/V4-14.13 Source References#^ref-zhang2019takeover|Zhang et al. 2019]]). For this illustrative comparison, the selected design value is the 90th percentile of the condition means, 4.33 s, computed from the study’s published data. With the same 135 ms console term, the takeover delay is ${T_{\Sigma,2}} =$ 4.5 s, during which the machine covers 5.80 m, more than five times the clear distance, before any brake command. The same solution bounds the machine to 0.189 m/s.

In Mode 2 at the aisle speed, the machine would reach a static obstacle at the rack end at full speed, because contact occurs before braking ever begins. Stronger braking can raise a finite stopping-speed ceiling, but cannot recover distance already spent waiting. As braking distance tends toward zero, reaction travel still sets a limiting ceiling. A percentile of condition means also understates the tail an individual operator can produce, because each condition’s own spread grows with its mean; the site must measure its operators, console, and task load before any of these values admits a speed. Road regulation sizes the same interval generously for a vehicle that can keep driving while it waits. For an unanswered transition demand in the cited 2021 version of UN Regulation No. 157, an automated lane-keeping system begins its minimum risk manoeuvre no earlier than 10 s after it issues the demand ([[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/04 Governing the Machine/14 Supervisory Intervention/V4-14.13 Source References#^ref-unece2021r157|United Nations Economic Commission for Europe 2021]]), 2.2× the design takeover here. Severe system or vehicle failures permit an immediate minimum risk manoeuvre, and an obstacle can require an emergency response during the transition phase. The vehicle can afford that wait because it stays in its lane under automated control throughout. At the aisle speed the same wait would carry the mobile manipulator 13 m, so the machine cannot borrow the road’s interval, only its structure of a demand, a bounded wait, and an automatic fallback. [[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/04 Governing the Machine/14 Supervisory Intervention/V4-14.03 Human Authority, Reaction Time, and Clearance#^sec-intervention-clearance-deadlines|Section 14.2.4]] turns the two design values into the speeds at which the machine may request each kind of takeover.

Drawn to scale, the Mode 1 budget shows where the clearance goes ([[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/04 Governing the Machine/14 Supervisory Intervention/V4-14.03 Human Authority, Reaction Time, and Clearance#^fig-14-in-loop-takeover-strip|figure 2]]). The 48.7 mm spare of the complete budget corresponds to 37.5 ms at the 1.3 m/s aisle speed, so the machine has spent it long before the 104 ms video stage ends and before the operator has perceived anything. The perceptual, cognitive, and motor cycles and the handshake that follow are all travel the budget cannot absorb, 786.5 mm in total.

**Napkin Math 14.1: The takeover tax**

**Problem**: At the 1.3 m/s aisle speed, does the finished stopping budget still fit the rack-end clear distance once an in-loop or an out-of-loop takeover delay is inserted before braking, and by how much does each overrun it?

**Variables**:

- ${v} =$ 1.3 m/s (Chosen) and ${D_{\text{clear}}} =$ 1.10 m (Illustrative).
- ${\tau_{\text{delay}}} =$ 133.6 ms, the machine’s own pre-brake delay (Derived; **[[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/03 Running the Machine/12 Safety Enforcement/V4-12.04 Stopping Envelopes#^tbl-enforcement-stopping-budget|The warehouse mobile manipulator's stopping budget]]**), which excludes the dispatch-to-admission interval, and ${\Delta_{\text{da}}} =$ 63.3 ms, the allocation of **[[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/03 Running the Machine/13 Workload Placement/V4-13.07 Silicon Isolation Decisions#^sec-placement-dispatch-admission|The board-link budget and the dispatch-to-admission interval]]** (Illustrative), which a takeover still spends before braking.
- ${d_{\text{suffix}}}({v}) = {v}^2/(2{a_{\text{brake}}}) + {v}\,{a_{\text{brake}}}/(2{j_{\max}})$, the jerk-limited resident stop of **[[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/03 Running the Machine/11 Trajectory Planning/V4-11.06 Behavior at the Seam#^eq-plan-c2-stopping-suffix|equation 11.17]]**, with ${a_{\text{brake}}} =$ 2 m/s² (Illustrative) and ${j_{\max}} =$ 7.11 m/s³ (Derived).
- ${\delta} = {\delta_{\text{loc}}} + {\delta_{\text{margin}}} + {\epsilon_{\text{track}}} =$ 190 mm (Illustrative and Chosen).
- ${T_{\Sigma, 1}} =$ 605 ms in-loop and ${T_{\Sigma,2}} =$ 4.5 s out-of-loop (Derived, from the measured human and transport terms of Modes 1 and 2 plus the chosen handshake).

**Math**: Every term is in millimeters. The machine alone, charged with the dispatch-to-admission travel, leaves a 48.7 mm spare:

$$
d_{\text{stop},0} = v\,\tau_{\text{delay}} + v\,{\Delta_{\text{da}}} + d_{\text{suffix}}(v) + \delta = 173.68 + 82.30 + 605.31 + 190 = 1051.30\text{ mm}
$$

Each takeover adds ${v}\,{T_{\Sigma}}$ before the same stop:

$$
d_{\text{stop},1} = v\,\tau_{\text{delay}} + v\,{\Delta_{\text{da}}} + v\,T_{\Sigma,1} + d_{\text{suffix}}(v) + \delta = 173.68 + 82.30 + 786.5 + 605.31 + 190 = 1837.80\text{ mm}
$$

$$
d_{\text{stop},2} = v\,\tau_{\text{delay}} + v\,{\Delta_{\text{da}}} + v\,T_{\Sigma,2} + d_{\text{suffix}}(v) + \delta = 173.68 + 82.30 + 5804.5 + 605.31 + 190 = 6855.80\text{ mm}
$$

**Result**: The in-loop takeover overruns the clear distance by 737.8 mm and the out-of-loop takeover by 5.76 m; the second stop is 3.7× the first.

**Systems insight**: The waiting term, not the braking term, decides the verdict. The in-loop takeover alone covers 16.1× the spare, so no stronger brake can make ${d_{\text{stop}}} \le {D_{\text{clear}}}$ hold at this speed; only a lower ${v}$ or a stop before the request can. The delay, braking, and allowance terms follow the stopping-distance model of **[[05 Reference/Volume IV - Physical AI/V4 Systems and Hardware#^sec-appendix-systems-stopping|Kinematic Stopping Envelopes and Information Age Lag]]**.

### Takeover Speed Ceilings from Available Clearance

^sec-intervention-speed-ceilings

Because no braking force can recover distance spent waiting, the machine must bound its operating speed before an intervention is ever requested. One inequality sets both bounds that this section and the next derive. If the machine continues at speed ${v}$ for a waiting duration ${T_{\text{wait}}}$ while awaiting the operator’s response, the finished stopping budget of **[[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/03 Running the Machine/12 Safety Enforcement/V4-12.04 Stopping Envelopes#^sec-enforcement-composite-budget|Sizing the composite stopping budget]]** gains one term, the travel during that wait. The pre-brake delay ${\tau_{\text{delay}}}$, the jerk-limited resident stop ${d_{\text{suffix}}}({v})$ (**[[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/03 Running the Machine/11 Trajectory Planning/V4-11.06 Behavior at the Seam#^eq-plan-c2-stopping-suffix|equation 11.17]]**), and the fixed allowances keep the meanings defined there, and the sum must still not exceed ${D_{\text{clear}}}$ measured at the same reference event:

$$
{d_{\text{stop}}}({T_{\text{wait}}}) = {v}({T_{\text{wait}}} + {\tau_{\text{delay}}}) + {d_{\text{suffix}}}({v}) + {\delta_{\text{loc}}} + {\delta_{\text{margin}}} + {\epsilon_{\text{track}}} \le {D_{\text{clear}}}. \tag{2}
$$

 Setting ${T_{\text{wait}}} = {T_{\Sigma}}$ and solving for speed gives the takeover speed ceiling of this section. Fixing the speed and solving for ${T_{\text{wait}}}$ gives the latest fallback deadline of [[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/04 Governing the Machine/14 Supervisory Intervention/V4-14.03 Human Authority, Reaction Time, and Clearance#^sec-intervention-clearance-deadlines|section 14.2.4]].

^eq-intervention-stopping-inequality

The three stops of notebook [14.1](https://mlsysbook.ai/vol4/14_intervention/14_intervention.html#nbk-intervention-operator-takeover-budget-spatial-clearance-margin) differ only in how long the machine coasts before an identical jerk-limited resident stop begins ([[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/04 Governing the Machine/14 Supervisory Intervention/V4-14.03 Human Authority, Reaction Time, and Clearance#^fig-14-takeover-timeline|figure 3]]). Every trace ends with the same braking curve, so the whole difference between stopping 48.7 mm short of the clear distance and stopping at 6.86 m is coasting.

[[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/04 Governing the Machine/14 Supervisory Intervention/V4-14.03 Human Authority, Reaction Time, and Clearance#^tbl-14-teleop-stability|Table 2]] prices each sourced response time. Every row adds the 135 ms console term to the human response, adds the total to ${\tau_{\text{delay}}}$, and solves the finished stopping budget for the largest speed at which the machine still stops inside the rack-end clear distance. Before charging the missing admission delay, the ceiling falls from 1.01 m/s for an attending operator who meets the expected stimulus at the nominal pace to 0.12 m/s for the slowest percentile of takeover conditions. No row fits at the 1.3 m/s aisle speed. Two facts dominate the table. Expectation matters more than the console, because moving from an expected to a surprising stimulus costs more speed than the whole console term does. The tail matters more than the average, because a speed chosen for the mean take-over time is overrun by every condition slower than the mean.

A ceiling above the resident stop’s transition speed ${a_{\text{brake}}}^2/{j_{\max}}$ is the positive root derived for the machine’s own stop in **[[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/03 Running the Machine/12 Safety Enforcement/V4-12.04 Stopping Envelopes#^eq-enforce-speed-ceiling|equation 12.2]]**, where the half jerk ramp acts as an added delay before full braking (the rigid-stop form is **[[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/01 Machine Anatomy/02 The Physical Body/V4-02.04 Kinetic Momentum#^eq-body-velocity-ceiling|equation 2.3]]**). A takeover adds one more such interval, so setting ${T_{\text{wait}}} = {T_{\Sigma}}$ in [[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/04 Governing the Machine/14 Supervisory Intervention/V4-14.03 Human Authority, Reaction Time, and Clearance#^eq-intervention-stopping-inequality|equation 2]] folds the human delay into the same root:

$$
{v_{\max}}({T_{\Sigma}}) = {a_{\text{brake}}}\left[\sqrt{{b}^2 + \frac{2\,({D_{\text{clear}}}-{\delta})}{{a_{\text{brake}}}}} - {b}\right],\qquad {b} = {\tau_{\text{delay}}} + {T_{\Sigma}} + \frac{{a_{\text{brake}}}}{2{j_{\max}}}. \tag{3}
$$

 The term ${b}$ collects every interval during which the machine still moves at ${v}$: its own delay, the human’s, and the half jerk ramp before full braking. Below ${a_{\text{brake}}}^2/{j_{\max}}$ the stop never reaches full deceleration and the cubic branch of **[[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/03 Running the Machine/11 Trajectory Planning/V4-11.06 Behavior at the Seam#^eq-plan-c2-stopping-suffix|equation 11.17]]** applies instead, which is why the table’s slower rows come from the solver rather than from this root.

^eq-intervention-speed-ceiling

**Napkin Math 14.2: The speed a human buys**

**Problem**: What is the highest speed at which the mobile manipulator may request an in-loop takeover from an attending operator, and does any brake, however strong, admit the aisle speed?

**Variables**:

- ${a_{\text{brake}}} =$ 2 m/s² (Illustrative) and ${j_{\max}} =$ 7.11 m/s³ (Derived).
- ${\tau_{\text{delay}}} =$ 0.1336 s (Derived) and ${T_{\Sigma, 1}} =$ 0.605 s (Derived, from the measured human and transport terms of Mode 1 plus the chosen handshake).
- ${D_{\text{clear}}} - {\delta} =$ 0.91 m (Illustrative, less the fixed allowances).

**Math**: ${b} =$ 0.1336 $+$ 0.605 $+$ 2 $/ (2 \times$ 7.11$) =$ 0.1336 $+$ 0.605 $+$ 0.1406 $=$ 0.8792 s. Then ${b}^2 + 2 \times$ 0.91 $/$ 2 $=$ 1.6830 s², whose square root is 1.2973 s, so by [[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/04 Governing the Machine/14 Supervisory Intervention/V4-14.03 Human Authority, Reaction Time, and Clearance#^eq-intervention-speed-ceiling|equation 3]] ${v_{\max}} =$ 2 $\times$ (1.2973 $-$ 0.8792). The root lies above ${a_{\text{brake}}}^2/{j_{\max}} =$ 0.562 m/s, so the quadratic branch was the right one. If both the deceleration and jerk bounds are removed so that the resident stopping distance tends to zero, the limiting ceiling is $({D_{\text{clear}}} - {\delta})/({\tau_{\text{delay}}} + {T_{\Sigma}})$.

**Result**: ${v_{\max}} =$ 0.836 m/s, the design in-loop row of [[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/04 Governing the Machine/14 Supervisory Intervention/V4-14.03 Human Authority, Reaction Time, and Clearance#^tbl-14-teleop-stability|table 2]]; instantaneous braking would raise it only to 1.23 m/s.

**Systems insight**: Even a perfect brake leaves the ceiling below the 1.3 m/s aisle speed, because the travel during ${\tau_{\text{delay}}} + {T_{\Sigma}}$ alone exceeds the clear distance at that speed. The waiting term of ${d_{\text{stop}}} \le {D_{\text{clear}}}$ sets the request speed, so faster brakes cannot buy back a slow human; adding the 63.3 ms admission allocation to ${b}$ lowers the root to 0.797 m/s.

The last four are percentiles of the mean take-over time across 520 experimental conditions, computed from the published data of Zhang et al. Each total adds the console term (video glass-to-glass latency ([[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/04 Governing the Machine/14 Supervisory Intervention/V4-14.13 Source References#^ref-schimpe2022opensource|Schimpe et al. 2022]]) plus the four-phase exchange) and excludes the dispatch-to-admission interval, so every ceiling is optimistic; charging that allocation lowers the design in-loop ceiling to 0.797 m/s. Bold rows are the design values.

^tbl-14-teleop-stability

The table also locates the other interfaces a site might offer. Continuous velocity guidance or supervisory waypoint dispatch places the operator somewhere between an expected-stimulus reaction and an out-of-loop takeover, and only a measured response distribution for that interface, console, and task says where. A hardwired protective input removes the console term but not the human response, so its operator still needs the local stop to hold the machine safe while that response runs.

### Clearance-Derived Fallback Deadlines

^sec-intervention-clearance-deadlines

If a machine reaches the edge of its operating domain at speed and requests a takeover, an operator who is distracted, asleep, or incapacitated will fail to complete the handshake before the machine runs out of clearance. An autonomous policy’s handover request can therefore never wait indefinitely, nor can its timeout be a fixed software constant: a single fixed window that is safe at a crawl will overrun the clearance at transport speed ([[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/04 Governing the Machine/14 Supervisory Intervention/V4-14.10 Fallacies and Pitfalls#^sec-intervention-fallacies-pitfalls|section 14.9]] prices such a window at both speeds). Safe timeouts must instead be derived at runtime from the machine’s current speed, line-of-sight clearance, and braking deceleration.

The deadline is the second reading of the stopping inequality. The speed ceiling fixed the wait and solved for speed; the deadline fixes the current speed and solves for the wait. Setting equality in [[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/04 Governing the Machine/14 Supervisory Intervention/V4-14.03 Human Authority, Reaction Time, and Clearance#^eq-intervention-stopping-inequality|equation 2]] (${d_{\text{stop}}}({T_{\text{wait}}}) = {D_{\text{clear}}}$) and solving for the maximum permissible waiting time yields the latest fallback deadline ${T_{\text{deadline,fallback}}}$:

$$
{T_{\text{deadline,fallback}}} = \frac{{D_{\text{clear}}}-{\delta_{\text{loc}}}-{\delta_{\text{margin}}}-{\epsilon_{\text{track}}}-{d_{\text{suffix}}}({v})}{{v}}-{\tau_{\text{delay}}},\qquad {T_{\text{timeout}}}=\max\!\left(0,\min({T_{\text{config}}},{T_{\text{deadline,fallback}}})\right). \tag{4}
$$

 For a live check, the safety microcontroller dispatches fallback **no later than** the last permission tick before the resulting deadline. A nonpositive ${T_{\text{deadline,fallback}}}$ calls for immediate fallback and may mean stopping is already infeasible. The configured protocol ceiling cannot extend that physical deadline or the expiry of active permission.

^eq-intervention-latest-fallback

**Napkin Math 14.3: The waiting allowance**

**Problem**: How long may the mobile manipulator wait after a handover request before it must dispatch its own stop, at the 1.3 m/s aisle speed and at the 0.75 m/s slow-down, and does the 605 ms in-loop takeover fit either allowance?

**Variables**:

- ${D_{\text{clear}}} - {\delta} =$ 0.91 m (Illustrative) and ${\tau_{\text{delay}}} =$ 0.1336 s (Derived).
- ${d_{\text{suffix}}}({v}) = {v}^2/(2{a_{\text{brake}}}) + {v}\,{a_{\text{brake}}}/(2{j_{\max}})$ (Derived; **[[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/03 Running the Machine/11 Trajectory Planning/V4-11.06 Behavior at the Seam#^eq-plan-c2-stopping-suffix|equation 11.17]]**): 0.6053 m at 1.3 m/s and 0.2461 m at 0.75 m/s.
- ${T_{\Sigma, 1}} =$ 605 ms (Derived, measured human and transport terms plus the chosen handshake) and ${\Delta_{\text{da}}} =$ 63.3 ms (Illustrative; the unmeasured allocation of **[[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/03 Running the Machine/13 Workload Placement/V4-13.07 Silicon Isolation Decisions#^sec-placement-dispatch-admission|The board-link budget and the dispatch-to-admission interval]]**).

**Math**: By [[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/04 Governing the Machine/14 Supervisory Intervention/V4-14.03 Human Authority, Reaction Time, and Clearance#^eq-intervention-latest-fallback|equation 4]], ${T_{\text{deadline,fallback}}} =$ (0.91 $-$ 0.6053)/1.3 $-$ 0.1336 $=$ 0.1008 s at the aisle speed, and (0.91 $-$ 0.2461)/0.75 $-$ 0.1336 $=$ 0.7516 s at the slow-down. Reserving the takeover leaves 751.6 ms $-$ 605 ms $=$ 146.6 ms, and charging ${\Delta_{\text{da}}}$ leaves 83.3 ms.

**Result**: At the aisle speed the takeover overruns a 100.8 ms allowance; at the slow-down it fits with 83.3 ms to spare on the complete budget.

**Systems insight**: Slowing from 1.3 to 0.75 m/s multiplies the waiting allowance 7.5×, because both the stop term and the travel per millisecond of ${d_{\text{stop}}} \le {D_{\text{clear}}}$ shrink with speed. The deadline is a function of the current state, so no single configured timeout can be correct at both speeds.

At the aisle speed, the 100.8 ms of notebook [14.3](https://mlsysbook.ai/vol4/14_intervention/14_intervention.html#nbk-intervention-waiting-allowance), or 131.0 mm of travel, is the unallocated timing allowance of the stopping subtotal in **[[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/03 Running the Machine/12 Safety Enforcement/V4-12.04 Stopping Envelopes#^sec-enforcement-composite-budget|Sizing the composite stopping budget]]**, and it is a partial-budget value. Charging the 63.3 ms dispatch-to-admission allocation of **[[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/03 Running the Machine/13 Workload Placement/V4-13.07 Silicon Isolation Decisions#^sec-placement-dispatch-admission|The board-link budget and the dispatch-to-admission interval]]** completes the budget. At speed ${v}$ the charge costs ${v}{\Delta_{\text{da}}}$ of travel, so at the aisle speed only 37.5 ms of waiting survives it. Wherever the rest of the chapter reports a waiting allowance, it gives the partial value and then the charged one, and the charged value is the one a design must meet. Rounded down to whole permission ticks, the partial aisle-speed allowance of 100.8 ms becomes 100 ms, which illustrates tick rounding rather than an absolute fallback time. The 500 ms configured ceiling is only an outer protocol limit, because the clearance deadline binds first.

Both readings of the stopping inequality also need a clock origin, and the chapter uses the two that [[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/04 Governing the Machine/14 Supervisory Intervention/V4-14.03 Human Authority, Reaction Time, and Clearance#^tbl-14-clock-origins|table 3]] sets side by side. The design budget behind every displayed number counts from the observation’s capture. A live check on the safety microcontroller counts from now.

|                      | Design budget (capture origin)                                                                                                                 | Live check (now origin)                                                                                     |
|:---------------------|:-----------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------|:------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------|
| Where it is used     | Every number this chapter displays                                                                                                             | The safety microcontroller, at each permission tick of a pending request                                    |
| Clear distance       | ${D_{\text{clear}}}$ measured at the observation’s capture event                                                                               | The current tick’s remaining clearance, already reduced for the motion since capture                        |
| Delay before braking | ${\tau_{\text{delay}}} + {\Delta_{\text{da}}}$, from capture to the onset of the physical response                                             | Only the delay still ahead: dispatch to response onset plus any unpaid processing, never a fresh full lease |
| Meaning of the wait  | ${T_{\text{wait}}}$ is additional delay inserted into that budget, with any overlap with its processing, lease, or transport intervals removed | The time left before the last permission tick at which a stop still fits                                    |

Table 3: **Two clock origins for one stopping inequality**: Either column is correct on its own. Mixing a cell from one column with a cell from the other charges elapsed travel twice or not at all.

^tbl-14-clock-origins

The two ways to mix them fail in opposite directions. A live check that takes the current clearance, which already reflects the travel since capture, and then also subtracts the capture-origin delay pays for the elapsed part of that delay twice and dispatches its fallback early, which costs availability. The reverse mix, the capture-time clearance with a delay counted only from now, never pays for the travel since capture and dispatches its fallback late, which costs clearance.

The 31 ms protocol exchange fits the aisle-speed allowance on both budgets. The sourced 605 ms in-loop response does not. It exceeds the aisle-speed allowance several times over, so an in-loop request needs a slower machine, and the human response time, not the machine, sets how much slower. Assuming a faster human gives the wrong answer. A slow-down to 1.2 m/s would suit an operator who responds in a small fraction of a second; there the budget leaves 184.1 ms, and the sourced response overruns it by 420.9 ms. Such a design passes every check that uses an optimistic human and fails the first operator who needs the time the literature measures.

A 31 milliseconds handshake bar and a 605 milliseconds in-loop takeover bar are drawn against a red dashed limit at 100.8 milliseconds, the waiting allowance at 1.3 meters per second, which the takeover bar crosses. A dotted mark at 751.6 milliseconds shows the allowance at 0.75 meters per second, beyond the end of the takeover bar.*

*The handshake fits the aisle-speed allowance; the whole takeover needs the slow-down.*

The design slows to 0.75 m/s before requesting an in-loop takeover. That speed comes from solving the budget with both the sourced response and ${\Delta_{\text{da}}}$ added to ${\tau_{\text{delay}}}$, which gives a complete-budget ceiling of 0.797 m/s, rounded down to the next 0.05 m/s step. At that speed the capture-origin budget leaves 563.7 mm, or 751.6 ms of additional delay. Reserving the response leaves 146.6 ms, and charging ${\Delta_{\text{da}}}$ still leaves 83.3 ms. That is a conservative design allocation, not a claim that every request at that speed receives the same live timeout, and it holds only while the operator meets the expected stimulus. An unexpected hazard moves the operator to the slower rows of [[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/04 Governing the Machine/14 Supervisory Intervention/V4-14.03 Human Authority, Reaction Time, and Clearance#^tbl-14-teleop-stability|table 2]], whose ceilings the slow-down exceeds.

Solving with both intervals matters. The partial-budget ceiling, with the response alone added, is 0.84 m/s, and a designer who stopped there might choose 0.8 m/s, which sits under it. At that speed the response leaves 58.3 ms, less than the 63.3 ms allocation, so once the omitted interval is charged the slow-down misses its deadline by 5.0 ms. A budget that leaves out one interval of a few tens of milliseconds admits a speed that the complete budget rejects.

The out-of-loop takeover forces a larger change. Its 4.5 s design response caps the request speed at 0.19 m/s, so the crawl before an out-of-loop request is 0.15 m/s, where the budget leaves 5.79 s and the design response leaves 1.32 s of it unspent. The crawl survives the ${\Delta_{\text{da}}}$ charge, which lowers its ceiling only to 0.189 m/s. It still fails the 99th-percentile condition, whose 7.1 s response admits only 0.12 m/s, and an individual operator’s tail is longer than any condition mean. A crawl slow enough for every operator is no longer motion worth keeping. The robust design therefore stops the machine automatically before handing over. The local stop runs first, and the out-of-loop takeover begins from standstill, where waiting costs time but no clearance. Both moving candidates still require qualified admission delay, current clearance, and a feasible stopping continuation. The hidden pallet leaves no room for an anticipatory slow-down at all, so the machine answers it with its own local stop and requests the takeover from standstill. A planned slow-down or crawl can prepare an anticipated request; neither permits waiting at aisle speed for a newly discovered obstacle.

Every one of these designs rests on two properties the chapter has so far assumed. Authority, once granted, must not outlive a proposer that falls silent, and it must move between proposers on a single tick without two of them ever commanding the same channel. [[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/04 Governing the Machine/14 Supervisory Intervention/V4-14.04 Authority Transitions#^sec-intervention-authority-transitions|Section 14.3]] builds both.
