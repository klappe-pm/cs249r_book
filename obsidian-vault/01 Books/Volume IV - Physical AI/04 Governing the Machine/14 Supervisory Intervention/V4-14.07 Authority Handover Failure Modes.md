# V4-14.07 Authority Handover Failure Modes

[[Home|Home]] · [[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/Volume 4 Overview|Volume Overview]] · [[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/04 Governing the Machine/14 Supervisory Intervention/V4-14 Supervisory Intervention|V4-14 Supervisory Intervention]]

^sec-intervention-failure-modes

When the warehouse mobile manipulator’s perception policy meets an unmodeled specular reflection from wet shrink-wrap on a pallet, depth artifacts drop its confidence and the runtime asks a remote assistant to reposition the gripper. If the console grants manual joystick authority over the arm without locking the base channel or announcing that the base is still moving, an assistant focused on aligning the fingers assumes the chassis is stationary. Meanwhile the autonomous navigation thread keeps driving the wheels into a rack upright. Every individual hardware and software component in that handover operates within its specification. The collision comes from mode confusion, the failure class that Sarter and Woods identified in automated aircraft cockpits ([[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/04 Governing the Machine/14 Supervisory Intervention/V4-14.13 Source References#^ref-sarter1995mode|Sarter and Woods 1995]]). Every operating mode added to a control loop multiplies the state a human supervisor must track. When an interface changes modes without unambiguous telemetry of which agent holds each channel, operators take actions that are right for their assumed mode and hazardous in the actual one ([[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/04 Governing the Machine/14 Supervisory Intervention/V4-14.13 Source References#^ref-sarter1995mode|Sarter and Woods 1995]]). In embodied physical systems, the most dangerous form of this failure is **authority ambiguity**: a state in which the autonomous runtime assumes the human has taken over a physical degree of freedom, while the human assumes the automated controller still guides it.

**Definition 14.3: Mode confusion**

***Mode confusion*** is a mismatch between the operational state a human supervisor believes an automated system occupies and its actual execution mode, leading the supervisor to take actions that would be correct for the presumed state but are hazardous in the real one.

1.  **Significance**: When control channels transfer authority independently, an operator’s mental model of one channel can easily go stale while their attention is consumed by another. For example, an operator dedicates the 605 ms in-loop response window entirely to an arm alignment, under the false belief that the base is parked. The base, running under the autonomous policy at 1.3 m/s aisle speed, then travels 786.5 mm, which is 16.1× the 48.7 mm margin the complete budget leaves for ${d_{\text{stop}}} \le {D_{\text{clear}}}$.
2.  **Distinction**: Conflicting command streams between concurrent software modules represent an arbitration fault, which single-owner bus gating resolves by construction. Mode confusion, by contrast, resides within the supervisor’s mental model of the machine. Resolving it requires committed channel masks and explicit per-channel status annunciation at the console, because deterministic ownership on the CAN bus or Ethernet backplane cannot correct a human’s false assumptions.
3.  **Common pitfall**: Telemetry confirming that an operator’s gaze is directed at a primary task monitor does not guarantee the absence of mode confusion. Tracking an operator’s visual focus onto an end-effector camera stream reveals nothing about whether that operator realizes the autonomous navigation stack still holds kinetic authority over the drive chassis.

On a machine with several axes, control authority splits naturally across distinct groups of joints, such as the mobile base’s planar velocity (${\dot{x}}, {\dot{y}}, {\dot{\theta}_{\text{head}}}$) and the manipulator arm’s joint positions. In the wet-shrink-wrap collision, those channels transferred independently. Telemetry logs reveal the fault as disjointed control flags toggling asynchronously across execution threads. At the human-machine interface, however, the split state stays invisible until physical clearance is exhausted, because neither the policy nor the human receives an immediate, unambiguous indicator of the other’s partial withdrawal.

To prevent this desynchronization, handover protocols must enforce explicit, per-channel bitmasks across every phase of negotiation. Every `Request`, `Acknowledge`, and `Commit` message must declare the exact subset of degrees of freedom being transferred. The formal transition state machine of [[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/04 Governing the Machine/14 Supervisory Intervention/V4-14.04 Authority Transitions#^sec-intervention-authority-transitions|section 14.3]] moves proposal tokens exclusively for those designated channels, and the subsequent `Confirm` phase explicitly annunciates active ownership on each channel back to the operator console. A channel left out of the bitmask may keep its autonomous owner, so the base can keep navigating while the operator takes the arm. This holds only while the console annunciates that the base is still moving under the policy and the operator’s acknowledgment covered that state. Without that annunciation, the commit places the untransferred channel in a certified holding state, such as an active brake hold, rather than letting autonomous execution continue silently. The wet-shrink-wrap collision above is the second rule’s case, an untransferred base left running without annunciation.

Explicit channel ownership protocols solve arbitration on the digital bus, but they cannot restore the situational awareness of an out-of-loop human. For this reason, the in-loop and out-of-loop takeovers of [[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/04 Governing the Machine/14 Supervisory Intervention/V4-14.03 Human Authority, Reaction Time, and Clearance#^sec-intervention-reaction-drift|section 14.2.2]] must be treated as distinct operational states with different timing budgets, the 605 ms and 4.5 s that set different request speeds. While gaze tracking can verify where an operator directs their eyes, it cannot verify cognitive comprehension or justify truncating the required takeover latency budget. Local stopping envelopes must remain continuously armed and active, even when the console reports an attentive supervisor. Two field incidents show what happens when either half of this two-layer protection is missing.

In the Tempe, Arizona collision, the National Transportation Safety Board (NTSB) determined that the safety driver was visually distracted by a personal smartphone, identifying operator complacency and inadequate monitoring policies as primary contributing factors ([[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/04 Governing the Machine/14 Supervisory Intervention/V4-14.13 Source References#^ref-ntsb2019uber|National Transportation Safety Board 2019]]). The vehicle architecture had disabled automatic emergency braking during testing and relied entirely on the human supervisor to perceive hazards and intervene. In the San Francisco Cruise incident, an autonomous vehicle executed an emergency stop after striking a pedestrian who had been thrown into its path by an adjacent human-driven car. The onboard planner, however, subsequently initiated an automated pull-over maneuver, dragging the pedestrian beneath the chassis ([[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/04 Governing the Machine/14 Supervisory Intervention/V4-14.13 Source References#^ref-quinnemanuel2024cruise|Quinn Emanuel Urquhart and Sullivan, LLP 2024]]). The Tempe disaster demonstrates why an operator-monitoring contract must always be backed by an autonomous, local stopping envelope capable of bringing the machine to a halt within the remaining sensor clearance, regardless of operator attentiveness. The Cruise incident demonstrates that unexpected physical contact, tactile sensor anomalies, or sudden external force spikes must immediately lock out all motion authority into a verified physical inspection hold, rather than allowing automated post-collision clearing routines to execute.

↩︎ The Tempe timeline, including the action suppression that withheld emergency braking, is established in **[[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/00 Introduction/01 The Causal Boundary/V4-01.09 When the Responsibilities Fail Together#^sec-boundary-when-fails|When the Responsibilities Fail Together]]**.

A human operator’s manual command can oppose an active safety barrier without creating a multi-writer conflict on the actuator bus. When an operator commands a manipulator toward a prohibited obstacle, the downstream safety enforcer either projects the trajectory onto the safe half-space or rejects the command entirely, dispatching only the admitted control action to the motor drives. However, the supervisory console must make this active constraint immediately visible to the human. If the interface conceals why the robot has stopped responding, the operator will instinctively command higher control effort or larger joystick displacements, leading to severe command saturation and instability once the constraint clears. Furthermore, when the console incorporates bilateral teleoperation or force-feedback interfaces, system stability depends directly on the passivity-based energy bounds established in [[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/04 Governing the Machine/14 Supervisory Intervention/V4-14.05 Transfer without Discontinuity#^sec-intervention-haptic-passivity|section 14.4.5]]; ownership tokens alone cannot prevent feedback oscillations across a physical-virtual coupled loop.

Authority ambiguity also corrupts downstream imitation learning. If the record cannot say who held each channel, interventions are mislabeled as autonomous behavior or the reverse, and a behavioral-cloning policy learns to reproduce the very errors that triggered the takeover. A low offline validation loss cannot catch this, because it only shows that the policy memorized the wrong labels. Causal intervention masking, developed in [[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/04 Governing the Machine/14 Supervisory Intervention/V4-14.09 The Authority Record#^sec-intervention-recovery-eligibility|section 14.8.3]], depends on ownership bits that the authority record stamps at commit time.

**War Story 14.1: Ground-contact interlocks on flight 2904**

![[90 Assets/Volume IV/14 Intervention/Ws Flight2904 Authority Lockout ffbe999253.svg]]

*To-scale timing strip over fifteen seconds after the right main gear touches down. The right gear is on the ground from time zero; the left gear strut stays uncompressed until 9 seconds. A crew command bar for brakes and reversers runs from touchdown. Ground spoilers and thrust reversers are locked out until 9 seconds and deployed afterward. Wheel brakes show no braking while the wheels aquaplane until 13 seconds, then braking.*

**Context**: On Lufthansa Flight 2904 at Warsaw, the flight crew executed an approach based on outdated wind reports, encountering an unreported tailwind that caused the aircraft to touch down at high speed far down a waterlogged runway. Upon touchdown, the crew immediately commanded maximum manual braking and deployed thrust reversers, but the aircraft’s automated flight-control logic refused to grant deceleration authority ([[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/04 Governing the Machine/14 Supervisory Intervention/V4-14.13 Source References#^ref-bfu1994warsaw|Main Commission Aircraft Accident Investigation Warsaw and German Federal Bureau of Aircraft Accident Investigation 1994]]).

**Mechanism**: Crosswinds and runway hydroplaning delayed the left gear compression: the right main gear made contact 9 s before the left. The aircraft’s deceleration interlocks were rigid. Ground spoiler deployment required weight-on-wheels shock-strut compression on both main gears **or** wheel spin-up above 72 knots. Thrust reversers required weight compression on both main struts. Mechanical wheel braking was locked out until the main wheels achieved synchronized spin-up. Because hydroplaning prevented wheel spin-up and an asymmetric roll angle kept the left strut uncompressed, the control logic determined the aircraft was still airborne. Consequently, the flight computer locked out spoilers and reversers for 9 s and inhibited wheel brakes for even longer.

**Impact**: Wheel braking was inhibited until 13 s after initial touchdown. With braking authority locked out across the primary deceleration zone, the aircraft could not dissipate its kinetic energy along the remaining wet runway. It overran the runway threshold at 72 knots, struck an airport embankment, and caught fire, resulting in two fatalities and total hull loss.

**Response**: Official accident investigators cited the ground-contact interlock logic as a critical contributing cause alongside crew decision-making and delayed meteorological updates. The crew could not override the spoiler and reverser lockouts. The report identifies those design constraints alongside crew actions and incomplete landing-distance guidance; it does not establish that a manual override alone would have prevented the overrun.

**Systems lesson**: Embodied robots share this exact failure topology whenever automated sensor preconditions, such as docking latch checks, wheel-slip traction observers, or ground-contact switches, gate emergency braking or reverse-torque commands issued by human supervisors. Unmodeled wheel slippage or uneven terrain can violate a sensor predicate precisely when deceleration is urgently required. Any control architecture that gates physical actuation behind automated preconditions must satisfy three rules. First, it must immediately annunciate unsatisfied interlocks across the operator console. Second, it must explicitly identify the unfulfilled sensor predicate. Third, it must provide a deterministic fallback mode for sensor disagreement. Rather than bypassing safety interlocks blindly, the supervisory architecture must define a certified fallback deceleration path that executes based on conservative, physically verifiable stops.

The formal authority arbiter detailed in [[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/04 Governing the Machine/14 Supervisory Intervention/V4-14.04 Authority Transitions#^sec-intervention-authority-transitions|section 14.3]] exists to prevent these failures. Safe embodied control architectures cannot rely on implicit authority handoffs, assumed human vigilance, or ad hoc torque summing on the actuator bus. Evaluating post-incident behavior requires a tamper-evident, channel-by-channel telemetry record captured at every control cycle, with explicit gaps and bounded event timing. The surviving record can help distinguish a safety boundary breach, an invalid token exchange, and electrical noise on a safety bus only to the extent that its independent measurements, capture coverage, and timing bounds separate those events. Its storage path must therefore survive unexpected power interruptions, kernel panics, and real-time scheduling stalls.
