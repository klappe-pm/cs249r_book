# V4-14.02 Shared Authority

[[Home|Home]] · [[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/Volume 4 Overview|Volume Overview]] · [[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/04 Governing the Machine/14 Supervisory Intervention/V4-14 Supervisory Intervention|V4-14 Supervisory Intervention]]

^sec-intervention-shared-authority

When a pallet blocks an aisle and the warehouse mobile manipulator requests a remote assistant to take over, the transfer introduces a delay that no test on the chassis can bound, because it belongs to a person. Part III left the running machine with a stopping budget whose every term belongs to the hardware, each with a declared bound and a loaded test that could refute it, even where the test has not yet run, as for the dispatch-to-admission interval ${\Delta_{\text{da}}}$ that **[[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/03 Running the Machine/13 Workload Placement/V4-13.07 Silicon Isolation Decisions#^sec-placement-dispatch-admission|The board-link budget and the dispatch-to-admission interval]]** allocated but did not measure. Part IV begins where a person enters that loop.

Supervisory intervention transfers the right to propose actions from an autonomous policy to a human operator, and eventually returns that authority to the machine. Nearly every embodied machine learning system operating in the physical world relies on this exchange. Remote teleoperation assistants clear blocked aisles and resolve scenes that warehouse robots and autonomous vehicles cannot interpret on their own. Safety drivers and teach-pendant operators step in whenever an operational policy drifts outside its design envelope, and their corrections protect the hardware and supply demonstration data for later retraining. Engineering block diagrams often reduce this supervision to a single switch or an external arrow pointing into the plant. In the physical world, however, kinetic energy does not pause while a multiplexer toggles inputs. Authority transfer is a control maneuver with its own deadlines, and it must be executed in coordination with the slowest and least predictable element in the loop, the human operator.

How often that exchange happens on a mature fleet is a matter of public record in one jurisdiction. California requires every company testing autonomous vehicles on its public roads to file annual reports of autonomous miles and of the disengagements, transfers of control to a person, that occurred during them ([[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/04 Governing the Machine/14 Supervisory Intervention/V4-14.13 Source References#^ref-cadmv2026disengagement|California Department of Motor Vehicles 2026]]). [[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/04 Governing the Machine/14 Supervisory Intervention/V4-14.02 Shared Authority#^fig-14-disengagement-record|Figure 1]] plots the filings of one long-running fleet, Waymo’s.

Over the decade the safety-driver rate rose from about 1,200 to about 19,200 miles per disengagement, yet the fleet still reported 174 takeovers in its latest year, and the series is far from monotone. Between 2020 and 2021 the rate fell 3.8× while the miles driven grew 3.7×. Driverless testing reports one disengagement every 71,800 miles, 54 in its latest year. Two consequences follow for the machine. Human authority is not a phase a capable system grows out of; even the most experienced fleet exercises it every year, so the transfer mechanism is a permanent part of the design. The reported rate also depends on where the fleet drove and on what its operator chose to count, which is why an intervention count tells a reviewer how often the exchange happened but not, by itself, how well either party performed it.

↪ The two permit series as two stages of permission to operate, and how a machine leaves one stage for the next, are developed in **[[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/04 Governing the Machine/17 Responsible Physical AI/V4-17.05 Permission in Stages#^sec-responsibility-staged-permission|Permission in Stages]]**.

The central engineering problem of supervisory intervention is how authority can change hands on a moving machine without exhausting stopping clearance, letting two controllers drive the same actuator at once, or losing track of who commanded each instant. The fourth law, permission must be earned (principle [[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/04 Governing the Machine/V4 Governing the Machine#^pri-vol4-evidence-bounds-authority|4]]), frames the answer, and here Part IV applies it for the first time to a person. Like an autonomous policy, a human operator receives control authority only while valid evidence supports that grant, and loses it the moment that evidence lapses. Until the platform’s permission path checks it, every command from an operator is an untrusted request that cannot cross the causal boundary. A safe transfer must therefore bind each authenticated role to explicit actuator channels and timing bounds, commit ownership atomically on a control tick, blend reference changes within drivetrain rate limits, and log who held authority at every cycle for incident review and policy retraining.

For the warehouse mobile manipulator, this timing constraint is direct and unforgiving. When the blocked aisle prompts the machine to request a remote assistant, at its chosen 1.3 m/s aisle speed a 4.5 s pause before braking, the design out-of-loop takeover time that [[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/04 Governing the Machine/14 Supervisory Intervention/V4-14.03 Human Authority, Reaction Time, and Clearance#^sec-intervention-reaction-drift|section 14.2.2]] derives from driver take-over studies, carries the machine 5.8 m, more than five times its 1.10 m rack-end clear distance. An operator’s intervention request reaches a chassis that is already in motion under an admitted command; human intent cannot erase physical momentum or make an actuator respond in zero time. The permission path resolves this transition in two stages. The arbiter that selects among the fallback ladder’s rungs also decides, for each channel, which source holds the right to propose: the learned policy, the operator, or, once that authority lapses, a fallback rung. The enforcer then admits or rejects each setpoint from the selected source against physical envelope invariants. Both stages run deterministically on a dedicated safety microcontroller (MCU). Source selection precedes the enforcer’s check, and rung selection follows it whenever the check fails, so human intervention adds a new input to the arbiter, a person, rather than a new component. Throughout this process, the plant must keep enough clearance to bring itself to a safe halt. Switching setpoints in a single step demands a sudden torque jump that strains motor drives, gearboxes, and payload stability. Delaying the transfer, in turn, uses up the remaining stopping margin even when both the learned policy and the human supervisor produce sensible trajectories on their own.

↪ Whether a human role that needs this much time can count as a safety measure at all, and who is blamed when it cannot, is developed in **[[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/04 Governing the Machine/17 Responsible Physical AI/V4-17.04 Oversight a Person Can Perform#^sec-responsibility-meaningful-oversight|Oversight a Person Can Perform]]**.

Because momentum and kinetic energy sit on the physical side of the causal boundary, the machine keeps moving under the last admitted setpoint while the remote operator pieces together what is happening in the aisle. That mental reconstruction is slow. It is the paradox Lisanne Bainbridge called the *ironies of automation* ([[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/04 Governing the Machine/14 Supervisory Intervention/V4-14.13 Source References#^ref-bainbridge1983ironies|Bainbridge 1983]]): engineers automate routine work to remove human error, yet leave human operators to handle the hardest, unmodeled edge cases that the automation cannot solve, where plant dynamics are abnormal and time is shortest. An operator who only monitors a screen also loses both manual feel and a current mental model of how the plant is moving. When an unexpected hazard forces a sudden takeover, that operator must act under tight time pressure with little context. Human vigilance fades quickly when watching for rare faults, and automation complacency makes the problem worse whenever secondary tasks compete for the supervisor’s attention ([[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/04 Governing the Machine/14 Supervisory Intervention/V4-14.13 Source References#^ref-parasuraman2010complacency|Parasuraman and Manzey 2010]]). Decades of human factors research confirm that passive monitoring erodes situation awareness[[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/04 Governing the Machine/14 Supervisory Intervention/V4-14.14 Footnotes#^fn1|¹]] and adds long decision delays when automation fails without warning ([[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/04 Governing the Machine/14 Supervisory Intervention/V4-14.13 Source References#^ref-endsley1995outofloop|Endsley and Kiris 1995]]). The machine must therefore protect its own collision-free stopping envelope throughout the takeover window. Faster networks can trim milliseconds from packet transport, but they cannot shorten the time a person needs to see a hazard, decide what to do, and move a hand control.

↩︎ The causal boundary, across which admitted motion cannot be recalled, is established in **[[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/00 Introduction/01 The Causal Boundary/V4-01.05 Defining the Causal Boundary#^sec-boundary-causal-boundary|Defining the Causal Boundary]]**.

Cutting power to a moving base lets it coast, whereas cutting power to a loaded manipulator arm without a holding brake drops the payload. Each stage of an intervention therefore needs a fallback matched to the machine’s current state: a moving base may need controlled regenerative braking followed by a mechanical brake once stopped, while an arm carrying a tote needs an active, gravity-compensated position hold. Each response depends on the specific sensors and power rails qualified to carry it out. Because local damping, torque inhibition, and full power isolation produce different physical trajectories, the system cannot treat them as interchangeable steps on a generic escalation ladder; a protective input must invoke the validated fallback built for the plant’s current motion state.

↩︎ The fallback ladder, from which the arbiter selects a state-matched protective response on every tick, is established in **[[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/03 Running the Machine/12 Safety Enforcement/V4-12.10 The Fallback Ladder#^sec-enforcement-fallback-ladder|The Fallback Ladder]]**.

Autonomous and manual proposal rights operate under separate, explicit leases. The learned policy proposes trajectories under the action-chunk lease established in **[[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/01 Machine Anatomy/04 The Nervous System/V4-04.05 Chunk Lease Sizing from the Stopping Budget#^sec-nervous-lease-sizing|Chunk Lease Sizing from the Stopping Budget]]**, while a teleoperation session receives a distinct, clearance-budgeted manual lease. Rejecting an invalid replacement command from an operator does not invalidate an active, previously admitted setpoint whose evidence, state bounds, and lease duration remain healthy. If either controller exhausts its lease duration or violates its underlying safety premises, the permission path immediately triggers the appropriate local fallback.

The permission path prevents split-brain command contention by pairing an upstream source arbiter with **exclusive actuator-output ownership**. In the 1 kHz control architecture analyzed here, the right to propose on a set of channels is a single object, the **proposal token**, and the arbiter grants it to one source at a time. An authority commit revokes the learned policy’s proposal token and latches its last approved setpoint; the admission enforcer remains the sole, non-bypassable writer to the physical actuators before, during, and after the transition. Standard bus protocols such as Controller Area Network (CAN) arbitration serialize message frames from multiple transmitters, but bus-level arbitration does not establish control-layer authority. If two nodes broadcast conflicting yet syntactically valid frames to the same motor drive, the actuator chatters between setpoints and loses stability. Gating the output path in firmware and selecting a single source per tick prevents that contention. Across continuous state spaces, the enforcer executes a bumpless transfer, a command handoff without reference step discontinuities ([[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/04 Governing the Machine/14 Supervisory Intervention/V4-14.05 Transfer without Discontinuity#^sec-intervention-bumpless-transfer|section 14.4]]), interpolating between validated endpoints while enforcing command-rate limits and honoring measured plant limits.

Intervention events also directly shape the machine learning lifecycle. If a flight recorder or logging pipeline captures a rescued episode without marking the exact handover instant, imitation algorithms treat the near-failure states that triggered the rescue as if they were intentional expert demonstrations. As **[[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/02 Teaching the Machine/05 Physical Data/V4-05.09 Intervention and Recovery Data#^sec-data-intervention-recovery|Intervention and Recovery Data]]** establishes, the states visited by the learner right before a takeover supply training data for recovery policies, but autonomous commands from a failing interval require review rather than automatic admission as expert targets. Downstream learning algorithms, from Dataset Aggregation (DAgger) ([[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/04 Governing the Machine/14 Supervisory Intervention/V4-14.13 Source References#^ref-ross2011reduction|Ross et al. 2011]]) to modern human-gated policy correction methods ([[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/04 Governing the Machine/14 Supervisory Intervention/V4-14.09 The Authority Record#^sec-intervention-recovery-eligibility|section 14.8.3]]), depend on knowing which controller held authority over each logged timestep. To resolve this attribution, the architecture keeps an authority record (the twelfth record in the platform’s record chain, specified in [[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/04 Governing the Machine/14 Supervisory Intervention/V4-14.09 The Authority Record#^sec-intervention-authority-log|section 14.8]]), which retains the captured evidence preceding and following the takeover, with explicit gaps and timing bounds. Its per-tick entries timestamp every transition against the safety microcontroller’s monotonic clock. An authority commit ends policy ownership, ensuring that autonomous exploration, the enforcer’s transition blend, and verified human recovery remain distinct, auditable intervals.

Every shared-authority design begins by defining which named intervention may act upon which physical channel and establishing the maximum latency the machine can tolerate while awaiting human action. The protocol handshakes, setpoint blending algorithms, and authority records that follow all rest upon this initial grant of authority.
