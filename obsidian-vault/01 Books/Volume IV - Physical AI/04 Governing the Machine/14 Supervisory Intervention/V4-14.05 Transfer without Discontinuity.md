# V4-14.05 Transfer without Discontinuity

[[Home|Home]] · [[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/Volume 4 Overview|Volume Overview]] · [[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/04 Governing the Machine/14 Supervisory Intervention/V4-14 Supervisory Intervention|V4-14 Supervisory Intervention]]

^sec-intervention-bumpless-transfer

At commit time ${t}_0$, the enforcer latches the last admitted policy command ${\mathbf{u}}_0$ and a checked human target ${\mathbf{u}}_1$. Switching them in one permission tick may exceed the channel’s allowed command rate. In classical process control, bumpless transfer between manual and automatic modes is achieved by making the inactive controller track the active one’s output, so its internal state, including its integrator, already agrees at the switch ([[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/04 Governing the Machine/14 Supervisory Intervention/V4-14.13 Source References#^ref-astrom2006advanced|Åström and Hägglund 2006]]). [[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/04 Governing the Machine/14 Supervisory Intervention/V4-14.05 Transfer without Discontinuity#^sec-intervention-integrator-prebiasing|Section 14.4.3]] applies that tracking to the console-side controller that turns operator input into a command. Here **bumpless transfer** also names a second mechanism layered on top, which spreads the remaining command change across a finite window while matching the required endpoint derivatives. The quintic seam bridges of **[[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/03 Running the Machine/11 Trajectory Planning/V4-11.05 Kinodynamic Feasibility#^sec-planning-quintic-splines|Quintic splines and C^2 boundary matching]]** provide the construction; takeover adds a new admission decision because waiting for the operator has already consumed some clearance.

### Command Steps and Mechanical Drivetrain Shock

^sec-intervention-command-steps

A step change in torque or acceleration excites torsional resonances and snaps gearboxes across their backlash, while a slower blend lowers the command rate at the cost of stopping reserve. Because the ${L_{\text{bus}}}\,d{I}/d{t}$ coupling to the DC supply also turns a current step into a voltage sag on the shared rail, the command transition must satisfy the drive’s rate limits while leaving enough clearance for fallback. A smooth profile therefore needs both a rate bound and a check against the remaining physical envelope.

↩︎ The ${L_{\text{bus}}}\,d{I}/d{t}$ coupling through which a current step sags the shared DC bus is established in **[[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/01 Machine Anatomy/02 The Physical Body/V4-02.07 Electrical Power Integrity#^sec-body-power-distribution-impedance|Power distribution impedance, transient droop, and brownout resets]]**.

**Definition 14.2: C² bumpless authority transfer ramp**

***${C}^2$ bumpless authority transfer ramp*** is the enforcer-owned command profile between two checked, latched references ${\mathbf{u}}_0$ and ${\mathbf{u}}_1$. It is parameterized by a monotonic weighting function ${\alpha}({t}) \in [0,1]$ with vanishing first and second boundary derivatives (${\dot{\alpha}} = {\ddot{\alpha}} = 0$) over a finite blending window ${\tau_{\text{blend}}}$. The profile keeps that command and its first two derivatives continuous across the fixed-endpoint handoff. When the command is acceleration, its first derivative is commanded jerk; when it is torque, the first derivative is torque rate.

1.  **Significance**: A setpoint step has an unbounded ideal derivative. A ${C}^2$ command profile removes that discontinuity; measured actuator tracking and plant limits still decide physical feasibility.
2.  **Distinction**: Unlike a linear interpolation ramp whose derivative jumps at both endpoints, a ${C}^2$ quintic spline enforces vanishing first and second derivatives (${\dot{\alpha}} = {\ddot{\alpha}} = 0$) at entry and exit, making the interpolated command and its first two derivatives continuous for stationary endpoint derivatives. The physical meaning of those derivatives depends on the command channel.
3.  **Common pitfall**: Choosing a window shorter than the rate limit allows for a fixed acceleration-command change (the bound [[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/04 Governing the Machine/14 Supervisory Intervention/V4-14.05 Transfer without Discontinuity#^eq-intervention-blend-window|equation 7]] derives below) breaches the stated command-jerk limit even though the profile is smooth. Resonance, slip, and stopping distance require plant tests.

### Sizing the Blend Window and the Stopping-Distance Cost

^sec-intervention-blend-window

Smoothing an acceleration command from cruising speed to emergency braking delays the onset of maximum deceleration, so bumpless transfer consumes clearance. It trades stopping reserve for lower mechanical stress, and sizing the blend window means balancing the actuator’s jerk limit against the clearance that remains.

The enforcer blends with the quintic smoothstep. For normalized progress ${\hat{t}} = ({t} - {t_{\text{start}}}) / {\tau_{\text{blend}}} \in [0, 1]$, the polynomial weighting function is:

$$
{\alpha}({\hat{t}}) = 10{\hat{t}}^3 - 15{\hat{t}}^4 + 6{\hat{t}}^5. \tag{6}
$$

 The polynomial in [[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/04 Governing the Machine/14 Supervisory Intervention/V4-14.05 Transfer without Discontinuity#^eq-intervention-quintic-smoothstep|equation 6]] is the fixed-endpoint result derived in **[[05 Reference/Volume IV - Physical AI/V4 Control and Dynamics#^sec-appendix-control-splines|Smooth Trajectories and Bumpless Transfer]]**. Its derivative ${\alpha'}({\hat{t}}) = 30{\hat{t}}^2(1-{\hat{t}})^2$ peaks at ${\hat{t}} = 1/2$ with value $30/16 = 1.875$, so the profile’s peak slope is $1.875/{\tau_{\text{blend}}}$ and an acceleration change must satisfy:

$$
{\tau_{\text{blend}}} \ge \frac{1.875 \lVert {\Delta} {\mathbf{u}} \rVert}{{j_{\max}}}. \tag{7}
$$

^eq-intervention-blend-window

^eq-intervention-quintic-smoothstep

For a torque command, replace the acceleration difference and jerk ceiling with the torque difference and torque-rate ceiling. The illustrative 15 N·m joint-torque change under a 750 N·m/s ceiling therefore requires 37.5 ms, rounded up to 38 ms on the permission clock. These bounds assume stationary endpoint derivatives; a transfer from a moving ramp must match its boundary state explicitly.[[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/04 Governing the Machine/14 Supervisory Intervention/V4-14.14 Footnotes#^fn6|⁶]]

Increasing ${\tau_{\text{blend}}}$ reduces the profile’s peak commanded jerk, but delays the full human target and can lengthen the stopping trajectory. Consider the remote assistant’s takeover of the mobile manipulator’s base, committed while the machine cruises at the ${v} =$ 0.75 m/s it slowed to before requesting it ([[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/04 Governing the Machine/14 Supervisory Intervention/V4-14.03 Human Authority, Reaction Time, and Clearance#^sec-intervention-clearance-deadlines|section 14.2.4]]). Suppose the assistant commands a stop at the scenario’s braking deceleration, a fixed step of $\lvert {\Delta} {a} \rvert =$ 2 m/s² from the latched cruise command. The enforcer holds the blend to ${j_{\max}} =$ 7.11 m/s³, the jerk bound of the resident jerk-limited stop. That bound is a stipulated property of the loaded base, not of its speed, so the blend window does not depend on the speed at which the takeover commits. Measured load response still requires qualification. The smoothstep bound then sets a minimum blending window of 527.34 ms, longer than the 281.25 ms the resident stop spends ramping its deceleration at the same jerk bound, because the smoothstep reaches its peak slope only at mid-window. The smoothstep weight averages one half over the window, so the base would leave the blend at 0.22 m/s after traveling 316.1 mm. Integrating the smoothstep weight a first time subtracts one half of $\lvert {\Delta} {a} \rvert\,{\tau_{\text{blend}}}$ from the entry speed ${v}$, whereas integrating that speed profile across the window subtracts only one seventh of $\lvert {\Delta} {a} \rvert\,{\tau_{\text{blend}}}^2$ from the unbraked travel ${v}\,{\tau_{\text{blend}}}$ because most of the deceleration occurs in the second half of the ramp. Braking from there at the full target would take another 12.4 mm, for 328.4 mm to zero speed in this partial model. It holds full deceleration until rest, then switches acceleration to zero instantaneously. The value is therefore a lower-bound comparison for the ramp followed by ideal braking, not a complete smooth stopping trajectory. A feasible terminal transition needs its own distance and boundary-state check.

An instantaneous step takeover would stop in 140.6 mm, so even this partial ramp-and-brake model adds 187.8 mm of travel. The partial model is also 82.4 mm longer than the 246.1 mm resident stop from the same speed. For the one-dimensional model starting at zero acceleration and ending at rest with zero acceleration, the resident profile is a minimum-distance stop under the stated deceleration and jerk bounds (**[[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/03 Running the Machine/11 Trajectory Planning/V4-11.06 Behavior at the Seam#^sec-planning-c2-stopping-suffix|The C^2 stopping suffix and jerk-limited braking]]**), so a different complete transition into braking must be checked against that baseline. The partial comparison above lacks the required terminal transition and is not itself a competing complete profile. For a braking command the resident stop is already the bumpless transfer. The base’s blend window therefore governs base command changes that do not stop the machine, such as the first manual command after an automatic stop, where the blend starts from rest and the enforcer still checks the resident stop from every blended state. An idealized step stop is mathematically shorter. Executing an unblended emergency stop, however, risks wheel lockup, dynamic rollover, or load shedding (such as throwing a loaded tote off the manipulator rack), which can increase the actual physical stopping distance beyond the ideal theoretical rigid-body stop. Command smoothing limits the requested rate; physical stability and sufficient clearance still require qualification. The enforcer admits each blended tick only while the resident stop from the resulting state still fits. Where no blend fits, the base receives the resident stop itself, the suffix the fallback ladder’s stop rung executes. Bypassing the blend window by applying the acceleration step within a single 1 ms permission tick would instead request a command jerk of about 2,000 m/s³ over that tick. The idealized step is only a comparison baseline.

### Initial Condition Matching and Integrator Pre-Biasing

^sec-intervention-integrator-prebiasing

Command blending removes the chosen output discontinuity, but it cannot remove one that a controller upstream of the blend produces. On the remote assistant’s side that controller is the console-side tracking loop, the software that turns joystick deflection, or a measured guiding force (the force-guided input that [[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/04 Governing the Machine/14 Supervisory Intervention/V4-14.05 Transfer without Discontinuity#^sec-intervention-impedance-admittance|section 14.4.4]] describes next), into a velocity target for the channel. While the policy still owns the channel, that loop runs against a plant it does not drive, and its integrator accumulates the difference between its target and a motion that never follows. At commit it would emit that accumulated correction as a jump in the manual target, a step the blend window was never sized to absorb. **Initial condition matching** pre-biases that loop against the measured state before commit, so its internal controller state matches the output required at transfer rather than releasing accumulated error. It does not force the deliberate human target to equal the last policy command. The enforcer latches both endpoints, sizes the blend for their actual difference, and checks the resulting command and stopping reserve throughout transfer.[[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/04 Governing the Machine/14 Supervisory Intervention/V4-14.14 Footnotes#^fn7|⁷]]

To verify a transfer, record the command profile, motor current, encoder motion, and chassis inertial response on a shared timebase with bounded alignment error. In the ideal model, a step command has an infinite derivative at the switch, which excites high jerk, unmodeled compliance, and drivetrain ringing in physical hardware. A fixed-endpoint quintic polynomial removes the command discontinuity by zeroing the boundary derivatives, so its command rate stays bounded across the whole window ${\tau_{\text{blend}}}$ instead of concentrating in one impulse ([[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/04 Governing the Machine/14 Supervisory Intervention/V4-14.05 Transfer without Discontinuity#^fig-14-bumpless-transfer-dynamics|figure 6]]). The measured plant response, not the command profile, establishes whether the handover met its physical limits.

^fig-14-bumpless-transfer-dynamics

The two panels distinguish the command’s mathematical continuity from the plant’s measured response. Removing the step bounds the requested rate under the stated endpoint assumptions; it does not establish that the drive tracks the command or that the load remains stable. Those measurements decide whether the selected blend window is acceptable.

The enforcer’s 1 kHz loop gathers the four-phase handshake, integrator pre-biasing, and the ${C}^2$ quintic blend into one protocol on the safety microcontroller. Each tick it samples plant state, stopping reserve, protective inputs, and the active execution lease. It commits an ownership change only on the qualified commit tick that follows a verified acknowledgment, blends only between latched endpoints and checks every blended candidate before dispatch, and falls back on a lapsed lease or a failed clearance check.

↪ **Protocol:** The enforcer’s cadence, memory and write invariants, and per-state execution steps are given in **[[05 Reference/Volume IV - Physical AI/V4 Systems and Hardware#^sec-appendix-systems-authority-protocol|Authority-transfer protocol]]**.

### Mapping Operator Input to Arm Motion

^sec-intervention-impedance-admittance

Once the operator holds the arm channel, commanding a rigid position target into a bin wall or rack upright builds contact force until the enforcer trips, whereas guiding the arm through measured contact force lets the wrist comply. Position streaming supplies kinematic targets directly; force guiding uses measured contact force to generate a motion target through an admittance controller[[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/04 Governing the Machine/14 Supervisory Intervention/V4-14.14 Footnotes#^fn8|⁸]] ([[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/04 Governing the Machine/14 Supervisory Intervention/V4-14.13 Source References#^ref-hogan1985impedance|Hogan 1985]]). Either mapping remains subject to the arm’s qualified limits. The choice also changes the logged learning target, so the record must retain whether a sample is a requested pose, a force measurement, or an admitted command.

### Bilateral Teleoperation and Haptic Feedback Passivity

^sec-intervention-haptic-passivity

A force-reflecting console adds an actuated device at the operator’s hands. Delayed feedback[[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/04 Governing the Machine/14 Supervisory Intervention/V4-14.14 Footnotes#^fn9|⁹]] can destabilize that coupled loop even when authority ownership is correct ([[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/04 Governing the Machine/14 Supervisory Intervention/V4-14.13 Source References#^ref-anderson1989bilateral|Anderson and Spong 1989]]). Such a console therefore needs a separate interaction-energy argument, including its port conventions, controller sampling, delay and loss assumptions, and actuator saturation. The energy-storage condition and wave-variable construction are developed in **[[05 Reference/Volume IV - Physical AI/V4 Control and Dynamics#^sec-appendix-control-passivity|Passivity and Energy-Bounded Interaction]]**; they do not replace the clearance or force limits. The basic warehouse takeover does not require bilateral force reflection. If a site adds it, pre-biasing and ramping the console’s force output must prevent a command step at the operator’s hand within its own measured limits, because the arm’s accepted blend duration does not automatically qualify the console. Whichever input mapping and console a site chooses, the session that carries them must still fit the clearance left after the operator’s response.

### Admitting the Remote-Assistant Session

^sec-intervention-session-admission

Every interval priced so far in this chapter now draws from the same finite stopping reserve when a remote session begins. Admitting the remote assistant requires bounding four timing parameters against the machine’s limits: the operator reaction budget (the takeover latency ${T_{\Sigma}}$ of [[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/04 Governing the Machine/14 Supervisory Intervention/V4-14.03 Human Authority, Reaction Time, and Clearance#^eq-intervention-reaction-floor|equation 1]] for the takeover mode in use), the **handshake protocol latency** ${T_{\text{handshake}}}$, the manual lease ${T_{\text{lease,man}}}$, and the minimum blending window ${\tau_{\text{blend}}}$. [[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/04 Governing the Machine/14 Supervisory Intervention/V4-14.05 Transfer without Discontinuity#^tbl-14-authority-timing-contract|Table 6]] states them for the machine’s two command channels, the base and the arm, which one remote-assistant session commands together.

↪ How many machines one remote assistant can serve before requests wait minutes for a person is developed in **[[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/04 Governing the Machine/17 Responsible Physical AI/V4-17.04 Oversight a Person Can Perform#^sec-responsibility-supervisor-utilization|One person, many machines]]**.

| Machine channel           | Command change and chosen rate ceiling                           | Smoothstep window ${\tau_{\text{blend}}}\ge1.875\lvert{\Delta} {u}\rvert/{r}$, whole ticks | Human / protocol time | Fallback contract                                                  |
|:--------------------------|:-----------------------------------------------------------------|-------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------:|:----------------------|:-------------------------------------------------------------------|
| Base acceleration command | Zero to 2 m/s²; ${r}=$ 7.11 m/s³, the resident stop’s jerk bound |                                                                                     528 ms | 605 ms / 31 ms        | 100 ms manual lease; 500 ms pending ceiling shortened by clearance |
| Arm joint torque command  | 15 N·m; torque rate ${r}=$ 750 N·m/s                             |                                                                                      38 ms | 605 ms / 31 ms        | The same manual lease; its loss invokes the arm’s validated stop   |

Table 6: **Blending windows on the mobile manipulator**: One remote-assistant session commands both channels, and each window is rounded up to whole 1 ms permission ticks. The rate ceiling ${r}$ bounds the derivative of each *command*. The base window is the same at every speed, because the jerk bound belongs to the loaded base. At the in-loop request speed a braking blend does not fit the clearance that remains, so the base receives the resident stop and its window applies only to non-braking changes. Every lease and pending deadline must fit the state-dependent stopping reserve.

^tbl-14-authority-timing-contract

Consider an illustrative remote-assistant in-loop takeover, requested after the mobile manipulator has slowed to 0.75 m/s with the assistant’s `manual_drive` entry. The four parameters take the values already derived. The reaction budget ${T_{\Sigma}}$ is the 605 ms in-loop takeover of [[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/04 Governing the Machine/14 Supervisory Intervention/V4-14.03 Human Authority, Reaction Time, and Clearance#^sec-intervention-reaction-drift|section 14.2.2]], which already includes transport and the 31 ms four-phase exchange of [[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/04 Governing the Machine/14 Supervisory Intervention/V4-14.04 Authority Transitions#^tbl-14-handshake-protocol|table 5]], so the handshake is part of ${T_{\Sigma}}$, not an addition to it. The manual lease, the longest silence tolerated from the assistant before the permission path falls back, is 100 ms, and [[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/04 Governing the Machine/14 Supervisory Intervention/V4-14.05 Transfer without Discontinuity#^tbl-14-authority-timing-contract|table 6]] gives the blending window for each channel.

An in-loop session at 0.75 m/s closes its complete budget by only a few centimeters, and only because the base takes its resident stop rather than a braking blend. A session that begins at the 0.15 m/s crawl or from standstill has far more room, so those are the configurations worth qualifying. [[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/04 Governing the Machine/14 Supervisory Intervention/V4-14.05 Transfer without Discontinuity#^tbl-14-session-budget|Table 7]] charges the in-loop session’s intervals one at a time against the clearance the finished budget leaves at that speed.

| Interval charged at the in-loop request speed                       |         Travel |                                 Clearance left |
|:--------------------------------------------------------------------|---------------:|-----------------------------------------------:|
| None (the finished budget’s spare)                                  |                |                                       563.7 mm |
| In-loop takeover ${T_{\Sigma}}$, handshake included                 |       453.8 mm |                                       110.0 mm |
| Dispatch-to-admission allocation ${\Delta_{\text{da}}}$             |        47.5 mm |                                        62.5 mm |
| Manual lease in place of the chunk lease in ${\tau_{\text{delay}}}$ |          30 mm |                                        32.5 mm |
| Base braking blend (reserve it would use)                           | up to 157.0 mm | Does not fit; the base takes its resident stop |

Table 7: **Clearance budget for an in-loop remote-assistant session**: Each row charges one interval at the 0.75 m/s request speed, in the capture-origin design budget, and the last column carries what remains. The first four rows close the complete budget by a few centimeters; the blend row would overrun it. These are screening values for an allocated, unmeasured ${\Delta_{\text{da}}}$, not a qualified admission result.

^tbl-14-session-budget

Three rows of the table decide that verdict. The remainder after the takeover, the 146.6 ms by which the takeover beats ${T_{\text{deadline,fallback}}}$ ([[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/04 Governing the Machine/14 Supervisory Intervention/V4-14.03 Human Authority, Reaction Time, and Clearance#^sec-intervention-clearance-deadlines|section 14.2.4]]), bounds the transfer itself. Any further delay before a stop could begin while authority is changing hands must fit inside it, and the enforcer checks the resident stop against it at every tick of the transfer. The lease row exists because, once the assistant holds `MAN`, the manual lease replaces the 60 ms chunk lease inside ${\tau_{\text{delay}}}$, so its extra length becomes travel. The row is the upper bound on the lease set out in [[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/04 Governing the Machine/14 Supervisory Intervention/V4-14.04 Authority Transitions#^sec-intervention-arbiter-heartbeats|section 14.3.4]], and the chosen value passes it. The blend row fails. Blending the step to braking from 0.75 m/s grows the travel plus the resident stop from the blended state by up to 157.0 mm, well past the remainder, so in this session the base receives the resident stop and only the arm blends. This conservative per-tick check charges a fresh resident stop starting from zero deceleration at each blended speed rather than the partial model’s 82.4 mm constant-deceleration tail, because any fallback triggered during the blend begins from the standard resident profile. The slow-down was derived with ${\Delta_{\text{da}}}$ charged, so the complete budget closes, but by centimeters, another reason the base should complete its stop before accepting continued manual motion.

At the crawl, after a 4.5 s out-of-loop takeover, 198.4 mm remains before the same charges, and the base’s stop from the crawl is its resident stop, with no braking blend to reserve. The admission allocation and the lease extension cost travel in proportion to speed, so at the crawl they take only a small part of that remainder. Once the assistant holds `MAN`, the live check of [[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/04 Governing the Machine/14 Supervisory Intervention/V4-14.03 Human Authority, Reaction Time, and Clearance#^tbl-14-clock-origins|table 3]] uses the current remaining clearance, the measured speed, and only the unelapsed portions of those intervals; commit does not restore the distance spent waiting or blending. These figures screen the candidates. Qualification must still check the rounded executable profile and the state-matched fallback, including delay travel, over the full transition, against a measured ${\Delta_{\text{da}}}$.

Every command in that session, whether a streamed pose, a force-guided target, or a heartbeat that renews the manual lease, must also come from the authenticated operator that the `manual_drive` entry names. A budget that closes by a few centimeters leaves no room for a forged or replayed packet, which makes identity and packet admission the next constraint.
