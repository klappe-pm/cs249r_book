# V4-14.06 Authenticating an Intervention

[[Home|Home]] · [[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/Volume 4 Overview|Volume Overview]] · [[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/04 Governing the Machine/14 Supervisory Intervention/V4-14 Supervisory Intervention|V4-14 Supervisory Intervention]]

^sec-intervention-authenticating-intervention

On a shared wireless link, a legitimate `veto_path` intervention packet and an unauthorized drive-torque packet can arrive within the very same control cycle. The permission architecture must prevent an adversary or malformed network frame from seizing kinetic authority, yet it must never obstruct an authorized operator from arresting the machine before a collision. Systems engineers reconcile the two demands by classifying intervention operations according to the mechanical work they produce. Stopping the machine by removing power or engaging a mechanical holding brake is not symmetrical with injecting active motion commands, so the two actions take different verification paths. A protective stop brings the machine to a controlled halt along a braking or holding rung of the fallback ladder rather than abruptly cutting actuator torque. Commanding base velocities, wheel torques, or joint trajectories, by contrast, injects fresh mechanical energy into the plant: a forged or corrupted trajectory command can drive a moving mass into an obstacle, whereas an authenticated out-of-band protective input only requests a plant-specific minimum-risk stop.

Authentication adds delay to the remote intervention path, so it is priced against the same deadline as every other latency source, the latest fallback deadline ${T_{\text{deadline,fallback}}}$ of [[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/04 Governing the Machine/14 Supervisory Intervention/V4-14.03 Human Authority, Reaction Time, and Clearance#^eq-intervention-latest-fallback|equation 4]]. At the warehouse aisle velocity of 1.3 m/s, the partial spare permits at most 100.8 ms of added delay before charging the admission latency ${\Delta_{\text{da}}}$, which drops to 37.5 ms once the placement allocation is charged. Transferring published Cortex-M4 cycle counts to the reference microcontroller clock gives an illustrative estimate of 1.8 ms for one Ed25519 signature verification[[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/04 Governing the Machine/14 Supervisory Intervention/V4-14.14 Footnotes#^fn10|¹⁰]] ([[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/04 Governing the Machine/14 Supervisory Intervention/V4-14.13 Source References#^ref-owens2024ed25519|Owens et al. 2024]]), which translates into 2.34 mm of physical travel and leaves 128.7 mm of spare displacement margin. The same cycle-count and clock-rate estimate for an AES-128 CMAC over one command frame is 5.0 μs ([[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/04 Governing the Machine/14 Supervisory Intervention/V4-14.13 Source References#^ref-dworkin2005cmac|Dworkin 2005]]; [[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/04 Governing the Machine/14 Supervisory Intervention/V4-14.13 Source References#^ref-schwabe2016aes|Schwabe and Stoffelen 2017]]). Per packet, both estimates fit within the deadline; loaded measurements on the target must qualify them. The design problem is therefore not choosing an algorithm but meeting five requirements on the admission path that carries the packets:

1.  **Protective stops never wait for verification.** The local hardware fallback timer and independently wired emergency inputs act unconditionally, regardless of whether cryptographic routines have completed or stalled. If a verification routine overruns its deadline, the permission subsystem marks remote assistance as unavailable and cedes control to the autonomous protection layer; it never postpones an emergency deceleration. As analyzed in the verifier-flooding pitfall of [[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/04 Governing the Machine/14 Supervisory Intervention/V4-14.10 Fallacies and Pitfalls#^sec-intervention-fallacies-pitfalls|section 14.9]], what causes a cryptographic verifier to miss real-time deadlines is rarely the algorithmic cost of an individual packet, but rather the ingress volume of hostile or unvetted network traffic saturating the execution queue.
2.  **Takeover requests must be attributable to an individual.** Because a symmetric Message Authentication Code (MAC) relies on a shared key known to both endpoints, a valid tag proves frame integrity but cannot prove which endpoint originated the transmission. An asymmetric digital signature, however, cryptographically binds the takeover request to the teleoperator’s private key, supporting attribution when credential enrollment, key custody, and compromise evidence also identify the operator. The architecture therefore mandates an asymmetric digital signature on the session-initiating `manual_drive` request, while subsequent streaming commands and periodic heartbeats rely on the lower-overhead symmetric MAC.
3.  **Freshness represents a spatial displacement budget, not merely a clock tolerance.** In embodied systems, packet latency translates directly into unmodeled vehicle travel. A replayed or delayed motion packet arriving at the boundary of a 100 ms freshness acceptance window commands an action based on an obsolete vehicle pose. Over that interval the mobile base travels 130 mm, consuming nearly the entire safety margin and leaving only 1.0 mm of the 131.0 mm partial spare. Once the admission latency ${\Delta_{\text{da}}}$ is factored in, such a replay completely overruns the available spare, so the allowable freshness window must shrink below 37.5 ms. The receiver drops counters no newer than the last authenticated, accepted counter before spending processor cycles on cryptography. It advances that stored counter only after authentication and freshness checks pass, so an unauthenticated high counter cannot invalidate later legitimate traffic. Furthermore, if heartbeat packets cease for longer than the lease duration ${T_{\text{lease,man}}}$, the controller immediately revokes manual authority and engages the certified local fallback ladder.
4.  **Malformed traffic never alters control authority.** Any packet with a corrupted checksum, an out-of-order sequence counter, or an invalid authentication tag is immediately rejected and recorded according to Rule A4 of [[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/04 Governing the Machine/14 Supervisory Intervention/V4-14.09 The Authority Record#^sec-intervention-authority-log|section 14.8]]. A corrupted packet neither modifies existing actuator channel assignments nor triggers a spurious emergency stop. Rejecting an isolated invalid packet preserves still-valid authority; interference or dropouts that prevent timely renewal still expire the lease and invoke the qualified fallback.
5.  **Every actuator-write path must be accounted for.** All side channels capable of driving physical hardware, including diagnostic ports, hardware bootloaders, debug interfaces, and direct fieldbus wiring, must be gated by the hardware permission authority or guarded by dedicated interlocks. The intervention threat model explicitly accounts for unauthorized remote packets injected across wireless links, replay attacks traversing internal communication buses, and rogue commands originating from a compromised high-level application processor. Physical tampering, by contrast, enters the safety assurance case only when explicitly mandated by the operational threat model.

These authentication and transport latencies directly impact policy learning and sim-to-real transfer. An embodied simulator that provides instantaneous human takeover underprices the danger of visiting marginal states. If an autonomous policy learns that intervention occurs instantaneously, it will tolerate trajectories that are physically unrecoverable once realistic communication, cryptographic verification, and actuator fallback latencies are introduced. Training and hardware-in-the-loop evaluation pipelines must therefore inject these bounded authentication, transport, and mechanical reaction delays.

Verifying an incoming packet authenticates its credential; the capability manifest separately determines its privileges. Cryptographic validity alone cannot guarantee dynamic stability during control transfer. When authority shifts while the chassis carries linear momentum and the manipulator actuators exert active joint torques, the permission path must commit one selected source per channel and announce that ownership to the operator. Local mutual exclusion and human mode awareness are separate requirements; a valid packet establishes neither by itself.
