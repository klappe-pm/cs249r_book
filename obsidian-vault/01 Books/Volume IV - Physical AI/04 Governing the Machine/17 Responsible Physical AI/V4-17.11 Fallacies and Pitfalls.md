# V4-17.11 Fallacies and Pitfalls

[[Home|Home]] · [[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/Volume 4 Overview|Volume Overview]] · [[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/04 Governing the Machine/17 Responsible Physical AI/V4-17 Responsible Physical AI|V4-17 Responsible Physical AI]]

^sec-responsibility-fallacies-pitfalls

Responsibility for a machine fails in predictable ways, and most of them come from treating it as something settled after an incident rather than designed before one. Each misconception below assumes that a record, a person, or an average carries a responsibility that no one has actually accepted.

**Fallacy**: *A complete runtime log makes someone answerable for what the machine did.*

The logs of the Cruise vehicle were complete enough for engineers to rebuild the pullover in detail, and still the question of who had decided the vehicle should pull over, and who decided what regulators would hear, took months and an outside investigation to answer. A log records events. The decisions that made an event possible (the operating envelope, the response rule, the staffing ratio, the permit application) were made earlier, by people who appear in no tick. Six such links, each retrievable with probability 0.90, form a chain an investigator can follow to the end only 53 percent of the time.

**Reconstruction is not attribution: only a record of decisions and their owners says who answers for an admitted action.**

**Pitfall**: *Naming a person as the countermeasure for a hazard faster than a person can act.*

A design that lists the remote assistant as the response to a person stepping out at a rack end asks for a takeover that needs about 4.5 s when the hazard leaves 0.85 s. The person in that role adds no protection and absorbs the blame for an outcome the design controlled. Utilization makes it worse: at 20 machines per assistant, a request waits 180 s on average before anyone looks at it.

**Architect’s countermeasure:** Test every human role the safety argument cites for time, information, and authority, mark it `FEASIBLE`, `INFEASIBLE`, or `UNKNOWN`, and let only feasible roles count as countermeasures.

**Fallacy**: *One detection rate for “people” describes how safe the machine is for everyone.*

A pooled rate is dominated by the largest group. At 200 people, a smaller group’s exact interval is about 8× wider than the rest of the set’s, too wide to resolve a gap of three points in detection. A group detected two frames later at the aisle speed loses 43 mm of clearance and is struck at 0.42 m/s.

**Detection must be reported per group, with a sample large enough for its interval to resolve the gap that matters, and converted into meters and joules.**

**Pitfall**: *Justifying a deployment for everyone exposed with a favorable fleet-wide comparison.*

An aggregate injury rate can fall while risk concentrates on the people who never chose the machine. When 80 percent of encounters happen at crossings used by 10 percent of workers, those workers carry 8× the average exposure, and the site-wide rate does not show it.

**Architect’s countermeasure:** Report exposure per group and per location, record whether each group chose it, and treat routing and operating-domain choices as decisions about who bears the risk.

**Fallacy**: *Keeping every recording is the safe choice, because an investigation might need it.*

Continuous recording keeps every frame of every worker for as long as the storage lasts. Event-triggered retention keeps the windows an investigation actually uses, about 1 percent of the day, and lets the rest roll off within seconds. Records kept without a declared purpose and period drift into uses no one approved, such as ranking operators by their takeovers.

**Each record about people is kept only for a declared purpose and period, and read only by the readers that purpose names.**

**Pitfall**: *Carrying a permission stage across a configuration change.*

A stage authorizes a configuration in a domain under reporting duties. A new policy, a new enforcer build, or an expanded operating domain is a different configuration, and the failure-free hours that supported the old stage, about 2,996 for the claim in [[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/04 Governing the Machine/17 Responsible Physical AI/V4-17.05 Permission in Stages#^sec-responsibility-stage-exit|section 17.4.1]], do not transfer to it. A withheld or incomplete incident report breaks the stage as surely as the motion does.

**Architect’s countermeasure:** Bind each stage to a configuration digest in the permit block of the approval record, so an update reopens the stage along with the release claims it touches.

**Fallacy**: *Automation removes the work it automates.*

A fleet of 20 machines with one remote assistant creates about 17.3 person-hours of rescue, sweeping, and monitoring every operating day, and warehouse robotics has been associated with more minor injuries even as severe ones fell. The work moves to fewer, different people, often under a faster pace.

**A deployment accounts for the labor it moves and bars the use of its safety records to measure the people who do it.**
