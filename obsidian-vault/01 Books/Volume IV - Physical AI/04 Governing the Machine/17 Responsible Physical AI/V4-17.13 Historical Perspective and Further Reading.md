# V4-17.13 Historical Perspective and Further Reading

[[Home|Home]] · [[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/Volume 4 Overview|Volume Overview]] · [[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/04 Governing the Machine/17 Responsible Physical AI/V4-17 Responsible Physical AI|V4-17 Responsible Physical AI]]

^sec-responsibility-history

The question of who answers for an outcome that many people produced is older than computing. Thompson ([[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/04 Governing the Machine/17 Responsible Physical AI/V4-17.14 Source References#^ref-thompson1980manyhands|1980]]) named the problem of many hands in public administration, where officials who each contribute a small part to a harmful policy can each disclaim it. Nissenbaum ([[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/04 Governing the Machine/17 Responsible Physical AI/V4-17.14 Source References#^ref-nissenbaum1996accountability|1996]]) carried the problem into computing and identified the barriers specific to software: the many hands that build it, the treatment of failures as unavoidable bugs, the use of the computer as a scapegoat, and the ownership of software without liability for it. Matthias ([[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/04 Governing the Machine/17 Responsible Physical AI/V4-17.14 Source References#^ref-matthias2004responsibility|2004]]) argued that machines which learn make the gap wider, because their behavior is not fully determined by any rule their makers wrote. The field’s founding case remains Therac-25, the radiation therapy machine whose software removed the hardware interlocks that had protected patients ([[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/04 Governing the Machine/17 Responsible Physical AI/V4-17.14 Source References#^ref-leveson1993investigating|Leveson and Turner 1993]]).

A second line of work asked when a person placed in the loop actually controls the outcome. Sheridan ([[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/04 Governing the Machine/17 Responsible Physical AI/V4-17.14 Source References#^ref-sheridan1992telerobotics|1992]]) laid out supervisory control, in which a person sets goals and monitors while the machine acts, and Bainbridge ([[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/04 Governing the Machine/17 Responsible Physical AI/V4-17.14 Source References#^ref-bainbridge1983ironies|1983]]) showed why the person left to handle only the rare emergency is the one least prepared to handle it. Santoni de Sio and Hoven ([[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/04 Governing the Machine/17 Responsible Physical AI/V4-17.14 Source References#^ref-santonidesio2018meaningful|2018]]) turned the debate over autonomous weapons into general conditions for meaningful human control, and Elish ([[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/04 Governing the Machine/17 Responsible Physical AI/V4-17.14 Source References#^ref-elish2019moral|2019]]) named the moral crumple zone after tracing how blame was assigned in accidents from aviation to nuclear power.

A third line measured who the machine serves well. Buolamwini and Gebru ([[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/04 Governing the Machine/17 Responsible Physical AI/V4-17.14 Source References#^ref-buolamwini2018gender|2018]]) showed that the benchmarks used to evaluate face analysis left out the people the systems failed on, Wilson et al. ([[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/04 Governing the Machine/17 Responsible Physical AI/V4-17.14 Source References#^ref-wilson2019predictive|2019]]) and Li et al. ([[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/04 Governing the Machine/17 Responsible Physical AI/V4-17.14 Source References#^ref-li2025biaswheel|2025]]) found the same pattern in pedestrian detection. Nissenbaum ([[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/04 Governing the Machine/17 Responsible Physical AI/V4-17.14 Source References#^ref-nissenbaum2009privacy|2009]]) recast privacy as the question of whether information flows in the way its context expects, which is the question a declaration about recorded people answers. Regulation followed the same arc, from the General Data Protection Regulation ([[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/04 Governing the Machine/17 Responsible Physical AI/V4-17.14 Source References#^ref-gdpr2016|European Parliament and Council of the European Union 2016]]) to the European Union Artificial Intelligence Act ([[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/04 Governing the Machine/17 Responsible Physical AI/V4-17.14 Source References#^ref-eu2024aiact|European Parliament and Council of the European Union 2024]]) and the state and federal reporting duties for driverless vehicles ([[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/04 Governing the Machine/17 Responsible Physical AI/V4-17.14 Source References#^ref-cadmv2018testingregs|California Department of Motor Vehicles 2018a]]; [[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/04 Governing the Machine/17 Responsible Physical AI/V4-17.14 Source References#^ref-nhtsa2021sgo|National Highway Traffic Safety Administration 2021]]).

Six works reward careful study:

- Thompson ([[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/04 Governing the Machine/17 Responsible Physical AI/V4-17.14 Source References#^ref-thompson1980manyhands|1980]]) defines the problem of many hands and the forms of responsibility that survive it, the starting point for recording decisions with their owners.
- Nissenbaum ([[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/04 Governing the Machine/17 Responsible Physical AI/V4-17.14 Source References#^ref-nissenbaum1996accountability|1996]]) names the four barriers to answering for computer systems, each of which reappears in a physical machine’s chain of decisions.
- Elish ([[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/04 Governing the Machine/17 Responsible Physical AI/V4-17.14 Source References#^ref-elish2019moral|2019]]) introduces the moral crumple zone and shows, case by case, how blame settles on the person nearest the event.
- Santoni de Sio and Hoven ([[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/04 Governing the Machine/17 Responsible Physical AI/V4-17.14 Source References#^ref-santonidesio2018meaningful|2018]]) states the conditions under which human control of an autonomous system is real rather than nominal.
- Wilson et al. ([[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/04 Governing the Machine/17 Responsible Physical AI/V4-17.14 Source References#^ref-wilson2019predictive|2019]]) measures how pedestrian detection differs across skin types, the evidence behind measuring detection per group.
- Nissenbaum ([[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/04 Governing the Machine/17 Responsible Physical AI/V4-17.14 Source References#^ref-nissenbaum2009privacy|2009]]) explains privacy as appropriate flow, the frame for declaring what each record about people may be used for.

**What’s Next: From responsibility to engineering judgment**

What do the release verdict and its approval record say about the question this book opened with? On the book’s own records, the warehouse mobile manipulator is refused. The stipulated branch earns it only a restricted mission, and the approval record adds entries that cannot close before release: a per-group detection sample that is still filling, a staffing plan that depends on a hire, and a crossing rule that no sensor checks. **[[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/04 Governing the Machine/18 Engineering Physical AI/V4-18 Engineering Physical AI#^sec-frontier|Engineering Physical AI]]** reads this verdict as the answer to why a better prediction can still produce a worse physical action. It transfers the same four-law method to an unfamiliar machine. It also gives each premise left open, whether physical or human, an owner, a restriction, and a closure test in the residual-claims register.
