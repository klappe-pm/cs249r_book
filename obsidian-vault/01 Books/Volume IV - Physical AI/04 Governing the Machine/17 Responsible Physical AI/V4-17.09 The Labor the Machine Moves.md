# V4-17.09 The Labor the Machine Moves

[[Home|Home]] · [[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/Volume 4 Overview|Volume Overview]] · [[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/04 Governing the Machine/17 Responsible Physical AI/V4-17 Responsible Physical AI|V4-17 Responsible Physical AI]]

^sec-responsibility-worker-burden

Automating a task does not remove the labor the task needs. It moves that labor to the people who take over a machine when its planner stalls, the technicians who sweep and inspect the corridors the machine’s restrictions depend on, the crews who keep the floor in the state the release assumed, and the operators who record demonstrations for the next policy. The intervention frequency logged during release testing defines the human work the deployment creates: how often a technician is dispatched, how fast that person must arrive, and where on the floor that person must be stationed.

↩︎ The authority record that logs each takeover, its actor, and its timing is established in **[[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/04 Governing the Machine/14 Supervisory Intervention/V4-14.09 The Authority Record#^sec-intervention-authority-log|The Authority Record]]**.

**Napkin Math 17.3: Where the tote's labor went**

**Problem**: The site of notebook [17.1](https://mlsysbook.ai/vol4/17_responsibility/17_responsibility.html#nbk-responsibility-supervisor-load) runs 20 machines for 16 hours a day with one remote assistant, and sweeps the restricted corridors 4 times a day. How many person-hours of work does the fleet create each day?

**Variables**:

- Assistant utilization: 50 percent (Derived, notebook [17.1](https://mlsysbook.ai/vol4/17_responsibility/17_responsibility.html#nbk-responsibility-supervisor-load)); operating day: 16 hours (Illustrative).
- Corridor sweeps: 4 per day of 20 min each (Illustrative).

**Math**: The assistant must be staffed for all 16 h of operation, of which 8 h go to requests and 8 h to watching and waiting. Sweeps add 1.3 h. The fleet creates 17.3 person-hours of work a day, about 2.2 full-time shifts.

**Result**: The fleet needs more than two people’s working days of human labor every day it runs, before counting maintenance, charging, or the collection of new demonstrations.

**Systems insight**: The waiting hours are labor too. A person paid to watch a console for half of every shift, ready to act within seconds, does a job that monitoring research has long shown people perform poorly ([[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/04 Governing the Machine/17 Responsible Physical AI/V4-17.14 Source References#^ref-bainbridge1983ironies|Bainbridge 1983]]), and the design that creates the job owns its conditions.

The burden is not only hours. Across United States local labor markets, greater exposure to industrial robots between 1990 and 2007 lowered both employment and wages ([[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/04 Governing the Machine/17 Responsible Physical AI/V4-17.14 Source References#^ref-acemoglu2020robots|Acemoglu and Restrepo 2020]]). Inside warehouses, the effect on safety cuts both ways. Burtch, Greenwood, and Ravindran found warehouse robotics associated with a 40 percent decrease in severe injuries and a 77 percent increase in non-severe ones, an increase they attribute at least partly to the faster pace of work at robotic facilities ([[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/04 Governing the Machine/17 Responsible Physical AI/V4-17.14 Source References#^ref-burtch2025warehouse|Burtch et al. 2025]]). Across United States commuting zones, Gihleb and colleagues found that greater exposure to industrial robots lowered work-related injury rates while raising other harms, including drug- and alcohol-related deaths ([[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/04 Governing the Machine/17 Responsible Physical AI/V4-17.14 Source References#^ref-gihleb2022robots|Gihleb et al. 2022]]). A deployment can make the most dangerous injuries rarer and the work around the machine harder at the same time. Whether mobile manipulators in logistics follow either pattern has not yet been measured at scale. The task, takeover, and technician-hour records built in the book can inform that measurement; estimating causal effects on workers also requires comparison sites and labor-market data that no single deployment holds.

The labor block of the approval record therefore lists the work a deployment moves: takeover hours, technician dispatches, sweep hours, staffed monitoring hours, and the pace targets set for workers who share the floor. It also states the uses of worker data that the deployment bars, so that the labor the machine creates is not measured with records collected for safety. A site that cannot fill these fields does not know what its machine costs the people who work beside it.
