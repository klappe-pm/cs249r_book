# V4-17.07 Who Bears the Risk

[[Home|Home]] · [[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/Volume 4 Overview|Volume Overview]] · [[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/04 Governing the Machine/17 Responsible Physical AI/V4-17 Responsible Physical AI|V4-17 Responsible Physical AI]]

^sec-responsibility-who-bears-risk

The people a machine puts at risk are not only the people who chose it. The facility operator chose to buy the machine and the integrator chose how to configure it; a rider chooses to take a robotaxi. The pedestrian in the crosswalk, the coworker at an aisle crossing, and the visitor walking through a loading area made no such choice. An aggregate comparison, such as a fleet’s injury rate against a human benchmark, can improve while the remaining risk concentrates on people who never agreed to carry it. The risk such people carry without having chosen it is **unconsented risk**, and it has to be counted separately because no aggregate figure reveals it.

↩︎ Fleet crash rates reported outcome by outcome, each with its own interval, are established in **[[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/04 Governing the Machine/16 Deployment Release/V4-16.10 Maintaining Release Through Updates#^sec-release-fleet-exposure-liability|Fleet evidence and accountable release]]**.

Choices that look purely operational move that risk around. The site crossing rule, which lets people enter the aisle only at marked points, buys the machine its speed by asking pedestrians to wait and to comply. Suppose 80 percent of the encounters between machines and people happen at three crossings used by 10 percent of the workforce. Those workers then carry 8× the site’s average exposure per person, while everyone else carries 22 percent of it, and the site-wide encounter rate looks the same either way. Routing, the boundaries of the operating domain, and the hours a machine runs all shift exposure among groups in the same way.

↩︎ The site crossing rule, and the access restriction it places on pedestrians, are established in **[[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/04 Governing the Machine/16 Deployment Release/V4-16.05 The Three Verdicts#^sec-release-verdicts-ssm|Restricted operation and access boundaries]]**.

The requirement that follows is a reporting one. The exposure block of the approval record lists each group exposed to the machine, whether its members chose that exposure, how their exposure is measured (encounters per person-hour, near misses, close passes), and the routing and domain decisions that changed it. A group that bears risk it did not choose is owed two things a fleet-wide statistic cannot give it: a measurement of its own exposure, and a way to see the machine coming.

### Legible Intent

^sec-responsibility-legible-intent

People protect themselves from a moving machine by predicting where it will go and getting out of the way. Yet a machine that follows an efficient path until the last moment leaves nearby coworkers unable to tell whether it will continue straight or turn across their path. Dragan, Lee, and Srinivasa distinguish two properties of robot motion: motion is predictable when it matches what an observer who knows the goal expects, and **legible** when it lets an observer who does not know the goal infer it quickly ([[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/04 Governing the Machine/17 Responsible Physical AI/V4-17.14 Source References#^ref-dragan2013legibility|Dragan et al. 2013]]). A mobile base that drifts toward the side of the aisle it will turn into, or an arm that moves toward the shelf it will reach before it reaches, is legible. A machine that moves along the efficient path until the last moment is predictable only to someone who already knows its plan.

Explicit signals add to legible motion. A light that projects the planned path on the floor, a sound before a reversing move, or a display that shows which way the machine will turn at a crossing lets a coworker begin to yield earlier. A person who yields earlier approaches the machine more slowly, which shrinks the approach-speed term of the separation the enforcer must keep. Signals help only if people’s trust follows what the machine can actually do. Lee and See showed that people rely on automation in proportion to their trust in it. Trust miscalibrated in either direction causes harm: too much leads to people stepping in front of a machine that will not stop for them, too little to people ignoring a machine that would ([[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/04 Governing the Machine/17 Responsible Physical AI/V4-17.14 Source References#^ref-lee2004trust|Lee and See 2004]]). A signal that the machine slows for people should therefore be shown only where the machine’s detection of people has been measured, group by group, to support it. Physical motion in the aisle is not the only burden the machine places on the people around it; the cameras and logs that verify that motion also record everyone who shares the floor.

↩︎ The human separation distance, whose approach-speed term a person who yields earlier reduces, is established in **[[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/03 Running the Machine/12 Safety Enforcement/V4-12.04 Stopping Envelopes#^sec-enforcement-stopping-envelopes|Stopping Envelopes]]**.
