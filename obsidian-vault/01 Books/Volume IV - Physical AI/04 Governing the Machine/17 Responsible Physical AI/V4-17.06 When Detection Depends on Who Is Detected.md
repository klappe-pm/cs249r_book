# V4-17.06 When Detection Depends on Who Is Detected

[[Home|Home]] · [[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/Volume 4 Overview|Volume Overview]] · [[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/04 Governing the Machine/17 Responsible Physical AI/V4-17 Responsible Physical AI|V4-17 Responsible Physical AI]]

^sec-responsibility-perception-disparity

A machine that earns open-floor permission on aggregate obstacle-detection metrics can still fail systematically on specific groups of people sharing the aisle or roadway. Buolamwini and Gebru found that commercial face-analysis systems misclassified darker-skinned women far more often than lighter-skinned men, and that the benchmarks used to evaluate them contained few of the people they failed on ([[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/04 Governing the Machine/17 Responsible Physical AI/V4-17.14 Source References#^ref-buolamwini2018gender|Buolamwini and Gebru 2018]]). Pedestrian detection shows the same pattern. Wilson, Hoffman, and Morgenstern annotated pedestrians in a driving dataset by skin type and found that standard detectors reached an average precision of 67.1 percent on lighter-skinned pedestrians and 55.9 percent on darker-skinned ones under a strict overlap criterion. Time of day and occlusion did not explain the gap ([[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/04 Governing the Machine/17 Responsible Physical AI/V4-17.14 Source References#^ref-wilson2019predictive|Wilson et al. 2019]]). Testing widely used pedestrian detectors, Li and colleagues found the proportion of children left undetected 20.14 percent higher than that of adults, and significant gender bias at night ([[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/04 Governing the Machine/17 Responsible Physical AI/V4-17.14 Source References#^ref-li2025biaswheel|Li et al. 2025]]).

A warehouse has its own groups. A detector trained mostly on people walking upright may see a kneeling worker, a person carrying a carton that hides the torso, a wheelchair user, or a worker without a high-visibility vest later or less often. Where the machine relies only on its certified safety scanner, which stops for any object above its resolution without deciding what it is, these differences do not matter. They matter wherever the design relies on a learned detector to recognize a person: to slow down when a person is near, to keep a wider separation from people than from pallets, or to predict where a person will walk. In those places, a difference in detection becomes a difference in how close the machine comes to each group before it begins to brake.

### From a Detection Gap to a Stopping Gap

^sec-responsibility-range-to-stop

When a detector sees one group of people less reliably than another, the machine travels farther before braking begins and can strike a person in the less-detected group even when the stop was sized to clear the best-detected group. The conversion from a detection rate to a stopping shortfall runs through the camera’s frames. Suppose a design requires that the chance of missing a person in every frame of a window be no more than ${p_{\text{miss}}}$, and that a detector reports a person in view in each frame with probability ${p_{\text{frame}}}$, independently from frame to frame. Because independent misses multiply across frames so the probability of missing the person in all ${N_{\text{frames}}}$ frames is $(1-{p_{\text{frame}}})^{{N_{\text{frames}}}} \le {p_{\text{miss}}}$, the number of consecutive frames the person must be in view before the design may count on detection is

$$
{N_{\text{frames}}} = \left\lceil \frac{\ln {p_{\text{miss}}}}{\ln\left(1-{p_{\text{frame}}}\right)} \right\rceil. \tag{3}
$$

 A group the detector sees less often needs more frames, and each extra frame is travel before the machine can count on knowing the person is there. If the stop was sized for the best-detected group, a group that needs ${\Delta}{N_{\text{frames}}}$ more frames, at camera frame period ${T_{\text{sample}}}$ and speed ${v}$, loses a distance

$$
{\Delta d_{\text{detect}}} = {v}\,{T_{\text{sample}}}\,{\Delta}{N_{\text{frames}}} \tag{4}
$$

 before braking begins. A stop that would have ended just short of a person in the reference group now ends at the person, and the machine is still moving when it gets there. Because the reference braking distance ${v}^2/(2\,{a_{\text{brake}}})$ was sized to shed all of the initial squared speed ${v}^2$ just as the machine reaches the person, losing a distance ${\Delta d_{\text{detect}}}$ before braking begins leaves that final ${\Delta d_{\text{detect}}}$ of braking uncompleted. While the shortfall is smaller than the braking distance, braking at ${a_{\text{brake}}}$ over the remaining distance therefore leaves an impact speed and an impact energy of

$$
{v_{\text{impact}}} = \sqrt{2\,{a_{\text{brake}}}\,{\Delta d_{\text{detect}}}}, \qquad {E_k} = \tfrac{1}{2}{m}{v_{\text{impact}}}^2 = {m}\,{a_{\text{brake}}}\,{\Delta d_{\text{detect}}}. \tag{5}
$$

 The last form says that the energy delivered to the person grows in direct proportion to the distance lost to late detection. Fairness for a moving machine is therefore measured in meters and joules, not only in percentage points of recall.

**Napkin Math 17.2: Two frames late**

**Problem**: The running machine slows near people only after its learned detector reports a person. Its stop is sized for a group the detector sees in 95 percent of frames. A second group, kneeling workers, is seen in 75 percent of frames. At the aisle speed, what impact speed and energy does the second group receive from a stop that would have just cleared the first?

**Variables**:

- ${p_{\text{miss}}} =$ $10^{-3}$ (Chosen); ${p_{\text{frame}}} =$ 0.95 and 0.75 (Illustrative).
- ${T_{\text{sample}}} =$ 16.7 ms (Datasheet, navigation camera); ${v} =$ 1.3 m/s (Chosen); ${a_{\text{brake}}} =$ 2 m/s² (Illustrative); ${m} =$ 368 kg (Derived, loaded).

**Math**: By [[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/04 Governing the Machine/17 Responsible Physical AI/V4-17.06 When Detection Depends on Who Is Detected#^eq-responsibility-detection-frames|equation 3]], the first group needs 3 frames and the second 5, so ${\Delta}{N_{\text{frames}}} =$ 2, or 33.3 ms. By [[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/04 Governing the Machine/17 Responsible Physical AI/V4-17.06 When Detection Depends on Who Is Detected#^eq-responsibility-range-shortfall|equation 4]], ${\Delta d_{\text{detect}}} =$ 43 mm, well inside the 0.42 m braking distance. By [[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/04 Governing the Machine/17 Responsible Physical AI/V4-17.06 When Detection Depends on Who Is Detected#^eq-responsibility-impact|equation 5]], ${v_{\text{impact}}} =$ 0.42 m/s and ${E_k} =$ 32 J.

**Result**: A stop designed to end short of a standing worker ends in contact with a kneeling one at 0.42 m/s.

**Systems insight**: A few centimeters of late detection become a collision because the stop was sized with no margin for the slower group. The result also assumes the detector’s misses are independent from frame to frame; a detector that systematically fails on a pose misses it in many frames in a row, and the shortfall grows well beyond this best case.

[[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/04 Governing the Machine/17 Responsible Physical AI/V4-17.06 When Detection Depends on Who Is Detected#^tbl-17-detection-shortfall|Table 1]] repeats the calculation for three lower-detection groups. The relationship is steep at the low end: a group seen in 60 percent of frames needs 5 extra frames and receives a 0.66 m/s impact carrying 80 J. [[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/04 Governing the Machine/17 Responsible Physical AI/V4-17.06 When Detection Depends on Who Is Detected#^fig-17-detection-shortfall|Figure 2]] plots the speed of the machine against distance for the reference group and for that group.

### Sample Sizes for Each Group

^sec-responsibility-subgroup-intervals

A detection gap can be managed only if it can be measured, and measuring it per group takes more data than measuring it overall. Suppose an evaluation set holds 10,000 people, of whom 2 percent belong to a smaller group, 200 people, and the detector finds 0.95 of both groups. The exact 95 percent interval for the larger group runs from 0.945 to 0.954. For the smaller group it runs from 0.910 to 0.976, about 8× wider, too wide to tell a detection rate of 0.95 from one of 0.92. A pooled detection rate reported for “pedestrians” or “workers” hides exactly the group the machine serves worst.

↩︎ The exact binomial bound, and why a few observed failures drop it sharply, are established in **[[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/02 Teaching the Machine/07 Closed-Loop Evaluation/V4-07.07 Confidence from a Finite Campaign#^sec-evaluation-bounds-with-failures|Bounds when failures occur]]**.

Telling a detection rate of 0.95 from one of 0.92 with 80 percent power takes about 1,059 people in each group, about 11 percent of the whole evaluation set for every group the evaluation must resolve. A group that makes up a few percent of the people a site employs will not reach that count by chance; the evaluation has to recruit it deliberately. The same arithmetic sets how long field monitoring must run before a per-group detection rate can be trusted, and it is why the sample size for each group belongs in the plan before data collection begins, not in the report after it.

### Where the Measurement Lives

^sec-responsibility-measurement-home

If per-group detection rates live only in a research benchmark report, the release gate cannot check them before admitting the machine to an open floor. Tracking them does not need a new pipeline; it needs a new dimension in records the book already keeps. The observation contract states its detection qualification for each group the machine must slow down for. The fault list adds a coverage dimension that seeds kneeling, carrying, and seated people as deliberately as it seeds stale frames. The evaluation record reports detection per group with its confidence interval rather than only in aggregate. From those qualified measurements, the exposure block of the approval record stores, for each group the machine relies on detecting, the sample size, the rate, its interval, and the distance shortfall it implies at the operating speed. A group with no entry is an unknown premise, and the release gate treats it as one.

↩︎ The observation contract, which states what each measurement may be relied on for, is established in **[[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/03 Running the Machine/08 Sensor Perception/V4-08.09 The Observation Contract#^sec-perception-observation-contract|The Observation Contract]]**.

**Checkpoint 17.2: Detection gaps and sample sizes**

**Conversion**: A detector reaches a person kneeling between racks two frames later than a standing person. Can you compute, at the running machine’s aisle speed, loaded mass, and credible deceleration, the impact speed and energy if the stop was sized for standing workers?

**Remedy**: Can you compare three remedies (a lower speed near racks, a sensor that does not depend on the person’s pose, and a zone rule that keeps the machine out while people kneel there) by what each costs and what evidence each needs?

**Sample**: Can you size an evaluation that distinguishes 0.95 from 0.92 detection at 80 percent power, and say what share of a 10,000-person test set each group must be?
