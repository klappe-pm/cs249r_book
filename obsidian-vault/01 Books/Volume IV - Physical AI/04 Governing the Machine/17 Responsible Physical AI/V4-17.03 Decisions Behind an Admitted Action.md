# V4-17.03 Decisions Behind an Admitted Action

[[Home|Home]] · [[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/Volume 4 Overview|Volume Overview]] · [[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/04 Governing the Machine/17 Responsible Physical AI/V4-17 Responsible Physical AI|V4-17 Responsible Physical AI]]

^sec-responsibility-many-hands

Consider one command the enforcer admitted in the aisle: a base velocity of 1.3 m/s toward a rack end. The enforcement record shows that it passed the stopping check at that tick, under the thresholds in force, yet behind that single log line stand at least six decisions made by different people at different times. Four of them leave traces in the engineering records built in earlier chapters. The learning team trained and approved the policy weights that proposed the command. The safety engineering team built and signed the enforcer and its thresholds. The intervention design and the remote assistant on shift set the authority state at that tick. The lead systems adjudicator signed the operating envelope into the release manifest, including the aisle speed and the floor-friction premise beneath it. The other two decisions govern the site rather than the machine: facility management wrote the crossing rule that lets people enter the aisle only at marked points, while operations decided how many machines one remote assistant would serve. [[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/04 Governing the Machine/17 Responsible Physical AI/V4-17.03 Decisions Behind an Admitted Action#^fig-17-decision-chain|Figure 1]] draws the six links and the record, if any, that holds each one.

↩︎ The lead systems adjudicator, whose signature binds the release verdict to a named person, is established in **[[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/04 Governing the Machine/16 Deployment Release/V4-16.05 The Three Verdicts#^sec-release-three-verdicts|The Three Verdicts]]**.

Figure 1: **Six decisions behind one admitted command**: An aisle command admitted at one tick traces back to decisions made weeks earlier by six different parties. Four of them land in a record the book has built (the policy manifest, the enforcement record, the authority record, and the release manifest); the crossing rule and the staffing ratio land in none, so no log can name who made them. The approval record lists each decision with its owner and refuses operation while any link has no owner.

^fig-17-decision-chain

When each of these decisions belongs to a different party, every party can truthfully say that the harm came from someone else’s part. Thompson called this the **problem of many hands** in his study of public officials, where responsibility for a policy that many people shaped ends up resting on no one ([[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/04 Governing the Machine/17 Responsible Physical AI/V4-17.14 Source References#^ref-thompson1980manyhands|Thompson 1980]]). Nissenbaum showed that computer systems make it worse: they are built by many people, they fail in ways no single person caused, and their failures are easily excused as bugs that no one could have prevented ([[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/04 Governing the Machine/17 Responsible Physical AI/V4-17.14 Source References#^ref-nissenbaum1996accountability|Nissenbaum 1996]]). A learned proposer adds a further excuse. Its behavior was learned rather than written, so no engineer wrote the rule it followed, and Matthias argued that machines which learn create a real gap in who can be held responsible for what they do ([[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/04 Governing the Machine/17 Responsible Physical AI/V4-17.14 Source References#^ref-matthias2004responsibility|Matthias 2004]]). The architecture of this book closes that gap at the point where it matters. The model proposes and never admits (principle [[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/03 Running the Machine/V4 Running the Machine#^pri-vol4-proposal-permission|3]]), so every admitted action passed a check that someone designed, under an envelope that someone signed, and “the model did it” is never a complete answer.

### Decisions That Leave No Trace in a Log

^sec-responsibility-untraced-decisions

When an incident investigator opens a machine’s runtime logs, the decisions that made the incident possible are missing because those logs capture only runtime events: an observation and its age, a proposal, an admission, a change of authority, or a fault trial. The choices that shaped those events happened weeks or months earlier in meetings and documents, long before a command crossed the causal boundary. A safety engineer approves an operating envelope, an operations manager sets a staffing ratio, a team disables a protective function, a company applies for a permit, and a data team declares what its camera traces may be used for. In the Cruise case, the rule that a side impact should trigger a pullover was a design decision made long before October 2, and the choices about what to tell regulators were made in the days after. Neither could appear in the vehicle’s log, because the vehicle did not make them.

Such decisions have to be recorded on purpose, and a chain of them is less complete than any one of its links. Suppose each of the 6 links behind the aisle command can be retrieved when an investigator asks with probability 0.99, independently of the others. The whole chain then resolves with probability $0.99^{6} = 0.94$. At 0.95 per link it resolves with probability 0.74, and at 0.90, roughly what a decision held only in memory and email achieves once its author has changed jobs, with probability 0.53. An investigator would then find the full chain in about 53 percent of incidents. The same arithmetic makes a conjunctive release argument weaker than any one of its premises, and the remedy is also the same: each link has to be written down as part of the design, not left to accumulate.

↩︎ The conjunctive arithmetic that makes an argument no stronger than its weakest premise is established in **[[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/04 Governing the Machine/16 Deployment Release/V4-16.04 Safety Cases and Claims#^sec-release-contextual-assumptions|Contextual assumptions and the weakest link]]**.

These links are held in the decision block of the approval record ([[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/04 Governing the Machine/17 Responsible Physical AI/V4-17.10 The Approval Record#^sec-responsibility-approval-record|section 17.9]]). For each decision it names the type of decision, the role that owns it, the signed approval, the evidence it relied on, and the date after which it must be made again. A link with no owner is not a gap in paperwork; it is a premise of the machine’s authority that no one has accepted. Even when every link has a named owner, however, many of those approvals rest on a further premise: that a human operator in the loop can step in when the machine’s own case runs out.
