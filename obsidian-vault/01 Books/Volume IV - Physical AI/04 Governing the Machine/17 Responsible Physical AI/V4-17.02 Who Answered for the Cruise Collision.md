# V4-17.02 Who Answered for the Cruise Collision

[[Home|Home]] · [[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/Volume 4 Overview|Volume Overview]] · [[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/04 Governing the Machine/17 Responsible Physical AI/V4-17 Responsible Physical AI|V4-17 Responsible Physical AI]]

^sec-responsibility-who-answered

Release left the running machine with a signed manifest: a verdict, the records it binds by digest, and the standing conditions that withdraw it. The manifest names the lead systems adjudicator who signed it. It does not name who decided the aisle speed the warehouse would accept, who decided that one remote assistant would serve every machine on the floor, or who would report an incident, to whom, and how fast, if the machine hurt someone. Those decisions shape what the machine does once a command crosses the causal boundary as surely as the enforcer’s thresholds do, and none of them appears in a per-tick log.

The Cruise collision in San Francisco on the night of October 2, 2023 shows what happens when they are left unassigned. A human-driven car struck a pedestrian in a crosswalk and threw her into the path of a driverless Cruise robotaxi, which braked hard, ran over her, and stopped. With her still beneath it, the vehicle then began a pullover toward the curb and moved about 20 ft (6.1 m) at up to 7.7 mph before it stopped ([[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/04 Governing the Machine/17 Responsible Physical AI/V4-17.14 Source References#^ref-quinnemanuel2024cruise|Quinn Emanuel Urquhart and Sullivan, LLP 2024]]). The engineering failure belongs to the permission path. A post-collision classifier placed the pedestrian at the side of the vehicle rather than in front of it, and the fallback it selected moved the vehicle without any evidence that the space beneath it was clear.

↩︎ The fallback rule that forbids renewed motion while the space beneath a vehicle is unverified is established in **[[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/03 Running the Machine/12 Safety Enforcement/V4-12.10 The Fallback Ladder#^sec-enforcement-four-rungs|The four rungs of the fallback ladder]]**.

The machine’s own records were enough to reconstruct that motion. The engineers who analyzed the event for the company’s boards rebuilt it from the vehicle’s logs and video, including the dropped pedestrian track before impact and the pullover after it ([[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/04 Governing the Machine/17 Responsible Physical AI/V4-17.14 Source References#^ref-quinnemanuel2024cruise|Quinn Emanuel Urquhart and Sullivan, LLP 2024]]). What failed after the collision was the account given to the people who had licensed the vehicle. On October 3, a Cruise representative told an analyst at the California Public Utilities Commission (CPUC) by telephone that the vehicle had stopped on impact and contacted remote assistance; the description left out the pullover and the dragging. The commission later counted 15 days, October 3 through 18, in which Cruise withheld a full account, and it received the full video on October 19 ([[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/04 Governing the Machine/17 Responsible Physical AI/V4-17.14 Source References#^ref-cpuc2024cruisedecision|California Public Utilities Commission 2024]]). Cruise’s crash reports to the National Highway Traffic Safety Administration (NHTSA) also omitted the dragging ([[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/04 Governing the Machine/17 Responsible Physical AI/V4-17.14 Source References#^ref-doj2024cruise|U.S. Attorney’s Office, Northern District of California 2024]]). On October 24, the California Department of Motor Vehicles (DMV) suspended Cruise’s permits for driverless testing and driverless deployment, citing among its grounds that the company had misrepresented information related to the safety of its autonomous technology ([[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/04 Governing the Machine/17 Responsible Physical AI/V4-17.14 Source References#^ref-cadmv2023cruise|California Department of Motor Vehicles 2023]]). A review commissioned by Cruise’s boards found no evidence that anyone intended to mislead regulators, but it described a “passive, non-transparent approach” to disclosure and a leadership focused on correcting media coverage of the crash ([[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/04 Governing the Machine/17 Responsible Physical AI/V4-17.14 Source References#^ref-quinnemanuel2024cruise|Quinn Emanuel Urquhart and Sullivan, LLP 2024]]).

Each group involved held one piece of the outcome: the engineers who designed the classifier and the fallback, the remote-assistance staff, the people who drafted the reports, and the executives who managed the response. Each could describe its own piece truthfully, and none of those descriptions said who had decided the whole. The CPUC had granted Cruise commercial driverless service in San Francisco at any hour seven weeks earlier ([[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/04 Governing the Machine/17 Responsible Physical AI/V4-17.14 Source References#^ref-cpuc2023cruiseapproval|California Public Utilities Commission 2023]]). The logs answered what the vehicle did. They did not answer who had decided that a driverless vehicle should pull over after a side impact without checking beneath itself, who decided what regulators would be told, or who answered for either decision. Five years earlier, after the Uber test vehicle killed a pedestrian in Tempe, the person who answered in court was the vehicle’s backup operator, who pleaded guilty to endangerment in 2023 ([[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/04 Governing the Machine/17 Responsible Physical AI/V4-17.14 Source References#^ref-mcao2023vasquez|Maricopa County Attorney’s Office 2023]]).

Responsibility for a physical machine therefore has to be engineered like any other premise of its authority: written down before the machine moves, attached to the decisions that set its limits, and checked when it is released. The same questions arise on the warehouse machine without a public road in sight. A remote assistant answers for takeovers, facility management for the crossing rule that keeps people out of the aisle, a data team for the camera traces the black box keeps, and someone, so far unnamed, for the decision that one person can watch every machine on the floor. The sections that follow take these decisions in turn: the links behind one admitted action, the human roles the design relies on, the stages of permission to operate, the detection of different groups of people, the people who bear the machine’s risk and appear in its records, and the labor it moves rather than removes. The approval record collects these decisions in one place that the release gate reads, so that a decision with no named owner blocks operation the way an unknown premise does.
