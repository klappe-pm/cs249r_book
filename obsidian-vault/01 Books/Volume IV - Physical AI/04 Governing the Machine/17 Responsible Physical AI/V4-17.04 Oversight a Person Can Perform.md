# V4-17.04 Oversight a Person Can Perform

[[Home|Home]] · [[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/Volume 4 Overview|Volume Overview]] · [[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/04 Governing the Machine/17 Responsible Physical AI/V4-17 Responsible Physical AI|V4-17 Responsible Physical AI]]

^sec-responsibility-meaningful-oversight

Naming a human operator in that decision chain, whether a safety driver behind the wheel, a remote assistant at a console, or a spotter walking beside a robot during commissioning, often shifts blame onto that person without adding any real protection. A person in the loop provides genuine oversight only if three conditions hold: the person must have the time to act before the hazard arrives, the information to understand what the machine is doing, and the authority to change what it does, not only to report it. Santoni de Sio and van den Hoven state the same requirement in two parts: the system must respond to the reasons of the people meant to control it, and every outcome must trace back to someone who understood both the system and their own role in it ([[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/04 Governing the Machine/17 Responsible Physical AI/V4-17.14 Source References#^ref-santonidesio2018meaningful|Santoni de Sio and Hoven 2018]]).

**Definition 17.1: Human oversight role**

A ***human oversight role*** is a duty that a design assigns to a person, such as stopping the machine, taking over a channel, or approving a motion, and that the safety argument relies on. The role counts as a countermeasure only if its holder has the time, the information, and the authority to carry it out within the time the hazard leaves.

1.  **Significance**: A role that fails any of the three conditions adds no protection, yet it moves blame onto the person who holds it, so every role the safety case cites must be tested like any other premise.
2.  **Distinction**: The takeover budget measures how long a person needs; the oversight role asks whether the design leaves that much time, gives that person what they need to see, and lets them act on it.
3.  **Common pitfall**: A frequent misconception is that adding a person to the loop makes a design responsible. A person who cannot act in time is a place to put blame, not a safety layer.

When a role fails any of these conditions, the person in it absorbs responsibility for control they never had. Elish calls the person in that position a **moral crumple zone**. Like the crumple zone of a car, the person absorbs the impact, except that here the impact is blame, and what the crumpling protects is the reputation of the system rather than the person ([[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/04 Governing the Machine/17 Responsible Physical AI/V4-17.14 Source References#^ref-elish2019moral|Elish 2019]]). The three conditions give a design team a test it can apply before the machine moves.

### Time to Act

^sec-responsibility-oversight-time

Time is the condition most often violated, and it is the easiest to check. At the 1.3 m/s aisle speed, a person who steps out 1.10 m ahead of the machine leaves 0.85 s before the rack end. A remote assistant who has been monitoring rather than driving needs about 4.5 s to take over, the design out-of-loop takeover time, during which the machine travels 5.8 m. The person needs about 5× the time the hazard leaves. No training, attention, or diligence closes a gap of that size, so a design that names the remote assistant as the countermeasure for a person stepping out at a rack end has not added a layer of protection. It has recorded in advance who will be blamed.

↩︎ The out-of-loop takeover time and its price in the stopping budget are established in **[[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/04 Governing the Machine/14 Supervisory Intervention/V4-14.03 Human Authority, Reaction Time, and Clearance#^sec-intervention-reaction-drift|Human reaction time and travel before braking]]**.

The engineering consequence is already in the book’s design. The machine stops itself first and asks for help from standstill, where waiting costs time but not clearance. What the time test adds is the duty to write that choice down. For every human role the safety case relies on, the approval record lists the response time the role requires, the time the hazard leaves, and a verdict of `FEASIBLE`, `INFEASIBLE`, or `UNKNOWN`. A role marked `INFEASIBLE` may stay in the design as a productivity resource; it may not appear in the safety argument as a countermeasure.

### One Person, Many Machines

^sec-responsibility-supervisor-utilization

A remote assistant who serves many machines is a server in a queue, and queueing theory says how busy that person is. If help requests arrive from the whole fleet at rate ${\lambda_{\text{arr}}}$ and each holds the assistant for a mean time ${\bar{T}}$, the share of time the assistant is busy, the utilization, is

$$
{\rho_{\text{serv}}} = {\lambda_{\text{arr}}}\,{\bar{T}}. \tag{1}
$$

 A request that arrives while the assistant is busy waits. Under the simplest queueing model, with random arrivals and random service times, the mean wait before service begins is

$$
{W_{q}} = \frac{{\rho_{\text{serv}}}\,{\bar{T}}}{1-{\rho_{\text{serv}}}}. \tag{2}
$$

 The wait grows without bound as ${\rho_{\text{serv}}}$ approaches one. Moreover, a request arriving while the assistant is busy, which happens with probability ${\rho_{\text{serv}}}$ in this model, gets no human attention at all until the request ahead of it is finished.

**Napkin Math 17.1: One assistant, twenty machines**

**Problem**: A site plans to run 20 mobile manipulators with one remote assistant. Each machine asks for help 0.5 times per operating hour, and each request holds the assistant for 3 min on average. How busy is the assistant, and how long does a request wait?

**Variables**:

- Requests per machine: 0.5 per operating hour (Illustrative); mean service time ${\bar{T}} =$ 180 s (Illustrative).
- Machines served: 10, 20, or 32 (Illustrative).

**Math**: By [[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/04 Governing the Machine/17 Responsible Physical AI/V4-17.04 Oversight a Person Can Perform#^eq-responsibility-supervisor-load|equation 1]], 20 machines give ${\rho_{\text{serv}}} =$ 0.50, and [[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/04 Governing the Machine/17 Responsible Physical AI/V4-17.04 Oversight a Person Can Perform#^eq-responsibility-supervisor-wait|equation 2]] gives a mean wait ${W_{q}} =$ 180 s. One request in a hundred waits longer than 23 min. With 10 machines the figures are 0.25 and 60 s; with 32 they are 0.80 and 720 s, about 12 min.

**Result**: The assistant is busy 50 percent of the time, and a typical request waits minutes. Adding machines past that point makes the wait grow much faster than the fleet.

**Systems insight**: Every one of these waits is longer than the 0.85 s a person stepping out at a rack end leaves. The ratio of machines to assistants is therefore a productivity budget and never a safety layer. A staffing plan that caps utilization at 0.5 limits this assistant to 20 machines, and the approval record lists that cap as the decision of whoever set it.

↩︎ Admitting a remote-assistant session only when the machine is in a state where waiting costs no clearance is established in **[[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/04 Governing the Machine/14 Supervisory Intervention/V4-14.05 Transfer without Discontinuity#^sec-intervention-session-admission|Admitting the remote-assistant session]]**.

The queue shows why the staffing ratio is a decision someone must own. A manager who adds machines to one assistant’s load is not changing a number in a spreadsheet; that manager is deciding how long a stranded machine, and the people working around it, will wait for a person. If that decision is written down with the utilization it implies and an owner, it can be reviewed when the fleet grows. If it is not, it changes silently every time a machine is added.

### Information and Authority

^sec-responsibility-oversight-information

Time is necessary but not enough. A person with time to act must also know what the machine is doing and be able to change it. A console that shows the camera feed but not which channel the machine still owns leaves the operator guessing about the most important fact of a handover, and an operator who believes they hold a channel they do not hold can make a takeover worse. A role whose only output is a report, a flag raised to a supervisor or a note in a log, gives its holder no authority over the motion at all. The test for authority is mechanical. The person must be able to stop the machine, through a channel the permission path honors within the time the hazard leaves, without asking anyone else.

↩︎ Mode confusion, and the per-channel annunciation that keeps an operator’s picture of who owns each channel current, are established in **[[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/04 Governing the Machine/14 Supervisory Intervention/V4-14.07 Authority Handover Failure Modes#^sec-intervention-failure-modes|Authority Handover Failure Modes]]**.

For each human role, the approval record therefore holds three fields beside the timing verdict: the information channel the person reads, the authority channel the person can act through, and whether that channel reaches the permission path. A remote assistant on the running machine passes the information and authority tests for a stranded machine at standstill and fails the time test for a person stepping out at a rack end. Both facts belong in the record, because they assign that one person two different duties.

**Checkpoint 17.1: Oversight a person can perform**

A design names three people in its safety argument: the remote assistant, a teach-pendant operator during commissioning, and a floor supervisor who watches the fleet dashboard.

**Time**: Can you state, for each role, the time the role requires and the time the relevant hazard leaves, and mark the role `FEASIBLE`, `INFEASIBLE`, or `UNKNOWN`?

**Load**: A site runs 24 machines with one assistant; requests arrive at 0.8 per machine-hour and take 75 s. Can you compute the assistant’s utilization and mean wait, and say whether the assistant may be listed as a countermeasure for any hazard faster than a minute?

**Authority**: Can you say which of the three roles can stop the machine through a channel the permission path honors, and which can only report?
