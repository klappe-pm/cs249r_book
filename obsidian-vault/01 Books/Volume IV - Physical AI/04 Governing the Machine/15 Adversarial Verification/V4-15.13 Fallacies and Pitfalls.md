# V4-15.13 Fallacies and Pitfalls

[[Home|Home]] · [[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/Volume 4 Overview|Volume Overview]] · [[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/04 Governing the Machine/15 Adversarial Verification/V4-15 Adversarial Verification|V4-15 Adversarial Verification]]

^sec-verification-fallacies-pitfalls

A physical machine can pass every isolated component fault test yet catastrophically fail in deployment when a shared power rail, communication bus, or reference clock couples multiple subsystems or blinds their diagnostic monitors. These failures stem from **unintended causal, electrical, and temporal coupling** across architectural boundaries. Compound verification tests catch such cross-domain hazards only when enough galvanically and logically independent instrumentation survives the fault to establish the ground-truth sequence of events.

**Fallacy**: *Independent local watchdog timers guarantee end-to-end physical deadline compliance.*

A perception front-end, the chunk policy, and the safety permission monitor can each execute comfortably within their local scheduling slices while an unmonitored inter-process queue buffers stale observations between them. Local watchdog timers confirm only that isolated tasks kick their respective heartbeats within software deadlines; they reveal nothing about the total age of the physical state traversing the distributed pipeline.

Consider an asynchronous buffer that inserts delay into the machine’s 133.6 ms pre-deceleration budget. The importance-sampling example of [[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/04 Governing the Machine/15 Adversarial Verification/V4-15.11 Interpreting Campaign Statistics#^sec-verification-importance-sampling|section 15.10.2]] already priced this queue. At 1.3 m/s it exhausts the spare after 100.8 ms, only 75.4 percent of the nominal pre-deceleration interval, and once the dispatch-to-admission allocation is charged it does so after 37.5 ms. The fallacy lies in what the watchdogs report while it happens. Every task-level watchdog shows nominal health as the queue expands, because no task has missed its own slice. To detect this silent failure, verification experiments must isolate each stage of the causal chain: target appearance, optical capture, message dispatch, bus admission, lease expiration, and actuator torque onset. Without stage-by-stage timestamping, latency intervals are easily overlooked or double-counted.

**Local watchdogs verify component execution, not end-to-end physical propagation.**

**Architect’s countermeasure:** Enforce monotonic capture-to-torque evidence epoch timestamps across all processing nodes. Compare the sensor’s physical capture timestamp directly against the safety monitor’s decision tick, discarding any control proposal whose total elapsed latency exceeds the safety budget regardless of healthy local thread heartbeats.

**Pitfall**: *Allowing diagnostic logging and high-throughput telemetry to share fieldbuses with real-time safety loops.*

Real-time fieldbuses in physical AI (such as CAN FD and EtherCAT) are engineered for deterministic, low-latency control: EtherCAT cyclic Process Data Objects (PDOs) and CAN FD priority-arbitrated frames carry microsecond-accurate torque targets and lease-renewal heartbeats. When engineering teams attempt to capture rich diagnostic traces by multiplexing high-throughput telemetry (diagnostic core dumps, raw LiDAR point clouds, uncompressed camera debug streams) through Ethernet-over-EtherCAT (EoE) or bulk CAN FD payloads, they risk overflowing communication channels and starving safety-critical control loops.

On CAN FD networks, message arbitration is non-preemptive: once a low-priority diagnostic frame begins transmission on the physical layer, a newly ready high-priority emergency-stop or torque-command frame cannot preempt it. The resulting blocking latency depends on frame length, arbitration and data bit rates, and bit stuffing. Unregulated diagnostic bursts therefore inflate queueing jitter and exhaust hard timing budgets, even though every frame on the bus is structurally valid. Because CAN controllers do not register arbitration delays or link saturation as electrical errors, hardware error counters remain clean while safety deadlines expire. Similarly, while an EtherCAT master partitions cyclic process data from acyclic mailbox traffic, shared host network interface cards, protocol stack buffers, and slave microcontroller processing pipelines can still experience severe resource contention under heavy mailbox load. If mailbox servicing delays PDO packet processing or causes host-side scheduling jitter, safety heartbeats fail.

When unbudgeted telemetry delays a safety lease renewal or drive update past its deadline, the affected watchdog must invoke its qualified local fail-safe response. Furthermore, if a physical emergency-stop command travels across that same saturated bus, transmission delays directly consume stopping clearance. Verification trials must capture synchronized electrical communication traces alongside physical vehicle trajectories; observing a software timeout in isolation cannot prove that the machine arrested motion within its certified clearance envelope.

**Unbudgeted diagnostic traffic can delay the commands and evidence needed for a bounded response.**

**Architect’s countermeasure:** Physically isolate high-bandwidth diagnostic telemetry from real-time safety buses on separate network media, or enforce rigorous protocol-specific traffic shaping. On CAN FD, this means strict frame-length caps, reserved identifier priorities, and rate-limiting ingress filters. On EtherCAT, it means dedicated cyclic and mailbox schedule partitioning with strict execution time budgets. Ethernet Time-Sensitive Networking (TSN) scheduling and frame preemption standards (IEEE 802.1Qbv and 802.1Qbu) apply only to compliant Ethernet hardware; they do not provide preemption across CAN FD or standard EtherCAT links.

**Pitfall**: *Relying on software anomaly detectors that share electrical power or failure modes with monitored actuators.*

Actuator dynamics, including rapid motor acceleration, locked-rotor stall currents, and sharp regenerative braking transitions, cause severe electrical disturbances on shared power rails and analog ground references. Inverter switching produces steep inductive voltage transients (${v} = {L_{\text{bus}}}\,d{I}/d{t}$), while wiring harnesses and internal battery impedance introduce substantial voltage drops that can pull unregulated bus voltages below the minimum dropout threshold of downstream DC-DC converters. Adversarial testing campaigns (such as fault category F4) inject calibrated rail transients to measure whether these disturbances propagate into supposedly isolated sensor and safety controller supplies.

When digital transducers monitoring actuator velocity or position share a power distribution board, ground plane, or non-isolated buck regulator with motor gate drivers, supply dips and ground bounce propagate directly into sensor microcontrollers. A common failure mode is a frozen peripheral register: the transducer continues transmitting cyclically valid bus frames whose measurement payload has stopped updating. System behavior depends on representation: if an encoder freezes its cumulative position counter, a downstream velocity estimator that computes numerical derivatives will incorrectly deduce that the motor has reached zero velocity. Conversely, if the sensor freezes an internally computed velocity register, it may continuously report a nominal cruising speed.

For example, if a frozen velocity register continues reporting 1.3 m/s while the motor drive erroneously executes a full-throttle acceleration command, the telemetry frames will carry valid cyclic checksums and plausible values even though they no longer reflect physical reality. A software anomaly detector that monitors only motor telemetry cannot identify the divergence without an independent physical motion witness, such as a secondary wheel encoder, an inertial measurement unit, or optical flow sensors. The adversarial verification suite must explicitly inject both the electrical transient and the resulting register freeze, using external ground-truth tracking to monitor vehicle kinematics through the commanded stop.

**A power fault that disrupts an actuator can simultaneously blind the sensor tasked with detecting it.**

**Architect’s countermeasure:** Power safety-critical transducers and monitor microcontrollers from galvanically isolated, held-up auxiliary supplies equipped with sufficient energy-storage capacitance to ride through worst-case bus transients. Transducers must be configured so that internal hardware faults cannot masquerade as plausible measurements. For analog transducers, pulling an output to 0 V fails this requirement whenever 0 V represents a valid physical reading (such as zero speed or minimum distance). Reliable systems employ a “live zero” architecture with out-of-band diagnostic voltage levels, patterned after the NAMUR NE 43 standard for industrial transmitters ([[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/04 Governing the Machine/15 Adversarial Verification/V4-15.16 Source References#^ref-namurne43|NAMUR 2021]]). For digital fieldbuses, enforce end-to-end communication protections featuring monotonic rolling sequence counters, timestamp freshness checks, and payload CRCs to detect repeated or corrupted frames ([[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/04 Governing the Machine/15 Adversarial Verification/V4-15.16 Source References#^ref-autosare2e|AUTOSAR 2024]]). A frozen measurement register can still travel in a fresh, correctly sequenced packet, so detecting that failure also requires monitoring physical acquisition progress and cross-checking the reported state against an independent motion witness.

**Fallacy**: *Treating an unobservable test outcome where diagnostic loggers dropped packets as evidence of safety.*

High-severity fault injection, such as short-circuiting an inverter drive phase, opening a contactor under full load, or coupling high-voltage electromagnetic interference (EMI) onto fieldbus cabling, generates intense radiated and conducted electrical transients within the test cell. Ground bounce and high inductive kickback routinely couple into the external data acquisition (DAQ) harness, desynchronizing high-speed digitizer clocks, overflowing PCIe frame-grabber ring buffers, or crashing host logging daemons into kernel panics.

Meanwhile, the physical mobile base comes to rest: either its spring-applied mechanical brakes engaged, its drive back-drove the gearbox to a stall, or it drifted into the test cell’s mechanical end-stop buffer. In an undisciplined test campaign, observers look at the stopped machine and declare the run a “PASS” (“no crash occurred and the system reached rest”), while dismissing the missing telemetry as an irrelevant “instrumentation glitch” or repeating the trial until the logger manages to survive.

This practice introduces **survivorship bias**. The trials in which the logger crashes are often those with the most violent electrical transients, which are the conditions most likely to cause the safety microcontroller to hang in an exception handler without commanding deceleration. An unrecorded trial cannot reveal which mechanism stopped the base, and an operational warehouse aisle has no end-stop buffer. Under a rigorous qualification methodology, any test run lacking continuous, verifiable telemetry must be classified as `UNOBSERVABLE` and treated as an immediate test defect. The campaign must report the explicit count of each verdict alongside all untested configurations. Summary ratios such as ${N_{\text{pass}}} / {N_{\text{total}}}$ serve as test-suite execution statistics rather than empirical failure-probability estimates derived from valid, independently scored trials. Before repeating that condition, the team must establish why the witnesses failed and qualify evidence capture under the injected environment.

**Discarding unobservable trials conceals the exact vulnerability under test through survivorship bias.**

**Architect’s countermeasure:** Categorize any test run with dropped, incomplete, or corrupted evidence as `UNOBSERVABLE`, retaining it as an evidence deficit whose cause must be investigated. Report all four verdict counts. Credit a required trial toward a protective claim only when its required evidence is complete and verified; repeated observable runs do not erase missing evidence from earlier trials.
