# V4-15.10 Searching Beyond the First Trial

[[Home|Home]] · [[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/Volume 4 Overview|Volume Overview]] · [[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/04 Governing the Machine/15 Adversarial Verification/V4-15 Adversarial Verification|V4-15 Adversarial Verification]]

^sec-verification-search-campaign

A single successful fault trial demonstrates that the safety path can arrest the vehicle under one declared set of operating conditions. Physical machinery, however, rarely fails under tidy nominal conditions; subtle vulnerabilities hide in the narrow corners of the state space where timing jitter, payload shifts, and degraded surface traction align against the enforcer. Exhaustive testing across this continuous operational envelope is physically impossible, yet uniform random sampling requires an intractable number of runs to uncover rare edge-case failures. Searching beyond isolated trials requires systematically steering disturbances toward the boundary conditions that drive the system closest to its safety limits. Rather than replacing the formal coverage record, automated search routines prioritize which candidate executions deserve empirical evaluation on the physical test track or dynamometer.

### Signal Temporal Logic and Quantitative Robustness

^sec-verification-stl-robustness

A binary `PASS` or `FAIL` verdict tells an automated search algorithm nothing about whether a passing stop cleared the rack by 15 cm or by 1 mm, leaving the optimizer blind to near misses. Classical temporal logic ([[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/04 Governing the Machine/15 Adversarial Verification/V4-15.16 Source References#^ref-pnueli1977temporal|Pnueli 1977]]) checks whether a sequence of states satisfies a property. **Signal Temporal Logic (STL)** extends the specification to continuous time and real-valued physical signals (such as staying inside a spatial corridor or halting before a deadline ([[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/04 Governing the Machine/15 Adversarial Verification/V4-15.16 Source References#^ref-maler2004monitoring|Maler and Nickovic 2004]])), and it equips every formula with **quantitative robustness semantics** ([[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/04 Governing the Machine/15 Adversarial Verification/V4-15.16 Source References#^ref-fainekos2009robustness|Fainekos and Pappas 2009]]). Instead of returning a boolean flag, quantitative robustness computes a real-valued signed margin ${\rho}({\phi}, {s}, {t}) \in \mathbb{R}$ that measures *how far* a trajectory ${s}: \mathbb{R}_{\ge 0} \to \mathbb{R}^{{n}}$ sits from violating or satisfying the temporal property ${\phi}$ at time ${t}$.

↪ The syntax and semantics of signal temporal logic are developed in **[[05 Reference/Volume IV - Physical AI/V4 Machine Learning Background#^sec-appendix-ml-stl|Signal Temporal Logic and Continuous Robustness]]**.

For an atomic physical predicate $\pi \equiv ({f}({s}({t})) \ge 0)$ (such as spatial clearance ${d}({t}) - {d_{\text{safe}}} \ge 0$), quantitative robustness evaluates the signed predicate margin: ${\rho}(\pi, {s}, {t}) = {f}({s}({t}))$. Across temporal operators, safety invariants evaluate worst-case (minimum) margins, whereas reachability deadlines evaluate best-case (maximum) margins:

$$
{\rho}(\square_{[0, {T}]} {\phi}, {s}, 0) = \min_{t \in [0, {T}]} {\rho}({\phi}, {s}, {t}), \qquad {\rho}(\lozenge_{[0, {\tau}]} {\phi}, {s}, 0) = \max_{t \in [0, {\tau}]} {\rho}({\phi}, {s}, {t}). \tag{3}
$$

 Here $\square_{[0,{T}]}$ denotes “always on $[0, {T}]$” and $\lozenge_{[0,{\tau}]}$ denotes “eventually within ${\tau}$.”

^eq-verification-stl-recursive-robustness

The sign and magnitude of ${\rho}$ in [[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/04 Governing the Machine/15 Adversarial Verification/V4-15.10 Searching Beyond the First Trial#^eq-verification-stl-recursive-robustness|equation 3]] establish an immediate operational interpretation:

$$
{\rho} > 0 \implies \text{satisfied with positive margin } {\rho}, \qquad {\rho} < 0 \implies \text{violated with shortfall depth } |{\rho}|.
$$

 For example, if the spatial clearance margin is ${\rho} = +0.15\text{ m}$, the minimum clearance observed along the trajectory exceeded the safety threshold by 15 cm. Conversely, if ${\rho} = -0.04\text{ m}$, the machine breached the prescribed clearance boundary by 4 cm, with physical collision depending on whether the safety limit included an explicit standoff buffer.

This signed value margin constitutes **space robustness**, quantifying how far the signal’s physical magnitude can perturb before the verdict flips. Space robustness captures amplitude margins rather than timing margins: a delayed brake actuation represents a temporal shortfall rather than an instantaneous spatial error. Capturing temporal slack requires **time robustness**, a complementary semantics that evaluates how far a signal trajectory may shift along the time axis before the verdict changes ([[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/04 Governing the Machine/15 Adversarial Verification/V4-15.16 Source References#^ref-donze2010robust|Donzé and Maler 2010]]). Because spatial and temporal margins govern different physical dimensions, combining them into a single unweighted formula introduces semantic ambiguity. For the F1 specification, the cleanest engineering formulation isolates the onset deadline outside the Signal Temporal Logic expression as an explicit scalar predicate on measured reaction time, while evaluating stopping distance via spatial robustness.

For this reason [[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/04 Governing the Machine/15 Adversarial Verification/V4-15.03 The Operating Envelope#^eq-verification-f1-oracle|equation 1]] keeps its two predicates apart. The onset predicate, deceleration within 82 ms of the last valid renewal, is a scalar check on measured reaction time, and the travel predicate, at most 751.9 mm at aisle speed, is a space-robustness predicate on vehicle position. Both are measured from the same reference event, so neither can silently absorb slack from the other; a different reference event would change the elapsed timing and require a correspondingly adjusted distance bound.

To illustrate this dual evaluation numerically, consider an F1 trace at aisle speed, timed channel by channel in [[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/04 Governing the Machine/15 Adversarial Verification/V4-15.10 Searching Beyond the First Trial#^fig-15-hardware-fault-injection|figure 4]], where powered deceleration begins 30 ms late, occurring 112 ms after the last valid renewal. The onset margin against the 82 ms bound evaluates to -30 ms. Because the mobile base travels 39 mm further before braking engages, total travel from renewal to rest reaches 750.9 mm, yielding a distance margin of 1 mm against the 751.9 mm oracle. The onset predicate fails, while nominal travel remains within the distance allowance. The verification results retain both margins in their native physical units; the tracking inset belongs in the oracle and is not additional travel in the plotted speed integral. Because taking an unweighted minimum across milliseconds and millimeters produces an uninterpretable quantity, synthesizing them into a unified score via `min` requires normalizing each margin by a predeclared positive scale in its corresponding unit. Logging both the raw margins and their normalization factors ensures the synthesized verdict remains physically meaningful. Choosing scales equal to the verification bounds themselves yields normalized margins of -0.366 for onset timing and 0.001 for stopping distance, making the onset predicate the governing term in the combined score. When several predicates have negative margins, alternative scales can change which one governs the compound score, so test engineers must fix normalization factors before executing verification runs. In this nominal trace only onset fails, so positive rescaling cannot make distance the governing predicate. These numerical values reflect model arithmetic over a stipulated reference trace rather than empirical physical measurements.

^fig-15-hardware-fault-injection

Recording every monitored channel on one timeline, as illustrated in [[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/04 Governing the Machine/15 Adversarial Verification/V4-15.10 Searching Beyond the First Trial#^fig-15-hardware-fault-injection|figure 4]], helps localize a failed trial to a stage of the response path when the independent witnesses remain valid. A lease that expires on schedule and a stop command issued at the next tick, followed by a late braking onset, direct the investigation toward command delivery, drive execution, or physical brake response rather than the lease-expiry check.

By producing a continuous, signed margin rather than a binary pass/fail flag, quantitative robustness transforms verification from passive testing into **temporal logic falsification as adversarial optimization**. In classical computer vision and deep learning, adversarial attacks such as the fast gradient sign method (FGSM) ([[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/04 Governing the Machine/15 Adversarial Verification/V4-15.16 Source References#^ref-goodfellow2015explaining|Goodfellow et al. 2015]]) and projected gradient descent (PGD) ([[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/04 Governing the Machine/15 Adversarial Verification/V4-15.16 Source References#^ref-madry2018towards|Madry et al. 2018]]) search for an input perturbation ${\boldsymbol{\delta}_{\text{adv}}}$ bounded within an ${\epsilon_{\text{adv}}}$-ball ($\|{\boldsymbol{\delta}_{\text{adv}}}\|_\infty \le {\epsilon_{\text{adv}}}$) that maximizes task loss: $\max_{\|{\boldsymbol{\delta}_{\text{adv}}}\|_\infty \le {\epsilon_{\text{adv}}}} {\mathcal{L}}({f_{\theta}}({x} + {\boldsymbol{\delta}_{\text{adv}}}), {y})$. In cyber-physical systems, temporal logic falsification provides the continuous dynamic counterpart. Rather than perturbing static feature vectors, an adversarial optimizer searches over initial state spaces and time-varying physical disturbance trajectories within a bounded physical disturbance envelope to minimize quantitative Signal Temporal Logic robustness. The robustness metric ${\rho}$ acts directly as the adversarial objective function. Whereas uniform random sampling across a high-dimensional operational state space requires an intractable number of physical trials to expose rare corner-case collisions, an adversarial falsification engine actively steers disturbance sequences toward trajectories that drive the system closest to violating its safety invariants.

### Formulating Falsification as Trajectory Optimization

^sec-verification-falsification-trajectory-formulation

An unconstrained mathematical search over arbitrary disturbance signals can easily invent discontinuous spikes that no real actuator, sensor, or contact surface can physically produce, wasting the search on impossible counterexamples. To restrict the search to physically realizable disturbances, let ${\mathbf{x}}_0 \in {\mathcal{X}_0}$ represent the machine’s initial state vector (translational speed, payload mass, heading angle, and battery state-of-charge), and let ${w_{\text{dist}}}({t}) \in {\mathcal{W}}$ denote an adversarial disturbance trajectory spanning the test horizon $[0, {T}]$. Because physical hardware has finite bandwidth, mechanical inertia, and thermal limits, disturbance trajectories are parameterized over a compact parameter space ${\boldsymbol{\psi}} \in {\Psi}$ using smooth basis functions, such as B-splines, cubic splines, or Fourier series:

$$
{w_{\text{dist}}}({t}; {\boldsymbol{\psi}}) = \sum_{k=1}^{{K}} {\psi}_k {B}_k({t})
$$

 where ${B}_k({t})$ are time-dependent basis functions and ${\psi}_k$ are bounded parameter coefficients.

The temporal logic falsification problem finds the worst-case parameter combination that minimizes quantitative robustness:

$$
\min_{{\mathbf{x}}_0 \in {\mathcal{X}_0}, \, {\boldsymbol{\psi}} \in {\Psi}} {\rho}({\phi}, {s}({\mathbf{x}}_0, {w_{\text{dist}}}({\boldsymbol{\psi}})), 0) \quad \text{subject to} \quad \|{w_{\text{dist}}}({t}; {\boldsymbol{\psi}})\|_\infty \le {\Delta_{w}}, \quad \forall {t} \in [0, {T}]. \tag{4}
$$

^eq-verification-adversarial-falsification

The disturbance budget ${\Delta_{w}}$ in [[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/04 Governing the Machine/15 Adversarial Verification/V4-15.10 Searching Beyond the First Trial#^eq-verification-adversarial-falsification|equation 4]] bounds the peak magnitude of optical, acoustic, contact, and execution disturbances derived from the operational envelope. Yet magnitude bounds alone do not guarantee that a test bench can deliver the waveform. The basis functions, coefficient bounds, and experimental test fixtures must also respect realistic rates of change and duration limits. For example, a programmable load bank that cannot slew fast enough to replicate a steep simulated voltage transient leaves that synthesized counterexample untested on hardware.

A verification campaign must therefore log both the synthesized disturbance command and its independently measured physical trace. If the test fixture fails to deliver the precommitted disturbance trajectory within specified tolerances, the trial is `INVALID` for that test condition even if the machine comes to a safe stop. Conversely, while an empirically realized counterexample refutes a safety claim outright, a search that finds no violation within a restricted parameter family leaves arbitrary disturbances outside that basis open to question.

### Selecting a Search Method

^sec-verification-optimization-engines

Frictional contact transitions and discrete controller state jumps often make the quantitative robustness landscape nonsmooth and non-convex, causing gradient descent to stall or step in the wrong direction unless those transitions are smoothed or handled explicitly. Derivative-free optimizers avoid this trap. The Covariance Matrix Adaptation Evolution Strategy (CMA-ES) ([[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/04 Governing the Machine/15 Adversarial Verification/V4-15.16 Source References#^ref-hansen2001completely|Hansen and Ostermeier 2001]]) updates a multivariate normal distribution over disturbance parameters across generations, discovering correlated failure modes across speed, surface friction, and execution timing. When physical trials dominate the cost because each run requires mechanical resets, hardware inspection, and thermal cool-down, Bayesian optimization[[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/04 Governing the Machine/15 Adversarial Verification/V4-15.17 Footnotes#^fn10|¹⁰]] fits a probabilistic surrogate model to previous runs to choose the next test point. Neither method guarantees convergence to a global minimum over a finite campaign, so the verification record must preserve search parameter bounds, random seeds, evaluated test vectors, and fixture limits so that auditors can see exactly which regions were challenged.

Two software toolboxes implement this search loop for cyber-physical models: S-TaLiRo ([[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/04 Governing the Machine/15 Adversarial Verification/V4-15.16 Source References#^ref-annpureddy2011staliro|Annpureddy et al. 2011]]) and Breach ([[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/04 Governing the Machine/15 Adversarial Verification/V4-15.16 Source References#^ref-donze2010breach|Donzé 2010]]). Both ingest a formal Signal Temporal Logic requirement and a simulation model, compute quantitative robustness on each simulated rollout, and steer stochastic or local optimizers toward negative robustness. A related family of algorithms treats the disturbance sequence as a sequential decision process. **Adaptive stress testing** ([[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/04 Governing the Machine/15 Adversarial Verification/V4-15.16 Source References#^ref-lee2020adaptive|Lee et al. 2020]]) explores the disturbance sequence via Monte Carlo tree search or reinforcement learning to find the most probable path to failure rather than an arbitrary, physically improbable edge case, ranking counterexamples by operational plausibility. A survey of these black-box methods groups them by their output ([[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/04 Governing the Machine/15 Adversarial Verification/V4-15.16 Source References#^ref-corso2021survey|Corso et al. 2021]]): finding an isolated counterexample, finding the most likely failure trajectory, or estimating an aggregate failure probability. Only the third output measures operational risk, using the estimation procedure developed in [[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/04 Governing the Machine/15 Adversarial Verification/V4-15.11 Interpreting Campaign Statistics#^sec-verification-importance-sampling|section 15.10.2]]. Whatever search method is chosen, it returns candidate counterexamples and measured robustness margins; when those margins come from a simulator, counting them toward a physical safety claim requires bounding how far the simulator’s trajectories can drift from the real machine’s.

### Bounding the Sim-To-Real Transfer Gap

^sec-verification-sim-to-real-bounds

A simulator that passes every fault-injection rollout can still hide a physical crash if its modeled trajectories differ from the real machine’s by more than the simulated safety margin. While high-throughput software-in-the-loop simulation is indispensable for exploring combinatorial corner cases, an unvalidated simulator can confirm only what its underlying model assumes. To use simulation sweeps as evidence in a safety case, the engineering team must validate and bound the **sim-to-real transfer gap** ${\epsilon_{\text{gap}}}$.

Let ${s_{\text{sim}}}({t}; {\boldsymbol{\xi}}, {\mathbf{u}}) \in {\mathcal{S}}$ and ${s_{\text{real}}}({t}; {\boldsymbol{\xi}}, {\mathbf{u}}) \in {\mathcal{S}}$ denote the system state trajectories in simulation and on target physical hardware under matched declared conditions ${\boldsymbol{\xi}}$ (initial speed, payload mass, floor friction) and control input history ${\mathbf{u}}$. Replaying identical open-loop input sequences establishes a baseline comparison of plant dynamics, but certifying a deployed closed-loop policy requires bounding discrepancy under autonomous execution, where perturbed sensor observations produce divergent actuator commands. The continuous transfer discrepancy is the supremum state error across the test horizon $[0, {T}]$ under a task-weighted physical norm:

$$
{\epsilon_{\text{gap}}} = \sup_{t \in [0, {T}]} \|{s_{\text{real}}}({t}) - {s_{\text{sim}}}({t})\|_{{W}} \tag{5}
$$

 where $\|\cdot\|_{{W}}$ normalizes position, velocity, and actuator torque errors by predeclared positive reference scales in meters, meters per second, and newton-meters, respectively, before applying dimensionless task weights. The verification plan fixes those scales and weights in advance; the resulting discrepancy ${\epsilon_{\text{gap}}}$ is dimensionless, while the Lipschitz constant ${L_{\phi}}$ carries the physical units of the predicate margin.

^eq-verification-sim2real-gap

A sound safety warrant requires a discrepancy bound that holds across all trajectories in the claimed operational envelope, not just the largest error observed in a small test sample. That modeling error must be deducted directly from the simulated robustness margin. Consider a Signal Temporal Logic safety requirement ${\phi}$ whose atomic predicates ${f}({s}({t})) \ge 0$ are Lipschitz continuous with constant ${L_{\phi}} = \sup_{{s}_1 \ne {s}_2} |{f}({s}_1) - {f}({s}_2)| / \|{s}_1 - {s}_2\|_{{W}}$. Here ${L_{\phi}}$ is the maximum rate at which the predicate margin degrades per unit of normalized state discrepancy. For a spatial clearance predicate evaluated in meters against position error normalized by a 1 m reference scale with unit task weight, ${L_{\phi}}$ is 1 m per unit of normalized discrepancy. By Lipschitz continuity, the physical predicate error is bounded pointwise by the state discrepancy:

$$
|{f}({s_{\text{real}}}({t})) - {f}({s_{\text{sim}}}({t}))| \le {L_{\phi}} \|{s_{\text{real}}}({t}) - {s_{\text{sim}}}({t})\|_{{W}} \le {L_{\phi}} {\epsilon_{\text{gap}}}
$$

 which guarantees that at every instant ${t} \in [0, {T}]$:

$$
{f}({s_{\text{real}}}({t})) \ge {f}({s_{\text{sim}}}({t})) - {L_{\phi}} {\epsilon_{\text{gap}}}.
$$

 Taking the infimum across the temporal horizon ${t} \in [0, {T}]$ for an invariance property ${\phi} \equiv \square_{[0, {T}]} ({f}({s}({t})) \ge 0)$, the guaranteed lower bound on physical robustness in the real world is:

$$
{\rho_{\text{real}}} \ge {\rho_{\text{sim}}} - {L_{\phi}} {\epsilon_{\text{gap}}}. \tag{6}
$$

 The term ${L_{\phi}} {\epsilon_{\text{gap}}}$ in [[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/04 Governing the Machine/15 Adversarial Verification/V4-15.10 Searching Beyond the First Trial#^eq-verification-robustness-discount|equation 6]] acts as a **sim-to-real robustness discount**. If simulated robustness ${\rho_{\text{sim}}}$ does not exceed ${L_{\phi}} {\epsilon_{\text{gap}}}$, the simulation pass guarantees no physical margin at all: the real machine can breach the safe invariant set (${\rho_{\text{real}}} < 0$) even when every simulated rollout returns a positive margin.

^eq-verification-robustness-discount

The rack-end claim makes this discount concrete. Scoring the distance predicate in meters and normalizing position error by a 1 m reference scale with unit task weight gives a Lipschitz constant of 1 m per unit of normalized discrepancy. Suppose a simulated stop finishes with the entire 131.0 mm reserve intact. The lower bound in [[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/04 Governing the Machine/15 Adversarial Verification/V4-15.10 Searching Beyond the First Trial#^eq-verification-robustness-discount|equation 6]] certifies a positive physical margin when ${\epsilon_{\text{gap}}} <$ 0.131, meaning the justified position-error bound is smaller than the reserve under this normalization. A larger discrepancy bound leaves the actual physical margin unresolved. A simulator whose stopping position is experimentally validated to within a few centimeters transfers nearly all of its verified margin; a simulator that has never been calibrated against physical stopping runs transfers none of it.

Coupled cyber-physical dynamics determine what this discount must cover: drivetrain compliance and gear backlash alter delivered torque, surface contamination and tire wear change braking traction, and real-time execution jitter shifts braking onset time. A discrepancy bound calibrated on an unloaded, cool machine cannot be silently extended to a loaded vehicle operating under thermal throttling. The verification record must state the operational envelope across which the discrepancy bound holds, keeping that justified bound distinct from the empirical maximum error observed during paired test runs.

Measuring the empirical discrepancy ${\hat{\epsilon}_{\text{gap}}}$ builds on the paired probing protocols used to qualify simulation models for policy training. These protocols drive identical command histories into both simulated and physical hardware and score channel residuals against precommitted tolerances (**[[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/02 Teaching the Machine/06 Policy Training/V4-06.05 The Simulator as a System Component#^sec-training-contact-exploit|The sim-to-real contact exploit]]**). They are the same measurements that bound a simulator’s screening validity in **[[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/02 Teaching the Machine/07 Closed-Loop Evaluation/V4-07.05 Evidence Regimes#^sec-evaluation-proxy-transfer-law|Simulation as a conditional proxy]]**. Verification adapts this qualification procedure in two ways. First, excitation inputs are tailored to the specific safety invariant under test, such as full-rate deceleration from aisle speed for the rack-end claim rather than low-speed docking maneuvers. Second, physical trajectories must be captured with external instrumentation independent of the robot’s onboard sensor suite, so that shared sensor calibration errors or estimator drift cannot cancel out of the residual. The resulting empirical discrepancy ${\hat{\epsilon}_{\text{gap}}}$ is archived in the test records alongside the environmental conditions under which it was gathered. If the sim-to-real safety case relies on domain randomization distributions ${\mathcal{D}_{\text{DR}}}$ to cover hardware variations, it must justify set inclusion across all declared operational conditions:

$$
{\mathcal{S}_{\text{real}}}({\boldsymbol{\xi}}) \subseteq \bigcup_{{\tilde{\boldsymbol{\xi}}} \sim {\mathcal{D}_{\text{DR}}}} {\mathcal{S}_{\text{sim}}}({\tilde{\boldsymbol{\xi}}}).
$$

 Finite paired measurements can falsify this inclusion by exposing an outlier, but they cannot prove it globally. If unmodeled physical effects such as fieldbus latency jitter, magnetic stator saturation, transmission backlash, or Coulomb stiction cause physical trajectories to escape the randomized simulation bundle, the sim-to-real transfer guarantee fails, and software-in-the-loop simulation passes cannot serve as evidence in the release safety case.

**Checkpoint 15.3: Search, robustness, and transfer**

A signed margin turns testing into search, and a simulated margin counts toward a physical claim only after its transfer discount.

**Quantitative robustness interpretation**: Can you explain how Signal Temporal Logic replaces binary pass/fail assertions with a signed predicate margin, and how its units and reference limit determine the meaning of a negative score?

**Adversarial optimization of disturbance trajectories**: Can you describe how black-box optimizers use quantitative robustness scores to discover worst-case combinations of latency, noise, and friction?

**Test invalidation vs. failure criteria**: Can you distinguish between an execution that falsifies a safety invariant and an invalid trial where testbed preconditions or fault injection mechanisms failed?

**Normalization scale estimate**: For the late F1 trace of [[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/04 Governing the Machine/15 Adversarial Verification/V4-15.10 Searching Beyond the First Trial#^sec-verification-stl-robustness|section 15.9.1]], suppose the record had fixed the distance scale at the 40 mm tracking inset while keeping the 82 ms onset scale. Can you estimate both normalized margins and say whether the governing predicate changes for this trace?

**Transfer discount**: A simulated F1 stop keeps 131.0 mm of reserve. Can you state the largest discrepancy bound that still guarantees a positive physical margin, and explain why a bound measured on an unloaded, cool machine cannot certify a loaded, throttled one?
