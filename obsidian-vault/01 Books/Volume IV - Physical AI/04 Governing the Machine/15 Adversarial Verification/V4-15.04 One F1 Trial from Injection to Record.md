# V4-15.04 One F1 Trial from Injection to Record

[[Home|Home]] · [[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/Volume 4 Overview|Volume Overview]] · [[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/04 Governing the Machine/15 Adversarial Verification/V4-15 Adversarial Verification|V4-15 Adversarial Verification]]

^sec-verification-f1-trial

The campaign’s first trial is F1, a proposer failure during transit, planned at the candidate restricted envelope point of 1 m/s with the full declared payload on a test floor qualified to the inspected friction. The numerical oracle remains a screening specification until loaded braking qualification supplies the actual deceleration bound. The safety claim under test is conditional. Once trajectory proposal renewals cease, the low-level permission path and motor drives must autonomously execute the resident powered stop within their allocated stopping envelope. The test deliberately halts or freezes the chunk policy immediately following its last valid lease renewal while leaving its underlying timer heartbeat active. This fault pattern cleanly separates true algorithmic progress in the proposer from the superficial liveness of an unblocked timer interrupt.

Verifying this stopping claim in its entirety requires a full physical rig: the production mobile base, motor drives, representative payload mass, and a qualified test surface. A processor-in-the-loop (PIL) bench, which runs the enforcer’s compiled binary on its target microcontroller against a simulated plant, can validate lease expiration but not the plant’s response, and a pure software simulation approximates both the target’s execution timing and the contact mechanics. Neither bench environment alone provides empirical ground truth for physical stopping distance. Because the machine operates at the edge of its operational envelope during fault injection, an external physical containment fixture must guard the testing volume independently of whatever safety action the machine itself takes.

The temporal reference event for F1 is the last successful admission check that renewed the trajectory lease, not the upstream planner dispatch or the subsequent fault injection trigger. A complete diagnostic trace captures both the final renewal and the injection event across isolated hardware channels that independently record lease expiration, the permission path’s stop command, the physical onset of powered deceleration, and external tracking of base position through complete standstill. Calibrated motor-current or torque measurements alongside wheel encoders identify drive effort and wheel response; external base-motion tracking is still needed to establish chassis deceleration under possible wheel slip. Timing uncertainty belongs in the onset margin. An auxiliary contact switch on the mechanical brake verifies subsequent holding-brake engagement. Relying on the mechanical brake switch alone would mischaracterize stopping latency, as it cannot record the initial phase of regenerative or active electric braking.

Instrumentation must capture the machine’s state trajectory, fault detector identity, refusal timestamp, and recovery time to a safe state, and it must be electrically and logically decoupled from the robot. Examples are external optical ground-truth cameras and optical shaft encoders routed to isolated digitizers. An onboard logging daemon cannot serve, because the fault under evaluation, whether a panicked kernel or a saturated interconnect bus, delays or drops the very log it would write.[[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/04 Governing the Machine/15 Adversarial Verification/V4-15.17 Footnotes#^fn2|²]] Independent external measurement helps expose false passes, where an anomalous run appears successful for reasons outside the formal safety claim. For example, injecting a dropped-frame fault might unintentionally trigger an unmonitored thermal breaker or an auxiliary ground loop that cuts drive power. If that happens, the spring-applied mechanical brake halts the base well within the distance budget, even while the software safety enforcer remains deadlocked in a priority inversion. A naive verification oracle that checks only final stopping distance would mark that trial as a success. A rigorous test harness must verify that the exact causal enforcer path specified in the safety claim commanded and governed the deceleration.

Superficial liveness signals are a second source of false passes. **[[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/01 Machine Anatomy/04 The Nervous System/V4-04.05 Chunk Lease Sizing from the Stopping Budget#^sec-nervous-supervisory-timers|Supervisory timers and proposal wire contracts]]** required that a watchdog be refreshed only when a safety-critical cycle completes, never from an independent periodic interrupt. That section grounded the rule in the Toyota unintended-acceleration litigation, where expert testimony argued that a timer interrupt could keep a hardware watchdog satisfied after the control tasks had stopped ([[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/04 Governing the Machine/15 Adversarial Verification/V4-15.16 Source References#^ref-barr2013toyota|Barr 2013]]). A design rule on paper does not show that the built machine obeys it, and F1 is the test of whether it does. The harness halts the chunk policy’s task or freezes its command output while keeping the periodic interrupt handler alive (perspective [15.1](https://mlsysbook.ai/vol4/15_verification/15_verification.html#psp-verification-watchdog-liveness)), and it measures the actuator response and watchdog state with external instruments. A host daemon can keep reporting a valid process heartbeat after the policy inference thread has frozen, so trial F1 passes only when physical deceleration onset is recorded at the motor drives, never on the strength of a software heartbeat logged in memory. The Toyota record adds a second question that the design rule alone does not raise, whether the supervising monitor shares an input with the processor it supervises (war story [15.1](https://mlsysbook.ai/vol4/15_verification/15_verification.html#ws-verification-toyota-unintended-acceleration-limits-task-blind)).

**Systems Perspective 15.1: The watchdog liveness invariant**

A watchdog serviced by a periodic timer interrupt proves only that the timer interrupt service routine executed, not that safety-critical application tasks met their deadlines, computed valid outputs, or avoided memory corruption. A qualified fault-injection campaign explicitly decouples application progress from interrupt liveness, verifying that the system initiates physical shutdown under task termination, thread starvation, and frozen control registers.

**War Story 15.1: Testing a task-blind watchdog hypothesis**

**Context**: In *Bookout v. Toyota* (Oklahoma County, 2013), a jury heard expert testimony on the electronic throttle control system (ETCS-i) of a 2005 Camry. The plaintiffs’ software expert argued that safety-critical control tasks could fail silently while supervisory mechanisms remained blind to the failure ([[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/04 Governing the Machine/15 Adversarial Verification/V4-15.16 Source References#^ref-barr2013toyota|Barr 2013]]). **[[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/01 Machine Anatomy/04 The Nervous System/V4-04.05 Chunk Lease Sizing from the Stopping Budget#^sec-nervous-supervisory-timers|Supervisory timers and proposal wire contracts]]** drew its watchdog rule from this testimony; the same record also bears on a second claim, the independence of the monitor that backs the watchdog.

**Mechanism**: The engine control module serviced its hardware watchdog from a recurring timer-tick interrupt rather than requiring verified progress from active control tasks. The expert testified that if the task managing throttle angle and critical fail-safes terminated due to memory corruption or a stack overflow, the throttle command could freeze at its last valid state while the timer interrupt continued to pacify the watchdog ([[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/04 Governing the Machine/15 Adversarial Verification/V4-15.16 Source References#^ref-barr2013toyota|Barr 2013]]). The architecture included a secondary monitor processor, but testimony highlighted that this monitor could not inspect task scheduling; it could detect a dead throttle task only indirectly, through changes in brake-pedal state. A single analog-to-digital converter also digitized accelerator-pedal position for both processors, placing a common-cause failure point across the supervision boundary ([[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/04 Governing the Machine/15 Adversarial Verification/V4-15.16 Source References#^ref-dunn2013toyota|Dunn 2013]]). Recovery could therefore require the counterintuitive act of lifting a foot off the brake during the event.

**Impact**: On October 24, 2013, the jury awarded \$3 million in compensatory damages and found that Toyota had acted with reckless disregard; the parties settled before the jury could consider punitive damages ([[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/04 Governing the Machine/15 Adversarial Verification/V4-15.16 Source References#^ref-srs2013toyota|Safety Research & Strategies 2013]]). The verdict did not establish an uncontested technical cause. NASA’s engineering review for the federal investigation found no evidence that an electronic malfunction caused large unintended accelerations ([[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/04 Governing the Machine/15 Adversarial Verification/V4-15.16 Source References#^ref-nasa2011toyota|NASA Engineering and Safety Center 2011]]). It did document electronic faults, among them a tin whisker that formed a 248 Ω short within an accelerator-pedal position sensor from a 2003 Camry. Vehicle tests confirmed that while this fault altered how the throttle tracked the pedal, the throttle still returned to idle whenever the pedal was released ([[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/04 Governing the Machine/15 Adversarial Verification/V4-15.16 Source References#^ref-leidecker2011tinwhisker|Leidecker et al. 2011]]). The investigation attributed the explainable incidents to floor-mat entrapment, sticking pedals, and pedal misapplication. The task-blind mechanism therefore remains an attributed hypothesis, which is exactly the kind of claim only injection can settle.

**Response**: For the design under test, fault injection must establish which application progress checks reach the watchdog, verify whether the independent monitor shares any input pathway with the processor it supervises ([[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/04 Governing the Machine/15 Adversarial Verification/V4-15.07 Deriving the Fault List#^sec-verification-forward-analysis|section 15.6.2]]), and confirm which protective inputs can reliably command a physically suitable stop. The historical controversy highlights the danger, but only direct injection testing establishes these properties for the warehouse machine.

**Systems lesson**: A periodic watchdog heartbeat confirms only that an interrupt handler continues to cycle, not that application tasks are functioning. A monitor is independent only if no input, supply, or clock it depends on can fail together with the processor it supervises. Verification must therefore terminate the supervised task and inject faults at those shared points, measuring the actuator response with external, isolated instruments.

Safety intervention mechanisms must be evaluated under the exact operational stress that makes intervention necessary. A safety enforcer that takes over cleanly on an idle development bench offers little assurance for a production vehicle. In an operational emergency, the policy model operates at maximum inference throughput, memory buses are saturated with high-resolution sensor streams, DMA controllers move camera frame buffers, and the mobile base carries its loaded rack at aisle speed. The verification framework therefore injects faults under declared worst-case thermal and electrical loads, measuring both lease-expiration revocation latency and the elapsed time between diagnostic fault assertion and powered deceleration onset at the motor drives. If the intervention path shares a system bus or memory arbiter with the neural accelerator, safety takeover can stall behind an unpreemptible burst transfer. A safety case that assumes near-instantaneous intervention without a contention bound can credit a response the deployed hardware cannot deliver.

↩︎ How a small urgent read waits behind a large burst transfer on a shared memory interconnect is established in **[[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/03 Running the Machine/13 Workload Placement/V4-13.04 Contention for Shared Resources#^sec-placement-resource-contention|Contention for Shared Resources]]**.

A verification campaign executes every injection trial under a deterministic, automated protocol, ensuring that verdicts derive strictly from repeatable physical measurements rather than post hoc interpretation. The test orchestrator first audits all operating preconditions (ambient temperature, supply rail voltages, baseline wheel speed) against declared engineering tolerances; any trial starting out of bounds aborts with a verdict of `INVALID`, which charges the failure to the fixture rather than the machine, before fault injection begins. Once preconditions hold, the orchestrator synchronizes the external plant fixture, logic analyzer, and independent ground-truth digitizers to a common reference clock, while recording the target clocks without disciplining away their injected drift. After allowing the mobile base to settle at its envelope operating point, the orchestrator asserts the fault trigger across a dedicated hardware line, registering the fault injection timestamp on the unified timeline. The isolated logic analyzer captures both this injection mark and the formal reference event (the final valid lease renewal in trial F1), so fault detection latency, drive takeover duration, and total stopping time are each scored from their true temporal origins. Concurrently, external truth sensors tied to a fail-safe crowbar relay circuit[[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/04 Governing the Machine/15 Adversarial Verification/V4-15.17 Footnotes#^fn3|³]] stand ready to initiate the test cell’s qualified containment response within a measured response bound if the machine approaches its survival-envelope limit. A precommitted evaluation oracle then scores the synchronized trace against the pass/fail criteria defined in [[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/04 Governing the Machine/15 Adversarial Verification/V4-15.05 Fault Oracles and Acceptance#^sec-verification-oracles-acceptance|section 15.4]]. Runs with corrupted or dropped external measurement frames are flagged transparently as `UNOBSERVABLE`, the verdict for a trial whose scoring telemetry was lost ([[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/04 Governing the Machine/15 Adversarial Verification/V4-15.05 Fault Oracles and Acceptance#^sec-verification-four-verdicts|section 15.4.1]] sets out all four verdicts). Finally, the test orchestrator cryptographically signs the complete trial record header and seals it into the safety evidence bundle via a cryptographic digest.
