# V4-15.08 The Qualification Ladder

[[Home|Home]] · [[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/Volume 4 Overview|Volume Overview]] · [[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/04 Governing the Machine/15 Adversarial Verification/V4-15 Adversarial Verification|V4-15 Adversarial Verification]]

^sec-verification-qualification-ladder

A desktop simulator can stall the proposer of F1 across every combination of speed and payload overnight, but it cannot sag the drive bus of F4. The simulated power network has no buck-boost converter to lock out and no microcontroller to brown out and reset, so a million passing rollout assertions (`assert clearance >= 0`) there say nothing about F4. That is the digital twin delusion of [[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/04 Governing the Machine/15 Adversarial Verification/V4-15.02 The Claim a Fault Trial Must Test#^sec-verification-fault-injection|section 15.1]], and the remaining question is where, precisely, a given simulator stops being evidence.

The gap between simulation and reality stems directly from what the simulator abstracts away. When a physics simulation omits embedded target execution and electrical dynamics, it relies on five pervasive idealizations, each of which must be evaluated against the specific claim under test:

1.  **Computation takes zero virtual time:** The simulator pauses its simulation clock while the neural policy executes. This treats heavy neural forward passes as instantaneous, completely decoupling inference latency from physical base velocity (${d_{\text{blind}}} = {v} {\tau_{\text{delay}}}$).
2.  **Memory bandwidth is infinite:** Tensors pass across shared in-memory references. This completely omits the shared-memory contention, cache evictions, and DMA serialization stalls that govern real-time execution on an edge SoC.
3.  **Power rails are infinitely stiff:** Power supplies are modeled as idealized constant-voltage sources (${V_{\text{bus}}} = \text{const}$). This ignores the resistive droop (${\Delta} {V_{\text{bus}}} = {I} {R}$) of the drive bus under sudden current steps (sized for this machine in notebook [15.2](https://mlsysbook.ai/vol4/15_verification/15_verification.html#nbk-verification-supply-headroom)), the inductive flyback spikes from sharp current transients, and the resulting brownout resets.
4.  **Clocks are perfectly synchronized:** The simulation advances monotonically in fixed discrete time-steps (${\Delta} {t}$). This eliminates asynchronous clock drift, interrupt jitter, and transceiver arbitration races between the neural host and the real-time safety microcontroller.
5.  **Contact mechanics are simplified:** Numerical contact solvers approximate Coulomb friction with linearized friction cones or convex relaxations that permit artificial creep under static load ([[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/04 Governing the Machine/15 Adversarial Verification/V4-15.16 Source References#^ref-todorov2014convex|Todorov 2014]]). They routinely neglect the transition between static and kinetic friction, dynamic tire slip, and brake-lining glazing during emergency stops.

↩︎ Resistive droop on a shared drive bus and the brownout resets it causes are established in **[[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/01 Machine Anatomy/02 The Physical Body/V4-02.07 Electrical Power Integrity#^sec-body-power-distribution-impedance|Power distribution impedance, transient droop, and brownout resets]]**.

Each qualification environment closes, breaks, or substitutes different links in the chain connecting sensing, inference, communication buses, motor drives, and physical mechanisms. The **qualification ladder** orders four verification environments by how much of the cyber-physical loop is physically executed rather than simulated: software-in-the-loop, processor-in-the-loop (PIL), hardware-in-the-loop (HIL), and physical fault rigs. Software-in-the-loop is the lowest rung and the physical rig the highest, so climbing the ladder means making more of the loop physical. As physical hardware replaces numerical models, test fidelity rises, but trial throughput drops by orders of magnitude. This progression is not, however, an evidentiary hierarchy in which higher rungs subsume lower ones. Each rung yields a distinct category of empirical evidence while remaining blind to physical phenomena outside its boundary. A million successful software-in-the-loop rollouts reveal nothing about bus contention, DMA stalls, clock drift, or supply rail sag on the embedded hardware.

### What Each of the Four Rungs Makes Physical

^sec-verification-qualification-rungs

On the warehouse mobile manipulator, software-in-the-loop evidence terminates at the boundary of the numerical plant model; a mechanical brake modeled merely as a first-order lag cannot capture pad thermal glazing, wheel slip, or torque saturation. Processor-in-the-loop (PIL) execution introduces the cross-compiled binary running on the actual target silicon (such as an ARM Cortex-R or RISC-V safety core), validating fixed-point quantization, instruction timing, and stack headroom. Hardware-in-the-loop (HIL) benches mount the two-processor architecture, with its physical fieldbus, power regulators, and motor drive stages, against a hard real-time FPGA plant model or an instrumented dynamometer. Finally, physical fault rigs close the full loop with the production chassis, mechanical brakes, wire harness, and physical warehouse floor.

↩︎ The two-processor architecture, which runs the permission path on a safety microcontroller apart from the learned proposer’s application processor, is established in **[[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/01 Machine Anatomy/04 The Nervous System/V4-04.03 The Independence Requirement and the Two-Processor Hypothesis#^sec-nervous-silicon-partitioning|The Independence Requirement and the Two-Processor Hypothesis]]**.

Two operational testing paradigms exist alongside this qualification ladder, but both break the closed loop entirely. Trace replay feeds recorded sensor streams into the candidate controller; while it preserves authentic sensor noise and optical artifacts, the recorded frames reflect the actions of the original historical policy rather than reacting to the candidate’s new commands. Shadow operation runs the candidate model in parallel with an incumbent, validated controller. If the candidate proposes an aggressive velocity vector that would drive the base into a storage rack, the logger records the telemetry while the incumbent controller safely halts the base. Because neither approach permits candidate actions to alter future state observations, neither reveals the closed-loop physical consequences of an erroneous command.

A production shadow deployment cannot allow a stalled proposer to drive toward a human worker at a rack end to see if the brakes engage in time. A software simulator cannot expose a timing race between transceiver silicon buffers. An instrumented physical dynamometer cannot execute the tens of millions of randomized runs required to bound extreme distribution tails. Systems engineers must therefore route each fault injection scenario to the specific rung on the ladder that embodies its underlying physical and causal mechanism. Coverage of the causal loop grows with each higher rung, from decision logic alone to drives and floor contact, while trial throughput shrinks, so no rung dominates the others ([[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/04 Governing the Machine/15 Adversarial Verification/V4-15.08 The Qualification Ladder#^fig-15-qualification-ladder|figure 2]]).

Selecting the proper qualification rung requires identifying the exact premise that a fault challenges. Validating the software lease expiry under CPU contention in F1 requires target silicon execution to capture true processor scheduling interference. In contrast, evaluating supply rail droop in F4 demands the production power distribution network and independent oscilloscope instrumentation across the DC bus. Similarly, certifying a stopping-distance budget requires a fully ballasted physical chassis operating over a qualified floor surface. A sound verification strategy uses high-throughput simulation to search the combinatorial operational space for edge cases, then reproduces those critical scenarios on the specific hardware rung that contains the physical mechanism under test. Agreement between two rungs validates only the shared causal links common to both; [[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/04 Governing the Machine/15 Adversarial Verification/V4-15.08 The Qualification Ladder#^tbl-15-qualification-ladder|table 3]] outlines these strict evidentiary boundaries.

### Where Each Rung’s Simulation Boundary Cuts the Loop

^sec-verification-qualification-limits

Replacing physical wire harnesses, ground returns, and distributed actuator nodes with desktop simulations strips away inductive voltage droop, transmission reflections, and electromagnetic interference (EMI). Every qualification rung has a simulation boundary, the interface where numerical models, emulated buses, or replayed traces sever the exchange of energy and information between computation and mechanics, and no environment can certify properties that depend on physics outside that boundary. Software-in-the-loop testing falsifies algorithmic decision logic and state-machine transitions across broad parametric sweeps. PIL uncovers numerical divergences from cross-compilation and profiles worst-case execution time on target silicon, yet because it funnels input and output over a hardware debug interface (such as a JTAG port or serial UART link), it cannot stress physical communication buses. HIL brings in the true physical transceivers, bus contention, and electrical transients, while leaving contact mechanics to real-time models. Finally, physical fault rigs subject the structural plant to unmodeled Coulomb friction, tire deformation, and thermal dissipation.

A higher rung therefore does not subsume or obsolete the lower ones. Verification is an exercise in constrained optimization across a Pareto frontier: parallel software-in-the-loop clusters explore millions of permutations of obstacle layouts and lighting variations. Physical rigs, by contrast, capture true contact mechanics at the expense of manual resets, mechanical wear, and thermal recovery between runs. Each stage occupies a unique operating point balancing combinatorial test throughput against physical causal fidelity. A mechanism omitted from a model cannot fail within it. If a simulator omits Coulomb breakaway friction and stator inductive lag, a simulated robot joint moves immediately upon receiving a torque command. On real hardware, however, that same joint remains stationary during a stiction dead time before lagging behind the commanded trajectory. This is a classic manifestation of the omitted-mechanism gap, which no amount of software simulation can uncover.

To guard against false confidence, every empirical verification claim must explicitly catalog its causal envelope: which links in the sensing-computation-actuation loop were physically real, which were simulated, which were replayed from static traces, which were bypassed, and which remained unobserved. Stating that the safety permission path halted the mobile base within 969.0 mm is meaningless in isolation. An engineer must specify whether the wheel-speed velocity signal was computed by a numerical ODE solver, streamed from a physical optical encoder over a saturated CAN bus, or read out from an in-memory replay buffer. Mapping this boundary exposes the physical assumptions behind each pass and prevents latent hardware hazards from hiding behind passing software assertions.

### Scope of a Formal Verification Result

^sec-verification-formal-limits

A formal proof that a permission filter preserves safety over a mathematical plant model still cannot prevent a crash if the tires slip on a wet floor, the camera frames arrive late, or the proof is asked to cover a vision-language-action proposer fifty times larger than any network a complete verifier can solve. Formal proof provides a fifth source of evidence alongside the physical qualification ladder, but a proof holds only inside its mathematical model of plant states, sensor noise, and bounded disturbances. Translating that guarantee to the embodied machine still rests on empirical measurements of plant dynamics, observation deadlines, bus latencies, and actuator torque limits. Which components of the running machine a proof can reach at all depends on the current scale limits of neural-network verification.

Tools for formal neural-network verification operate in one of two regimes: open loop or closed loop. An open-loop verifier checks an **open-loop property**: given any input in a specified bounded set (for example, every camera frame within an $\ell_\infty$ ball around a nominal image, meaning every frame whose pixels each lie within ${\epsilon_{\text{adv}}}$ of the nominal values), the network’s output is guaranteed to satisfy a declared constraint. Two algorithmic primitives drive these tools:

- **Bound propagation** pushes the input set forward through the network layer by layer. By replacing each nonlinear activation with linear relaxation bounds, it computes a sound, conservative bounding box around the outputs. If this enclosure satisfies the property, the proof succeeds immediately.
- When bound propagation leaves property satisfaction ambiguous, **branch and bound** partitions the input space or splits ambiguous piecewise-linear activations into distinct linear regimes, recomputing bounds over each sub-domain. For supported network and property classes, a complete search can decide the property in principle, but it scales exponentially with the number of split activations in the worst case; a timeout or unknown result is not a proof. Solvers such as Reluplex ([[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/04 Governing the Machine/15 Adversarial Verification/V4-15.16 Source References#^ref-katz2017reluplex|Katz et al. 2017]]) and Marabou ([[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/04 Governing the Machine/15 Adversarial Verification/V4-15.16 Source References#^ref-wu2024marabou|Wu et al. 2024]]) formulate these activation states as satisfiability modulo theories (SMT) or mixed-integer linear programming (MILP) problems, whereas the α,β-CROWN verifier accelerates bound propagation and branch-and-bound exploration directly on GPUs ([[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/04 Governing the Machine/15 Adversarial Verification/V4-15.16 Source References#^ref-xu2021alphacrown|Xu et al. 2021]]; [[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/04 Governing the Machine/15 Adversarial Verification/V4-15.16 Source References#^ref-wang2021betacrown|Wang et al. 2021]]).

Verifying a **closed-loop property** poses a far steeper challenge. Rather than checking a static input-output mapping, closed-loop verification asks whether the continuous state of a physical plant driven by a neural policy remains within a safe invariant set over an extended time horizon. To answer this, reachability analysis evaluates the continuous trajectories of the physical plant under bounded disturbances. Forward reachability tools, such as NNV, Verisig, and POLAR, compose the network’s output bounding box with the differential equations of the physical plant at each discrete control step, computing forward reachable sets for compact controllers on low-dimensional dynamical systems ([[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/04 Governing the Machine/15 Adversarial Verification/V4-15.16 Source References#^ref-tran2020nnv|Tran et al. 2020]]; [[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/04 Governing the Machine/15 Adversarial Verification/V4-15.16 Source References#^ref-ivanov2019verisig|Ivanov et al. 2019]]; [[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/04 Governing the Machine/15 Adversarial Verification/V4-15.16 Source References#^ref-huang2022polar|Huang et al. 2022]]). Backward reachability via **Hamilton-Jacobi (HJ) reachability analysis** instead frames safety as a zero-sum differential game between the control policy and worst-case environmental disturbances. It computes the exact maximal safe invariant set as the zero level set of an optimal control value function (**[[05 Reference/Volume IV - Physical AI/V4 Control and Dynamics#^sec-appendix-control-hj-reachability|Hamilton-Jacobi reachability and worst-case differential games]]**) ([[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/04 Governing the Machine/15 Adversarial Verification/V4-15.16 Source References#^ref-mitchell2005time|Mitchell et al. 2005]]; [[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/04 Governing the Machine/15 Adversarial Verification/V4-15.16 Source References#^ref-bansal2017hamilton|Bansal et al. 2017]]). While Hamilton-Jacobi reachability provides non-conservative safety certificates for continuous nonlinear dynamics, solving the underlying Hamilton-Jacobi-Isaacs partial differential equation on a Eulerian grid scales exponentially with continuous state dimension. Consequently, Hamilton-Jacobi reachability is computationally tractable for decoupled, low-dimensional subsystems (such as a single revolute joint or longitudinal base stopping), whereas forward reachability tools struggle with the rapid accumulation of over-approximation error across long planning horizons. NNV also supports bounded image-input robustness analysis through ImageStars ([[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/04 Governing the Machine/15 Adversarial Verification/V4-15.16 Source References#^ref-tran2020nnv|Tran et al. 2020]]), but those image results do not establish an end-to-end closed-loop guarantee for the running machine’s high-resolution vision-language-action stream. Its permission path instead exposes a handful of calibrated physical states and a declared stopping invariant (**[[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/03 Running the Machine/12 Safety Enforcement/V4-12 Safety Enforcement#^sec-enforcement|Safety Enforcement]]**).

[[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/04 Governing the Machine/15 Adversarial Verification/V4-15.08 The Qualification Ladder#^tbl-15-verification-state-of-practice|Table 4]] benchmarks the state of the art in formal verification. In the 2025 International Verification of Neural Networks Competition (VNN-COMP 2025), the largest scored benchmark network contained 138M parameters, with the vast majority of evaluated models containing fewer than a million parameters ([[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/04 Governing the Machine/15 Adversarial Verification/V4-15.16 Source References#^ref-kaulen2025vnncomp|Kaulen et al. 2025]]). By contrast, the running machine’s intent model, the larger of its two learned proposers, contains 7B parameters, roughly 51× larger than that benchmark outlier. Both proposers also ingest an unconstrained temporal stream of high-resolution camera frames rather than an analytically bounded input set, and a local pixel-perturbation specification alone does not establish stopping safety across changing warehouse scenes and physical states.

The cited results do not establish complete formal verification of this deep proposer under its deployment stream; both computational scale and the absence of a justified visual specification remain barriers. This separation is the third law, proposal is not permission (principle [[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/03 Running the Machine/V4 Running the Machine#^pri-vol4-proposal-permission|3]]), seen from the verifier’s side. The learned proposer is evaluated and stress-tested empirically, and formal proof is reserved for the compact, deterministic permission path, which reads a handful of calibrated physical measurements (wheel speeds, lidar clearances, payload mass) and enforces an analytically tractable stopping invariant that a proof can reach.

Even within the permission path, high-dimensional reachability calculations, nonlinear contact dynamics, and hybrid switching modes can cause mathematical over-approximations to become prohibitively conservative. These practical limits do not render formal analysis useless. Instead, they dictate engineering restraint: they favor designing compact, mathematically transparent permission filters with explicit physical premises that can be verified in minutes, leaving the unmodeled electrical and mechanical dynamics to be systematically challenged on the physical rungs of the qualification ladder.

**Checkpoint 15.2: Rung choice and the reach of a proof**

A rung, like a proof, supports only the mechanisms it contains.

**Rung selection**: For F2 (a frame held at the image signal processor), F3 (lost fieldbus frames), and an actuator jam, can you name the lowest rung that contains the causal mechanism and one mechanism that rung still idealizes away?

**Agreement between rungs**: Can you explain why a software-in-the-loop pass and a HIL pass on the same scenario support only the mechanisms both rungs represent?

**Replay vs. shadow operation**: Can you explain why neither trace replay nor shadow operation shows the physical consequence of the candidate’s command?

**Proof scope**: Can you explain why the cited closed-loop results support analyzing the permission path without establishing a guarantee for the learned proposers, and list the premises such a proof leaves to physical test?
