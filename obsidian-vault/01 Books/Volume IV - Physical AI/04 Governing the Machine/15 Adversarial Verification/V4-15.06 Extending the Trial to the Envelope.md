# V4-15.06 Extending the Trial to the Envelope

[[Home|Home]] · [[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/Volume 4 Overview|Volume Overview]] · [[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/04 Governing the Machine/15 Adversarial Verification/V4-15 Adversarial Verification|V4-15 Adversarial Verification]]

^sec-verification-extending-trial

The F1 record fixes one trial at one envelope point, and even a clean pass there says nothing about the points around it. The operating-envelope definition already warned that the danger lies at coupled corners, where two parameters that are each within their declared ranges combine to break the claim. Extending the trial therefore means choosing which further points to test, and that choice follows from how the machine’s physics couples its variables.

### Coupled Envelope Corners Versus One-Variable Sweeps

^sec-verification-boundary-coupling

Sweeping travel speed alone on dry concrete and floor friction alone at a crawl passes both checks while missing the corner where aisle speed meets an inspected floor. Because physical mechanics, compute pipeline latency, and electrical power distribution interact at run time, independent minimum and maximum sweeps for individual variables leave the dangerous corners of the operating envelope untested:

![[90 Assets/Volume IV/15 Verification/Margin Floor Friction Ceiling Knee c92591f2d6.svg]]

*Optimistic all-weight screening curve of the speed ceiling against floor friction: flat at 1.44 meters per second for friction above 0.204, falling below it to 1.23 meters per second at a friction of 0.12. A dashed horizontal line at the 1.3 meters per second aisle speed turns red, over a shaded region, where it lies above the curve.*

*The 1.3 m/s aisle speed clears the dry-floor ceiling but not the inspected floor’s.*

- **Surface friction and travel speed**: The 1.3 m/s aisle speed and an inspected floor friction of 0.12 each fall well within their declared individual operating ranges. In combination, however, they fail even the optimistic all-weight traction screen. On low-friction surfaces, the tire-floor interface, not mechanical braking capacity, sets an upper limit on deceleration; in the all-weight screen it falls to 1.18 m/s². Consequently, the resident stopping maneuver elongates: the required stopping distance at 1.3 m/s expands to 1.19 m, exceeding the available 1.10 m clearance. The optimistic screening ceiling on that floor drops to 1.23 m/s, and the actual speed limit must use the qualified loaded lower bound rather than assuming this ceiling is attainable.
- **Contact nonlinearities and dynamic load transfer**: Real warehouse floors rarely have a constant friction coefficient ${\mu}$. Instead, surface friction varies dynamically based on local contamination (such as concrete dust, ambient moisture, or oil films), wheel tread heating, and transient wheel loading. On the running machine only the two driven hub wheels brake against the floor, so the traction ceiling is ${\mu} {g} {\phi_{\text{drive}}}$, where ${\phi_{\text{drive}}}$ is the share of the weight those wheels carry (derived in the free-body diagram of **[[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/01 Machine Anatomy/02 The Physical Body/V4-02 The Physical Body#^sec-body|The Physical Body]]**, **[[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/01 Machine Anatomy/02 The Physical Body/V4-02.04 Kinetic Momentum#^fig-02-braking-fbd|figure]]**). During hard braking, the inertial load ${m}{a_{\text{brake}}}$ acting at the center of gravity pitches the body forward and can move normal force off the driven wheels onto the front caster, lowering ${\phi_{\text{drive}}}$ exactly when traction is needed. If the drives demand braking torque sized for the static load share, the driven wheels slip and the net braking force collapses. Under a constant-friction approximation, the braking distance then grows as ${v}^2 / (2{\mu} {g} {\phi_{\text{drive}}})$, inversely with the friction actually delivered at the driven wheels and quadratically with speed.
- **Computational and electrical coupling**: Memory and power subsystems couple directly into safety margins. A compute-heavy forward pass of the vision-language-action intent model executing on the application SoC can saturate unified memory bandwidth. This contention delays camera-buffer processing and postpones the compact evidence handoff to the safety microcontroller, inflating the observation age and, with it, the pre-brake delay ${\tau_{\text{delay}}}$. Because stopping distance scales linearly with total reaction delay (**[[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/01 Machine Anatomy/02 The Physical Body/V4-02.04 Kinetic Momentum#^eq-body-stopping-distance|equation 2.2]]**), a 30 ms memory stall consumes 39 mm of physical clearance at aisle speed. On the electrical bus, the dominant transient coupling originates from the motor drive actuators rather than the neural compute accelerator. At its maximum 60 W draw, the SoC pulls approximately 1.6 A from a depleted battery bus, only 2.2 percent of the overall 70 A transient, whereas the two drive motors demand up to 50 A during peak torque transients. Severe acceleration can sag the common DC bus across battery internal resistance and wiring harness impedance, while bursty inference activity primarily stresses the SoC’s local point-of-load regulators, a localized transient that the F4 campaign must isolate and measure separately.
- **Payload mass and kinematic posture**: Arm posture and payload distribution couple directly to stopping performance through vehicle stability and load transfer. Under braking, the base tips about its front caster whenever deceleration exceeds ${g}\,{d_{\text{lead}}}/{h_{\text{cg}}}$, where ${h_{\text{cg}}}$ is the height of the center of gravity and ${d_{\text{lead}}}$ its distance behind that caster. This is the tip-over ceiling computed in **[[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/01 Machine Anatomy/02 The Physical Body/V4-02.05 Actuator Transmission Limits#^sec-body-electrical-time-constants|Electrical time constants, back-EMF, and current clamping]]**, which the credible deceleration respects only while ${h_{\text{cg}}}/{d_{\text{lead}}}$ stays below the ratio computed there. Raising the arm above a storage rack raises ${h_{\text{cg}}}$, and reaching forward shortens ${d_{\text{lead}}}$, so posture moves the machine toward that ceiling. The tip-over threshold can bind before tire traction saturates, and ISO 3691-4 mandates physical stability verification for driverless industrial trucks ([[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/04 Governing the Machine/15 Adversarial Verification/V4-15.16 Source References#^ref-iso3691part4|International Organization for Standardization 2023]]). Unloaded trials support only their tested configurations; they cannot establish the fully loaded deceleration or the complete operating envelope. The braking-limit check of **[[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/01 Machine Anatomy/02 The Physical Body/V4-02.04 Kinetic Momentum#^sec-body-stopping-distance-prebrake|Kinematic stopping distance and pre-brake delay]]** left both lengths unmeasured, so the envelope cannot yet certify that the demanded deceleration stays below the tipping threshold; a tilt-table test or multi-axis corner-scale measurement of the fully loaded machine must supply them.
- **Thermal throttling and fallback execution**: Sustained thermal stress can induce dynamic processor throttling, delaying action proposal generation and degrading mission throughput. In an architecture protected by an independent, verified resident stop, a missed watchdog renewal safely triggers a bounded, fail-safe stopping trajectory rather than indefinitely expanding stopping delay. Nevertheless, multi-axis compound verification must ensure that simultaneous bus contention, sensor packet drops, or voltage droops do not compromise that fallback routine itself.

### Physical Disturbances Versus Digital Input Perturbations

^sec-verification-physical-perturbations

A vision model that survives synthetic pixel noise injected into an input tensor can still go blind when low-angle warehouse glare saturates the physical camera lens and its auto-exposure loop. Digital perturbations and physical disturbances challenge an embodied machine at different interfaces. Synthetic tensor noise tests how sensitive a network’s decision boundary is to small numerical shifts in representation space while bypassing the physical sensor altogether. An optical disturbance, by contrast, exercises the lens, coatings, auto-exposure and gain control, demosaicing, and camera interface timing. Neither test substitutes for the other, so a threat model must state whether an injected fault corrupts digital packets in flight, perturbs the physical environment, or hits both at once.

Evaluating the rack-end safety claim requires injecting realistic physical disturbances: stray illumination and direct glare that blind camera sensors, mechanical chassis vibration that corrupts inertial measurements, and floor transitions that slash available braking traction. Every physical injection requires a repeatable physical actuator and an independent ground-truth witness. For instance, glare injection requires calibrated illumination measurements beside the lens together with the declared optical geometry, spectrum, and temporal waveform. Chassis vibration requires external optical motion capture independent of onboard wheel odometry. Floor variations require standardized friction-measurement drag sleds. In cyber-physical verification, receiving a syntactically valid sensor packet with correct checksums over an internal bus confirms only that the digital transmission succeeded; it provides no guarantee that the estimated state accurately reflects physical reality.

### What the Envelope Claim Leaves Open

^sec-verification-falsification-formulation

Passing the initial F1 trial at one speed and arm posture still leaves neighboring speeds, higher centers of gravity, and worst-case injection timings untested. That first diagnostic run suppresses trajectory renewal while the base is in motion and measures the deceleration-onset timestamp and stopping distance. Subsequent falsification campaigns systematically vary initial velocities, arm configurations, and injection phases to hunt for minimal operating margins ([[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/04 Governing the Machine/15 Adversarial Verification/V4-15.10 Searching Beyond the First Trial#^sec-verification-falsification-trajectory-formulation|section 15.9.2]]). Formal methods, high-fidelity simulation, and physical hardware testing provide complementary evidence, but none can compensate for an unstated physical premise or an unverified operating assumption.

An operating boundary remains defensible only when every parameter carries its physical unit, traceable provenance, calibrated uncertainty, and designated engineering owner. Specifying a credible deceleration of 2 m/s² remains an incomplete assertion until the verification evidence documents its origin. The evidence must show whether it was measured during physical stopping trials at maximum payload mass, extracted from an actuator manufacturer’s datasheet, or computed using a theoretical friction model. It must also give the measurement uncertainty of the external tracking reference. Any hardware modification, whether a new polyurethane wheel tread compound or new motor-drive firmware pushed across the EtherCAT fieldbus, obligates the component owner to re-evaluate whether the operational boundary has shifted.

Validating an operating envelope requires attacking its boundary surfaces rather than testing comfortable interior states. Evaluating an unloaded base crawling across a dry floor with pristine camera frames proves only that nominal execution works under ideal conditions. Instead, the verification harness distributes test points across three regimes. Points *just inside* the envelope verify that the control policy preserves closed-loop stability under the largest permissible operational stress. Points *on the boundary* confirm that safety margins survive nominal sensor noise and compute jitter. Points *just outside* verify that the safety enforcer reliably preempts the policy, executing a deterministic stop that halts the base within the survival envelope before clearance is exhausted.

A physical hardware malfunction and an algorithmic functional insufficiency require different verification strategies. A stereo camera can transmit uncorrupted, on-time video packets over PCIe while ambient glare blinds its depth estimation. A complete campaign must therefore inject faults into both the physical execution mechanisms and the sensory streams on which those mechanisms rely. Adding an enforcer module does not let it observe what its sensors cannot measure; when physical sensing cannot distinguish safe states from hazardous ones, the architect must restrict the operating envelope to conditions where safety can be enforced. Once every timing constraint, latency budget, and environmental threshold of that envelope is specified in physical units, the design claims from earlier chapters become concrete targets for fault injection, each paired with a boundary threshold that must trigger protective intervention.

**Systems Perspective 15.2: The hardware falsification principle**

A hardware-dependent safety claim needs an observable challenge to its physical premises. If the campaign cannot inject the relevant disturbance or retain the required witness, the record must preserve that verification gap.
