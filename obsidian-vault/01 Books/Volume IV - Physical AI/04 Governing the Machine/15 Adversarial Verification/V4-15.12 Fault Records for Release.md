# V4-15.12 Fault Records for Release

[[Home|Home]] · [[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/Volume 4 Overview|Volume Overview]] · [[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/04 Governing the Machine/15 Adversarial Verification/V4-15 Adversarial Verification|V4-15 Adversarial Verification]]

^sec-verification-fault-manifest

A raw telemetry trace sitting on a testbench digitizer cannot certify an autonomous machine for release if nothing proves which safety claim, envelope corner, or software and hardware build produced it. In an adversarial campaign, a completed F1 trial records the full event chain across independent physical channels: the final proposal renewal, the watchdog lease expiration, the active braking response, and the final mechanical stop. The release authority can credit that trial only when the measured trace is verifiably bound to the safety claim, the tested envelope point, and the exact software and hardware builds under evaluation. The fault record establishes this link by binding the structured test header to the retained evidence bundle through a cryptographic digest.

The release authority receives these claim-specific **fault records** together with the coverage records needed to judge their physical scope. Combined, they form an auditable **fault-record set** that joins the individual fault records, qualification environments, justified sim-to-real transfer bounds, statistical tail estimates, and known coverage gaps. Rather than silently omitting unmeasured or inapplicable fields, the schema marks them explicitly so that untested operating regimes cannot pass for verified boundaries. This fault-record set feeds directly into the operational safety case for release, though its schema and cryptographic signatures carry no authority beyond the physical evidence recorded inside them.

Along with design contracts, the motion-authority cases A1–A4, and the placement allocations established during system staging, the fault records draw on two upstream records: the omission log of the policy manifest (**[[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/02 Teaching the Machine/06 Policy Training/V4-06.09 The Policy Manifest#^sec-training-policy-manifest|The Policy Manifest]]**) and the coverage-gap catalog of the evaluation record (**[[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/02 Teaching the Machine/07 Closed-Loop Evaluation/V4-07.09 The Evaluation Record#^sec-evaluation-logs|The Evaluation Record]]**). Those upstream records list the operating conditions, sensor degradation modes, and environmental disturbances that the learned policy proposer never saw during training. By pairing the record header with its evidence bundle, each fault record preserves the full provenance of the trial: the asserted claim, physical operating point, fault injection mechanism, hardware and software revisions, raw high-speed telemetry, detection mechanisms, response latencies, physical stopping outcomes, and any secondary anomalies observed during the run.

### The Fault-Record Set Specification

^sec-verification-contract-matrix

A pile of individual pass verdicts leaves a release review unable to tell which top-level safety claims have been substantiated across the envelope and which operating conditions remain untested. Every record in the fault-record set therefore exists to support one named assurance claim ${C}$ within the release safety case, and its header names that claim explicitly. In the safety case structure developed in **[[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/04 Governing the Machine/16 Deployment Release/V4-16.04 Safety Cases and Claims#^sec-release-assurance-grammar|Claims, arguments, and warrants]]**, the top-level assurance claim ${C}_0$ states a core physical invariant, such as halting within the clearance corridor (${d_{\text{stop}}} \le {D_{\text{clear}}}$), and splits into sub-claims ${C}_i$ covering specific operating envelopes, hardware fault classes, and fallback behaviors. The F1, F2, and F3 records support that stopping bound across communication and timing faults, showing that the safety permission path halts the loaded mobile base within its travel bound once proposal renewals cease (F1), when sensor observations arrive stale (F2), or when fieldbus frames drop in transit (F3). Where F1–F3 test information and timing loss, the F4 and actuator-jam records test electrical and mechanical survival. F4 shows that the held-up auxiliary power rail keeps the permission path alive through a primary supply collapse long enough to complete an emergency stop and set all mechanical holding brakes. The actuator-jam records show that the chassis stays within its lateral clearance corridor during a wheel-lock fault without driving the stator windings into thermal runaway. At the control boundary, the A1–A4 records support the claims that motion authority keeps a single writer, blocks control bypass, runs a deterministic fallback when supervisory handovers stall, and rejects forged command tokens. Alongside them, the proposer-injection records show whether tested adversarial proposals were caught by the runtime permission checks. Extending that result to every possible proposal still requires an architectural or formal argument that no source can bypass the permission path.

A physical trace supports an assurance claim ${C}$ only through a structured engineering argument. In a rack-end deceleration claim, for instance, a raw brake-onset trace supplies empirical evidence, but the accompanying argument must explain why the digitizer clock synchronization, fault-injection timing, payload distribution, and floor traction conditions test the claimed physical response. Gathering evidence across the qualification ladder (formal reachability bounds for the permission filter, bounded sim-to-real falsification campaigns, and hardware fault-injection trials on the rig) lets each assurance claim ${C}$ rest on complementary legs of evidence rather than a single isolated trial. How claims, arguments, and evidence fit together into an auditable assurance case, and the notation used to write that hierarchy down, are developed in **[[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/04 Governing the Machine/16 Deployment Release/V4-16.04 Safety Cases and Claims#^sec-release-assurance-grammar|Claims, arguments, and warrants]]**.

The fault records also separate three failure domains, because each asks a different engineering question about the same physical machine, even when a compound fault spans more than one domain:

- *Functional safety*: hardware and software component faults inside the safety-related control system, such as encoder failures, dropped fieldbus frames, or auxiliary power brownouts.
- *Safety of the intended functionality* (SOTIF): hazards that arise without hardware faults from algorithmic blind spots or sensor limits, such as perceptual occlusions, out-of-distribution optical flare, or corner-case proposer errors.
- *Security*: deliberate adversarial tampering with physical sensors, communication buses, or cryptographic tokens.

The consensus engineering standards covering each subsystem of the machine, along with their publication editions, are mapped in **[[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/04 Governing the Machine/16 Deployment Release/V4-16.04 Safety Cases and Claims#^sec-release-standards-map|The standards map for the running machine]]**. Passing an adversarial fault-injection campaign does not by itself establish regulatory compliance or justify operation in unmodeled environments; instead, the release argument weighs exactly which claims the assembled records substantiate and where residual operational risks remain. To support that review, the individual fault records, the per-rung coverage of the qualification ladder, the empirical sim-to-real discrepancy bounds, and the extreme value theory (EVT) tail parameters assemble into one cryptographically signed set. This fault-record set is the thirteenth record in the record chain, after the authority record and before the release manifest, and it carries verification evidence to the release gate.

↪ The record chain, numbered from the limit record to the residual-claims register, is developed in **[[05 Reference/Volume IV - Physical AI/V4 Systems and Hardware#^sec-appendix-systems-record-register|The record register]]**.

The signed fault-record set defines the evidentiary handoff between adversarial testing and operational release, with its full data schema specified in [[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/04 Governing the Machine/15 Adversarial Verification/V4-15.12 Fault Records for Release#^tbl-15-verification-matrix-contract|table 5]]:

1.  **Upstream Custody Lineage**:
    - Consumes the **authority record** (**[[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/04 Governing the Machine/14 Supervisory Intervention/V4-14.09 The Authority Record#^sec-intervention-authority-log|The Authority Record]]**) to check handover deadline timers (${T_{\text{timeout}}}$, **[[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/04 Governing the Machine/14 Supervisory Intervention/V4-14.03 Human Authority, Reaction Time, and Clearance#^sec-intervention-clearance-deadlines|Clearance-derived fallback deadlines]]**), duplicate-writer rejections (A1), and supervisory token integrity.
    - Consumes the **placement record** (**[[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/03 Running the Machine/13 Workload Placement/V4-13.09 The Placement Record#^sec-placement-hardware-allocation|The Placement Record]]**) to check bus contention limits, power-rail droop tolerances, and thermal dissipation thresholds.
    - Consumes the **enforcement record** and its admitted commands (**[[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/03 Running the Machine/12 Safety Enforcement/V4-12.12 The Enforcement Record#^sec-enforcement-telemetry|The Enforcement Record]]**) to check control barrier function (CBF) invariant sets, quadratic program (QP) filter execution deadlines, and fallback ladder transitions.
    - Consumes the **evaluation record** (**[[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/02 Teaching the Machine/07 Closed-Loop Evaluation/V4-07.09 The Evaluation Record#^sec-evaluation-logs|The Evaluation Record]]**) to cross-check offline covariate drift and simulation test catalogs against physical target-hardware runs.
2.  **Downstream Release Deliverables**:
    - Delivers to the **release manifest** and its safety case (**[[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/04 Governing the Machine/16 Deployment Release/V4-16.04 Safety Cases and Claims#^sec-release-safety-cases-claims|Safety Cases and Claims]]**) the empirical, claim-specific evidence and operational bounds needed by the release argument.
    - Delivers to the **residual-claims register** (**[[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/04 Governing the Machine/18 Engineering Physical AI/V4-18.04 The Epistemic Frontier#^sec-frontier-residual-claims-register|A residual-claims register]]**) the explicit list of unverified operating regimes, test-fixture bandwidth limits, and coverage gaps.

Capturing these eight fields in a signed record set prevents post-hoc threshold tuning and silent omission of failed or unobservable trials. Even a complete, cryptographically signed fault-record set, however, can be undermined if hidden electrical, bus, or clock couplings on the test bench corrupt the trials or blind the witnesses that score them. Four recurring pitfalls illustrate how shared resources across watchdogs, fieldbuses, power rails, and loggers can subvert a verification campaign before a single record reaches release.
