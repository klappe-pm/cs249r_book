# V4-15.05 Fault Oracles and Acceptance

[[Home|Home]] · [[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/Volume 4 Overview|Volume Overview]] · [[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/04 Governing the Machine/15 Adversarial Verification/V4-15 Adversarial Verification|V4-15 Adversarial Verification]]

^sec-verification-oracles-acceptance

A valid verification trace for the F1 safety claim must establish both timely deceleration onset and bounded stopping distance; observing that the robot base eventually stopped establishes neither guarantee. Without an oracle fixed in advance, post-hoc inspection takes over: engineers eyeball time-series plots after the run, seizing on gross symptoms such as whether Joule heating melted a motor phase wire or a controller threw an unhandled exception. The trial degenerates into an anecdote.

**Definition 15.2: Test oracle**

***Test oracle*** is the predicate, fixed in full before a trial runs, that compares each independently witnessed quantity of the trace with its bound from a declared reference event and returns the trial’s verdict.

1.  **Significance**: For F1, the oracle comprises two predicates evaluated from the last valid renewal: powered deceleration onset within 82 ms and total travel within 751.9 mm at aisle speed. Each predicate evaluates to a signed margin in its native physical unit. Retaining signed margins rather than binary passes enables near misses to inform subsequent trials and guides adversarial search algorithms toward razor-thin safety margins.
2.  **Distinction**: A pass criterion extracted from telemetry after a run is chosen with the outcome already visible; an oracle is mathematically precommitted. The oracle also differs from the design budget from which it is derived: the 969.0 mm design subtotal starts at sensor capture and protects a planned target trajectory, whereas a stalled proposer produces no planned trajectory to protect.
3.  **Common pitfall**: A persistent misconception in physical verification is that a robot base stopping short of a rack signifies a passed trial. In reality, stopping within the 1.10 m clearance distance can easily conceal a safety controller that missed its deceleration deadline, or a stop triggered by an unmodeled side effect, such as a tripped thermal breaker that cut drive-bus power, rather than the deterministic safety path named in the claim.

### Four Verdicts and the Invalid-Test Rule

^sec-verification-four-verdicts

If traction control derates motor torque over an uneven floor seam and slows the mobile base before fault injection, the robot encounters the fault below its declared operating speed. Conversely, if a hardware frame injector overflows its queue and drops an adversarial CAN message before it reaches the transceiver, the robot never encounters the fault at all. Scoring either aborted run as a pass manufactures artificial confidence, while scoring it as a system failure blames the vehicle for a defective test harness. Rigorous verification therefore enforces an **invalid-test rule** that separates testbed anomalies from the machine’s own performance.

Under that rule, the oracle returns one of four explicit verdicts. A `PASS` indicates that every precommitted timing and physical predicate held under verified preconditions. A `FAIL` indicates that the system violated one or more measured predicates. An `INVALID` verdict indicates that the fixture failed to establish declared test preconditions or the fault injection itself miscarried. An `UNOBSERVABLE` verdict indicates that ground-truth telemetry required to score the trial was corrupted or lost, while the partial trace is retained for diagnosis. Under an `INVALID` or `UNOBSERVABLE` verdict, any unmeasured quantity is logged as unavailable rather than set to zero. Neither verdict may ever be converted into a `PASS`, and neither is ever omitted from aggregate coverage reports. An `UNOBSERVABLE` trial supplies no verdict on the unmeasured claim; its missing evidence requires investigating both the witness system and the injected fault, rather than assuming the harness alone failed. Silently discarding runs with dropped telemetry or treating unobservable outcomes as passes introduces survivorship bias. If an inductive kick from an emergency brake solenoid emits an electromagnetic interference burst that crashes the data acquisition (DAQ) logger, discarding that run conceals the exact cyber-physical failure under test.

Precommitment separates empirical evidence from after-the-fact excuses. In physical fault injection, cyber-physical interactions are noisy and counterintuitive, so criteria negotiated after inspecting the trace inevitably drift to accommodate anomalous behavior. An unexpected overrun is easily dismissed as floor variation, or a deceleration onset that exceeds the 82 ms bound is excused because the vehicle came to rest before hitting the rack.

A precommitted record prevents that drift by locking down every physical predicate, measurement unit, normalization scale, response-time bound, and invalidation trigger before the first fault is triggered. The evaluation engine computes and logs a signed margin for each predicate, issuing a `PASS` verdict only when the declared preconditions and injection are verified, all required predicates are observable, and every timing bound and physical margin remains non-negative. If stopping distance exceeds its limit by five millimeters or powered braking begins $2\text{ ms}$ past its deadline, the record pinpoints the exact predicate that failed so engineers can trace whether the cause was degraded tire-floor friction, mechanical brake lag, motor thermal derating, or software diagnostic latency. Precommitment enforces the fourth law, permission must be earned (principle [[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/04 Governing the Machine/V4 Governing the Machine#^pri-vol4-evidence-bounds-authority|4]]), at the test boundary. Production deployment grants operational authority on the basis of these records, so any verdict scored under rules relaxed after the fact would grant authority on the accident of an outcome rather than the guarantee of a verified machine.

### The F1 Record and Its Missing Coverage

^sec-verification-manifest-coverage

A stopping trace separated from its precondition checks or oracle digest cannot prove which threshold it was scored against or whether the floor and payload matched the claim. A **fault record** prevents that separation by cryptographically binding a finalized metadata header to the independently retained sensorimotor trace. Identity fields in the header specify the trial identifier and safety claim, the physical envelope point, the fault injection parameters (mode, magnitude, and target subsystem), and a cryptographic digest of the precommitted oracle. Outcome fields record the measured detection and takeover latencies, the signed physical margins, the four-state verdict, a digest of the complete evidence bundle, and an asymmetric digital signature over the entire header, while the high-rate telemetry trace is archived inside the bundle.

The planned F1 record at the candidate restricted envelope point, 1 m/s on an inspected floor with a 50 kg payload, fills these fields ([[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/04 Governing the Machine/15 Adversarial Verification/V4-15.05 Fault Oracles and Acceptance#^tbl-15-f1-record|table 1]]). As with every proposer stall, the stopping distance oracle is anchored to the timestamp of the last valid lease renewal. Under the optimistic all-weight screen on a floor with friction coefficient ${\mu} =$ 0.12, [[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/04 Governing the Machine/15 Adversarial Verification/V4-15.03 The Operating Envelope#^eq-verification-f1-oracle|equation 1]] yields a stopping bound of 629.6 mm (corresponding to the sensitivity boundary in notebook [15.1](https://mlsysbook.ai/vol4/15_verification/15_verification.html#nbk-verification-reference-event-gap)). This distance is strictly shorter than the 831.2 mm capture-referenced budget subtotal at that speed. The capture-referenced subtotal accounts for upstream sensor evidence age and fixed geometric margins while omitting dispatch-to-admission communication delay; consequently, it cannot be substituted for the renewal-referenced oracle of F1 or an end-to-end appearance-referenced bound. Of the three bounds, only the renewal-referenced one contains the complete timing path; its braking premise still awaits loaded qualification. The capture-referenced subtotal has a value but omits the dispatch-to-admission interval. The appearance-referenced bound has no value at all, because it still lacks a witnessed appearance-to-capture interval as well as a measured dispatch-to-admission interval ([[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/04 Governing the Machine/15 Adversarial Verification/V4-15.05 Fault Oracles and Acceptance#^fig-15-reference-events|figure 1]]).

In the planned record ([[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/04 Governing the Machine/15 Adversarial Verification/V4-15.05 Fault Oracles and Acceptance#^tbl-15-f1-record|table 1]]), the outcome field contains no numerical value and is marked unavailable. Because no physical trials have been executed in this book, the field specifies the physical measurement that will eventually populate it; until empirical data are recorded and verified by independent witnesses, the record remains uncommitted and carries no verdict. The trial count in that field is a design choice of its own. The number of passing trials needed to bound the miss probability follows the zero-failure count of **[[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/02 Teaching the Machine/07 Closed-Loop Evaluation/V4-07.08 Trial Budgets, Stopping Rules, and Comparisons#^sec-evaluation-trials-per-claim|Sample size requirements per empirical claim]]**, which [[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/04 Governing the Machine/15 Adversarial Verification/V4-15.11 Interpreting Campaign Statistics#^sec-verification-campaign-statistics|section 15.10]] applies to an injected fault population.

The record schema maintains strict forensic separation among verdicts on a per-field basis. If an upstream breaker trips and prevents an injection, the harness marks the run `INVALID` and records the detection latency, takeover delay, and stopping margin as unavailable rather than coercing them to zero. Because the evidence bundle attaches an explicit validity flag and missing-data reason code to each measurement, the scoring oracle can inspect those flags before running any statistical or arithmetic evaluation, keeping unmeasured fields from corrupting pass-rate calculations, latency percentiles, or margin statistics.

A single aggregate percentage conceals where the verification campaign never ventured. A system that survives 98 percent of five thousand fault injection trials demonstrates little if every trial ran at nominal room temperature under negligible memory bus contention. Comprehensive verification requires reporting coverage across orthogonal dimensions: functional safety claims, physical envelope extremes, physical and logical injection sites, timing distribution tails, hardware build revisions, and concurrent multi-fault scenarios. For the warehouse mobile manipulator, physical envelope coverage verifies whether faults were injected under both empty and fully loaded rack states, at nominal aisle speed on dry concrete, at restricted speed on inspected surfaces, and under depleted DC drive-bus voltages. Timing tail coverage isolates injections scheduled to coincide with p99 operating system scheduling jitter in the permission loop or saturated DMA bus traffic. System verification cannot be declared complete when an aggregate coverage score exceeds a target percentage; rather, the minimum coverage across every individual dimension must satisfy the safety case requirements.[[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/04 Governing the Machine/15 Adversarial Verification/V4-15.17 Footnotes#^fn4|⁴]]

Repeated trials provide statistical evidence only when underlying physical or digital states vary. Executing a deterministic fault script five hundred times on a digital bench with identical initial register states, fixed clock dividers, and synchronized inputs traverses the exact same execution path on every iteration; it contributes no more statistical information than a single run. Conversely, on a physical test aisle, repetition samples real-world stochasticity: micro-variations in wheel-floor contact friction, asynchronous hardware interrupt arrivals, bus arbitration jitter, and phase drift between the camera exposure clock and the policy inference cycle. In that setting, repeated trials characterize the variance and map the dangerous tails of the end-to-end response-time distribution.

**Checkpoint 15.1: Oracles, verdicts, and reference events**

An F1 result carries meaning only through the oracle, reference event, and verdict rule fixed before the run.

**Scoring a trial**

**Verdict assignment**: Can you score three trials, one in which the injector dropped the fault packet before it reached the transceiver, one in which the logger crashed while the base stopped short of the rack, and one in which every predicate held on independent witnesses, and name the record fields each trial leaves unavailable?

**Reference event**: Can you explain why the F1 distance oracle starts at the last valid renewal and leaves out the evidence age and the two margins that the capture-referenced subtotal carries?

**Repetition vs. variation**: Can you explain why five hundred runs of a deterministic bench script count as one trial, while repeated runs on the test aisle sample a distribution?

**Estimating a margin**

**Onset estimate**: A trial at 1.3 m/s records powered deceleration onset 90 ms after the last valid renewal, with the lease expiring and the stop command issuing on time. Can you estimate the onset margin, the extra travel before braking begins, and whether the trial can pass even though the base stops well short of the rack?
