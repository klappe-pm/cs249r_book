# V4-15.07 Deriving the Fault List

[[Home|Home]] · [[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/Volume 4 Overview|Volume Overview]] · [[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/04 Governing the Machine/15 Adversarial Verification/V4-15 Adversarial Verification|V4-15 Adversarial Verification]]

^sec-verification-deriving-fault-list

The proposer stall fault (F1) originates from negating an operational claim: that proposal renewal proceeds reliably within its granted lease. Deriving a comprehensive fault list, however, requires two complementary methodologies. Inverting design records tests the explicit assumptions codified across the system architecture. Working forward from the physical realities of mechanics, power networks, and human interaction exposes the hazards and unmodeled edge cases that software design records routinely omit. Human interaction includes who is in view. A fault list that seeds only people standing upright leaves untested the kneeling, carrying, and seated people a learned detector may see later, so the groups of people the machine must detect enter the list as a coverage dimension of their own.

↪ How many people of each group a test campaign needs before its detection estimate can resolve the gap that matters is developed in **[[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/04 Governing the Machine/17 Responsible Physical AI/V4-17.06 When Detection Depends on Who Is Detected#^sec-responsibility-subgroup-intervals|Sample sizes for each group]]**.

### Inverting Design Records and the Five-Element Chain

^sec-verification-inverting-records

If the machine’s specification claims that the chunk policy renews its trajectory proposal every 50 ms at every speed up to the 1.5 m/s drive limit, the verification suite turns that claim inside out. It injects stalled renewals, delayed observations, and out-of-range encoder readings to see whether the permission path catches the violation in time. Every preceding chapter supplies claims that can be inverted in the same way. **[[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/02 Teaching the Machine/05 Physical Data/V4-05 Physical Data#^sec-data|Physical Data]]** through **[[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/02 Teaching the Machine/07 Closed-Loop Evaluation/V4-07 Closed-Loop Evaluation#^sec-evaluation|Closed-Loop Evaluation]]** state the learned policy’s training distribution, state-action assumptions, and inference latency ceilings. **[[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/03 Running the Machine/08 Sensor Perception/V4-08 Sensor Perception#^sec-perception|Sensor Perception]]** through **[[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/03 Running the Machine/13 Workload Placement/V4-13 Workload Placement#^sec-placement|Workload Placement]]** state bus allocations, observation freshness, and estimation bounds. **[[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/04 Governing the Machine/14 Supervisory Intervention/V4-14 Supervisory Intervention#^sec-intervention|Supervisory Intervention]]** states how quickly authority leaves a failing policy and which subsystem takes over.

Where the five-part test contract of [[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/04 Governing the Machine/15 Adversarial Verification/V4-15.02 The Claim a Fault Trial Must Test#^sec-verification-fault-injection|section 15.1]] states what makes any trial valid evidence, a **five-element chain** states how a single inverted claim is built into such a trial. Its first element supplies the contract’s bounded claim, its last element supplies the contract’s oracle, and the three between them specify the mechanism the trial exercises:

1.  **The negated condition:** the exact architectural or timing invariant violated (for example, proposal renewal halted after lease issue);
2.  **The physical injection point:** the exact hardware or bus interface where the disturbance enters (for example, an inline field-programmable gate array (FPGA) serializer, CAN FD disturbance node, or programmable DC load);
3.  **The expected detector:** the deterministic monitoring primitive tasked with catching the violation (for example, a lease countdown timer or evidence-epoch comparator);
4.  **The bounded response:** the deterministic transition executed by the enforcer (for example, revoking proposal authority and commanding the resident stop suffix); and
5.  **The observable pass condition:** the quantitative, independently witnessed metric that scores the trial (for F1 at aisle speed, onset $\le$ 82 ms and travel from renewal $\le$ 751.9 mm).

A recorded design property becomes a testable fault only when all five elements are fully defined and observable. If any element of this chain cannot be defined or measured, the architecture has a **verification gap**: the underlying claim cannot be falsified by empirical test. Systems engineers cannot ignore such gaps; resolving them requires dedicated hardware instrumentation (such as external optical trackers or non-intrusive trace macros), a narrower operational design domain, or a structural redesign of the execution path.

Inversion also applies to the learned proposer’s implicit assumptions, where distribution shift can induce functional insufficiency even when every observation frame arrives intact and on time. For example, a chunk policy trained in simulation on dry concrete with friction coefficient 0.60 proposes decelerations that an inspected floor at 0.12 turns into wheel slip. Inverting that evaluation claim requires injecting degraded traction conditions at the physical contact patch. Sensor bias, calibration drift, and input perturbations are injected for the same reason: a policy with high nominal reward can still command high-frequency actuator chatter once its inputs leave the training distribution. Under these conditions, an overly permissive execution path can destabilize the plant while executing syntactically valid commands, demonstrating that software commands and mechanical dynamics remain tightly coupled across the causal boundary.

At each inter-module contract, the fault list negates architectural guarantees directly at their physical handoff points: observation freshness, state validity, intent lifetime, planning deadlines, enforcement independence, and resource allocation. For the 60 ms chunk lease, the test harness replays the expired admitted record 65 ms after its last valid renewal, verifying that the permission path firmly refuses to extend execution authority. A concurrent proposal represents an entirely separate admission attempt: it must satisfy fresh evidence, kinematic state validity, mode consistency, and stopping-reserve checks before receiving a lease. To test the enforcer’s claimed independence from the policy processor on the selected discrete placement, the verification suite saturates the application processor’s memory interconnect with DMA traffic while independently timing the safety microcontroller. Host contention may delay proposal delivery and age its supporting evidence, but the enforcer must still satisfy its 400 μs execution deadline within every 1 ms control tick. This rigorous inversion prevents arbitrary over-testing: bus contention, clock skew, and supply fluctuations are injected precisely where the architecture claims tolerance, rather than drawing from generic fault menus that impose requirements the machine was never specified to meet.

Inversion has a structural limit: it surfaces only faults rooted in assumptions the design team explicitly wrote down. The resulting fault list is therefore only as comprehensive as the recorded architectural claims. If no engineering record documents how the base’s spring-applied brake behaves when its friction lining glazes under repeated emergency stops, inversion cannot synthesize the failure mode, and the hazard never reaches the test suite.

### Forward Physical Analysis and Common-Cause Modes

^sec-verification-forward-analysis

Drive wheels skid when commanded deceleration exceeds the traction limit of the contact patch. Brake linings glaze under repeated thermal cycles. Solid tires wear down and alter the rolling radius that wheel odometry relies on. Structural vibration gradually loosens camera mounts until spatial calibration drifts. Because these hazards arise from contact mechanics, heat, and wear rather than software bugs, no software abstraction represents them, and inverting design records will not uncover them. A second, independent fault source therefore works forward from the physical machine (its mechanical body, power distribution network, floor interface, and operating environment). It uses Nancy Leveson’s **Systems-Theoretic Accident Model and Processes (STAMP)** framework[[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/04 Governing the Machine/15 Adversarial Verification/V4-15.17 Footnotes#^fn5|⁵]] ([[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/04 Governing the Machine/15 Adversarial Verification/V4-15.16 Source References#^ref-leveson2011engineering|Leveson 2011]]) to trace how physical and control interactions produce hazards even when no software module has crashed.

Consider a motor drive that continues producing positive torque after receiving a stop command. A shorted inverter switch, welded contactor, or stuck gate-driver enable line can defeat the normal powered stopping trajectory. The test harness must independently observe delivered phase torque, rotor velocity, and the engagement of the secondary isolation path. While Safe Torque Off (STO) inhibits torque generation without providing electrical isolation, it cannot enforce nominal stopping distances on low-friction floors or prevent back-driving on gravity-loaded articulated joints. The safety architecture must specify a separately justified containment response alongside an independent physical measurement oracle. If no such path exists, the fault exposes an architectural omission rather than an operational delay that can borrow from the normal stopping budget.

↩︎ Safe Torque Off and the industrial stop categories are established in **[[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/03 Running the Machine/12 Safety Enforcement/V4-12.10 The Fallback Ladder#^sec-enforcement-stop-categories|Industrial stop categories and the safe torque off trap]]**.

Working forward exposes hardware fault classes that software design records rarely imply: loss of reference clocks, power supply brownouts that leave digital logic in indeterminate voltage states, pulse-width modulation (PWM) inverter switching noise coupling into differential encoder lines, corrupted non-volatile configuration registers, and reset lines that bridge domains intended to remain galvanically or logically isolated. For instance, if the safety microcontroller and the high-performance application processor share a single power-management integrated circuit (PMIC), a transient voltage droop during a sudden drive-current surge can reset both processors simultaneously just as the learned proposer loses trajectory tracking. A software interface model that assumes an ideal DC power supply omits this failure. An enforcer cannot arrest an errant policy if it loses operating power alongside the workload it supervises; the deterministic safety runtime must outlive the faulted workload using independently qualified execution and power resources. The running machine uses an isolated, capacitor-backed auxiliary supply to survive the declared supply fault.

Forward physical analysis likewise evaluates the architecture’s common-cause independence claims. Subsystems that appear strictly isolated on an architectural block diagram frequently share physical vulnerabilities. For example, the primary lidar communication lines and redundant safety bumper wiring may share a single mechanical cable carrier subject to pinch wear, depend on a shared analog voltage reference inside a multichannel converter, or rely on timer silicon containing identical silicon errata. An electrical transient that latches up both channels simultaneously destroys any safety argument predicated on statistical independence. **Common-cause analysis** traces harness routing, power distribution paths, and clock distribution trees to identify shared failure paths and justify independence against the declared fault set, rather than assume separation from the block diagram.

↩︎ The independence claims for the permission path’s execution unit, oscillator, power rail, and memory are established in **[[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/01 Machine Anatomy/04 The Nervous System/V4-04.08 Hardware Isolation and Fault Containment#^sec-nervous-hardware-isolation|Hardware Isolation and Fault Containment]]**.

This physical analysis extends directly to human error and foreseeable operational misuse. Warehouse personnel may jumper an optical rack-end sensor during maintenance, leave a manual pallet jack parked across an automated transit corridor, or unplug an acoustic warning beacon to silence workplace noise, while remote teleoperators may request transit speeds exceeding local aisle restrictions. When a remote assistant commands the vehicle’s full 1.5 m/s drive limit within a narrow aisle, the verification suite must confirm that the arbiter denies the command by capability constraint or clamps it to the admitted manual mode speed under an active `manual_drive` state. Furthermore, the deterministic permission check must evaluate the assistant’s command with the same scrutiny applied to autonomous proposals.

Working forward from the physical plant adds a representative mechanical failure mode, the actuator jam, to the record-derived inventory in [[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/04 Governing the Machine/15 Adversarial Verification/V4-15.07 Deriving the Fault List#^tbl-15-fault-classification|table 2]], though full verification still requires a plant-specific hazard analysis tailored to the vehicle’s mechanical kinematics and operational envelope. Alongside inverted design claims and forward physical hazards, the fault suite in [[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/04 Governing the Machine/15 Adversarial Verification/V4-15.07 Deriving the Fault List#^tbl-15-fault-classification|table 2]] also evaluates the four authority transition cases (A1–A4) established in **[[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/04 Governing the Machine/14 Supervisory Intervention/V4-14.09 The Authority Record#^sec-intervention-evidence-tuples|Per-tick evidence tuples and the fault injection suite]]** under the operating state that makes intervention most urgent: a fully loaded base approaching the rack end at velocity. The test campaign injects takeover requests at two speeds to distinguish the candidate moving transition from a request the clearance screen rejects; the operative design stops before handing over. At the 0.75 m/s slow-down, the partial-budget waiting margin is 751.6 ms, enough for the 605 ms in-loop takeover and the 63.3 ms dispatch-to-admission allocation together. **[[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/04 Governing the Machine/14 Supervisory Intervention/V4-14.03 Human Authority, Reaction Time, and Clearance#^sec-intervention-clearance-deadlines|Clearance-derived fallback deadlines]]** therefore identifies a candidate moving in-loop handover for qualification, subject to the complete session budget and live stopping checks. The trial must show that a handover left pending still ends in a local stop by its deadline. At the full 1.3 m/s aisle speed the margin is 100.8 ms, only 37.5 ms once the allocation is charged, which cannot hold the takeover, so the arbiter must reject the moving handover and dispatch a local stop before the deadline expires. An out-of-loop takeover begins only after a stop, from standstill, in an admitted mode. Evaluating either sequence solely while the vehicle is parked proves nothing about dynamic performance; the trial must demonstrate that rejection, timeout, and braking occur with bounded latency while the base moves.

### Physical Sensor Spoofing and Proposer Injection

^sec-verification-transduction-attacks

Whether sun glare washes out a camera lens by accident or an attacker shines a laser into the optics on purpose, the camera interface delivers a well-formed, checksum-valid packet whose physical reading is false. In both ambient disturbances and **transduction-layer cyber-physical attacks**, checksum validation and bus integrity checks pass cleanly while the robot’s view of the aisle is corrupted. When evaluating such an optical disturbance (retroreflective glare, strobed lighting, or lens occlusion), the test protocol must specify the illumination levels and vehicle approach vectors that challenge obstacle detection, the cross-sensor plausibility checks tasked with identifying the discrepancy, and the deterministic fallback maneuver. An independent ground-truth optical tracking system monitors obstacle clearance and base trajectory while camera streams are disturbed. The verification record must distinguish an actively detected anomaly followed by a controlled deceleration from a missed obstacle where collision was avoided purely by chance. If a monitoring subsystem cannot distinguish corrupted sensor data from valid physical evidence, that operational scenario constitutes a verification gap; documenting an aspirational safety monitor in an architecture diagram does not ensure it will fire under physical stress.

The learned proposer is itself a primary attack surface; targeting it tests the proposal boundary far more directly than conventional bus or memory faults. Because a vision-language-action (VLA) model consumes natural language instructions and camera frames directly, an adversary can manipulate the model’s outputs through either modality without touching a physical wire or intercepting a bus. Robey and colleagues demonstrated automated prompt generation that induced robots controlled by language models, including a commercial quadruped evaluated strictly as a black box, to execute harmful physical actions across diverse operational settings despite high-level safety guardrails ([[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/04 Governing the Machine/15 Adversarial Verification/V4-15.16 Source References#^ref-robey2025jailbreaking|Robey et al. 2025]]). On the visual side, Wang and colleagues showed that introducing a compact adversarial patch[[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/04 Governing the Machine/15 Adversarial Verification/V4-15.17 Footnotes#^fn6|⁶]] into a VLA’s camera field of view could degrade simulated manipulation success rates to zero ([[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/04 Governing the Machine/15 Adversarial Verification/V4-15.16 Source References#^ref-wang2025exploring|Wang et al. 2025]]). On a deployed warehouse machine, physical injection points encompass task instruction strings, floor placards, shipping container barcodes, or printed warning signs within the camera’s visual cone.

In semantic target grounding (**[[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/03 Running the Machine/10 Grounded Intent/V4-10.04 Semantic Request Translation to Geometric Proposals#^eq-intent-conformal-set|equation 10.3]]**), the intent model uses a conformal prediction set ${\mathcal{C}}$ calibrated to a marginal miscoverage rate ${\epsilon}$ to filter ambiguous hypotheses by requiring a singleton output ($|{\mathcal{C}}| = 1$). In an adversarial or out-of-distribution physical setting, however, an adversarial patch or optical distortion violates the exchangeability premise of the conformal calibration. An attacker can force an erroneous candidate to pass as a singleton ($|{\mathcal{C}}| = 1$), or the model can misclassify within its nominal ${\epsilon}$ error budget. Because the deterministic permission path does not parse task instructions or inspect the conformal set ${\mathcal{C}}$, it cannot distinguish an adversarial proposal from an authentic user command. Instead, under its qualified measurement and timing premises, the enforcer rejects or modifies an injected proposal when the requested physical action violates its monitored constraints: velocity and torque envelopes, dynamic stopping reserves, swept-volume occupancy, evidence freshness, or active lease duration. If an adversarial proposal meets these physical constraints and the authority checks, the enforcer may admit it, even if the robot picks an unintended bin, deposits cargo in an unauthorized clear aisle, or actuates an access gate prematurely. The permission path supports physical containment and dynamic feasibility under its declared premises; those checks do not establish semantic task correctness. This operational asymmetry highlights why systems architects decouple proposal synthesis from safety enforcement: protective safety guarantees must hold regardless of how an action was synthesized, while task-level semantic fidelity cannot be guaranteed by low-level enforcers. Consequently, the proposer-injection row in [[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/04 Governing the Machine/15 Adversarial Verification/V4-15.07 Deriving the Fault List#^tbl-15-fault-classification|table 2]] evaluates trials solely against independent physical oracles, recording uncommanded actions that remain within the admissible safety envelope as semantic coverage deficits rather than as safety violations.

↩︎ The limit that the permission path enforces physical feasibility rather than semantic task correctness is established in **[[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/03 Running the Machine/10 Grounded Intent/V4-10.08 Intent Grounding Failure Modes and Their Checks#^sec-intent-failure-modes|Intent Grounding Failure Modes and Their Checks]]**.

### Choosing the Injection Phase

^sec-verification-fault-optimization

Injecting a proposer stall immediately after a lease renewal forces the base to coast on open-loop setpoints for nearly the full lease duration, stressing tracking error and clearance. Injecting the same stall microseconds before lease expiration, by contrast, catches the arbiter right at the boundary between renewal and revocation. Because the instant a fault arrives within the control cycle changes which hardware and software paths bear the load, a verification campaign cannot treat repeated fault injections at arbitrary times as interchangeable trials. Recording the exact injection phase relative to the control tick turns phase into a controlled test variable. Sweeping across injection phase, fault duration, and disturbance magnitude exposes race conditions in the arbitration logic and supplies the signed safety margins that automated falsification algorithms search over ([[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/04 Governing the Machine/15 Adversarial Verification/V4-15.10 Searching Beyond the First Trial#^sec-verification-falsification-trajectory-formulation|section 15.9.2]]).

### Compound Faults and Coupled Disturbances

^sec-verification-compound-faults

When a drive-current surge sags a shared DC power rail, it does not wait for the perception pipeline or the safety microcontroller to finish its current cycle: a single electrical dip can brown out the application processor, freeze an image serializer, and jitter a shared clock all at once. While each row of [[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/04 Governing the Machine/15 Adversarial Verification/V4-15.07 Deriving the Fault List#^tbl-15-fault-classification|table 2]] tests one disturbance in isolation, real machines rarely fail one subsystem at a time. Combining rows at random would cause a combinatorial explosion without targeting realistic failures, so the campaign instead derives compound faults from shared hardware causes. The placement record (**[[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/03 Running the Machine/13 Workload Placement/V4-13.09 The Placement Record#^sec-placement-hardware-allocation|The Placement Record]]**) lists every physical resource that the permission path shares with the learned workloads, and each shared power rail, clock tree, or communication bus becomes a compound fault injected at the shared hardware root rather than at downstream software symptoms.

On the mobile manipulator, the primary shared vulnerability is the main DC drive bus, which powers the traction motor drives directly while feeding the application processor through an intermediate DC-DC converter. Meanwhile, the safety-critical permission path operates from an isolated, capacitor-backed hold-up rail. Notebook [15.2](https://mlsysbook.ai/vol4/15_verification/15_verification.html#nbk-verification-supply-headroom) evaluates the electrical headroom and timing budgets for both F4 fault injection trials using the vehicle’s electrical specifications.

**Napkin Math 15.2: The supply headroom**

**Problem**: At the depleted point, can the drive transient alone carry the shared bus below the lockout of the converter that feeds the application processor, and how much of the permission rail’s hold-up does the longest stop in the optimistic screening branch spend?

**Variables**:

- Depleted bus ${V_{\text{low}}} =$ 42 V and converter lockout ${V_{\text{uvlo}}} =$ 33 V (Datasheet).
- Source and harness resistance ${R} =$ 60 mΩ (Illustrative; needs a measured value) and drive transient ${I} =$ 70 A (Illustrative).
- Rail hold-up 2 s (Chosen) and spring-brake engagement ${t_{\text{engage}}} =$ 80 ms (Illustrative).
- Longest stop in the optimistic all-weight screen: from the 1.23 m/s inspected-floor ceiling at ${a} =$ 1.18 m/s² (Derived from floor friction with the entire weight on driven wheels) with ${j_{\max}} =$ 7.11 m/s³ (Derived).

**Math**:

*F4a, sag under the transient and the step that reaches lockout.*

$$
V_{\min} = V_{\text{low}} - I\,R = 42 - 70 \times 0.060 = 37.8\ \text{V}, \qquad I_{\text{cross}} = \frac{V_{\text{low}} - V_{\text{UVLO}}}{R} = \frac{42 - 33}{0.060} = 150\ \text{A}
$$

*F4b, flag to brakes set*, with the stop time of **[[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/03 Running the Machine/11 Trajectory Planning/V4-11.06 Behavior at the Seam#^eq-plan-c2-stopping-suffix|equation 11.17]]**.

$$
t_{\text{set}} = (T_{\text{tick}} + T_{\text{bus}} + T_{\text{act}}) + \left(\frac{v}{a} + \frac{a}{j_{\max}}\right) + t_{\text{engage}} = 22 + \left(\frac{1.231}{1.177} + \frac{1.177}{7.11}\right)\text{s} + 80 = 22 + 1045.9 + 165.5 + 80 = 1313.4\ \text{ms}
$$

**Result**: The transient leaves 4.8 V of headroom above the lockout, and reaching it takes a 150 A step, more than twice the transient. The 2 s hold-up outlasts the 1313.43 ms from flag to brakes set by 686.6 ms.

**Systems insight**: Neither trial can rest on the commanded load. F4a must step the supply itself, because the drive’s own current cannot reach the lockout, and F4b must open the feed during the longest stop admitted by the qualified loaded envelope, since a shorter stop passes on hold-up the claim never needed. The computed duration belongs to the optimistic all-weight screen; loaded qualification must replace its braking premise before fixing the physical F4b oracle. The headroom rests on an illustrative resistance and would vanish near 129 mΩ, about twice that value, so the source resistance measured under load is the first number the supply campaign must supply.

A compound fault trial must capture high-bandwidth electrical waveforms while independently recording the behavior of the learned policy, perception pipelines, motor controllers, and external telemetry witnesses. Engineers cannot infer that processors reset or sensor frames froze from software logs alone, because the electrical disturbance often corrupts the operating system writing the log. For example, a voltage droop on a shared power rail can induce oscillator jitter that slows the enforcer’s control cycle, freeze an image serializer so that a camera publishes identical frames with plausible timestamps, and reset an external flight recorder during the exact same interval. Similarly, electromagnetic burst interference across a shared fieldbus ages perceptual observations while stalling the permission path’s read requests and suppressing the diagnostic heartbeats that would otherwise signal the delay. A drift or jitter transient in a shared reference clock corrupts timestamps across the proposal-enforcement boundary at the same time, allowing stale data to pass as fresh evidence. The physical oracle evaluating a compound fault must therefore verify both that the protective response triggered within its deadline and that the witness instruments and evidence logs remained intact throughout the event ([[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/04 Governing the Machine/15 Adversarial Verification/V4-15.13 Fallacies and Pitfalls#^sec-verification-fallacies-pitfalls|section 15.12]]).

Because unmodeled physical couplings cannot be found from a resource inventory alone, forward physical analysis must always complement derivation from the inventory. Together, the resulting fault catalog unites four sources: inverted design contracts, forward-derived physical hazards, the authority transfer cases (A1–A4), and compound disturbances on shared hardware. Defining this catalog establishes *what* faults the machine must survive; choosing the physical fidelity of the test bench determines *which* of those claims a given trial can actually test.
