# V4-15.11 Interpreting Campaign Statistics

[[Home|Home]] · [[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/Volume 4 Overview|Volume Overview]] · [[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/04 Governing the Machine/15 Adversarial Verification/V4-15 Adversarial Verification|V4-15 Adversarial Verification]]

^sec-verification-campaign-statistics

Counting passed trials in an adversarial campaign can easily mislead a release review into treating a test-bench success rate as an operational reliability number. Any statistical summary describes only the specific population sampled by the test harness, and conditions that were never tested remain missing evidence no matter how sophisticated the statistical model applied afterward.

When ${n}$ independent Bernoulli trials sampled from a declared fault distribution yield zero missed mitigations, the outcome puts a one-sided upper confidence limit on the miss probability for that specific distribution. This is the zero-failure trial sizing of **[[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/02 Teaching the Machine/07 Closed-Loop Evaluation/V4-07.08 Trial Budgets, Stopping Rules, and Comparisons#^sec-evaluation-trials-per-claim|Sample size requirements per empirical claim]]**, applied here to an injected hardware fault population rather than closed-loop policy rollouts; at a 95 percent confidence level, it requires about $3/{p_{\text{fail}}}$ consecutive successful injections to bound the miss probability below ${p_{\text{fail}}}$. To turn these injection counts into a hardware coverage claim weighted by each component’s failure rate in FIT (failures in time, one per $10^9$ operating hours; notebook [15.3](https://mlsysbook.ai/vol4/15_verification/15_verification.html#nbk-verification-hardware-fault-injection-coverage-mean-time) gives the component weights ${\lambda}_i$), the injection harness must sample faults in direct proportion to their raw failure rates ${\lambda}_i$. Otherwise, the verification pipeline must apply an explicitly justified stratified weighting across each fault stratum.[[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/04 Governing the Machine/15 Adversarial Verification/V4-15.17 Footnotes#^fn11|¹¹]]

This statistical bound holds only when the physical trials satisfy the declared independence and sampling assumptions. On real hardware, repeated high-energy stops produce Joule heating (${I}^2{R}$) in the drive motor windings and mechanical brake linings. That heat buildup changes winding resistance and lining friction coefficients, coupling successive trials. As a result, a test campaign run on a dry aisle at room temperature cannot support a confidence bound for a contaminated floor or for the operating envelope’s 5 °C thermal extreme.

Notebook [15.3](https://mlsysbook.ai/vol4/15_verification/15_verification.html#nbk-verification-hardware-fault-injection-coverage-mean-time) draws its rates from four component strata (sensors, compute, transceivers and power management, gate drivers), and each stratum maps onto rows of [[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/04 Governing the Machine/15 Adversarial Verification/V4-15.07 Deriving the Fault List#^tbl-15-fault-classification|table 2]]. Sensor faults are injected as F2 and as the physical transducer disturbances, compute faults as F1, transceiver and power-management faults as F3 and F4, and gate-driver faults as the drive that keeps producing torque after a stop command ([[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/04 Governing the Machine/15 Adversarial Verification/V4-15.07 Deriving the Fault List#^sec-verification-forward-analysis|section 15.6.2]]). Sampling in proportion to those rates therefore fixes how the campaign’s trials divide among the rows.

**Napkin Math 15.3: The coverage count**

**Problem**: Illustrative component fault rates and a chosen ceiling on the unmitigated rate fix the diagnostic coverage the machine needs. How many zero-miss injections, sampled in proportion to those rates, would demonstrate that coverage?

**Variables**:

- Component fault rates ${\lambda}_i$ in FIT, one fault per $10^9$ operating hours (Illustrative): sensors 1,500, compute 1,000, transceivers and power management 2,500, and gate drivers 4,800.
- Target unmitigated rate ${\lambda_{\text{target}}} =$ 100 FIT, with a stricter 10 FIT alternative, at confidence ${C} =$ 95 percent (Chosen).
- Diagnostic coverage ${C_{\text{diag}}}$, the ${\lambda}_i$-weighted fraction of faults whose hazardous effect is mitigated, so that ${\lambda_{\text{unmitigated}}} = {\lambda_{\text{raw}}}(1 - {C_{\text{diag}}})$ and a single injection misses with probability ${p_{\text{fail}}} = 1 - {C_{\text{diag}}}$.

**Math**:

*Required coverage.*

$$
\lambda_{\text{raw}} = 1500 + 1000 + 2500 + 4800 = 9800\ \text{FIT}, \qquad C_{\text{diag}} \ge 1 - \frac{\lambda_{\text{target}}}{\lambda_{\text{raw}}} = 1 - \frac{100}{9800} = 0.9898
$$

*Injections to demonstrate it*, from the zero-failure count of **[[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/02 Teaching the Machine/07 Closed-Loop Evaluation/V4-07.08 Trial Budgets, Stopping Rules, and Comparisons#^sec-evaluation-trials-per-claim|Sample size requirements per empirical claim]]**.

$$
n \ge \frac{\ln(1 - C)}{\ln C_{\text{diag}}} = \frac{\ln 0.05}{\ln 0.9898} = 292.1 \;\Rightarrow\; n = 293, \qquad \frac{3}{p} = \frac{3 \lambda_{\text{raw}}}{\lambda_{\text{target}}} = \frac{3 \times 9800}{100} = 294
$$

*Sensitivity.* The count scales as $3{\lambda_{\text{raw}}}/{\lambda_{\text{target}}}$, so the 10 FIT target needs 99.90 percent coverage and 2,935 injections, against a rule-of-three estimate of 2,940.

**Result**: A 100 FIT target needs 98.98 percent coverage, which 293 zero-miss injections demonstrate at 95 percent confidence.

**Systems insight**: The target unmitigated failure rate, not the number of entries in the fault list, governs campaign size. Every tenfold tightening of the target rate demands roughly a tenfold increase in physical injection runs. Furthermore, this sample size holds only when injections are drawn in proportion to each component’s raw failure rate ${\lambda}_i$. Injecting uniformly across components with disparate failure rates tests a distorted distribution unless the harness weights the empirical outcomes and gathers enough trials within every stratum. Every trial must therefore log the exact injected mode, whether hardware detection triggered within the deadline, and whether the safety controller brought the plant to a verified safe state.

Even within the declared fault strata, a campaign rarely reaches every combination of load, floor friction, and temperature. The fault-record set must therefore catalog omitted test cases as carefully as completed runs, recording every parameter region skipped because of testbed physical limits, sensor bandwidth limits, or destructive testing costs. Unprobed regions cannot count as verified safety margins in the release case, nor can passive operational hours accumulated outside the test cell fill those gaps.

### Exposure and the Population a Statistic Describes

^sec-verification-exposure-wall

Logging thousands of incident-free fleet hours in normal warehouse aisles cannot bound rare safety-critical faults or substitute for targeted fault injection, because passive operation samples a different distribution from deliberate fault trials. Fleet exposure runs into the exposure wall of **[[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/02 Teaching the Machine/07 Closed-Loop Evaluation/V4-07.10 Verification Ceilings and the Exposure Wall#^eq-eval-exposure-wall|equation 7.9]]**: bounding a hazard rate ${\lambda_{\text{target}}}$ at 95 percent confidence requires about $3/{\lambda_{\text{target}}}$ failure-free operating hours, which Butler and Finelli ([[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/04 Governing the Machine/15 Adversarial Verification/V4-15.16 Source References#^ref-butler1993infeasibility|1993]]) showed is unreachable at safety-critical rates such as $10^{-9}$ failures per hour. Pooling those hours across a fleet adds exposure time but not environmental diversity, so the bound covers only the conditions the pooled machines share (**[[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/02 Teaching the Machine/07 Closed-Loop Evaluation/V4-07.10 Verification Ceilings and the Exposure Wall#^sec-evaluation-limits|Verification Ceilings and the Exposure Wall]]**), and normal fleet traffic rarely triggers the corner-case hardware faults this chapter injects. Verification also has to ask which configuration those hours describe. Any change to a safety filter, control policy, or actuator driver can alter the sampled outcomes, so earlier fleet hours count toward the updated configuration only when an explicit engineering argument shows that the change left the counted events unaffected. When a rare failure rate must be estimated across a high-dimensional disturbance space without waiting for billions of passive rollouts, the campaign must steer samples toward the failure region and weight them back to the operational distribution.

### Estimating a Rare Failure Rate by Importance Sampling

^sec-verification-importance-sampling

Naive Monte Carlo sampling from nominal operating conditions cannot estimate rare safety-critical failure rates because nearly every rollout ends in an unremarkable, safe stop, requiring billions of runs to observe even a handful of violations ([[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/04 Governing the Machine/15 Adversarial Verification/V4-15.11 Interpreting Campaign Statistics#^sec-verification-exposure-wall|section 15.10.1]]). While adversarial falsification finds whether an unsafe trajectory exists, release decisions also need to know how often such failures occur under nominal operating conditions. **Importance sampling**[[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/04 Governing the Machine/15 Adversarial Verification/V4-15.17 Footnotes#^fn12|¹²]] breaks this bottleneck by drawing rollouts from an alternative proposal distribution that concentrates samples near the failure boundary and then weighting each sample by its likelihood ratio to recover an unbiased estimate under the operational distribution.

Let the operational distribution of uncertain input parameters ${x}$ be denoted by ${p}({x})$, and let ${F}$ define the subset of conditions leading to a safety violation. Standard Monte Carlo estimation evaluates the failure probability ${p_F} = \Pr_{{p}}({x} \in {F})$ by computing the empirical failure fraction across ${N}$ independent draws from ${p}$. Importance sampling instead draws samples from an engineered **proposal distribution** ${q}({x})$ and weights each outcome by the **likelihood ratio** ${w}({x}) = {p}({x})/{q}({x})$:

$$
{\hat{p}_F} = \frac{1}{{N}}\sum_{i=1}^{{N}} \mathbb{1}[{x}_i \in {F}]\, {w}({x}_i), \qquad {x}_i \sim {q}. \tag{7}
$$

 This estimator remains mathematically unbiased as long as ${q}({x}) > 0$ whenever ${p}({x})\mathbb{1}[{x} \in {F}] > 0$, since the likelihood weight corrects for the shift in sampling probability ([[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/04 Governing the Machine/15 Adversarial Verification/V4-15.16 Source References#^ref-owen2013monte|Owen 2013]]). The variance of ${\hat{p}_F}$ depends entirely on the geometry of ${q}$. The theoretically optimal zero-variance proposal is the operational distribution ${p}$ conditioned on the failure region ${F}$ (${q}^*({x}) = {p}({x})\mathbb{1}[{x} \in {F}] / {p_F}$). Constructing ${q}^*$ directly is impossible because its normalizing constant is the very quantity ${p_F}$ under estimation. Practical verification pipelines must therefore construct parametric approximations to ${q}^*$.

^eq-verification-importance-sampling

The **cross-entropy method** ([[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/04 Governing the Machine/15 Adversarial Verification/V4-15.16 Source References#^ref-rubinstein2004cross|Rubinstein and Kroese 2004]]) systematically discovers such an approximation within a parameterized distribution family ${q_{\boldsymbol{\psi}}}$ by solving a sequence of intermediate rare-event problems. Beginning with an initial proposal aligned with the operational profile (${q_{\boldsymbol{\psi}_0}} = {p}$), the algorithm iteratively draws a sample batch from the current proposal and evaluates the performance metric. It then selects an intermediate threshold at the $(1-{\beta})$ quantile so that a fraction ${\beta}$ of the batch qualifies as elite runs. The parameter vector ${\boldsymbol{\psi}}$ is then updated by maximizing the likelihood-weighted log-density of these elite samples, which minimizes the Kullback-Leibler divergence (a measure of how far one probability distribution lies from another) to the optimal proposal on that intermediate slice. As the threshold steps forward across iterations, the proposal gradually shifts its probability mass toward the true failure boundary. For an exponential proposal parameterized by its mean, or a fixed-variance Gaussian proposal parameterized by its mean, this update admits an exact closed form: the updated mean is the weighted empirical mean of the elite draws.

Consider this method applied to the rack-end deceleration scenario. Traveling at nominal aisle velocity, the mobile base consumes its 131.0 mm stopping reserve from [[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/04 Governing the Machine/15 Adversarial Verification/V4-15.03 The Operating Envelope#^sec-verification-operating-envelope|section 15.2]] in ${T_{\text{deadline,fallback}}} =$ 100.8 ms, the latest-fallback deadline of **[[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/04 Governing the Machine/14 Supervisory Intervention/V4-14.03 Human Authority, Reaction Time, and Clearance#^eq-intervention-latest-fallback|equation 14.4]]** at aisle speed. Any unbudgeted message queue delay exceeding ${T_{\text{deadline,fallback}}}$ exhausts this safety buffer, causing the physical vehicle to breach the clearance boundary. To establish an analytical baseline, assume an illustrative operational distribution where queue delays ${T_q}$ follow an exponential distribution with mean ${\bar{T}_q} =$ 5 ms (in production verification, latency measurements gathered under the declared load must justify or replace this model). Under this formulation, the analytical failure probability is ${p_F} = e^{-{T_{\text{deadline,fallback}}}/{\bar{T}_q}} =$ 1.8 × 10⁻⁹ per stopping event. This closed-form quantity serves as an exact ground truth against which to benchmark competing sampling estimators. The threshold is deliberately the uncharged ceiling of [[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/04 Governing the Machine/15 Adversarial Verification/V4-15.03 The Operating Envelope#^sec-verification-operating-envelope|section 15.2]], which makes the example optimistic. Charging the dispatch-to-admission allocation leaves only 37.5 ms, and the same delay model then gives ${p_F} =$ 5.6 × 10⁻⁴, about 3 × 10⁵ times larger. The comparison below keeps the uncharged threshold because there the failure is rarest and the gap between estimators widest; at the charged threshold even plain Monte Carlo would need far fewer runs.

The sample sizing required across these estimators stems directly from the variance of sample averages. When an estimator averages ${N}$ independent draws with mean ${p_F}$ and per-sample variance ${\sigma}^2$, the estimator’s standard deviation is ${\sigma}/\sqrt{{N}}$, yielding a relative standard error ${r} = \sqrt{{\sigma}^2 / ({p_F}^2 {N})}$. Achieving a target relative precision ${r}$ requires ${N} = ({\sigma}^2 / {p_F}^2) / {r}^2$ trials, where ${\sigma}^2 / {p_F}^2$ denotes the per-draw **relative variance**. For naive Monte Carlo, each trial is an indicator variable with variance ${p_F}(1 - {p_F})$, demanding approximately $(1 - {p_F})/({p_F} {r}^2)$ simulated runs. Under the illustrative delay model, securing an estimate with ${r} =$ 10 percent relative error requires 5.7 × 10¹⁰ trials. By comparison, a zero-failure qualification campaign requires 1.7 × 10⁹ consecutive incident-free runs to establish an upper bound at the ${p_F}$ threshold (**[[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/02 Teaching the Machine/07 Closed-Loop Evaluation/V4-07.07 Confidence from a Finite Campaign#^eq-eval-rule-of-three|equation 7.4]]**). Under importance sampling for this exponential model, the per-draw variance equals the weighted failure indicator’s second moment minus ${p_F}^2$, which stays finite as long as the proposal rate is less than twice the operational rate.

The cross-entropy method using exponential proposals with an elite fraction of ${\beta} =$ 10 percent demonstrates how adaptive sampling overcomes this barrier. The initial threshold is set at the 90th percentile of the operational delay distribution (${\bar{T}_q}\ln 10$, or 11.5 ms). Because the exponential distribution is memoryless, the conditional distribution of delays exceeding any threshold ${\gamma}$ is identical to the original distribution shifted by ${\gamma}$. The likelihood-weighted mean of the elite samples is therefore simply ${\gamma} + {\bar{T}_q}$, which immediately defines the mean for the next proposal. Because the parametric proposal family consists of unshifted exponential distributions supported on the positive real line rather than truncated distributions starting at ${\gamma}$, matching this conditional mean stretches the exponential scale across the entire positive axis rather than shifting the lower support boundary. Successive iterations lift the intermediate threshold to 38.0 ms and 99.1 ms. By iteration 4, the intermediate quantile surpasses the critical threshold, clamping the final level at ${T_{\text{deadline,fallback}}}$ with a proposal mean of 105.8 ms, the conditional expectation of the delay given a boundary violation. Under this adapted proposal, 38.6 percent of the generated rollouts land in the failure set, and their small likelihood weights scale each observed failure back to the operational probability. The resulting per-draw relative variance drops to 27.1. Consequently, only 2,709 simulation runs are needed to reach the target 10 percent relative error, a factor of 2 × 10⁷ fewer runs than plain Monte Carlo and within 0.1 percent of the best exponential proposal. These numerical counts derive from the assumed exponential model; testing against an empirical delay histogram will yield distinct run requirements.

![[90 Assets/Volume IV/15 Verification/Margin Rare Event Runs Ladder 021f956925.svg]]

*Log-scale ladder of the runs needed to estimate one rare overshoot rate: 5.7 times 10 to the 10 for plain Monte Carlo, 1.7 times 10 to the 9 for a zero-failure campaign, and 2,709 for importance sampling with a cross-entropy proposal.*

Two statistical diagnostics safeguard the integrity of importance sampling estimates. The first is the **effective sample size**, defined as ${N_{\text{eff}}} = \left(\sum_{i=1}^{{N}} {w}_i\right)^2 / \sum_{i=1}^{{N}} {w}_i^2$ ([[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/04 Governing the Machine/15 Adversarial Verification/V4-15.16 Source References#^ref-owen2013monte|Owen 2013]]). This metric diagnoses concentration of the likelihood weights; it does not by itself establish the precision of the weighted failure estimate, which depends on the variance of the weighted failure indicator. For the 2,709 runs above, the expected effective sample size is approximately 250; a degenerate value approaching 1 would warn that a few outlying weights dominate the summation, rendering the estimate unstable. The second diagnostic is distribution support and tail coverage. Omitting a subregion of ${F}$ with positive operational probability violates the support condition and biases the estimate. A proposal that retains support but samples the operational tail too rarely can instead produce unstable or infinite variance, with deceptively small observed variance in a finite run. Sample variance cannot reveal an omitted failure path that generates no samples, so the campaign must justify coverage independently of its weight diagnostics. O’Kelly et al. ([[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/04 Governing the Machine/15 Adversarial Verification/V4-15.16 Source References#^ref-okelly2018scalable|2018]]) applied adaptive importance sampling to end-to-end verification of autonomous driving software stacks and obtained speedups of several orders of magnitude over naive Monte Carlo in estimating rare collision rates.

This structure separates importance sampling from adversarial falsification. Although an adversarial search engine also concentrates runs near the system’s safety boundaries, its sampling density is unknown. Because falsification runs lack likelihood-ratio weights, the fraction of falsification runs that fail says nothing about real-world failure frequency; in the illustrative example, 38.6 percent of the proposal runs fail even though the modeled operational failure probability is only 1.8 × 10⁻⁹. A quantitative failure-probability estimate belongs in a safety case only when paired with its declared operational model ${p}$, proposal distribution ${q}$, weight history, effective sample size, and confidence intervals. It remains a statement about that declared operating model rather than a direct physical measurement of the deployed hardware. Estimating how often a boundary is crossed still leaves a second question open: when a near-miss or excursion occurs, how far can the worst-case physical overshoot reach?

### Extreme Value Theory and Heavy-Tailed Safety Distributions

^sec-verification-extreme-value-theory

Binary pass-fail tallies throw away the margin history of near-miss stops, while fitting a Gaussian curve to nominal bus traffic or stopping-distance data underestimates the heavy tails caused by sporadic bus arbitration contention or contact slips. Deployment qualification often has to bound the physical magnitude of worst-case excursions across continuous telemetry streams: spatial clearance ${D_{\text{clear}}} - {d}({t})$, onset slack ${t_{\text{bound}}} - {t_{\text{onset}}}$, and actuator torque headroom ${\tau_{\max}} - {\tau_{\text{act}}}$. Extreme value theory (EVT) provides limiting distributions for these tail excursions, playing a role for extreme deviations similar to that of the central limit theorem for sample means ([[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/04 Governing the Machine/15 Adversarial Verification/V4-15.16 Source References#^ref-coles2001introduction|Coles 2001]]). Even so, engineers must check that the data belong to the assumed tail regime rather than treating a fitted mathematical endpoint as a physical barrier. While the block-maxima formulation (which yields the Generalized Extreme Value distribution) is developed in **[[05 Reference/Volume IV - Physical AI/V4 Machine Learning Background#^sec-appendix-ml-evt|Extreme Value Theory and Tail Risk]]**, cyber-physical verification primarily uses the threshold-exceedance formulation, which keeps every observation that crosses a high safety threshold rather than discarding all but the block maximum.

1.  **Peaks over threshold and the Generalized Pareto Distribution**: Let ${X}$ denote a continuous performance metric (such as stopping-distance error), and let ${u}$ represent a high baseline threshold. We model the exceedances ${Y} = {X} - {u}$ conditional on ${X} > {u}$. For distributions in an appropriate extreme-value domain of attraction, the Pickands-Balkema-de Haan theorem gives an asymptotic Generalized Pareto approximation to the conditional excess distribution as ${u}$ approaches the upper endpoint. Balkema and Haan ([[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/04 Governing the Machine/15 Adversarial Verification/V4-15.16 Source References#^ref-balkema1974residual|1974]]) and Pickands ([[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/04 Governing the Machine/15 Adversarial Verification/V4-15.16 Source References#^ref-pickands1975statistical|1975]]) proved this result independently:

$$
{F_{\text{GPD}}}({y}; {\tilde{\sigma}}, {\xi}) = 1 - \left( 1 + \frac{{\xi} {y}}{{\tilde{\sigma}}} \right)^{-1/{\xi}}, \quad {y} > 0, \tag{8}
$$

 defined for scale parameter ${\tilde{\sigma}} > 0$ on the support $1 + {\xi} {y}/{\tilde{\sigma}} > 0$, with the exponential distribution recovered as the continuous limit ${\xi} \to 0$. The **shape parameter** ${\xi}$ governs tail thickness: ${\xi} > 0$ indicates a heavy, power-law tail; ${\xi} = 0$ corresponds to an exponential tail; and ${\xi} < 0$ defines a bounded tail with finite endpoint ${u} - {\tilde{\sigma}}/{\xi}$.

    ^eq-verification-gpd-distribution

2.  **Return level**: For a chosen return period[[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/04 Governing the Machine/15 Adversarial Verification/V4-15.17 Footnotes#^fn13|¹³]] ${T}$ calibrated to the sampling interval, the **return level** ${z}_{{T}}$ is the threshold exceeded on average once every ${T}$ periods:

$$
{z}_{{T}} = {u} + \frac{{\tilde{\sigma}}}{{\xi}} \left[ ({T} {\zeta_u})^{{\xi}} - 1 \right] \tag{9}
$$

 where ${\zeta_u} = \Pr({X} > {u}) = {N_u} / {N_{\text{total}}}$ is the empirical exceedance rate across ${N_{\text{total}}}$ trials, with ${T}$ expressed in equivalent trial counts. This expression follows from multiplying the conditional Generalized Pareto survival probability $\left(1 + {\xi}({z}_{{T}} - {u})/{\tilde{\sigma}}\right)^{-1/{\xi}}$ by the threshold exceedance probability ${\zeta_u}$ and setting the resulting unconditional tail probability equal to $1/{T}$. In this subsection ${T}$ is therefore a count of trials, not the test horizon of [[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/04 Governing the Machine/15 Adversarial Verification/V4-15.10 Searching Beyond the First Trial#^eq-verification-adversarial-falsification|equation 4]]. [Equation 9](https://mlsysbook.ai/vol4/15_verification/15_verification.html#eq-verification-evt-return-level) holds for ${\xi} \ne 0$; its continuous limit at ${\xi} = 0$ is ${z}_{{T}} = {u} + {\tilde{\sigma}}\ln({T}{\zeta_u})$. Expressing a return period in calendar operating hours requires scaling by an independently justified hourly event arrival rate under a verified temporal dependence model. The product ${T}{\zeta_u}$ is dimensionless, and valid extrapolations must evaluate return levels at or above the conditioning threshold ${u}$.

    ^eq-verification-evt-return-level

#### Connecting Adversarial Falsification to EVT Tail Sampling

^sec-verification-falsification-evt

Under nominal operation, exceedances past a conservative margin threshold occur far too rarely to fit an empirical tail; the exposure wall of [[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/04 Governing the Machine/15 Adversarial Verification/V4-15.11 Interpreting Campaign Statistics#^sec-verification-exposure-wall|section 15.10.1]] starves extreme-value fits just as severely as binary failure counts. Adversarial falsification gets around this scarcity by concentrating rollouts near violation boundaries, solving the search problem of [[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/04 Governing the Machine/15 Adversarial Verification/V4-15.10 Searching Beyond the First Trial#^eq-verification-adversarial-falsification|equation 4]] over initial plant states and disturbance parameters. Let ${d_{\text{budget}}}$ denote the design budget at the trial speed, 969.0 mm at aisle speed, so that ${D_{\text{clear}}} - {d_{\text{budget}}}$ is the 131.0 mm spare. For the braking-distance overshoot metric ${X} = {d_{\text{stop}}} - {d_{\text{budget}}}$, the search populates the exceedance set

$$
{\mathcal{Y}_u} = \{ {Y}_i = {X}_i - {u} \mid {X}_i > {u} \}
$$

 with dense observations near the failure frontier. Those samples, however, reflect the adversary’s search policy rather than the operational environment. Fitting a Generalized Pareto Distribution directly to unweighted falsification runs models the optimizer’s search behavior, not the reliability of the physical plant. To inform operational risk, the runs must come from a declared proposal distribution and carry the likelihood-ratio weights of [[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/04 Governing the Machine/15 Adversarial Verification/V4-15.11 Interpreting Campaign Statistics#^eq-verification-importance-sampling|equation 7]], so that both the exceedance probability ${\zeta_u}$ and the fitted parameters reflect weighted operational likelihoods.

A fitted shape parameter serves as an engineering diagnostic rather than a standalone safety verdict:

- **Positive shape (${\xi} > 0$):** The fitted model extrapolates a heavy upper tail, warning of sensitivity to extreme deviations. This fit does not prove that physical overshoots are truly unbounded, but it requires the engineering team to check whether threshold choice, sensor noise, or unmodeled delays drive the heavy-tailed behavior.
- **Negative shape (${\xi} < 0$):** The fitted distribution implies a finite modeled upper endpoint at ${u} - {\tilde{\sigma}}/{\xi}$. That mathematical endpoint must never be mistaken for a certified hardware stop or taken as proof that a runtime safety filter will always contain motion. Verifying real-world containment requires auditing the filter’s dynamical assumptions and measuring its hardware response latencies under adversarial load.

#### Sim-To-Real Transfer Bounds on EVT Return Levels

^sec-verification-evt-sim-to-real

When high-throughput campaigns run on the software-in-the-loop and processor-in-the-loop (PIL) rungs of the qualification ladder, extreme value modeling produces a simulated return level ${z_{T, \text{sim}}}$. As shown in [[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/04 Governing the Machine/15 Adversarial Verification/V4-15.10 Searching Beyond the First Trial#^sec-verification-sim-to-real-bounds|section 15.9.4]], physical trajectories can diverge from simulated trajectories by up to the sim-to-real discrepancy bound ${\epsilon_{\text{gap}}} = \sup_t \|{s_{\text{real}}}({t}) - {s_{\text{sim}}}({t})\|_{{W}}$. When simulated and physical trials describe the same operational population and the overshoot metric obeys the pathwise discrepancy bound with constant ${L_{\phi}}$, that bound also limits the physical return level ([[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/04 Governing the Machine/15 Adversarial Verification/V4-15.11 Interpreting Campaign Statistics#^eq-verification-evt-sim2real-bound|equation 10]]):

$$
{z_{T, \text{real}}} \le {z_{T, \text{sim}}} + {L_{\phi}} {\epsilon_{\text{gap}}}. \tag{10}
$$

 To support a stopping-distance claim, this widened return level must fit inside the available physical reserve:

$$
{z_{T, \text{sim}}} + {L_{\phi}} {\epsilon_{\text{gap}}} \le {D_{\text{clear}}} - {d_{\text{budget}}}. \tag{11}
$$

^eq-verification-evt-release-condition

^eq-verification-evt-sim2real-bound

The margin inequality in [[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/04 Governing the Machine/15 Adversarial Verification/V4-15.11 Interpreting Campaign Statistics#^eq-verification-evt-release-condition|equation 11]] is a necessary sanity check rather than a sufficient release rule. Using it requires distributional consistency between simulation and physical testbeds, proper importance weighting when quoting operational return periods, confidence intervals on the fitted parameters, and a transfer bound ${\epsilon_{\text{gap}}}$ justified across the entire operating domain. A small trajectory discrepancy across a handful of bench tests does not establish an envelope-wide sim-to-real bound.

In the rack-end deceleration scenario, the physical safety margin is 131.0 mm, and only 48.7 mm once the dispatch-to-admission allocation is charged, so a return level of a few centimeters, widened by a transfer bound of a few centimeters more, already consumes it. Any proposed tail bound must state its sample population, proposal weights, parameter uncertainties, and transfer error bounds before being compared against this reserve. When a fault mode (such as mode F1) has zero measured physical trials, it supports neither an empirical tail fit nor a return-level estimate; a fitted mathematical endpoint cannot stand in for missing physical test evidence.

Every statistical metric in this section is conditional. A zero-miss count describes only the declared fault injection distribution. An exposure bound reflects only the exact hardware and software configuration that accumulated those hours. An importance-sampling estimate holds only under its declared operational model. An extreme value return level holds only relative to its fitted tail and sim-to-real discrepancy bound. Because any of these premises can fail, a verification campaign never hands release an isolated scalar margin; instead, it packages fault records that bind each empirical margin to the physical, environmental, and statistical conditions that support it.
