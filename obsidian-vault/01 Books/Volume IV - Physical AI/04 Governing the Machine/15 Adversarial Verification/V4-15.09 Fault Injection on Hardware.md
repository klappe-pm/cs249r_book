# V4-15.09 Fault Injection on Hardware

[[Home|Home]] · [[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/Volume 4 Overview|Volume Overview]] · [[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/04 Governing the Machine/15 Adversarial Verification/V4-15 Adversarial Verification|V4-15 Adversarial Verification]]

^sec-verification-hardware-fault-injection

A fault injected in simulation tests only the modeled response. On physical machinery, bus contention, microcontroller clock drift, DMA stalls, and transient voltage drops across inductive motor drivers determine whether a safety intervention executes before physical impact. When a simulator fails to capture and calibrate these physical interactions, simulation alone cannot substantiate claims that depend on them. Instead, **in-situ fault injection** runs directly on the target hardware while an external containment rig keeps the facility safe, deterministically introducing physical, electrical, and computational disturbances (sensor register latches, clock drift, bus contention, supply brownouts, and actuator load stalls) into production silicon, transceivers, timers, shared buses, and mechanics under authentic compute, thermal, and power loads.

### Physical Injection Needles Across Sensor, Bus, and Power

^sec-verification-injection-needles

A fault injected in software above the driver can exercise downstream responses but does not reproduce a disturbance originating at the physical interface; an empirical verification campaign must place its injectors at the physical boundaries themselves.[[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/04 Governing the Machine/15 Adversarial Verification/V4-15.17 Footnotes#^fn7|⁷]] Four complementary injection mechanisms cover the machine’s safety claims:

1.  *Sensor ingress injectors:* Inline interposers on camera serial links freeze frames, inject propagation delay while preserving original hardware timestamps, or corrupt pixel payloads at the physical layer without modifying driver software.
2.  *Fieldbus disturbance nodes:* Inline devices inject protocol-specific faults: corrupted frames, CAN collisions that drive target controllers into bus-off states,[[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/04 Governing the Machine/15 Adversarial Verification/V4-15.17 Footnotes#^fn8|⁸]] dropped EtherCAT distributed-clock synchronization frames, or babbling-idiot traffic that monopolizes shared resources.
3.  *Supply injectors:* Programmable power supplies emulating the battery pack, paired with dynamic electronic loads, step drive-bus and logic rails through calibrated voltage droop profiles. Delivered waveforms must be measured directly at the device terminals, because test-fixture slew-rate limits dictate which high-frequency transients the setup can faithfully reproduce.
4.  *Dynamometer actuator injectors:* Dynamometer load machines mechanically coupled to wheel hubs or arm joints command calibrated load steps within their measured bandwidth. These challenge responses to increased resistance or abrupt joint loads; reproducing bearing seizures, brake-lining glazing, or joint jams requires mechanism-specific fixtures while instrumentation records the closed-loop motor current response.

Verifying the dual-processor safety architecture requires targeted physical injection at the exact hardware boundaries established by the safety contract. [[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/04 Governing the Machine/15 Adversarial Verification/V4-15.07 Deriving the Fault List#^tbl-15-fault-classification|Table 2]] summarizes the corresponding detectors, recovery actions, and acceptance criteria; the following breakdown details the physical injection mechanics and timing instrumentation for each fault class:

- **Expired Lease Revocation (F1):** The test framework permits the chunk policy to issue a single valid renewal, then freezes its task or command output while leaving its periodic heartbeat active, verifying that superficial liveness cannot renew the lease. Dropping subsequent lease messages is a separate test of expiry under communication loss. High-speed logic analyzers time lease expiration, the enforcer’s formal revocation on the subsequent 1 ms control tick, and the mechanical onset of braking across dedicated hardware trigger lines.
- **Stale Observation Injection (F2):** An inline FPGA interceptor latches a navigation-camera frame at the image signal processor interface, or suppresses optical encoder updates, while preserving the original hardware timestamp intact. External instrumentation independently records the exact moment of detector trip, the onset of powered electrical deceleration, and the resulting wheel speed profile.
- **Lost-Frame Injection (F3):** A fieldbus disturbance node drops or corrupts EtherCAT frames directly at a servo drive’s slave port while the mobile robot cruises. The master’s frame-arrival, validity, and working-counter checks[[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/04 Governing the Machine/15 Adversarial Verification/V4-15.17 Footnotes#^fn9|⁹]] detect communication loss under their configured deadlines. At aisle velocity, each lost 1 ms cycle allows the vehicle to travel an unmonitored 1.3 mm, eating directly into the physical safety margin.
- **Drive-Bus Droop and Feed-Loss Injection (F4a, F4b):** A programmable supply standing in for the battery pack at its 42 V depleted point delivers the full 70 A transient demanded by aggressive drive acceleration while the application SoC operates at peak load. The supply injector then commands a voltage step that forces the drive bus below the 33 V converter undervoltage lockout, a threshold that operating load alone cannot breach (notebook [15.2](https://mlsysbook.ai/vol4/15_verification/15_verification.html#nbk-verification-supply-headroom)). Throughout this transient, the permission path must remain fully operational, sustained by the isolated, held-up rail. An independent digitizer records the permission-path heartbeat, and instrumentation measures the onset of powered deceleration relative to the undervoltage alarm flag. In trial F4b, an electronic switch opens the permission rail’s primary feed during the longest stop admitted by the qualified loaded envelope. The illustrative all-weight screen initiates its resident stop from the 1.23 m/s ceiling. Independent measurements must verify both surviving drive authority and energy absorption throughout dynamic braking. Sustaining logic hold-up alone leaves that premise unverified. Any reset of the permission path constitutes an immediate `FAIL` in either trial, regardless of stopping distance, because a vehicle that forfeits controlled deceleration to unpowered spring brakes mid-motion violates the deterministic stopping envelope budgeted by the safety contract.
- **Handover Stall Injection (A3):** The testbed intentionally freezes operator input while a remote takeover request remains in the handover-pending `PEND` state defined in **[[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/04 Governing the Machine/14 Supervisory Intervention/V4-14.04 Authority Transitions#^sec-intervention-discrete-states|Authority states and single-writer actuator ownership]]**, and it drops acknowledgment frames, at both base speeds. At the 0.75 m/s slow-down, a candidate moving handover is tested and any admitted candidate must time out into a local stop. At the 1.3 m/s aisle speed, the handover must be rejected. The operative configuration must stop before granting manual motion at either speed. High-speed probes separately time the enforcer’s fallback dispatch and physical actuation onset at the wheel drives. While the configured waiting allowance limits dispatch latency, physical onset must also satisfy the allocated command-to-response interval.

Duplicate writers (A1), tier bypass attempts (A2), and forged or replayed packets (A4) require no specialized mechanical fixtures beyond a secondary bus master and a packet injector configured according to [[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/04 Governing the Machine/15 Adversarial Verification/V4-15.07 Deriving the Fault List#^tbl-15-fault-classification|table 2]]. All four physical injector types and their witness instruments integrate onto a single test bench. The test rig of [[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/04 Governing the Machine/15 Adversarial Verification/V4-15.09 Fault Injection on Hardware#^fig-15-f1-test-rig|figure 3]] enforces strict physical separation: no witness instrument may share a power rail or a timebase with the target machine under evaluation.

### Timing Faults Across Clock Domains

^sec-verification-hardware-timing-search

Because camera frame capture, trajectory proposal generation, enforcer permission ticks, and fieldbus communication cycles run across asynchronous clock domains (**[[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/03 Running the Machine/08 Sensor Perception/V4-08.04 Dating and Placing the Measurement#^sec-perception-handoff|Dating and Placing the Measurement]]**), a fault injected at a fixed offset from the start of a run can miss the narrow alignment where a race condition trips. Reproducible qualification therefore records the disturbance’s phase relative to every relevant clock edge and sweeps across injection phases to expose race conditions that fixed-offset tests miss, while recognizing that empirical phase sweeps alone cannot prove the absence of a worse alignment.

Consider the stale-observation scenario (fault F2) running on production silicon. The navigation camera timestamps each image at the moment of physical exposure, and the perception pipeline budgets a maximum age of 51.6 ms upon trajectory dispatch (**[[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/03 Running the Machine/08 Sensor Perception/V4-08.05 Bounding Observation Age#^sec-perception-latency-waterfall|The latency waterfall]]**). Because the robot travels at 1.3 m/s, it covers 1.3 mm every millisecond. Unbudgeted perceptual latency drains the stopping distance reserve millisecond by millisecond. The 51.8 ms upper-age bound, however, governs only the dispatch instant; sensory evidence continues to age as the command traverses validation and motor execution. Consequently, the per-tick safety monitor cannot treat the dispatch limit as an open-ended pass; it must evaluate perceptual freshness dynamically against remaining stopping distance and active trajectory validity. The stall that matters is short. Once the dispatch-to-admission allocation is charged, only 37.5 ms of the aisle-speed spare remains ([[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/04 Governing the Machine/15 Adversarial Verification/V4-15.03 The Operating Envelope#^sec-verification-operating-envelope|section 15.2]]), so an undetected stall of that length leaves the machine no clearance to give.

Neither the nominal sensor readout latency from a component datasheet nor the timestamp logged on an isolated image frame proves when the safety enforcer will recognize a stall. Relying on empirical p99 response times provides no safety guarantee because a percentile alone does not bound shared-memory interference; a finite worst-case claim requires an explicit architectural and scheduling model (**[[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/01 Machine Anatomy/04 The Nervous System/V4-04.06 Real-Time Command Transport and Bus Determinism#^sec-nervous-rta-variance|Response time analysis and execution variance]]**). A defensible containment claim demands an analytic and experimental worst-case detection bound validated across maximum compute saturation, peak fieldbus load, and extreme operating temperatures.

### Distributed Clock Drift and Synchronization Blackouts

^sec-verification-clock-drift-sync

Two processors can each meet every local real-time deadline on their own crystals and still drift apart fast enough during a brief synchronization blackout to let a stale camera frame pass as fresh. The F2 stale-observation detector relies directly on the 0.2 ms clock-conversion bound established in the observation contract of **[[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/03 Running the Machine/08 Sensor Perception/V4-08.09 The Observation Contract#^sec-perception-observation-contract|The Observation Contract]]** when translating image capture timestamps into the safety microcontroller’s local timebase. That bound is not an axiomatic constant: **[[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/03 Running the Machine/08 Sensor Perception/V4-08.07 Spatial Error and Clearance Bounds#^sec-perception-qualifying-error|Qualifying the claimed error]]** measures it from the tail of the synchronization offset, the path asymmetry, and the drift between synchronization exchanges, and verification asks how much of it survives when those exchanges stop arriving. Consider illustrative oscillator tolerances: ±100 ppm for the application processor crystal, ±50 ppm for the safety microcontroller, and an additional 50 ppm worst-case thermal drift unaccounted for in standard room-temperature ratings. Together, these sources yield a maximum relative drift rate of 200 ppm. At this rate, uncorrected drift alone exhausts the entire 0.2 ms conversion budget in 1 s without active resynchronization. Under healthy operating conditions, periodic synchronization packets arriving every 100 ms confine accumulated drift to 20 μs, leaving the remainder of the error budget for network transit jitter and software conversion overhead.

Suppose electrical noise or packet collisions trigger a synchronization blackout where three consecutive sync frames are dropped, leaving two successful packets separated by 400 ms. Across this four-period blackout window, bounded clock drift alone accumulates to 80 μs. When measured synchronization jitter has a standard deviation of ${\sigma_{\text{jitter}}}$ = 15 μs, combining the drift rate ${\delta_{\text{skew}}}$ over the blackout duration ${T_{\text{blackout}}}$ with a one-sigma jitter excursion yields:

$$
{\Delta t_{\text{one-sigma scenario}}}={\delta_{\text{skew}}}\,{T_{\text{blackout}}}+{\sigma_{\text{jitter}}} \tag{2}
$$

 totaling 95 μs. The one-sigma estimate in [[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/04 Governing the Machine/15 Adversarial Verification/V4-15.09 Fault Injection on Hardware#^eq-verification-clock-drift|equation 2]] represents an illustrative operating point rather than a deterministic upper bound; certifying real-time safety requires bounding tail jitter analytically and stress-testing the conversion path on hardware. If the blackout extends to a fifth period (four consecutive frames dropped), accumulated oscillator drift alone reaches 100 μs before jitter is added, consuming half of the 0.2 ms error budget. The runtime must refuse proposals or enter its qualified fallback before synchronization uncertainty exceeds the conversion bound. If that guard is absent and clock drift or transit jitter breaches the boundary, two failure modes threaten the machine. In the first failure mode, a fresh camera frame appears artificially aged when compared against a fast microcontroller clock; the enforcer rejects valid proposals and initiates an unprompted emergency stop, stranding the vehicle mid-aisle. In the second, more dangerous mode, a stale observation appears artificially fresh against a slow microcontroller clock. The conversion error itself is cheap in distance. At 1.3 mm per millisecond, the whole 0.2 ms bound is worth less than a millimeter of travel. What the error corrupts is the refusal verdict. Because the admission check adds the 0.2 ms conversion bound to the measured timestamp difference to form a conservative upper age, the nominal navigation frame reaches the 51.8 ms refusal threshold exactly at dispatch, so the observation contract of **[[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/03 Running the Machine/08 Sensor Perception/V4-08.09 The Observation Contract#^sec-perception-observation-contract|The Observation Contract]]** left that threshold with zero slack. Once a slow microcontroller clock lags by more than the 0.2 ms allowance, the uncompensated clock lag subtracts directly from the measured elapsed age, so the age check can no longer certify that an admitted frame lies within budget. A frame held back by an F2 stall then passes as fresh whenever the uncorrected drift exceeds the stall’s extra age. Because drift keeps accumulating for as long as synchronization stays lost, the stall the check can miss grows with the blackout. The permission path may then admit proposals computed from obstacle coordinates older than the budget allows while the loaded base moves at 1.3 m/s.

Every physical fault injection trial concludes with a rigorous evidentiary burden. The test harness must simultaneously record the trip signal from the internal detector, the precise timestamp of proposal rejection, the actuation takeover at the motor drives, and the final physical stopping trajectory. All measurements must be captured by external, fault-isolated instruments synchronized to a single master reference clock. If a trial fails to record any of these intermediate transitions, it fails to produce actionable verification evidence. Physical fault injection campaigns do not yield simple pass/fail tallies; they generate auditable execution traces binding each trial to its architectural claim, its exact operating envelope, its software build artifacts, and its physical sensor waveforms.
