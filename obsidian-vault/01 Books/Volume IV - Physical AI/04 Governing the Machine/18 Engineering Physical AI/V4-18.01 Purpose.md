*Why does a better model not, by itself, make a better machine?*

The learned model is the part of a physical machine that is easiest to improve and easiest to score, so it attracts most of the effort. Useful service depends on everything around it as well. The body must sustain the task across loads, surfaces, and hours of duty. The learned behavior must hold in the states its own actions create. Each proposal must be admitted, corrected, or refused before the motion it asks for is already under way. The evidence for all of it must match the conditions of operation and stay true as the machine and its site change. Each of these can be engineered well in isolation while the machine as a whole remains unjustified, because doubt accumulates across every premise a physical claim depends on, and a premise that no one names or owns is assumed silently. A team that cannot join these parts into one argument keeps improving the model while an unmeasured floor, an unqualified detector, or an unowned restriction decides what the machine actually does. The same joined argument also chooses what to build next, since the most valuable measurement, experience, or design change is the one that retires the weakest open premise. At the causal boundary, irreversibility makes that weakest premise, not the best prediction, the measure of what a machine may be trusted to do.

**Learning Objectives**

- Explain why a better prediction can still produce a worse physical action, and synthesize the four laws into the design argument that answers it
- Distinguish an unobserved premise from a monitored unknown, and explain why only a standing restriction can contain an unobserved premise
- Apply the four laws to select and evaluate a candidate operating mode for an unfamiliar machine
- Construct residual-claims entries with supported restrictions, accountable owners, and predeclared closure tests
- Evaluate the throughput cost of an operating restriction against the cost of the closure campaign that would retire it
- Select the next measurement, learning experiment, or design change needed to extend a machine’s capability
