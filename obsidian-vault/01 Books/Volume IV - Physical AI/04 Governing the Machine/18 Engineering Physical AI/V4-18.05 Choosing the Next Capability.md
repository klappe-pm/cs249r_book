# V4-18.05 Choosing the Next Capability

[[Home|Home]] · [[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/Volume 4 Overview|Volume Overview]] · [[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/04 Governing the Machine/18 Engineering Physical AI/V4-18 Engineering Physical AI|V4-18 Engineering Physical AI]]

^sec-frontier-assumptions-change

Pouring engineering effort into a faster proposer when an unmeasured floor or an unseated tab actually limits the cell leaves the operating restriction in place. In a deployed packaging cell, restricted transit speeds and human-supervised sign-offs call for different investments. Speeding up proposal generation improves throughput inside the current safety envelope, whereas qualifying a new contact or engagement sensor can retire the need for human sign-off altogether. Each advance requires two deliverables: a predeclared task metric and a physical test of the operating premise it changes. Tuning execution inside an existing envelope and gathering the evidence to widen that envelope are distinct engineering programs, and the register’s open entries decide which one the next capability needs.

### Match the Experiment to the Missing Evidence

^sec-frontier-four-gap-classes

Treating every open entry in the residual-claims register by training another neural detector fails whenever the missing evidence is a field evaluation, a mathematical bound, or a fallback that the body cannot execute. The open entries do not all call for the same kind of work; each falls into one of four gap classes, and the class, not the entry’s urgency, decides which experiment can close it:

- *Unmeasured observables* require dedicated sensors that can tell the relevant physical states apart before the action deadline passes.
- *Evaluation deficits* require randomized field trials or statistical estimators that represent the actual operating population while accounting for correlated samples and rare tail events.
- *Theoretical deficits* require bounded analytical arguments whose physical premises can be proved mathematically or tested on the actual hardware plant.
- *Scope mismatches* require changing the system architecture or narrowing the operational design domain.

Naming the exact gap class directs the next experiment to the evidence a register entry actually lacks, turning the open premises in the claims register into distinct work programs. Undetected floor films, hidden cross-aisle pedestrians, ambiguous object identities, and unseated packaging tabs are unmeasured observables. Each calls for either a qualified sensor channel with a tested miss bound or an operating restriction that avoids the hazard, such as the downstream manual inspection that stands in for the tab sensor. Loaded dispatch-to-admission timing requires a supported timing bound under declared workload and resource limits, challenged by loaded and adversarial latency measurements. By contrast, the coverage factor ${c_h}$ for a safety shield requires statistical qualification on the declared population of hazardous proposals, whereas finite stress tests alone cannot establish a worst-case timing bound. Gaps between formal plant models and physical hardware are theoretical deficits, requiring robust control bounds. A scope mismatch differs from all three because no measurement, trial, or proof inside the current architecture can fix it. Claiming a de-energized safe stop for a balancing biped is the clearest example: cutting torque is itself the hazard, so the remedy is a different fallback architecture or a narrower claim, as [[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/04 Governing the Machine/18 Engineering Physical AI/V4-18.05 Choosing the Next Capability#^sec-frontier-four-open-challenges|section 18.4.3]] discusses.

Statistical extreme value theory bounds rare tail exceedances only when its asymptotic and independence assumptions hold; fitting a parametric curve to test data cannot validate unmodeled contact physics or reveal unobserved floor friction. Similarly, Hamilton-Jacobi reachability analysis yields invariant sets only for an explicitly declared plant and disturbance bound. Moving an unverified friction assumption from field testing into a rigid-body simulator or an analytical proof leaves it just as unverified as before.

↪ Extreme value theory and its asymptotic and independence assumptions are developed in **[[05 Reference/Volume IV - Physical AI/V4 Machine Learning Background#^sec-appendix-ml-evt|Extreme Value Theory and Tail Risk]]**.

↪ Hamilton-Jacobi reachability and its declared disturbance model are developed in **[[05 Reference/Volume IV - Physical AI/V4 Control and Dynamics#^sec-appendix-control-hj-reachability|Hamilton-Jacobi reachability and worst-case differential games]]**.

### What a Restriction Costs

^sec-frontier-deployment-economics

Knowing which experiment would close an entry does not say whether running it is worth the cost. An entry in the residual-claims register can buy protection at a throughput cost. On the warehouse machine, the oil-film restriction lowers cruising speed along the aisle from 1.3 m/s to 1 m/s, imposing a 23.1 percent speed penalty. At steady cruise, this derating stretches transit time across each 30 m aisle segment from 23.1 s to 30 s. That single constraint adds 30 percent more transit time per leg before accounting for acceleration profiles and docking maneuvers. Because transit time scales as $1/{v}$, the percentage increase in leg time exceeds the percentage cut in speed.

An operational restriction remains in force until its closure test passes. That test carries a tangible empirical cost: the 2,995 seeded-film physical trials specified in the register, for example. The separate claim about safety shield coverage requires 29,956 seeded hazardous proposals; that campaign does not close the film entry. The claims register therefore confronts systems engineers with an economic optimization problem alongside the technical one. On throughput savings alone, a formal closure campaign pays back when the cumulative value recovered exceeds the combined cost of the test campaign and the sensing instrument it qualifies. A full investment decision must also account for changes in exposure, maintenance, and the value of newly supported tasks. An operational restriction can remain the economical choice when its physical premise can be maintained reliably at low cost, such as through qualified cleaning and spill-control procedures along a few pallet-jack routes. Evaluating this trade-off requires site-specific parameters: the economic value of a completed task, the machine’s active duty hours on restricted routes, and the marginal expense of executing controlled test trials. In the absence of proprietary site economics, time provides the universal benchmark that transfers directly across facilities.

The same operational accounting governs fleet deployments. When commercial logistics facilities contract bipedal humanoids under robots-as-a-service agreements ([[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/04 Governing the Machine/18 Engineering Physical AI/V4-18.08 Source References#^ref-gxo2024agility|GXO Logistics 2024]]), commercial performance is measured by volume moved, 100,000 totes at an initial customer facility by late 2025 ([[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/04 Governing the Machine/18 Engineering Physical AI/V4-18.08 Source References#^ref-agility2025totes|Agility Robotics 2025]]). In these operational environments, physical interventions, speed restrictions, and withheld autonomy modes can reduce completed-task throughput; their billing effect depends on the service contract. The gating criteria established during release validation translate directly into ongoing operating expenses.

Intervention rates incur a double cost. Frequent human takeovers consume costly labor hours and tether fleet operations to local technician availability. Conversely, rare interventions introduce the classic ironies of automation: an operator who seldom intervenes struggles to maintain the situational awareness and manual dexterity needed when an emergency takeover occurs ([[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/04 Governing the Machine/18 Engineering Physical AI/V4-18.08 Source References#^ref-bainbridge1983ironies|Bainbridge 1983]]). A defensible business case therefore reports proposer autonomy, whole-machine task success, and protective responses separately, as the first fallacy in [[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/04 Governing the Machine/18 Engineering Physical AI/V4-18.06 Fallacies and Pitfalls#^sec-frontier-fallacies-pitfalls|section 18.5]] insists, because each carries a different price.

↩︎ The labor a deployment moves onto technicians, sweep crews, and remote assistants, and the uses of their data it bars, are established in **[[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/04 Governing the Machine/17 Responsible Physical AI/V4-17.09 The Labor the Machine Moves#^sec-responsibility-worker-burden|The Labor the Machine Moves]]**.

### Research That Changes an Engineering Result

^sec-frontier-four-open-challenges

The oil-film campaign can be priced because the closure test itself is already known: run 2,995 paired braking trials on a representative spill or qualify an online probe. Other open entries in a register cannot be closed by scheduling a standard test campaign because no engineering method yet supplies the missing evidence. In physical AI, research warrants industry and academic investment when its resolution improves useful task performance within justified limits or supplies support for a broader operating claim. Six central challenges define this research frontier as of 2026 ([[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/04 Governing the Machine/18 Engineering Physical AI/V4-18.05 Choosing the Next Capability#^tbl-18-frontier-agenda|table 3]]); each stresses one of the four laws, falls into one gap class of [[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/04 Governing the Machine/18 Engineering Physical AI/V4-18.05 Choosing the Next Capability#^sec-frontier-four-gap-classes|section 18.4.1]], and names the form of evidence that would count as progress.

Generalist and cross-embodiment policies stress the fourth law, permission must be earned, because evidence gathered on one body and one model version must now earn permission for another. A single pretrained vision-language-action (VLA) foundation model can now control diverse kinematics. For example, flow-matching architectures have been trained across single-arm, dual-arm, and mobile manipulation platforms from a unified model ([[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/04 Governing the Machine/18 Engineering Physical AI/V4-18.08 Source References#^ref-black2024pi0|Black et al. 2024]]), using datasets from different embodiments. Modern pipelines continuously fine-tune from production deployments via reinforcement learning with human interventions ([[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/04 Governing the Machine/18 Engineering Physical AI/V4-18.08 Source References#^ref-physicalintelligence2025recap|Physical Intelligence et al. 2025]]) or remap learned trajectories across distinct kinematic morphologies ([[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/04 Governing the Machine/18 Engineering Physical AI/V4-18.08 Source References#^ref-geminirobotics2025gemini15|Gemini Robotics Team 2025]]).

↩︎ Why demonstrations logged on one embodiment carry its dynamics and do not transfer unchanged to another is established in **[[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/02 Teaching the Machine/05 Physical Data/V4-05.10 Cross-Embodiment Transfer#^sec-data-cross-embodiment|Cross-Embodiment Transfer]]**.

However, empirical safety evidence compiled on a dual-arm cobot cannot be assumed to hold when that same policy executes on a mobile manipulator. Furthermore, policies that continually adapt in production require reopening the release claims whose supporting evidence depends on the changed behavior (**[[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/04 Governing the Machine/16 Deployment Release/V4-16.10 Maintaining Release Through Updates#^sec-release-deployment-lifecycle|Maintaining Release Through Updates]]**). The enduring challenge is establishing formal transfer guarantees across both physical embodiments and continuous model updates. Concrete progress requires rigorous transfer arguments with explicit kinematic and dynamic equivalence conditions, verifiable by predicting a new morphology’s closed-loop failure distributions before physical deployment. The cross-embodiment approach also reinforces the necessity of an independent permission pipeline: physical protection limits are calibrated to each specific mechanical chassis and remain invariant regardless of how frequently upstream generalist weights are updated.

Data scaling is the empirical frontier of the second law, endogenous data. Large-scale empirical studies encompassing more than 40,000 real-robot teleoperated demonstrations indicate that out-of-distribution generalization across novel environments and visual scenes follows an approximate power law governed by environmental diversity, whereas collecting redundant demonstrations within identical scenes shows sharp diminishing returns ([[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/04 Governing the Machine/18 Engineering Physical AI/V4-18.08 Source References#^ref-lin2025datascaling|Lin et al. 2025]]). In physical systems, structural diversity rather than raw sample count dictates data utility.

The open challenge is determining whether these empirical scaling laws generalize to contact-rich manipulation, long-horizon multi-step planning, and safety-critical edge states. In embodied settings, an agent’s closed-loop control actions actively determine its subsequent perceptual distributions, concentrating risk in rare, unmodeled corner cases. Meaningful progress requires publishing verified scaling curves that measure tail-risk closed-loop failure rates within declared operational design domains (ODDs), replacing benchmark evaluations that measure only average-case task completion rates.

↩︎ Why the diversity of pooled demonstrations, not their raw count, governs their value to a target task is established in **[[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/02 Teaching the Machine/05 Physical Data/V4-05.10 Cross-Embodiment Transfer#^sec-data-scaling-frontier|Choosing pooled experience for the target task]]**.

Generative world models also stress the fourth law, because they offer a way around the physical exposure wall only if their rollouts count as evidence. Latent dynamics formulations and high-fidelity video generation models increasingly act as general-purpose physics simulators ([[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/04 Governing the Machine/18 Engineering Physical AI/V4-18.08 Source References#^ref-nvidia2025cosmos|NVIDIA et al. 2025]]; [[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/04 Governing the Machine/18 Engineering Physical AI/V4-18.08 Source References#^ref-assran2025vjepa2|Assran et al. 2025]]). Researchers have begun deploying these generative models directly as policy evaluators, demonstrating strong correlation with real-robot task execution on benchmark tasks ([[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/04 Governing the Machine/18 Engineering Physical AI/V4-18.08 Source References#^ref-jeon2026roboworld|Jeon et al. 2026]]). Synthetic rollouts execute at large parallel scale for a fraction of the cost of physical hardware hours.

Nevertheless, strict evidentiary limits constrain synthetic evaluation. A learned world model may estimate latent physical variables, but its training fit does not validate those estimates in unobserved regimes or justify extrapolating friction discontinuities or structural compliance omitted from its training distribution. The central research question is establishing when synthetic evaluation in a learned world model constitutes valid evidentiary support for real-world release claims. Genuine progress requires formalizing proxy-transfer bounds (**[[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/02 Teaching the Machine/07 Closed-Loop Evaluation/V4-07.05 Evidence Regimes#^sec-evaluation-proxy-transfer-law|Simulation as a conditional proxy]]**) validated against the empirical tail of real-world physical failures rather than aggregate nominal success, accompanied by end-to-end dataset and architecture provenance tracking.

↩︎ Provenance records for learned world models, binding architecture, checkpoint, and dataset lineage, are established in **[[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/02 Teaching the Machine/06 Policy Training/V4-06.05 The Simulator as a System Component#^sec-training-world-models|Learned world models as simulators]]**.

Dynamically stable machines, such as bipedal humanoids and high-speed quadrupeds, are the most stringent test of the first law, irreversibility. As highlighted in perspective [4.2](https://mlsysbook.ai/vol4/04_nervous/04_nervous.html#capstone-nervous-humanoid-crucible), perspective [7.2](https://mlsysbook.ai/vol4/07_evaluation/07_evaluation.html#capstone-evaluation-humanoid-crucible), perspective [13.1](https://mlsysbook.ai/vol4/13_placement/13_placement.html#capstone-placement-humanoid-crucible), and perspective [16.1](https://mlsysbook.ai/vol4/16_release/16_release.html#capstone-release-humanoid-crucible), the entire architectural chain of proposal, permission, and admission applies directly to balancing robots, with one catastrophic exception: fallback safe states. For the wheeled platform or stationary manipulator considered here, the fallback first reaches a qualified, mechanically restrained standstill before removing drive torque. On a balancing biped in mid-stride, cutting motor power induces an unrecoverable ballistic fall, transforming the safety mechanism into the primary hazard. Instead of bringing the system to an immediate stop, a balancing machine must execute dynamic recovery maneuvers: taking a stabilizing step, initiating a controlled crouch, or executing an active powered descent to a mechanically supported resting posture. Traditional functional safety standards, however, were built around fail-passive de-energization (the torque-removal rungs of the fallback ladder, which **[[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/03 Running the Machine/12 Safety Enforcement/V4-12.10 The Fallback Ladder#^sec-enforcement-four-rungs|The four rungs of the fallback ladder]]** treats as fail-safe only for a plant already at a mechanically restrained standstill). This created what safety researchers term the *fail-passive gap*[[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/04 Governing the Machine/18 Engineering Physical AI/V4-18.09 Footnotes#^fn7|⁷]] ([[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/04 Governing the Machine/18 Engineering Physical AI/V4-18.08 Source References#^ref-ding2026failpassive|Ding et al. 2026]]). Bridging this divide requires certifying active closed-loop balance stabilization as a safety-rated critical function. Demonstrating meaningful progress requires designing certifiable dynamic reaction chains, analogous to automotive emergency braking profiles. These chains must reliably steer the robot from each qualified gait phase, under the declared fault conditions, into a stable, supported configuration within bounded peak power and kinetic energy budgets. The most capable learned machines are thus the ones for which a safe stop is hardest to define.

Contact-rich, dexterous manipulation centers on physical AI’s canonical unobserved state: high-frequency contact interaction. Determining whether a snap-fit latch yields, a packaging tab seats, or a fragile container begins to crush cannot be reliably deduced from joint motor currents or external RGB-D cameras alone. High-resolution tactile sensing provides a direct channel for several of these contact dynamics; each claimed observation still needs qualification for its materials, geometry, and deadline. Modern vision-based elastomeric tactile sensors directly capture surface deformation fields, contact geometry, and distributed shear stresses at the end-effector ([[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/04 Governing the Machine/18 Engineering Physical AI/V4-18.08 Source References#^ref-yuan2017gelsight|Yuan et al. 2017]]).

↩︎ Optical elastomer tactile sensing and its timing and calibration obligations are established in **[[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/03 Running the Machine/08 Sensor Perception/V4-08.03 Capture Physics and Calibration#^sec-perception-tactile-grounding|Tactile contact sensing]]**.

Closing the loop on contact, however, challenges the third law, proposal is not permission. Contact forces escalate within milliseconds of initial impact, far outstripping the round-trip latency of high-level neural planners and placing strict burdens on low-level passive or series elastic compliance ([[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/04 Governing the Machine/18 Engineering Physical AI/V4-18.04 The Epistemic Frontier#^sec-frontier-passive-compliance-containment|section 18.3.4.2]]). The open engineering challenge is integrating tactile feedback into the formal permission path. Concrete progress requires qualifying tactile perception channels with rigorous statistical miss rates for incipient slip and shear yield, coupled with low-latency impedance controllers whose force limits can be deterministically verified and bounded by safety hardware ([[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/04 Governing the Machine/18 Engineering Physical AI/V4-18.08 Source References#^ref-tedrake2023manipulation|Tedrake 2023]]).

Certifying learned models that directly implement safety functions is the central challenge of the fourth law. Although such components are assigned the notified-body route of **[[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/04 Governing the Machine/16 Deployment Release/V4-16.04 Safety Cases and Claims#^sec-release-standards-map|The standards map for the running machine]]**, the robotics industry lacks standardized methodologies to certify deep neural networks at automotive or industrial safety integrity levels (SIL) or PL, because current verification techniques face stark limitations. Exact satisfiability modulo theories (SMT)-based and mixed-integer linear programming verification tools can establish formal input-output properties over declared neural network weights ([[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/04 Governing the Machine/18 Engineering Physical AI/V4-18.08 Source References#^ref-katz2017reluplex|Katz et al. 2017]]), yet these proofs remain vulnerable to open-world modeling gaps outside the declared plant and observations. Purely statistical validation collides with the exposure wall, requiring billions of physical test cycles to establish low target failure probabilities. Runtime safety filters and shield wrappers transfer the verification burden onto the shield’s own coverage and latency envelope ([[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/04 Governing the Machine/18 Engineering Physical AI/V4-18.08 Source References#^ref-hsu2024safetyfilter|Hsu et al. 2024]]). The unresolved systems challenge is constructing hybrid safety arguments: composing analytical proofs where deterministic physics models are tractable with statistical bounds where perceptual models operate, and allocating risk budgets across both tiers. Until such composite certification arguments mature, the architecture developed here keeps learned proposers outside the safety-critical permission path. Verifiable progress requires publishing complete, audited conformity dossiers for learned safety components alongside their formal assurance cases.

Two systems challenges cut across all six problems. The first is continual online adaptation, which turns the model into a moving target. Whether a proposer retrains asynchronously on aggregated fleet telemetry or adapts its latent state online to accommodate component wear and shifting terrain ([[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/04 Governing the Machine/18 Engineering Physical AI/V4-18.08 Source References#^ref-kumar2021rma|Kumar et al. 2021]]), background model updates must never starve the permission pipeline of compute or bus bandwidth. The second is multi-robot fleet coordination, which couples otherwise independent permission boundaries. Transmitting shared task intents across lossy wireless links cannot substitute for the local, hard-real-time sensor streams required by an individual robot’s admission gate. Physically cooperative manipulation of a shared payload likewise couples actuator contact dynamics directly across platforms. Across all of these problems, advancing physical AI does not require abandoning established systems rigor; it requires extending it. Unlocking the next capability begins by isolating a concrete physical task that the current system cannot safely execute, identifying the missing empirical premise or unmeasured observable, and engineering the precise evidential argument required to justify expanding the machine’s operational envelope.
