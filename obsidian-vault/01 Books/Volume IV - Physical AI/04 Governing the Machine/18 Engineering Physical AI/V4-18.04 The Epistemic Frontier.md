# V4-18.04 The Epistemic Frontier

[[Home|Home]] · [[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/Volume 4 Overview|Volume Overview]] · [[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/04 Governing the Machine/18 Engineering Physical AI/V4-18 Engineering Physical AI|V4-18 Engineering Physical AI]]

^sec-frontier-epistemic-limits

A packaging cell’s camera can confirm an arm’s position and approve a transport motion without verifying that a hidden mechanical tab has engaged. Similarly, a warehouse robot’s lidar and cameras can clear a navigation path across an aisle without determining whether the concrete floor provides enough traction to brake. These gaps are epistemic limits: boundaries on what the available sensors and analytical arguments can justify. Additional training can improve a policy’s action proposals, but machine learning cannot manufacture an unmeasured physical state. Systems engineers must therefore determine whether justifying the next operating claim requires broader training experience or a physical measurement absent from the existing sensor suite.

Consider an aisle where a pallet jack with a leaking hydraulic line has just crossed, leaving a thin, clear film of fluid on polished concrete. The mobile manipulator’s stopping budget assumes the floor supplies enough surface friction for credible deceleration, which dry industrial concrete reliably does. Under an oil film, however, available traction collapses, yet neither standard navigation cameras nor time-of-flight lidars can tell the clear fluid apart from dry concrete. In this failure mode the safety architecture runs exactly as specified. The software permission path meets every deadline, chunk leases expire on schedule, and the drive controller applies braking torque right on time. Even so, the robot slides past its stop line and collides at the rack end. The failure comes not from software latency or actuator lag, but from an unmonitored premise, the local wheel-floor friction coefficient before braking, and the stopping budget prices that premise.

The claim adjudication in **[[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/04 Governing the Machine/16 Deployment Release/V4-16.04 Safety Cases and Claims#^sec-release-argument-plant-alignment|Matching the argument to the machine]]** already encountered this premise: claim C3 (braking traction) was refused on this book’s own evidence and deferred to this chapter as an unresolved premise. The credible deceleration ${a_{\text{brake}}}$ that **[[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/01 Machine Anatomy/02 The Physical Body/V4-02 The Physical Body#^sec-body|The Physical Body]]** credited to the brakes was always conditional on the floor. Made explicit, that condition says the deceleration a machine actually has available is the smaller of what its brakes can deliver and what tire-floor traction can transmit:[[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/04 Governing the Machine/18 Engineering Physical AI/V4-18.09 Footnotes#^fn1|¹]]

$$
{a} = \min({a_{\text{brake}}}, {\mu} {g}). \tag{1}
$$

 The friction term is the traction ceiling of **[[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/01 Machine Anatomy/02 The Physical Body/V4-02 The Physical Body#^sec-body|The Physical Body]]** (**[[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/01 Machine Anatomy/02 The Physical Body/V4-02.04 Kinetic Momentum#^fig-02-braking-fbd|figure]]** and **[[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/01 Machine Anatomy/02 The Physical Body/V4-02.05 Actuator Transmission Limits#^sec-body-electrical-time-constants|Electrical time constants, back-EMF, and current clamping]]**), ${\mu} {\phi_{\text{drive}}} {g}$, taken with all of the weight on the driven wheels (${\phi_{\text{drive}}} = 1$). That is the case that sets the friction floor, the same optimistic all-weight screen that claim C3 was adjudicated against. At the drive wheels’ rated load share the floor is higher, as that notebook computes, so every friction margin below is the optimistic one. On an oily surface, interface friction rather than motor torque becomes the binding constraint.

**Napkin Math 18.2: The friction floor**

**Problem**: A clear hydraulic film lies across the aisle. Does the known-component stop from the candidate 1.3 m/s aisle speed still fit the rack end’s 1.10 m clear distance?

**Variables**:

- Credible deceleration ${a_{\text{brake}}}$ = 2 m/s² (Illustrative, **[[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/01 Machine Anatomy/02 The Physical Body/V4-02.04 Kinetic Momentum#^sec-body-kinetic-momentum|Kinetic Momentum]]**) and ${g}$ = 9.81 m/s² (Defined standard-gravity constant)
- Floor friction ${\mu}$ = 0.60 on dry industrial concrete and 0.05 under the film (both Illustrative; see the Reader Guide)
- Aisle speed ${v}$ = 1.3 m/s (Chosen) and clear distance ${D_{\text{clear}}}$ = 1.10 m (Illustrative)
- Known-component budget, the seven rows of **[[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/03 Running the Machine/12 Safety Enforcement/V4-12.04 Stopping Envelopes#^tbl-enforcement-stopping-budget|The warehouse mobile manipulator's stopping budget]]**, which leave out the dispatch-to-admission interval ${\Delta_{\text{da}}}$ that is allocated but not yet measured: pre-brake delay ${\tau_{\text{delay}}}$ = 133.6 ms, jerk bound ${j_{\max}}$ = 7.11 m/s³, and fixed overheads of 190 mm (all Derived)

**Math**:

1.  **Friction floor** ([[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/04 Governing the Machine/18 Engineering Physical AI/V4-18.04 The Epistemic Frontier#^eq-frontier-credible-deceleration|equation 1]]): The brake binds only while ${\mu} \ge {a_{\text{brake}}}/{g}$ = 2 m/s² $/$ 9.81 m/s² = 0.204. Dry concrete clears it; the film does not, so ${a} = {\mu} {g}$ = 0.05 $\times$ 9.81 m/s² = 0.49 m/s².
2.  **Dry-floor budget**: Delay travel ${v}{\tau_{\text{delay}}}$ $+$ braking ${v}^2/2{a}$ $+$ jerk-ramp travel ${v} {a}/2{j_{\max}}$ (the S-curve term of row 6, valid in the trapezoidal regime because ${v} > {a}^2/{j_{\max}}$) $+$ overheads = 173.7 mm $+$ 422.5 mm $+$ 182.8 mm $+$ 190 mm = 969.0 mm.
3.  **Film budget**: The same terms at ${a}$ = 0.49 m/s² give 173.7 mm $+$ 1,723.3 mm $+$ 44.8 mm $+$ 190 mm = 2.13 m.

**Result**: On the film the stop grows from 969.0 mm to 2.13 m, far past the 1.10 m clear distance, before the dispatch-to-admission interval is even added.

**Systems insight**: Below ${\mu}$ = 0.204 the floor, not the brake or the timing, sets the deceleration, and the braking term grows as $1/{\mu}$. Faster admission can recover only the 173.7 mm of delay travel, so no improvement in the permission path restores the dry-floor mode on the film. Only a restriction or a measurement at the tire-floor interface can keep ${d_{\text{stop}}} \le {D_{\text{clear}}}$.

The calculation omits the dispatch-to-admission interval, which the discrete placement of **[[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/03 Running the Machine/13 Workload Placement/V4-13.07 Silicon Isolation Decisions#^sec-placement-dispatch-admission|The board-link budget and the dispatch-to-admission interval]]** allocates but no loaded measurement yet confirms. That interval adds travel and reduces every derived speed ceiling, yet even the known-component budget already excludes the film. Moreover, the friction premise did not appear only when fluid leaked onto the concrete. The candidate normal operating mode of **[[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/03 Running the Machine/12 Safety Enforcement/V4-12.04 Stopping Envelopes#^sec-enforcement-composite-budget|Sizing the composite stopping budget]]** always assumed that unmeasured friction floor across every aisle, dry or wet. Software timing and physical traction are independent requirements, since bounding scheduling delays does not guarantee floor grip and confirming floor grip does not remove scheduling delays. On the oil film, the drive electronics continue to regulate motor current and commanded torque within milliseconds, yet that torque slows the base only through tire-floor contact. Neither a faster enforcer nor another layer of neural inference therefore replaces the interface measurement or the speed restriction the notebook calls for.

↩︎ Current regulation within milliseconds through electrical time constants and current clamping is established in **[[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/01 Machine Anatomy/02 The Physical Body/V4-02.05 Actuator Transmission Limits#^sec-body-electrical-time-constants|Electrical time constants, back-EMF, and current clamping]]**.

The manipulator arm’s contact limit at the door latch has the same structure. Safety depends on a latch that holds under any force below the arm’s contact ceiling. Mechanical wear, material fatigue, or corrosion can silently lower the latch’s yield force below that ceiling, even while joint torque sensors report readings identical to those against a sound strike plate. A wrist force-torque sensor whose zero-point calibration has drifted creates the same hazard for every contact threshold that depends on its signal ([[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/04 Governing the Machine/18 Engineering Physical AI/V4-18.04 The Epistemic Frontier#^sec-frontier-passive-compliance-containment|section 18.3.4.2]]).

In each case, a machine can leave its safe operating envelope while internal telemetry stays completely nominal. Successful test runs under nominal conditions provide no safety guarantee once an underlying physical premise fails. A safety case must explicitly list each unobserved premise, restrict operating authority while that premise remains unverified, and state the physical evidence required to close it. For every open entry, the engineer must determine what the machine can physically measure, what actuator limits can still prevent, and which operating claims must remain refused.

### What the Release Claim Rests On

^sec-frontier-method-limits

Extensive testing of one link in a safety case cannot compensate for an unmeasured premise in another: a thousand hours of fault-free perception trials will not stop a base whose tires lose traction on an oily floor.[[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/04 Governing the Machine/18 Engineering Physical AI/V4-18.09 Footnotes#^fn2|²]] A mobile manipulator’s release claim forms a chain of dependent arguments tied to its operating hazards and chosen mitigations. The complete safety case must establish the operational design domain, measured actuator performance, stopping bounds, component interface contracts, and independent permission filtering for learned proposals. Hazards managed by runtime detection require verified detector coverage and bounded monitoring latency, whereas hazards managed through passive compliance or operating restrictions require physical evidence that those safeguards hold across declared payloads, component wear, and site conditions.

Each premise in a safety case carries one of five statuses: established by measurement or derivation, assumed, decided by accountable judgment, unsupported, or outside the threat model. (The Reader Guide lists the same five with examples; the audits of this chapter and its problems use these exact labels.) The envelope trichotomy (definition [16.3](https://mlsysbook.ai/vol4/16_release/16_release.html#dfn-release-envelope-trichotomy)) classifies an actively monitored runtime condition as known-true, known-false, or unknown. That trichotomy presupposes a monitor. “Unknown” marks a monitored condition whose evidence has lapsed, and the permission path answers it at once with a validated stop or hold until fresh evidence returns. An unobserved premise has no runtime monitor whose report can trigger that transition; its missing evidence must instead be recorded in the release argument. Telemetry stays nominal while the premise fails, and holding the machine until a report arrives would hold it forever, because no report will come. Such premises require standing restrictions decided before operation; a fallback cannot be triggered by evidence the machine never receives. Release certification must therefore either validate and maintain these premises through external physical evidence or restrict operating authority accordingly. For the mobile base, stopping-distance guarantees rest on an assumed wheel-floor friction coefficient that cannot be observed before braking. For the manipulator arm, contact safety assumes a minimum yield strength for the door latch and the objects it handles, a threshold set by engineering judgment rather than real-time measurement. For the wrist sensor, force-limiting safety functions assume zero-point calibration stability, a condition internal telemetry cannot verify without an unloaded physical tare. Recording these premises explicitly does not weaken a release case; it marks the exact boundary where system guarantees hold and beyond which operating authority must be withdrawn.

Separating physical measurement from accountable judgment is essential to systems engineering. Testing and statistical analysis can estimate physical exposure, quantify sensor noise, and bound parameter uncertainty, but test data cannot choose an acceptable residual risk or decide which people in a facility must bear that risk. Those choices are accountable engineering decisions that must be recorded as explicit judgments rather than presented as empirical facts. Similarly, the verification campaign’s threat model bounds where the safety record holds. Field trials across normal fleet workflows and random component faults do not by themselves support claims about deliberate attacks outside the tested conditions. A test campaign showing that a vision pipeline tolerates lens flare, sensor noise, and partial occlusions provides no evidence that it resists adversarial optical projections, tampered policy weights, or deliberate denial-of-service flooding on the internal communication bus.

↩︎ The fault list that bounds what verification tests, including transduction-layer attacks on sensors and on the proposer, is established in **[[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/04 Governing the Machine/15 Adversarial Verification/V4-15.07 Deriving the Fault List#^sec-verification-deriving-fault-list|Deriving the Fault List]]**.

### Conditional Shield Credit and Common-Cause Bounds

^sec-frontier-shield-dilution

Treating an independent permission path as a substitute for a missing floor-friction measurement asks the safety shield to stop a hazard it cannot sense. Even for hazards within its view, a shield reduces the failure rate only in proportion to its own tested coverage. Pricing what the permission path actually contributes to a hazard-rate claim separates the hazards a shield can attenuate from the register entries it can never close. **[[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/03 Running the Machine/12 Safety Enforcement/V4-12.08 The Minimal Intervention Principle and Optimization Filters#^sec-enforcement-minimal-intervention|The Minimal Intervention Principle and Optimization Filters]]** established deterministic guarantees for each command a safety shield checks, but turning those per-command guarantees into a system-level hazard rate requires statistical accounting. Because a shield acts as a conditional filter, the resulting hazard rate depends on how often the policy proposes hazardous actions, how reliably the shield intercepts them in those exact states, and how often failures occur outside the shield’s sensing domain. Notebook [18.3](https://mlsysbook.ai/vol4/18_frontier/18_frontier.html#nbk-frontier-fleet-exposure-and-shielded-decomposition) prices these terms in an illustrative deployment.

Consider an illustrative system target of 1 × 10⁻⁹ $\text{h}^{-1}$. This number comes from civil aviation certification, where catastrophic failure conditions must be shown to be “extremely improbable,” on the order of $10^{-9}$ per flight hour[[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/04 Governing the Machine/18 Engineering Physical AI/V4-18.09 Footnotes#^fn3|³]] ([[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/04 Governing the Machine/18 Engineering Physical AI/V4-18.08 Source References#^ref-faa1988ac251309|Federal Aviation Administration 1988]]). While no industrial robotics standard requires this threshold, examining it shows the statistical burden a borrowed safety target imposes. As the exposure wall established, physical operating hours alone cannot certify failure rates of this scale.[[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/04 Governing the Machine/18 Engineering Physical AI/V4-18.09 Footnotes#^fn4|⁴]] The practical engineering question is what safety margin a runtime shield genuinely contributes against that target, and what supporting evidence its own operation requires.

↩︎ The exposure wall, the event-free operating hours needed to bound a rare hazard rate, is established in **[[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/02 Teaching the Machine/07 Closed-Loop Evaluation/V4-07.10 Verification Ceilings and the Exposure Wall#^sec-evaluation-limits|Verification Ceilings and the Exposure Wall]]**.

A safety shield argument is inherently *conditional*. Observed incident rates across an operating fleet already include the protective interventions of runtime filters and human supervisors, so multiplying an observed incident rate by a shield’s interception factor double-counts that protection. For a defined hazard class ${h}$, let ${\lambda_{\text{proposal}, h}}$ denote the arrival rate of unshielded, hazardous action proposals emitted by the policy before the permission layer. Let ${c_h} = \Pr(\text{intercept} \mid \text{hazardous proposal}, {h})$ denote the verified interception coverage for that hazard class. Including an independently bounded hardware fault and common-cause arrival rate ${\lambda_{\text{other}}}$, the conditional hazard rate obeys [[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/04 Governing the Machine/18 Engineering Physical AI/V4-18.04 The Epistemic Frontier#^eq-frontier-shielded-hazard|equation 2]]:

$$
{\lambda_{\text{system}, h}} \le {\lambda_{\text{proposal}, h}}(1-{c_h})+{\lambda_{\text{other}}} \tag{2}
$$

 Here, ${\lambda_{\text{system}, h}}$, ${\lambda_{\text{proposal}, h}}$, and ${\lambda_{\text{other}}}$ are rates per operating hour, while ${c_h}$ is a unitless conditional probability. The limiting cases make the shield’s role plain. With perfect detection and interception (${c_h} = 1$), the upper bound reduces to the hardware and common-cause term ${\lambda_{\text{other}}}$, a residual no coverage removes. Conversely, if the shield has zero coverage (${c_h} = 0$), the raw proposal hazard rate passes straight to the actuators. This relation turns the runtime assurance architecture, which pairs an unverified primary controller with a verified safety filter ([[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/04 Governing the Machine/18 Engineering Physical AI/V4-18.08 Source References#^ref-sha2001using|Sha 2001]]; [[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/04 Governing the Machine/18 Engineering Physical AI/V4-18.08 Source References#^ref-hsu2024safetyfilter|Hsu et al. 2024]]), into a checkable hazard-rate claim.

^eq-frontier-shielded-hazard

↩︎ The Simplex architecture, which pairs a capable unverified proposer with a verified protective controller, is established in **[[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/03 Running the Machine/12 Safety Enforcement/V4-12.02 The Per-Tick Gatekeeper#^sec-enforcement-deterministic-gatekeeper|The Per-Tick Gatekeeper]]**.

Using [[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/04 Governing the Machine/18 Engineering Physical AI/V4-18.04 The Epistemic Frontier#^eq-frontier-shielded-hazard|equation 2]] requires four premises. The first is a verified **counterfactual oracle**: an evaluation harness that determines whether an unshielded action proposal would have caused a safety violation. Without this oracle or another justified way to identify or conservatively bound hazardous proposals, the raw proposal error rate ${\lambda_{\text{proposal}, h}}$ has no supporting evidence. The remaining requirements are timely hazard detection, adequate braking authority, and a careful accounting of correlated failures. Designers frequently overlook this final common-cause term. Release claim C5 required physical testing rather than a theoretical independence assumption because separate implementations of the same specification fail together far more often than statistical independence predicts (**[[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/04 Governing the Machine/16 Deployment Release/V4-16.04 Safety Cases and Claims#^sec-release-contextual-assumptions|Contextual assumptions and the weakest link]]**). The product ${\lambda_{\text{proposal}, h}}(1-{c_h})$ uses interception coverage conditional on hazardous proposals; substituting coverage measured on unrelated states erases that conditioning. An occluded camera frame that causes the neural policy to propose a bad trajectory can corrupt the safety shield’s state estimator at the same moment. That shared failure does not merely add an independent background term to ${\lambda_{\text{other}}}$; it lowers ${c_h}$ in the same states where policy errors happen. Interception coverage ${c_h}$ must therefore be measured across the actual states where the policy fails rather than on an idealized set of synthetic test cases. The equation gives no credit for hazards the shield cannot sense, such as oil films or fatigue-weakened latches, and no credit for the shield at all until each of the four premises is supported. Until then the release must restrict operation instead.

Bounding the system hazard rate requires bounding the raw proposal rate ${\lambda_{\text{proposal}, h}}$ from an adjudicated execution log before crediting shield attenuation. Under a stationary Poisson arrival model with zero observed hazardous proposals, this upper bound follows the rule of three ([[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/04 Governing the Machine/18 Engineering Physical AI/V4-18.08 Source References#^ref-hanley1983nothing|Hanley and Lippman-Hand 1983]]) from **[[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/02 Teaching the Machine/07 Closed-Loop Evaluation/V4-07.07 Confidence from a Finite Campaign#^eq-eval-rule-of-three|equation 7.4]]**, with proposal operating hours in place of discrete trials. For an adjudicated log spanning ${T}$ operating hours at significance level ${\alpha_{\text{sig}}}$, the one-sided upper confidence bound is $-\ln{\alpha_{\text{sig}}}/{T}$, or roughly $3/{T}$ at 95 percent confidence. When the interception coverage ${c_h}$ is also estimated empirically from zero-miss seeded trials rather than given as a known constant, the product ${\lambda_{\text{proposal}, h}}(1-{c_h})$ rests on two separate confidence bounds that can each fail. Guaranteeing that both bounds hold simultaneously at overall significance ${\alpha_{\text{sig}}}$ requires splitting the error budget across the continuous proposal-log test and the discrete coverage-trial test via the union bound, testing each factor at significance ${\alpha_{\text{sig}}}/2$:

**Napkin Math 18.3: The coverage bill**

**Problem**: In an illustrative scenario, a fleet keeps a fully adjudicated proposal log for one release cycle, judged by a validated counterfactual oracle, and the log records no hazardous proposal. If a named shield’s interception coverage for that hazard class were independently established, would the modeled residual of [[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/04 Governing the Machine/18 Engineering Physical AI/V4-18.04 The Epistemic Frontier#^eq-frontier-shielded-hazard|equation 2]] meet the system target, and what evidence would it take to show that it does?

**Variables**:

- System target ${\lambda_{\text{target}}}$ = 1 × 10⁻⁹ $\text{h}^{-1}$ (Illustrative)
- Proposal log ${T}$ = 100 units $\times$ 90 days $\times$ 24 h = 2.16 × 10⁵ proposal-hours with zero hazardous proposals (Illustrative)
- Class-specific coverage ${c_h}$ = 0.9999 and ${\lambda_{\text{other}}}$ = 0 (Illustrative; the hardware and common-cause term is set to zero for this calculation)
- Confidence 95 percent, significance ${\alpha_{\text{sig}}}$ = 0.05 (Chosen)

**Math**:

1.  **Proposal bound** (rule of three with hours in place of trials): $-\ln{\alpha_{\text{sig}}} / {T}$ = 2.996 $/$ 2.16 × 10⁵ h = 1.387 × 10⁻⁵ $\text{h}^{-1}$.
2.  **Residual**: 1.387 × 10⁻⁵ $\times$ 1 × 10⁻⁴ $+$ 0 = 1.387 × 10⁻⁹ $\text{h}^{-1}$, **above** the target. Meeting it at this bound needs ${c_h} \ge 1 -$ 1 × 10⁻⁹ $/$ 1.387 × 10⁻⁵ = 0.9999279.
3.  **Proposal evidence at the assumed coverage**: The bound must fall to 1 × 10⁻⁹ $/$ 1 × 10⁻⁴ = 1 × 10⁻⁵ $\text{h}^{-1}$, which takes 2.996 $/$ 1 × 10⁻⁵ = 3.00 × 10⁵ proposal-hours, or 124.8 days for the fleet, 34.8 days beyond the cycle.
4.  **Coverage evidence**: Bounding ${c_h}$ itself takes $\ln$ 0.05 $/ \ln$ 0.9999 = 29,956 seeded hazardous proposals of that class with no miss, drawn from the states in which the policy errs.
5.  **Joint confidence**: The probability that at least one of two bounds fails is at most the sum of their failure probabilities, so a joint 95 percent statement needs each bound at 97.5 percent (the Bonferroni split). The proposal bound becomes 3.689 $/$ 2.16 × 10⁵ h = 1.708 × 10⁻⁵ $\text{h}^{-1}$, the coverage campaign 36,887 trials, and the residual 1.708 × 10⁻⁹ $\text{h}^{-1}$.

**Result**: The modeled residual misses the target even with the common-cause term set to zero. Supporting the claim takes both a proposal log longer than the release cycle and tens of thousands of seeded interceptions, and the confidence of the combined claim must be budgeted across them.

**Systems insight**: A shield changes the system claim only for hazard classes it can detect and intercept in time. Proposal frequency, conditional interception coverage, hardware faults, and common-cause failures need separate evidence. Oil or material failure outside the shield’s sensing coverage stays in the residual-claims register.

The notebook prices what evidence the exposure model and the safety filter would need; it supplies none for either. Formal verification carries the same scoping requirement, since any mathematical proof applies only to the declared kinematic model, sensor observations, disturbance bounds, and timing guarantees. Whenever a physical premise lacks empirical proof, the release argument must substitute an operating restriction assigned to an accountable owner.

↩︎ The scoping limits of formal certificates to their declared plant, observations, and disturbances are established in **[[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/04 Governing the Machine/15 Adversarial Verification/V4-15.08 The Qualification Ladder#^sec-verification-formal-limits|Scope of a formal verification result]]**.

### A Residual-Claims Register

^sec-frontier-residual-claims-register

Refusing claim C3 in the safety case did not remove the floor-friction premise behind it. Without a durable record tying that open premise to an operating restriction, a later model update could restore normal aisle speed while the floor remains unmeasured. The **residual-claims register** is the sixteenth and closing record in the systems record chain. Taking in the upstream release manifest and approval record, the register binds every unresolved physical premise directly to an operating restriction.

↪ The sixteen-record chain, which the residual-claims register closes, is developed in **[[05 Reference/Volume IV - Physical AI/V4 Systems and Hardware#^sec-appendix-systems-record-register|The record register]]**.

Each register entry names four required elements: the missing physical observable, the active containment mechanism or operating restriction, the accountable owner, and a predeclared closure test. Together they give principle [[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/04 Governing the Machine/V4 Governing the Machine#^pri-vol4-evidence-bounds-authority|4]] direct operational force. Unlike a software defect log or issue tracker, the register is a machine-readable deployment record with a fixed schema. Its parameters set runtime enforcer bounds, and its standing conditions bind facility operations. Operating authority stays derated until test evidence passes the predeclared closure test. Writing a warning note in documentation, or deferring an open premise to a future release without lowering speed or narrowing the operational design domain, provides zero physical protection.

↪ **Byte layout:** Wire formats, data fields, and structural invariants for register entries are documented in **[[05 Reference/Volume IV - Physical AI/V4 Systems and Hardware#^sec-appendix-systems-wire-residual-claims|Residual-claims register]]**.

Not every open premise is physical. Entries that the approval record of **[[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/04 Governing the Machine/17 Responsible Physical AI/V4-17.10 The Approval Record#^sec-responsibility-approval-record|The Approval Record]]** cannot close before release, such as a human role whose feasibility is still `UNKNOWN`, a decision waiting for an owner, or a group of people whose detection has not yet been measured, enter the register in the same form. Each carries the restriction that holds meanwhile, such as a lower speed wherever the unmeasured group works, an owner, and the test that would close it.

Engineers must state each **missing observable** in concrete physical quantities rather than modeling jargon. Calling a failure an “out-of-distribution latent state” does not say what physical sensor must measure it or when an enforcer must intervene. Naming an unmeasured friction coefficient or latch yield force, by contrast, makes the engineering choices concrete. The team can weigh adding a sensor, adding a passive mechanical limit, or narrowing the operational design domain, and everyone can see what test evidence is needed to retire the restriction.

The undetected oil film forms the first register entry for the mobile manipulator. Its premise is that every aisle floor supplies a friction coefficient of at least ${\mu} \ge$ 0.204, the floor needed for credible deceleration. The missing observable is the local friction coefficient under a clear fluid film, which neither onboard cameras nor lidars can tell apart from clean concrete. Because a stop sized for dry concrete overruns the available clearance on an oily floor, the normal speed limit is justified only on surfaces where friction is verified or actively controlled.

An open premise requires an immediate **operational response**. For warehouse aisles, release sign-off can authorize a derated speed of 1 m/s on designated routes maintained under a facility inspection procedure. In this restricted-service mode, scheduled floor scrubbing, spill isolation, and controlled access must maintain a friction coefficient of at least ${\mu} \ge$ 0.12. That friction level gives an available deceleration of 1.18 m/s² and a known-component speed ceiling of 1.23 m/s. This is the inspected-floor ceiling that the release envelope carries (**[[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/04 Governing the Machine/16 Deployment Release/V4-16.03 Physical Criteria for Release#^tbl-16-odd-invariants|Operating envelope of the warehouse mobile manipulator]]**), drawn as the dashed envelope in the stopping distance analysis of **[[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/03 Running the Machine/12 Safety Enforcement/V4-12 Safety Enforcement#^sec-enforcement|Safety Enforcement]]** (**[[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/03 Running the Machine/12 Safety Enforcement/V4-12.04 Stopping Envelopes#^fig-12-stopping-vs-speed|figure]]**). The restricted mode sits below that ceiling rather than at it, because the ceiling, like the budget it comes from, still leaves out ${\Delta_{\text{da}}}$ and any reserve for it. This inspected level is an illustrative baseline chosen below dry concrete to tolerate light surface contamination. A production deployment must set this value from friction surveys across the designated routes and show how spill prevention or bounded cleanup maintains it between inspections. In the illustrative project records, restricted-mode operation remains provisional until the full timing budget, including dispatch-to-admission latency and the required reserves, is verified under load. Routes outside the facility inspection program are excluded from automated navigation.

Restricted operation requires an accountable owner who can keep the underlying physical premise true. Facility operations owns this entry through its maintenance and spill-response procedures along pallet-jack routes, setting inspection intervals and specifying the site conditions that pause automated traffic. While the signed speed ceiling in the onboard permission path enforces the motion limit, a software speed setting alone cannot keep the floor dry.

Every register entry must also state a **closure test** before data collection begins. To detect surface films online, the closure test must qualify an independent physical sensor, since standard visual cameras and lidars cannot resolve clear fluids on concrete. Alternatively, higher-traction floor coatings or physical barriers separating leaky equipment could support a revised claim through facility evidence. One candidate closure campaign evaluates an optical film detector, requiring a miss rate of at most 0.1 percent demonstrated across 2,995 zero-failure trials on seeded fluid films, the trial count required by the zero-failure sizing of **[[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/02 Teaching the Machine/07 Closed-Loop Evaluation/V4-07.08 Trial Budgets, Stopping Rules, and Comparisons#^sec-evaluation-trials-per-claim|Sample size requirements per empirical claim]]**. Until the sensor passes this test, the lower speed limit stays in force. Smaller test campaigns do not provide enough statistical confidence. Zero misses across 1,000 independent trials bounds the miss probability only at 0.002991 at 95 percent confidence. In addition, the detector must see far enough ahead that sensing latency, permission processing, and braking finish before the wheels reach the fluid. Forward range, inference latency, false-alarm rate, and post-detection friction assumptions must all be declared in advance. Reactive wheel-slip reflexes can limit further acceleration once the wheels spin, but they cannot guarantee stopping distance before the tires hit the film.

While the target miss rate of 0.1 percent is chosen illustratively here, a production deployment must derive it from the site’s hazard budget. The detector works inside the conditional shield relation of [[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/04 Governing the Machine/18 Engineering Physical AI/V4-18.04 The Epistemic Frontier#^eq-frontier-shielded-hazard|equation 2]], with fluid film encounters in place of hazardous action proposals. If films appear along transit routes at an arrival rate ${\lambda_{\text{film}}}$ and facility engineering allocates a hazard budget ${\lambda_{\text{alloc}}}$ to this failure mode, the detector must satisfy ${\lambda_{\text{film}}}\,{p_{\text{miss}}} \le {\lambda_{\text{alloc}}}$. Choosing the risk allocation ${\lambda_{\text{alloc}}}$ is an accountable engineering judgment, whereas estimating the encounter rate ${\lambda_{\text{film}}}$ comes from maintenance and spill logs.

Aisle navigation adds a second register entry: moving people at blind corners. Stopping-distance budgets are sized against stationary obstacles, so a person who keeps walking toward the mobile base during the stop consumes clearance distance that the budget never set aside. As shown in **[[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/03 Running the Machine/12 Safety Enforcement/V4-12.04 Stopping Envelopes#^sec-enforcement-composite-budget|Sizing the composite stopping budget]]**, accounting for a person walking toward the robot throughout the stop caps the permissible base speed at the rack corner far below normal transit speeds. The safety premise behind normal aisle speed assumes that no person walks out of the occluded cross aisle during an emergency stop, leaving a person’s presence in the hidden cross aisle as an unobserved state. Restricted service therefore requires physical barriers or access controls that block human cross-traffic during automated runs; painted floor lines do not satisfy this condition. Facility operations owns this restriction. A proposed blind-corner presence sensor cannot earn credit in the safety case until its field of view, latency, and miss rate pass qualification under the same zero-failure statistical standard applied to the film detector.

The automated picking station adds a third entry, covering what the arm grasps. Its premise is that the object the gripper picks up, and then hands to a coworker through the `accept_item` interface, is the object the task names. As shown in **[[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/03 Running the Machine/10 Grounded Intent/V4-10.08 Intent Grounding Failure Modes and Their Checks#^sec-intent-failure-modes|Intent Grounding Failure Modes and Their Checks]]**, semantic grounding can fail silently. When a thin-walled red glass jar sits alone where the red ceramic mug was expected, the grounding model mistakes the jar for the mug and still satisfies every geometric check in the planner and permission layers. The missing observables are object identity and crush strength, which neither wrist cameras nor visual foundation models can verify reliably before contact.

#### Maintaining the Restrictions

^sec-frontier-operationalizing-claims

Writing an open assumption into a machine-readable register file does not turn it into a measured physical state. If the signed speed limits and site procedures are not wired into the release gate and daily floor work, the restriction exists only on paper. Operating restrictions in the register must therefore attach directly to the runtime enforcers and site workflows that govern daily operation. Cryptographically signed speed envelopes constrain the onboard permission path, while scheduled maintenance intervals bind facility operations. Release pipelines check that both controls are active in the authorized configuration, while runtime monitors enforce only the physical bounds that onboard sensors can read.

Model retraining or software updates must inherit every open restriction whose closure test has not yet passed. Release gates must check the signed operating limits and the status of their supporting evidence before authorizing a deployment. Dropping to a restricted mode requires its own validated safety case; an open register entry does not automatically clear a slower mode for service. At runtime, low-level controllers enforce speed ceilings and actuator limits, while facility crews maintain floor traction and conveyor loading rules at the scheduled intervals.

A restriction can also lapse in the middle of a motion, when a signed envelope expires, an inspection interval runs out while the base is in a route, or a deployment gate withdraws a mode. The lapse must hand over to the fallback ladder rather than to a generic stop. A trajectory chunk already executing inside the old restriction may finish only if its lease, state estimate, envelope, and supporting physical premises remain valid through completion. Withdrawing permission for new proposals can preserve an already admitted chunk under those conditions; loss of a premise that supports its execution cannot. Losing active permission altogether demands a physically feasible stop within the remaining budget. The rung must match the state, because controlled deceleration, holding brakes at zero velocity, torque removal, and full power isolation produce different transients. No register entry can replace those physical distinctions with a single emergency-stop primitive.

↩︎ The ladder of state-matched fallback responses, from minimal projection to complete torque removal, is established in **[[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/03 Running the Machine/12 Safety Enforcement/V4-12.10 The Fallback Ladder#^sec-enforcement-fallback-ladder|The Fallback Ladder]]**.

New sensors can justify wider operating envelopes only after they pass empirical qualification. Where real-time detection is impractical, the register must rely on a conservative action, passive containment, periodic proof testing, or a restricted operating domain (the responses to a hidden state that [[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/04 Governing the Machine/18 Engineering Physical AI/V4-18.04 The Epistemic Frontier#^sec-frontier-passive-compliance-containment|section 18.3.4.2]] develops). Choosing among those responses requires comparing the distance at which a detector could spot the hazard against the deadline for committed motion.

### Failures without Detectors

^sec-frontier-failures-without-detectors

An automated floor-film detector can retire the surface-friction restriction only if its coverage, false-alarm rate, and detection latency fit inside the route’s stopping budget rather than a standalone model benchmark. In the known-component budget, running at the candidate aisle speed leaves 131.0 mm of physical margin, which corresponds to an allowable delay of 100.8 ms. Setting aside 63.3 ms for dispatch-to-admission processing leaves 37.5 ms of timing reserve. That reserve is an upper bound on unallocated latency, not a certified budget for new perception pipelines, and it offers no protection against a pedestrian who keeps walking toward the robot during the stop.

A forward-looking fluid detector faces an even tighter spatial deadline. The required detection range, minus the distance the robot travels during sensor integration and neural inference, must cover the full dry-floor stopping distance before the tires reach the leading edge of the film:

$$
{d_{\text{detect}}} \ge {v}\,{t_{\text{detect}}} + {d_{\text{budget}}}({v}), \tag{3}
$$

 where ${d_{\text{budget}}}({v})$ denotes the known-component dry-floor budget of notebook [18.2](https://mlsysbook.ai/vol4/18_frontier/18_frontier.html#nbk-frontier-friction-floor), the seven rows of **[[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/03 Running the Machine/12 Safety Enforcement/V4-12.04 Stopping Envelopes#^tbl-enforcement-stopping-budget|The warehouse mobile manipulator's stopping budget]]**. The detection interval ${t_{\text{detect}}}$ covers only the film detector’s own work, from the moment the film enters its range to the moment it reports. That report is the observation that starts the pre-brake chain, so the observation age and admission already inside ${d_{\text{budget}}}$ follow it rather than overlap it. Where the two do overlap in a real pipeline, adding them is conservative. With a chosen sensing and inference latency of 100 ms at the 1.3 m/s aisle speed, the detector must see the film at least 1.10 m ahead. That range is almost exactly the 1.10 m clear distance, and not by coincidence. The dry budget and the spare together make up the clear distance, and the detector’s 100 ms of travel nearly equals the 100.8 ms that the spare buys. A detector that sees only as far as the clear distance therefore spends the whole timing allowance by itself, leaving nothing for the 63.3 ms dispatch-to-admission allocation. To fit beside that allocation, its latency must stay under the 37.5 ms reserve, or its range must grow. If classification occurs only after the drive wheels enter the fluid, the stopping distance expands to 2.13 m, far beyond the 1.10 m clear distance. A classification received after the wheels cross onto the film can serve incident analysis, but it arrives too late to avert the collision.

#### Observational Indistinguishability and the Action Deadline

^sec-frontier-observational-indistinguishability-sub

When an oil film and dry concrete produce identical camera and lidar readings until the robot is already inside its dry-floor stopping distance, no classifier running on those sensors can trigger a stop in time. That limit is observational indistinguishability. Consider two physical world states, ${s_A}$ and ${s_B}$. In state ${s_A}$, a commanded actuator trajectory ${\mathbf{u}}({t})$ runs safely. In state ${s_B}$, that same command ${\mathbf{u}}({t})$ causes structural damage or human injury, requiring the permission path to trigger an emergency stop. The constraint governing this stop is distance rather than elapsed time: because braking changes the robot’s speed along its path, the point of harm is a place in the room. Let ${d_{\text{rem}}}$ denote the remaining distance to that boundary when the distinguishing observation arrives. An emergency stop finishes short of the hazard only if:

$$
{d_{\text{rem}}} \ge {v}\,({T_{\text{comp}}} + {T_{\text{act}}}) + {d_{\text{brake}}}({v}), \tag{4}
$$

 matching the physical stopping relation used throughout this book. The enforcer’s compute time and the actuator’s response time together act as the pre-brake delay ${\tau_{\text{delay}}}$, counted from the arrival of the distinguishing observation rather than from a fixed capture event, and ${d_{\text{brake}}}({v})$ is the braking distance of the chosen stop. Dividing this distance constraint by the approach speed gives an equivalent observation deadline: ${t_{\text{deadline}}} = {t_{\text{harm}}} - {T_{\text{comp}}} - {T_{\text{act}}} - {d_{\text{brake}}}({v})/{v}$, where ${t_{\text{harm}}}$ is the instant the unbraked trajectory would reach impact. Because ${t_{\text{harm}}}$ marks when the robot would reach the hazard at constant cruising speed ${v}$, the quantity ${t_{\text{harm}}} - {t_{\text{deadline}}}$ expresses the required spatial clearance ${d_{\text{rem}}}$ in units of cruising-speed travel time. Once braking begins, the decelerating robot takes longer than ${d_{\text{brake}}}({v})/{v}$ to cover the remaining distance, so it safely comes to rest just short of the spatial boundary at a clock instant later than ${t_{\text{harm}}}$. A common mistake subtracts the full stopping duration ${T_{\text{stop}}}$ instead of the speed-normalized distance ${d_{\text{brake}}}/{v}$, treating the robot as if it kept moving at full cruising speed until the instant it came to rest. For the jerk-limited profile from 1.3 m/s, braking lasts 931.2 ms but covers a distance equal to only 465.6 ms of constant-speed travel. Writing the deadline purely in time over-reserves 465.6 ms, or 605.3 mm of clearance, an error larger than the entire 131.0 mm spare. The time form errs on the safe side, but it refuses operating modes that the distance form rightly admits. Manipulator contact follows the same logic: once an end-effector touches a stiff surface, contact force rather than position sets the observation deadline (**[[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/03 Running the Machine/12 Safety Enforcement/V4-12.08 The Minimal Intervention Principle and Optimization Filters#^sec-enforcement-intentional-contact|Intentional contact and in-contact energy barriers]]**).

A timely separating observation changes this argument only if the machine can obtain it within the same physical budget. When no such observation is available, the permission path must choose an action qualified for both candidate states or withhold the operating mode.

If the sensor stream ${\mathbf{y}}({t})$ produced under state ${s_A}$ matches that under ${s_B}$ up to ${t_{\text{deadline}}}$ for every admissible control input, no algorithm acting on those measurements can tell the safe state apart from the hazardous state in time to prevent harm:

**Definition 18.1: Observational indistinguishability**

***Observational indistinguishability*** is the condition in which two physical states that require different responses produce the same observation history, under every input the machine may admissibly apply, until the deadline by which a state-specific response must begin.

1.  **Significance**: It turns an observation limit into a stopping-budget violation. The deadline is set by [[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/04 Governing the Machine/18 Engineering Physical AI/V4-18.04 The Epistemic Frontier#^eq-frontier-action-deadline|equation 4]], so if the film and the dry floor look identical until the remaining distance falls below ${v}\,({T_{\text{comp}}} + {T_{\text{act}}}) + {d_{\text{brake}}}({v})$, that evidence cannot justify continuing the dry-floor mode. The machine must instead use a response already qualified for both states or withhold that mode.
2.  **Distinction**: Noise and weak detectors obscure information that more data or a better model can recover; indistinguishability means the information is absent from the channel. Unlike unobservability (definition [9.3](https://mlsysbook.ai/vol4/09_memory/09_memory.html#dfn-memory-observability)), which asks whether a state can ever be reconstructed from the output history, it is tied to a deadline and to the inputs the machine may apply.
3.  **Common pitfall**: A frequent misconception is that a more capable classifier on the same channel will eventually separate the states. Identical histories give every classifier identical inputs. A designed probe can separate states hidden under the current observation policy only when an admissible excitation reveals a difference before the deadline. If every admissible input leaves the histories identical, the response requires a new channel or an action valid in both states.

↩︎ The observation contract, which bounds the age and error of what a channel reports but cannot certify a state the channel never carries, is established in **[[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/03 Running the Machine/08 Sensor Perception/V4-08.09 The Observation Contract#^sec-perception-observation-contract|The Observation Contract]]**.

The qualification *under every input the machine may admissibly apply* matters because whether two states look different often depends on how the machine excites the plant, as shown in nonlinear observability theory ([[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/04 Governing the Machine/18 Engineering Physical AI/V4-18.08 Source References#^ref-hermann1977nonlinear|Hermann and Krener 1977]]). In some settings, an engineer can inject a small, safe control test input that makes the two states produce different sensor readings before the deadline. This technique is **active probing**: applying a bounded test excitation to expose hidden state differences in the sensor stream before the action deadline, the same excitation that **[[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/03 Running the Machine/09 Spatial Memory/V4-09.09 Observability Ceilings and Estimation Limits#^sec-memory-state-estimation-bounds|Observability Ceilings and Estimation Limits]]** uses to expose a drifting force-sensor bias. Active probing draws on auxiliary signal design[[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/04 Governing the Machine/18 Engineering Physical AI/V4-18.09 Footnotes#^fn5|⁵]] from fault detection ([[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/04 Governing the Machine/18 Engineering Physical AI/V4-18.08 Source References#^ref-campbell2004auxiliary|Campbell and Nikoukhah 2004]]). For example, a controlled tug at a qualified proof load can check whether a door latch holds at that load before the arm commits to its full pull, though it does not measure the latch’s remaining yield margin. On the floor, a brief torque pulse at low speed, applied before the base enters a route, makes the wheels slip measurably on a film and not on dry concrete, turning the friction under the tires into telemetry. Such a slip check measures only the patch of floor the wheels already stand on; it cannot see a film ahead, which is why the forward detector above has its own range condition. Active probing costs time and disturbs the motion, and both costs must fit inside the safety budgets. Where no admissible probe can separate the states within the timing budget, the design must fall back on conservative actions or passive mechanical containment.

#### Responses When the State Remains Hidden

^sec-frontier-passive-compliance-containment

Active probing is the first of five design responses when passive runtime observations leave candidate physical states indistinguishable. When an admissible excitation can separate the states before the deadline, *active probing* supplies the missing observation online; if every admissible input leaves them indistinguishable, the remaining four responses must carry the claim without online separation. *Proof testing* measures the state from outside the runtime channel at scheduled intervals, such as an unloaded tare of a sensor or a load test of a latch, and covers the time between tests by argument. A *conservative action* chooses a command safe in both candidate states, such as a speed low enough for the film. When control commands alone cannot bound the interaction, *passive containment* limits the physical consequence mechanically, such as a compliant element that caps contact force whatever the software believes. A *restricted operational domain* withholds authority altogether from states that cannot be told apart, such as routes without a spill procedure. The register entries above already used the conservative action and the restricted domain, and active probing was shown at the latch and the floor. The wrist sensor and the latch below show proof testing and passive containment at work on the running machine.

Sensor drift is the slow form of the same barrier. The wrist strain gauge of **[[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/03 Running the Machine/09 Spatial Memory/V4-09.09 Observability Ceilings and Estimation Limits#^sec-memory-state-estimation-bounds|Observability Ceilings and Estimation Limits]]**, whose bias wanders as thermal cycles and mechanical stress relaxation alter its bridge resistance, reports only the sum of contact force and bias. A drifting bias and a real change in force therefore produce the same reading and a zero innovation. What this chapter adds is the choice of response. Every contact threshold that depends on that signal inherits the drift, and no response chosen at runtime can see it. The response must therefore be proof testing, meaning an unloaded tare or a proof-test fixture at an interval short enough that the drift accumulated between tests stays inside the threshold’s margin.

↩︎ Mechanical degradation and thermal calibration drift as continuing obligations of a release verdict are established in **[[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/04 Governing the Machine/16 Deployment Release/V4-16.06 The Verdict’s Standing Conditions#^sec-release-calibration-drift|Degradation, drift, and observation cadence]]**.

A weakened door latch poses the same observability barrier on a millisecond timescale. Before the metal yields, joint torque sensors read the same reaction force against a fatigued latch as against a sound one, so the torque channel carries no information about how much yield margin remains. Because plastic yield is permanent, no software command sent after the latch bends can undo the damage, and running a faster classifier on joint torque signals adds zero protection. A credible safety strategy must bring in information from outside the runtime torque channel. Scheduled proof tests[[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/04 Governing the Machine/18 Engineering Physical AI/V4-18.09 Footnotes#^fn6|⁶]] check that the latch survives a specified proof load at fixed intervals, without measuring its ultimate yield strength. Even so, proof testing holds only if maintenance records show that fatigue, wear, and corrosion cannot push the latch’s yield strength below the arm’s contact ceiling between tests.

Adding a passive compliant element to the structure can mechanically cap interaction forces across a qualified deflection stroke, the principle behind series elastic actuators ([[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/04 Governing the Machine/18 Engineering Physical AI/V4-18.08 Source References#^ref-pratt1995series|Pratt and Williamson 1995]]) and mechanical torque limiters. Two physical conditions govern compliant load paths, both set by the stroke the arm *actively drives* after contact detection rather than the total uncompressed spring length. When a contact tripwire ${F_{\text{trip}}}$ triggers, the arm keeps moving during its controller response latency ${t_{\text{resp}}}$ before decelerating at rate ${a_{\text{arm}}}$, driving the mechanism through a post-contact stroke of ${\Delta x_{\text{driven}}} = {v}\,{t_{\text{resp}}} + {v}^2/(2{a_{\text{arm}}})$. To keep the contact force below the declared strength floor ${F_{\text{floor}}}$ (a lower bound on the latch’s strength, unrelated to the floor friction), the equivalent stiffness ${k_{\text{eq}}}$ of the combined series load path (compliant element and strike plate) must satisfy [[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/04 Governing the Machine/18 Engineering Physical AI/V4-18.04 The Epistemic Frontier#^eq-frontier-passive-compliance|equation 5]]:

$$
{F_{\text{trip}}} + {k_{\text{eq}}}\,{\Delta x_{\text{driven}}} \le {F_{\text{floor}}} \quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad {k_{\text{eq}}} \le \frac{{F_{\text{floor}}}-{F_{\text{trip}}}}{{v}\,{t_{\text{resp}}} + {v}^2/(2{a_{\text{arm}}})}. \tag{5}
$$

 In addition, the compliant element’s allowable stroke must exceed its deflection under the peak allowable force, $({F_{\text{floor}}}-{F_{\text{preload}}})/{k_{\text{spring}}}$, so the spring never bottoms out against a hard stop. The threshold ${F_{\text{floor}}}$ is not the latch’s actual yield strength, which remains unobserved between proof tests; it is a chosen lower strength bound recorded as an assumption in the register until physical evidence supports it. Accountable judgment chooses the design bound; it cannot create material strength.

^eq-frontier-passive-compliance

The warehouse door latch shows what passive compliance buys. During a guarded approach at 0.03 m/s, meeting a stiff strike plate of stiffness 4.0 × 10⁵ N/m satisfies [[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/04 Governing the Machine/18 Engineering Physical AI/V4-18.04 The Epistemic Frontier#^eq-frontier-passive-compliance|equation 5]] with a required arm deceleration of 2.95 m/s², matching the guarded latch stop derived in **[[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/03 Running the Machine/12 Safety Enforcement/V4-12.08 The Minimal Intervention Principle and Optimization Filters#^sec-enforcement-intentional-contact|Intentional contact and in-contact energy barriers]]**. At a faster approach speed of 0.10 m/s, however, the same stiff contact would require an impossible arm deceleration of 400 m/s², forcing the permission path to refuse the trajectory. Holding arm deceleration to the guarded stopping bound instead drives the arm through 1.89 mm after it crosses the 15 N tripwire, capping the allowable series stiffness at 44.9 kN/m. Because series compliance combines inversely ($1/{k_{\text{eq}}} = 1/{k_{\text{spring}}} + 1/{k_{\text{latch}}}$), pairing the compliant element with the strike plate requires a spring stiffness of at most 50.5 kN/m and an available stroke of at least 2.0 mm to stay within the 100 N design force.

This kinematic calculation assumes a stiff position-tracking drive that enforces the commanded ${a_{\text{arm}}}$ deceleration profile throughout the post-tripwire stroke regardless of contact reaction. Once spring compression exceeds ${m_{\text{eff}}}\,{a_{\text{arm}}}$, the passive spring alone would decelerate the ${m_{\text{eff}}}$ effective mass faster than ${a_{\text{arm}}}$, so a controller that tracks the gentler kinematic ramp keeps pushing forward into the spring until the stroke completes. If the safety response instead cuts motor torque completely, the arm’s effective mass 12 kg coasts into the spring, and energy conservation sets the peak force. A spring compressed to force ${F}$ stores potential energy ${F}^2/(2{k_{\text{eq}}})$; at the tripwire, the spring already holds ${F_{\text{trip}}}^2/(2{k_{\text{eq}}})$, and the coasting mass adds its kinetic energy ${m_{\text{eff}}}{}{v}^2/2$. The resulting peak impact force is $\sqrt{{F_{\text{trip}}}^2 + {k_{\text{eq}}}\,{m_{\text{eff}}}\,{v}^2}$, the dynamic impact regime that governs high-speed robot collisions ([[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/04 Governing the Machine/18 Engineering Physical AI/V4-18.08 Source References#^ref-haddadin2009requirements|Haddadin et al. 2009]]). With the series spring installed, this coasting peak is 74.9 N, below the 100 N that the braked case reaches. That result is not a paradox. The stiffness bound was sized for a drive that holds the arm to the gentle 2.95 m/s² stop and so keeps pushing through the whole 1.89 mm stroke, bringing the force right to the floor. With torque removed, nothing pushes; the spring alone slows the 12 kg effective mass, harder than that commanded stop, and halts it in a shorter stroke at a lower force. Striking the stiff plate without compliance, by contrast, produces peak forces of 219.6 N at the faster approach speed and 67.4 N under the guarded approach. Within the qualified approach-speed and stroke envelope, the compliant element keeps both modeled responses at or below the latch’s declared design strength; driving beyond that stroke would bottom out the spring and break the bound. Even so, passive compliance does not verify latch strength: qualification must still test damping, material fatigue, and structural load paths rather than spring stiffness alone.

The door latch, the oil film, and the packaging tab pose the same problem. When a safety premise cannot be evaluated before an execution deadline, the first question is whether an admissible probe can separate the states in time. If it can, active probing supplies the missing observation; otherwise proof testing, a conservative action, passive containment, or a restricted operational domain must support the claim. The choice among them depends on the claim each response can support and the evidence needed to maintain it, and every such mitigation must also state the evidence that would retire it.

**Checkpoint 18.2: Premises no sensor reports**

The warehouse machine’s hardest limits are premises its own telemetry cannot check, and each one needs an entry, a restriction, and a response chosen before the deadline.

**Friction estimate**: A friction survey measures an aisle at ${\mu}$ = 0.15. Given the 2 m/s² braking bound, compute the available deceleration from [[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/04 Governing the Machine/18 Engineering Physical AI/V4-18.04 The Epistemic Frontier#^eq-frontier-credible-deceleration|equation 1]] and determine whether the brake or the floor binds.

**Register entry**: For the weakened door latch, state the four components of its register entry: the missing observable, the restriction, the owner, and the closure test.

**Shield credit**: Explain why [[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/04 Governing the Machine/18 Engineering Physical AI/V4-18.04 The Epistemic Frontier#^eq-frontier-shielded-hazard|equation 2]] gives no credit against the oil film, and identify which term the film would enter.
