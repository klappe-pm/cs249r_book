# V4-11.07 Choosing a Trajectory Generator

[[Home|Home]] · [[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/Volume 4 Overview|Volume Overview]] · [[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/03 Running the Machine/11 Trajectory Planning/V4-11 Trajectory Planning|V4-11 Trajectory Planning]]

^sec-planning-paradigms

When a mobile manipulator reaches around a conveyor guard, a planner that searches configuration space can run out of time before finding a route. A local trajectory optimizer can get trapped in a non-convex pocket behind the guard. A learned action-chunk policy can propose a smooth reach that clips the guard or exceeds a joint’s torque limit. The three algorithmic families that propose motion, geometric search, continuous optimization, and learned action chunking, each fail in a different way if treated as self-certifying. In all three paradigms, deciding where the body can travel geometrically remains separate from determining how quickly actuators can safely traverse that path. Regardless of which family generates a proposal, the resulting trajectory remains an unverified candidate that requires exhaustive dynamic validation before the execution engine commits it to the hardware.

The conveyor reach also fixes what each family can afford. A replacement must be admitted every 50 ms, and 18 ms of every replacement’s dispatch-to-admission allocation is already reserved for whole-plan validation, while a chunk policy spends another 40 ms of it on inference. To sustain the intended renewal cadence, the generation pipeline must provide both the allocated latency and the throughput that overlapping requests require. A solver without a bounded completion time needs a timeout that leaves the resident fallback executable; it can also run outside the renewal loop, planning the route around the guard and handing the loop a corridor to follow. That deadline, more than the quality of the motion, sorts the three families below.

Sampling-based geometric planners explore configuration space without requiring an explicit algebraic representation of obstacles. Probabilistic Roadmaps (PRM) ([[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/03 Running the Machine/11 Trajectory Planning/V4-11.13 Source References#^ref-kavraki1996probabilistic|Kavraki et al. 1996]]) construct multi-query roadmaps by sampling collision-free configurations and connecting nearby neighbors with local collision-free segments, amortizing graph construction costs across multiple tasks. Conversely, Rapidly-exploring Random Trees (RRT) ([[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/03 Running the Machine/11 Trajectory Planning/V4-11.13 Source References#^ref-lavalle1998rapidly|LaValle 1998]]) target single-query problems by growing a space-filling tree from the initial configuration toward unexplored territory. Its asymptotically optimal counterpart, RRT\* ([[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/03 Running the Machine/11 Trajectory Planning/V4-11.13 Source References#^ref-karaman2011sampling|Karaman and Frazzoli 2011]]), continuously rewires parent connections within local neighborhoods as samples accumulate, ensuring that the discovered path cost converges almost surely to the theoretical optimum ([[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/03 Running the Machine/11 Trajectory Planning/V4-11.13 Source References#^ref-karaman2011sampling|Karaman and Frazzoli 2011]]). These methods resolve geometric routes through complex obstacle geometry, yet their completeness guarantees remain strictly probabilistic: the probability of discovering a feasible path approaches one only as the number of samples approaches infinity. When a sampling planner exhausts its computation budget without finding a path, its timeout provides no proof that the route is obstructed. The admission logic must therefore treat planner silence as an unresolved proposal rather than a verified safety determination. Furthermore, evaluating configuration samples and connecting edges requires repeated collision queries against environment geometry, usually accelerated by enclosing the manipulator links in bounding spheres or capsules, and these queries dominate the planner’s run time. Even when successful, search yields only an un-timed geometric path with discontinuous derivative transitions. The system must still apply time parameterization, smooth corner blends, joint torque verification, and a valid stopping continuation before dispatching the trajectory to physical actuators.

The complementary classical formulation is **constrained continuous trajectory optimization**, exemplified by Covariant Hamiltonian Optimization for Motion Planning (CHOMP) ([[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/03 Running the Machine/11 Trajectory Planning/V4-11.13 Source References#^ref-ratliff2009chomp|Ratliff et al. 2009]]), TrajOpt ([[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/03 Running the Machine/11 Trajectory Planning/V4-11.13 Source References#^ref-schulman2014motion|Schulman et al. 2014]]), and receding-horizon Model Predictive Control (MPC) ([[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/03 Running the Machine/11 Trajectory Planning/V4-11.13 Source References#^ref-tedrake2023manipulation|Tedrake 2023]]; [[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/03 Running the Machine/11 Trajectory Planning/V4-11.13 Source References#^ref-lynch2017modern|Lynch and Park 2017]]). Rather than evaluating discrete graph nodes, continuous trajectory optimization represents motion as a continuous, time-indexed curve ${\mathbf{q}}({t})$ and minimizes an objective functional that balances dynamic smoothness against obstacle proximity:

$$
\min_{{\mathbf{q}}(t)} \quad {\mathcal{U}}[{\mathbf{q}}] = \int_0^{T} \left( \frac{1}{2} \|\dddot{{\mathbf{q}}}({t})\|^2 + {\lambda_{\text{obs}}} {c_{\text{obs}}}({\mathbf{q}}({t})) \right) d{t}
$$

subject to physical boundary conditions and actuator invariants:

$$
{\mathbf{q}}(0) = {\mathbf{q}}_0, \quad {\dot{\mathbf{q}}}(0) = {\dot{\mathbf{q}}}_0, \quad {\ddot{\mathbf{q}}}(0) = {\ddot{\mathbf{q}}}_0
$$

$$
{\mathbf{q}}({T}) = {\mathbf{q}_{\text{goal}}}, \quad {\dot{\mathbf{q}}}({T}) = {\mathbf{0}}, \quad {\ddot{\mathbf{q}}}({T}) = {\mathbf{0}}
$$

$$
|{\dot{\mathbf{q}}}({t})| \le {\dot{\mathbf{q}}_{\max}}, \quad |{\mathbf{M}}({\mathbf{q}}){\ddot{\mathbf{q}}} + {\mathbf{C}}({\mathbf{q}}, {\dot{\mathbf{q}}}){\dot{\mathbf{q}}} + {\mathbf{g}}({\mathbf{q}})| \le {\boldsymbol{\tau}_{\max}}
$$

While CHOMP performs functional gradient descent and TrajOpt solves sequential convex subproblems using signed-distance collision constraints, both rely on local optimization. Consequently, non-convex obstacle geometry can trap the solver in infeasible local minima or geometric dead ends. Graphs of Convex Sets (GCS) resolve this susceptibility when the collision-free configuration space can be partitioned beforehand into convex regions. Finding the global sequence of regions and optimizing the polynomial spline within them then formulates as a mixed-integer convex program whose convex relaxation can be solved efficiently ([[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/03 Running the Machine/11 Trajectory Planning/V4-11.13 Source References#^ref-marcucci2023motion|Marcucci et al. 2023]]). Because generating these convex decompositions requires substantial offline computation, GCS suits static structures such as the conveyor guard far better than dynamic, rapidly shifting environments. Although geometric search or learned policies can propose alternative routes around unforeseen obstructions, any candidate motion must still undergo explicit verification against dynamic torque limits and actuator constraints.

Repeatedly re-optimizing from an updated state estimate forms the operational foundation of classical **Model Predictive Control (MPC)**[[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/03 Running the Machine/11 Trajectory Planning/V4-11.14 Footnotes#^fn11|¹¹]]. In this receding-horizon paradigm, an optimizer computes an optimal trajectory over a finite horizon ${T}$, dispatches an initial prefix to the actuator controllers, and re-solves the problem from the newly observed state on the subsequent cycle. The objective functional ranks candidate motions by performance, while hard constraints eliminate trajectories that exceed physical limits. Classical discrete-time MPC frames this trade-off as a constrained numerical optimization problem over steps ${k} = 0, \dots, {N_{\text{mpc}}}-1$ ([[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/03 Running the Machine/11 Trajectory Planning/V4-11.13 Source References#^ref-tedrake2023manipulation|Tedrake 2023]]; [[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/03 Running the Machine/11 Trajectory Planning/V4-11.13 Source References#^ref-lynch2017modern|Lynch and Park 2017]]):

$$
\begin{aligned} \min_{{\mathbf{u}_{0:N_{\text{mpc}}-1}}} \quad & \sum_{k=0}^{{N_{\text{mpc}}}-1} \ell({\mathbf{x}}_k, {\mathbf{u}}_k) + {\ell_f}({\mathbf{x}_N}) \\ \text{subject to} \quad & {\mathbf{x}}_{k+1} = {f}({\mathbf{x}}_k, {\mathbf{u}}_k), \quad {k} = 0, \dots, {N_{\text{mpc}}}-1 \\ & {\mathbf{x}}_k \in {\mathcal{X}_{\text{safe}}}, \quad {\mathbf{u}}_k \in {\mathcal{U}_{\text{adm}}}, \quad {k} = 0, \dots, {N_{\text{mpc}}}-1 \\ & {\mathbf{x}_N} \in {\mathcal{X}_{f}} \end{aligned} \tag{19}
$$

^eq-plan-mpc-formulation

The variables in [[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/03 Running the Machine/11 Trajectory Planning/V4-11.07 Choosing a Trajectory Generator#^eq-plan-mpc-formulation|equation 19]] decouple the predicted state sequence ${\mathbf{x}}_k$ from the selected control inputs ${\mathbf{u}}_k$. The dynamic transition map ${f}({\mathbf{x}}_k, {\mathbf{u}}_k)$ propagates the system state forward, requiring every intermediate step to remain admissible. The stage cost $\ell({\mathbf{x}}_k, {\mathbf{u}}_k)$ penalizes tracking error and control effort, while the safe state set ${\mathcal{X}_{\text{safe}}}$ enforces collision margins derived from the spatial perception pipeline. The admissible control set ${\mathcal{U}_{\text{adm}}}$ bounds actuator torques and voltages according to verified hardware limits.

The terminal constraint forces the horizon endpoint into a **terminal invariant set**, ${\mathbf{x}_N} \in {\mathcal{X}_{f}}$. In classical control theory, *recursive feasibility* guarantees that if a feasible solution exists at control cycle ${i}$, a feasible solution remains provably discoverable at cycle ${i}+1$. Establishing this property requires an accurate dynamic model and a terminal set ${\mathcal{X}_{f}}$ within which an invariant fallback control law maintains safety indefinitely. In an embodied robotic system, this terminal invariant is implemented through a validated braking continuation that brings the machine to rest within verified spatial clearance (${d_{\text{stop}}} \le {D_{\text{clear}}}$). Bringing the machine to a standstill does not guarantee indefinite safety on its own, because dynamic obstacles can move into the workspace of a stationary robot unless external interlocks preserve the boundary.

Continuous-time optimal control problems are typically transcribed into finite-dimensional nonlinear programs through **direct shooting** or **direct collocation**.[[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/03 Running the Machine/11 Trajectory Planning/V4-11.14 Footnotes#^fn12|¹²]] Direct shooting forward-integrates the control trajectory ${\mathbf{u}}({t})$, yielding a compact optimization problem that can nonetheless become ill-conditioned over long horizons. Direct collocation parameterizes both states and controls using piecewise polynomial splines, enforcing dynamic defect constraints ${\mathbf{f}}({\mathbf{x}}_k, {\mathbf{u}}_k) - {\dot{\mathbf{x}}}_k = {\mathbf{0}}$ at interior knots to generate large, sparse nonlinear programs that converge rapidly near smooth trajectories ([[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/03 Running the Machine/11 Trajectory Planning/V4-11.13 Source References#^ref-hargraves1987direct|Hargraves and Paris 1987]]). However, theoretical MPC guarantees hold only when the dynamic model is accurate, constraints are satisfied, and the numerical solver finishes before its real-time deadline. Contact transitions, stick-slip friction, and unexpected loads degrade solver convergence, often causing iterations to time out. Learned action chunking eliminates online numerical solves during inference, but neural policies do not enforce the terminal invariant constraint ${\mathbf{x}_N} \in {\mathcal{X}_{f}}$. An unvalidated action chunk can easily end at high velocity where the robot lacks adequate braking room.

Decoupling generation into a learned proposer and a constrained optimizer reconciles statistical representation with physical safety. The learned policy acts as a proposer, generating behavioral candidates conditioned on raw visual tokens and task semantics. A constrained trajectory generator then adjusts the proposed trajectory to satisfy boundary conditions, actuator limits, and spatial clearances. Because this numerical projection can become infeasible or miss its real-time deadline, it operates entirely on the untrusted proposal side of the architecture, upstream of independent safety admission.

Mathematically, this dual-stage architecture formulates trajectory generation as a constrained projection operator ${\Pi_{\mathcal{K}}}({\hat{\mathbf{a}}_{\text{learned}}})$:

$$
{\mathbf{q}}^*({t}) = {\Pi_{\mathcal{K}}}({\hat{\mathbf{a}}_{\text{learned}}}) = \arg\min_{{\mathbf{q}}(t)} \int_0^{T} \|{\mathbf{q}}({t}) - {\hat{\mathbf{q}}_{\text{learned}}}({t})\|_{{\mathbf{W}_q}}^2 d{t} + \int_0^{T} \|\dddot{{\mathbf{q}}}({t})\|_{{\mathbf{W}_j}}^2 d{t} \tag{20}
$$

^eq-plan-kinodynamic-projection

The projection begins at the planned activation seam, so its initial-state constraints refer to that seam rather than to the state measured when the planning request begins. Upstream synthesis uses the projected reference and its reachable-state envelope; at activation, the MCU instantiates and checks the bounded bridge against measured state. The candidate remains subject to physical boundary conditions and safety invariants across the execution interval ${t} \in [0, {T}]$:

$$
{\mathbf{q}}(0) = {\mathbf{q}_{\text{actual}}}, \quad {\dot{\mathbf{q}}}(0) = {\dot{\mathbf{q}}_{\text{actual}}}, \quad {\ddot{\mathbf{q}}}(0) = {\ddot{\mathbf{q}}_{\text{actual}}}
$$

$$
{\mathbf{q}}({t}) \in {\mathcal{C}_{\text{free}}}, \quad |{\dot{\mathbf{q}}}({t})| \le {\dot{\mathbf{q}}_{\max}}, \quad |{\mathbf{M}}({\mathbf{q}}){\ddot{\mathbf{q}}} + {\mathbf{C}}({\mathbf{q}}, {\dot{\mathbf{q}}}){\dot{\mathbf{q}}} + {\mathbf{g}}({\mathbf{q}})| \le {\boldsymbol{\tau}_{\max}}
$$

$$
{d_{\text{stop}}}({\mathbf{q}}({t_{\text{commit}}}), {\dot{\mathbf{q}}}({t_{\text{commit}}})) \le {D_{\text{clear}}}({t_{\text{commit}}})
$$

In [[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/03 Running the Machine/11 Trajectory Planning/V4-11.07 Choosing a Trajectory Generator#^eq-plan-kinodynamic-projection|equation 20]], the primary term penalizes tracking deviation from the learned nominal trajectory ${\hat{\mathbf{q}}_{\text{learned}}}({t})$ using positive semidefinite state weights ${\mathbf{W}_q}$, while the regularization term penalizes joint jerk $\|\dddot{{\mathbf{q}}}({t})\|_{{\mathbf{W}_j}}^2$ to minimize mechanical vibration and wear. The weighted quadratic norm is defined as $\|{\mathbf{z}}\|_{{\mathbf{W}}}^2 = {\mathbf{z}}^\top {\mathbf{W}} {\mathbf{z}}$, and ${\mathcal{K}}$ denotes the feasible set of trajectories satisfying the displayed physical constraints. These constraints enforce the dynamic invariants that unconstrained learned models cannot guarantee: ${C}^2$ continuity with the currently executing state, collision avoidance within the free configuration space ${\mathcal{C}_{\text{free}}}$, kinematic joint speed bounds, full rigid-body inverse dynamics torque limits, and certified stopping viability at the commitment horizon ${t_{\text{commit}}}$. Even when the optimizer finds a feasible trajectory, that solution remains contingent on model assumptions regarding payload mass and obstacle positions; the independent admission gate must verify those assumptions before granting hardware permission.

Altering the proposer modifies the computational budget and latency profile, but leaves the physical safety obligations unchanged. Action Chunking with Transformers (ACT) ([[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/03 Running the Machine/11 Trajectory Planning/V4-11.13 Source References#^ref-zhao2023learning|Zhao et al. 2023]]) infers an entire chunk of future joint setpoints in a single forward pass and smooths replanning boundaries by ensembling overlapping temporal chunks. However, simple coordinate averaging across predictions fails to enforce higher-order boundary derivatives, which can induce severe acceleration transients at chunk transitions. Diffusion policies ([[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/03 Running the Machine/11 Trajectory Planning/V4-11.13 Source References#^ref-chi2024diffusionpolicy|Chi et al. 2025]]) capture complex, multimodal behavioral distributions through iterative score-based denoising, but the multiple neural network evaluations required during inference add substantial latency to the replacement planning path. Alternatively, a neural model can warm-start a numerical trajectory optimizer by providing initial collocation knots. While a good warm-start drastically reduces sequential quadratic programming iterations, an out-of-distribution observation can seed the optimizer in an infeasible basin of attraction, causing solver divergence and missed execution deadlines. Generator selection therefore balances configuration-space exploration, constrained local tracking, and multimodal perceptual synthesis against the strict temporal deadlines required to validate and admit candidate trajectories. ### Mobile-Base Planning Under Nonholonomic Constraints

^sec-planning-mobile-base

Unlike the seven-joint arm, the mobile base cannot follow a straight line interpolated between two poses in the warehouse aisle, because its two coaxial drive wheels roll without slipping and therefore cannot slide sideways along their axle. Expressed in terms of heading angle ${\theta_{\text{head}}}$, forward linear speed ${v}$, and yaw rate ${\omega_z}$, the differential-drive (unicycle) kinematics satisfy

$$
{\dot{x}} = {v}\cos{\theta_{\text{head}}}, \qquad {\dot{y}} = {v}\sin{\theta_{\text{head}}}, \qquad {\dot{\theta}_{\text{head}}} = {\omega_z}, \qquad {\dot{x}}\sin{\theta_{\text{head}}} - {\dot{y}}\cos{\theta_{\text{head}}} = 0 \tag{21}
$$

^eq-plan-unicycle

The non-integrable relation in [[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/03 Running the Machine/11 Trajectory Planning/V4-11.07 Choosing a Trajectory Generator#^eq-plan-unicycle|equation 21]] forms a **nonholonomic constraint**,[[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/03 Running the Machine/11 Trajectory Planning/V4-11.14 Footnotes#^fn13|¹³]] restricting the instantaneous velocity space without diminishing the dimension of the reachable configuration space ${SE(2)}$ ([[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/03 Running the Machine/11 Trajectory Planning/V4-11.13 Source References#^ref-siciliano2009robotics|Siciliano et al. 2009]]). The base can reach any planar pose in the aisle, but not with any velocity. It can drive forward at ${v}$ and turn at ${\omega_z}$ about the center of its axle, while the no-slip condition forbids translation along the axle ([[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/03 Running the Machine/11 Trajectory Planning/V4-11.07 Choosing a Trajectory Generator#^fig-11-nonholonomic-base|figure 7]]). A pure sideways offset is therefore reached as a maneuver rather than a step: turn in place, drive along the new heading, and turn back. A base planner must therefore search directly over kinematically admissible control trajectories rather than straight geometric paths in configuration space. Furthermore, each wheel’s tangential speed equals ${v} \pm {\omega_z} {b}/2$ for track width ${b}$, so hardware motor limits constrain linear velocity and turning rate jointly.

Local base planners enforce nonholonomic constraints by planning directly in velocity space over a receding time horizon. The dynamic window approach (DWA) ([[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/03 Running the Machine/11 Trajectory Planning/V4-11.13 Source References#^ref-fox1997dynamic|Fox et al. 1997]]) maps each control pair $({v}, {\omega_z})$ to a circular trajectory arc and evaluates candidate pairs through two nested dynamic criteria. *Reachable velocities* comprise pairs achievable within actuator acceleration limits over the replanning window. With the mobile manipulator’s base acceleration limit of 2 m/s² and a 50 ms chunk renewal interval, this dynamic window spans 0.1 m/s on either side of the current forward velocity. *Admissible velocities* select the subset from which the base can decelerate to a complete stop before reaching the nearest obstacle along that arc, satisfying ${v} \le \sqrt{2\,{d_{\text{arc}}}({v},{\omega_z})\,{a_{\text{brake}}}}$. From these admissible candidates, DWA selects the control pair maximizing an objective that balances target heading alignment, obstacle clearance, and forward speed. MPPI ([[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/03 Running the Machine/11 Trajectory Planning/V4-11.13 Source References#^ref-williams2017information|Williams et al. 2017]]), the sampling-based MPC of [[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/03 Running the Machine/11 Trajectory Planning/V4-11.07 Choosing a Trajectory Generator#^tbl-11-planning-paradigms|table 3]], replaces this deterministic velocity grid by rolling forward thousands of stochastic control sequences through the dynamic model on a GPU, synthesizing an optimal control action as a cost-weighted trajectory distribution. Production navigation frameworks such as ROS 2 Nav2 incorporate both controller families ([[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/03 Running the Machine/11 Trajectory Planning/V4-11.13 Source References#^ref-macenski2023desks|Macenski et al. 2023]]).

The admissibility test applies the core stopping requirement across candidate arcs, yet its classical textbook formulation assumes instantaneous, constant-deceleration braking. On a straight trajectory with 1.10 m of rack-end clearance, this simplified test admits forward speeds up to 2.10 m/s, well above the physical drive limit of 1.5 m/s. However, when evaluated against the conditional stopping subtotal derived in [[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/03 Running the Machine/11 Trajectory Planning/V4-11.06 Behavior at the Seam#^sec-planning-base-stopping-budget|section 11.5.4]] (which accounts for sensor latency, communication delays, localization uncertainty margins, and jerk-limited deceleration), that same clearance fails to guarantee safety even at the nominal drive limit. A mobile-base trajectory generator, whether a classical DWA planner or a learned chunk policy, is therefore a proposer of candidate velocities. The real-time permission path must also charge dispatch-to-admission travel before validating each proposed trajectory against the full stopping budget.

The choice of trajectory generator governs how candidate motions are discovered and determines the computational burden placed on downstream verification. However, the system’s safety enforcement mechanisms must respond consistently regardless of how a proposal was generated. In particular, the execution architecture must cleanly differentiate between rejecting an invalid replacement candidate and responding to unexpected environmental changes. Rejecting an unvalidated proposal leaves the active trajectory in control only while its state, evidence, lease, and stopping reserve remain valid, whereas detecting an unexpected obstacle incursion along the executed path demands an immediate transition to verified braking.
