# V4-11.05 Kinodynamic Feasibility

[[Home|Home]] · [[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/Volume 4 Overview|Volume Overview]] · [[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/03 Running the Machine/11 Trajectory Planning/V4-11 Trajectory Planning|V4-11 Trajectory Planning]]

^sec-planning-kinodynamic-feasibility

A candidate trajectory that clears every obstacle in workspace and arrives before its deadline will still fault on the hardware if it asks a joint for more speed, torque, or current slew than the drive can supply. What an embodied model generates is a geometric and timed proposal, not permission to move. While high-level motion planners and learned policies navigate free configuration space ${\mathcal{C}_{\text{free}}}$, physical actuators operate under fixed electromotive, thermal, and structural limits. Kinodynamic feasibility bridges geometric intent and mechanical reality, ensuring that reference trajectories respect actuator torque limits, velocity boundaries, and transmission dynamics before the low-level controller dispatches setpoints to motor drives.

**Definition 11.3: Kinodynamic feasibility envelope**

***Kinodynamic feasibility envelope*** is the set of initial states from which the machine can track a candidate trajectory, including its transition seams and terminal stop, while every joint remains within its configured position, velocity, acceleration, jerk (the rate of change of acceleration), torque, and stator current limits.

1.  **Significance**: Obstacle clearance in configuration space is necessary but insufficient. At the 1 m/s TCP speed limit, a collision-free reach in the arm’s weakest kinematic direction at a right-angle elbow demands 3.5 rad/s from a single joint against its 2.175 rad/s ceiling. The spatial geometry succeeds while the timing fails. The envelope defines the state region where dynamic models predict trackable motion, which admission and runtime monitors must continuously verify.
2.  **Distinction**: Kinematic reachability evaluates whether a mechanism can geometrically achieve a configuration. Kinodynamic feasibility introduces time and momentum: the time derivatives that a trajectory profile implies, the dynamic torques produced through the manipulator equation, the effective inertia reflected through gear trains, and the physical stopping distance required to brake safely.
3.  **Common pitfall**: Evaluating feasibility solely at discrete waypoints. The continuous-time interpolant between discrete samples can overshoot velocity bounds or demand severe torque steps even when every discrete sample satisfies constraints.

### Rigid-Body Dynamics and Continuous Collision Checks

^sec-planning-rigidbody-dynamics

A trajectory can stay completely clear of obstacles in ${\mathcal{C}_{\text{free}}}$ and still fail on the physical machine because it demands actuator torques that saturate motor inverters, induces base accelerations that tip the platform, or drives current slew rates that trip motor protection circuits. Classical kinematic motion planning checked only whether the swept volume avoided obstacles; a moving body with momentum must satisfy configuration-space clearance and time-domain dynamic limits together.

On a single joint, such as the manipulator’s shoulder lifting a grasped payload, the balance of torques obeys the scalar equation of motion:

$$
{\tau} = {J_{\text{eff}}}\,{\ddot{q}} + {m} {g} {r}\cos {q} + {\tau_{\text{fric}}} \tag{7}
$$

^eq-plan-one-joint-dynamics

where ${J_{\text{eff}}}$ represents the effective inertia driven by the joint, ${m}$ is a concentrated mass whose center of gravity lies at distance ${r}$ from the joint axis, ${q}$ is the joint angle measured from the horizontal plane, and ${\tau_{\text{fric}}}$ is friction torque opposing motion. Mechanical inertia resists angular acceleration, while gravity exerts a static load proportional to the horizontal moment arm ${r}\cos {q}$. Holding the rated 3 kg payload horizontally at the manipulator’s full 0.70 m planar reach requires 20.6 N·m of static holding torque, which consumes 23.7 percent of the joint’s 87 N·m torque limit before motion begins ([[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/03 Running the Machine/11 Trajectory Planning/V4-11.13 Source References#^ref-franka2023robot|Franka Robotics GmbH 2023]]). The actuator’s reflected rotor inertia is the ${N_{\text{gear}}}^2$-amplified rotor term of **[[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/01 Machine Anatomy/02 The Physical Body/V4-02.05 Actuator Transmission Limits#^sec-body-reflected-rotor-inertia|Reflected rotor inertia and optimal inertia matching]]**, which the seam analysis below prices for a Panda-class joint. Adding it to the payload inertia produces an effective joint inertia of 1.65 kg·m² that every commanded acceleration must accelerate. Structural link mass and distributed inertia add to this baseline. Because manufacturer datasheets rarely provide link mass distributions, empirical evaluations rely on published identified parameters ([[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/03 Running the Machine/11 Trajectory Planning/V4-11.13 Source References#^ref-gaz2019dynamic|Gaz et al. 2019]]); an operational deployment requires system identification on the physical arm and attached tooling.

In multi-degree-of-freedom manipulators, dynamic coupling interconnects the joints. The scalar terms generalize into the matrix terms of the **manipulator equation**, derived from the Euler-Lagrange equations of motion ([[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/03 Running the Machine/11 Trajectory Planning/V4-11.13 Source References#^ref-craig2005introduction|Craig 2005]]; [[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/03 Running the Machine/11 Trajectory Planning/V4-11.13 Source References#^ref-lynch2017modern|Lynch and Park 2017]]):

$$
{\boldsymbol{\tau}}({t}) = {\mathbf{M}}({\mathbf{q}}){\ddot{\mathbf{q}}} + {\mathbf{C}}({\mathbf{q}}, {\dot{\mathbf{q}}}){\dot{\mathbf{q}}} + {\mathbf{g}}({\mathbf{q}}) - {\mathbf{J}}^\top({\mathbf{q}}){\mathbf{F}_{\text{ext}}} \tag{8}
$$

^eq-plan-rigidbody-dynamics

Here, the symmetric, positive-definite inertia matrix ${\mathbf{M}}({\mathbf{q}})$ generalizes scalar inertia ${J_{\text{eff}}}$ from [[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/03 Running the Machine/11 Trajectory Planning/V4-11.05 Kinodynamic Feasibility#^eq-plan-one-joint-dynamics|equation 7]]. Off-diagonal elements capture cross-joint inertial coupling (accelerating the elbow induces reaction torques at the shoulder), and matrix values vary continuously as the arm articulates. The quadratic velocity term ${\mathbf{C}}({\mathbf{q}}, {\dot{\mathbf{q}}}){\dot{\mathbf{q}}}$ aggregates centrifugal and Coriolis torques, which scale quadratically with joint velocity, while the gravity vector ${\mathbf{g}}({\mathbf{q}})$ generalizes ${m} {g} {r}\cos {q}$ to represent configuration-dependent static holding torque. The geometric Jacobian from [[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/03 Running the Machine/11 Trajectory Planning/V4-11.03 The Planning Handoff and the Trajectory Record#^eq-plan-two-link-kinematics|equation 1]] maps an external wrench ${\mathbf{F}_{\text{ext}}}$ acting on the end effector into equivalent joint torques via the transpose ${\mathbf{J}}^\top({\mathbf{q}})$; external contact wrenches that assist motion reduce actuator torque demands, while resistive loads increase them. Evaluating [[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/03 Running the Machine/11 Trajectory Planning/V4-11.05 Kinodynamic Feasibility#^eq-plan-rigidbody-dynamics|equation 8]] along a prospective trajectory ${\mathbf{q}}({t})$ is the problem of **inverse dynamics**. The recursive Newton-Euler algorithm computes inverse dynamics for an ${n}$-joint serial arm in $\mathcal{O}({n})$ arithmetic operations per trajectory sample ([[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/03 Running the Machine/11 Trajectory Planning/V4-11.13 Source References#^ref-luh1980online|Luh et al. 1980]]; [[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/03 Running the Machine/11 Trajectory Planning/V4-11.13 Source References#^ref-lynch2017modern|Lynch and Park 2017]]). An admission filter scanning an ${N}$-sample candidate trajectory therefore performs $\mathcal{O}({N} {n})$ operations whose worst-case execution time must fit within the system’s admission latency allowance.

Before dispatching a proposed trajectory chunk, the planning runtime screens it against the kinodynamic feasibility envelope, verifying that the physical state at the handoff seam satisfies position, velocity, torque, stator current, contact force, and collision boundaries over the planning horizon. For robotic arms, the rigid-body model maps joint trajectories ${\mathbf{q}}({t})$, ${\dot{\mathbf{q}}}({t})$, and ${\ddot{\mathbf{q}}}({t})$ to joint torques ${\boldsymbol{\tau}}({t})$ via [[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/03 Running the Machine/11 Trajectory Planning/V4-11.05 Kinodynamic Feasibility#^eq-plan-rigidbody-dynamics|equation 8]]. Those torques convert to quadrature-axis motor currents ${{i_q}}_i({t}) = {\tau}_i({t}) / {{K_t}}_i$ using the joint-effective torque constant ${{K_t}}_i$. (When using motor-side torque constants, the conversion must explicitly incorporate gear ratios and transmission mechanical efficiencies.) Initial screening by an unprivileged planner, however, does not constitute system admission. The real-time safety MCU executes an independent, deterministic verification check, admitting a bounded trajectory chunk only if configured dynamic limits and certified stop clearances hold for the verified initial state. Once admitted, the MCU monitors control tracking error, plan freshness, and stopping distance margins on every servo cycle.

Numerical optimizers can smooth geometric paths and expand obstacle clearance, but motion permission resides strictly in the admitted trajectory chunk and its resident stopping suffix. For example, the cuRobo motion-generation framework uses massively parallel GPU compute to generate collision-free, minimum-jerk trajectories across kinematic configurations ([[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/03 Running the Machine/11 Trajectory Planning/V4-11.13 Source References#^ref-sundaralingam2023curobo|Sundaralingam et al. 2023]]). Its collision pipeline evaluates signed distance fields across sampled swept-sphere volumes under a linear-motion approximation, leaving continuous inter-sample swept volume evaluation as an open requirement. Following trajectory optimization, cuRobo identifies the limiting time step where joint velocity, acceleration, or jerk encounters actuator bounds, rescaling trajectory duration before re-optimizing. This uniform temporal scaling represents an initial form of the rigorous time parameterization developed in [[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/03 Running the Machine/11 Trajectory Planning/V4-11.05 Kinodynamic Feasibility#^sec-planning-time-parameterization|section 11.4.5]]. Because cuRobo’s algorithmic formulation focuses on kinematic derivatives rather than full multi-body dynamics, torque boundaries, winding thermal accumulation, continuous swept-volume containment, and admission deadlines remain external system-level requirements. Under this systems architecture, the GPU trajectory generator operates as an unprivileged proposer, while the real-time safety MCU independently validates prospective trajectories and seam constraints within a strict admission deadline, latching an executable stopping continuation before authorizing motion.

### Overshoot Between Samples and Contracted Limits

^sec-planning-intrasample-overshoot

When the admission filter checks a candidate only at discrete waypoints spaced ${T_s}$ apart, two consecutive joint samples ${t}_k = {k} {T_s}$ that sit just below the velocity ceiling ${v_{\max}}$ can still bracket an overspeed fault midway between them. The reason is that physical actuators track a continuous interpolant across $({t}_k, {t}_{k+1})$. If that interpolator can command accelerations up to ${a_{\max}}$ within a sample period, an acceleration sign reversal across the midpoint lets continuous velocity rise above the values recorded at both endpoints.

Under acceleration-bounded interpolation, the maximum velocity excursion between two consecutive samples sharing equal velocity arises from a bang-bang acceleration reversal: $+{a_{\max}}$ applied over $[0, {T_s}/2]$, followed immediately by $-{a_{\max}}$ over $[{T_s}/2, {T_s}]$ to restore endpoint velocity. The velocity excursion reaches its maximum at the midpoint, corresponding to the integrated area of the initial acceleration phase:

$$
{\Delta} {\dot{q}} = {a_{\max}} \cdot \frac{{T_s}}{2} = \frac{1}{2} {a_{\max}} {T_s} \tag{9}
$$

^eq-plan-intrasample-overshoot

As [[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/03 Running the Machine/11 Trajectory Planning/V4-11.05 Kinodynamic Feasibility#^eq-plan-intrasample-overshoot|equation 9]] demonstrates, two consecutive waypoint samples evaluated just beneath velocity ceiling ${v_{\max}}$ can bracket an interior velocity peak that exceeds the limit, tripping drive overspeed protection between individually valid samples. To ensure continuous-time velocity compliance without evaluating an infinite continuum of points, the admission filter tests discrete waypoints against an analytically contracted velocity threshold:

$$
|{\dot{q}}_k| \le {v_{\max}} - \frac{1}{2} {a_{\max}} {T_s} \tag{10}
$$

^eq-plan-contracted-velocity-limit

The shaded acceleration area in [[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/03 Running the Machine/11 Trajectory Planning/V4-11.05 Kinodynamic Feasibility#^fig-11-intersample-overshoot|figure 3]] becomes the velocity rise between the sampled endpoints. Placing both endpoints one full rise below the physical ceiling keeps the continuous peak at that ceiling.

![[90 Assets/Volume IV/11 Planning/Intersample Velocity Overshoot 2315d34ba4.svg]]

*Aligned acceleration and velocity plots show a positive then negative acceleration step, a triangular velocity excursion above a dashed limit, and a lower triangular trajectory whose peak reaches the limit.*

Figure 3: **A Velocity Peak Between Valid Samples**: Positive acceleration over the first half of a sample interval raises velocity; the equal negative phase restores the original endpoint velocity. The bronze trajectory passes both endpoint checks but crosses the velocity limit. The pine trajectory uses the contracted sample ceiling, leaving the reserve derived in [[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/03 Running the Machine/11 Trajectory Planning/V4-11.05 Kinodynamic Feasibility#^eq-plan-intrasample-overshoot|equation 9]]. The schematic assumes an acceleration bound and an instantaneous sign reversal; it does not impose a finite jerk bound.

^fig-11-intersample-overshoot

The endpoint check succeeds only when the interpolant obeys the assumptions used to reserve the margin. A sampled value below the physical ceiling alone supplies no such guarantee.

This contraction creates a safety margin that absorbs the worst-case overshoot any acceleration-bounded interpolator can generate across a sample period ${T_s}$. A smaller reserve requires additional boundary conditions. If consecutive samples have equal velocity and zero acceleration, and the interpolator enforces ${j_{\max}} \le 4{a_{\max}}/{T_s}$, the extremal reversal consists of a positive triangular acceleration pulse followed by its negative counterpart, each of base ${T_s}/2$. Their peaks reach ${a_{\max}}$ only at equality in the jerk condition, so $\frac{1}{4} {a_{\max}} {T_s}$ is a conservative velocity reserve for this restricted interpolation. A jerk bound alone does not justify that reserve when endpoint accelerations are nonzero. For polynomial spline interpolants, the admission filter can instead compute exact interior extrema analytically before certifying continuous-time feasibility.

### Seam Acceleration Jumps and Reflected Rotor Inertia

^sec-planning-seam-torque-inertia

When the arm hands off from one action chunk to the next while moving, two chunks that are each feasible in isolation can still form an infeasible seam between them. Matching joint positions alone (${C}^0$ continuity) leaves an unconstrained velocity step across the boundary. Matching both position and velocity (${C}^1$ continuity) eliminates velocity jumps but leaves an instantaneous acceleration discontinuity, which demands an immediate step in joint torque. Under a rigid-inertia approximation, commanding a velocity step ${\Delta}{\dot{q}}$ across a single servo period ${T_s}$ requires an inertial torque of:

$$
{\tau_{\text{request}}} = {J_{\text{eff}}} \frac{{\Delta}{\dot{q}}}{{T_s}} \tag{11}
$$

^eq-plan-seam-torque

where ${J_{\text{eff}}} = {J_{\text{load}}} + {N_{\text{gear}}}^2 {J_{\text{rotor}}}$ in [[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/03 Running the Machine/11 Trajectory Planning/V4-11.05 Kinodynamic Feasibility#^eq-plan-seam-torque|equation 11]] represents the total effective inertia referenced to the joint output. This term adds link and payload inertia ${J_{\text{load}}}$ to the reflected rotor inertia established in **[[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/01 Machine Anatomy/02 The Physical Body/V4-02.05 Actuator Transmission Limits#^sec-body-reflected-rotor-inertia|Reflected rotor inertia and optimal inertia matching]]**, the motor rotor inertia ${J_{\text{rotor}}}$ seen through gear reduction ratio ${N_{\text{gear}}}$. Because the rotor turns ${N_{\text{gear}}}$ times faster than the joint, its kinetic energy, written in joint velocity, carries the factor ${N_{\text{gear}}}^2$:

$$
{E_k} = \frac{1}{2} {J_{\text{rotor}}} {\omega_m}^2 = \frac{1}{2} {J_{\text{rotor}}} ({N_{\text{gear}}} {\dot{q}})^2 = \frac{1}{2} ({N_{\text{gear}}}^2 {J_{\text{rotor}}}) {\dot{q}}^2
$$

In precision robotic manipulators equipped with high-ratio strain-wave gearing[[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/03 Running the Machine/11 Trajectory Planning/V4-11.14 Footnotes#^fn7|⁷]] (${N_{\text{gear}}} = 50\text{--}100$), reflected rotor inertia is magnified by a factor of $2{,}500$ to $10{,}000$. It can then rival or exceed link inertia, most often for an unloaded arm or a folded pose, although a rated payload held at full reach outweighs it. In the one-joint example the payload supplies most of the 1.65 kg·m² total, against 0.18 kg·m² from the rotor. Either way, the reflected term sits in every joint’s ${J_{\text{eff}}}$ regardless of payload, and on its own it makes seam torque spikes large. At a ${C}^0$ boundary, an instantaneous velocity step implies an idealized Dirac acceleration impulse ${\delta}({t})$, which no physical motor drive can supply. At a ${C}^1$ boundary, an acceleration jump ${\Delta}{\ddot{q}}$ demands a step change in electromagnetic torque, ${\Delta}{\tau} = {J_{\text{eff}}} {\Delta}{\ddot{q}}$. Such abrupt torque transients excite structural resonance in flexible transmissions, accelerate mechanical wear, and degrade control tracking. On the strain-wave joint priced in the notebook below, an unblended one-tick velocity step of 0.2 rad/s asks the reflected rotor inertia for a 36.7 N·m torque pulse, while a 50 ms ${C}^2$ quintic bridge closing the same gap peaks at 1.1 N·m ([[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/03 Running the Machine/11 Trajectory Planning/V4-11.05 Kinodynamic Feasibility#^fig-11-action-chunk-seam-continuity|figure 4]]). Commercial industrial arms enforce this exact discipline at their low-level control interfaces. For example, the Franka Research 3 arm samples setpoints at 1 kHz, comparing each incoming command against the previous cycle. If inter-cycle setpoint differences exceed factory derivative thresholds, the drive immediately aborts motion with an unrecoverable discontinuity fault (such as `joint_motion_generator_velocity_discontinuity`) ([[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/03 Running the Machine/11 Trajectory Planning/V4-11.13 Source References#^ref-franka2026troubleshooting|Franka Robotics GmbH 2026]], [[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/03 Running the Machine/11 Trajectory Planning/V4-11.13 Source References#^ref-franka2023robot|2023]]). An unblended seam can therefore abort nominal tracking before repeated transitions accumulate mechanical fatigue. Physical stopping still requires the drive’s qualified response delay and deceleration profile.

When the arm carries an open cup of liquid, steady velocity does not spill the contents, whereas rapid acceleration transitions and high jerk provoke sloshing and spillage ([[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/03 Running the Machine/11 Trajectory Planning/V4-11.06 Behavior at the Seam#^sec-planning-c2-stopping-suffix|section 11.5.2]] models this deceleration threshold directly). A trajectory interface that continuously matches position, velocity, and acceleration constitutes a **${C}^2$ spline seam**. Enforcing ${C}^2$ continuity eliminates reference acceleration steps and prevents the associated torque jump ${\Delta}{\tau} = {J_{\text{eff}}}{\Delta}{\ddot{q}}$. Concurrently, bounding trajectory **jerk** (${j_{\text{joint}}}={\dot{a}}=\dddot{{x}}$) limits the rate at which joint acceleration changes. Neither mathematical property alone guarantees payload stability or mechanical longevity, however; an interpolating bridge that matches boundary conditions perfectly can still violate velocity, jerk, actuator torque, or obstacle clearance limits in its interior.

Mechanical transmissions govern how actuator torque couples into physical link motion. In a standard two-inertia drivetrain comprising load inertia ${J_{\text{load}}}$, reflected rotor inertia ${J_{\text{ref}}}={N_{\text{gear}}}^2{J_{\text{rotor}}}$, and torsional coupling stiffness ${K_{\text{joint}}}$, the antiresonance and resonance frequencies ([[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/03 Running the Machine/11 Trajectory Planning/V4-11.13 Source References#^ref-li2024optimal|Li et al. 2024]]) are:

$$
{f_{\text{anti}}} = \frac{1}{2\pi}\sqrt{\frac{{K_{\text{joint}}}}{{J_{\text{load}}}}}, \qquad {f_{\text{res}}} = \frac{1}{2\pi}\sqrt{{K_{\text{joint}}}\left(\frac{1}{{J_{\text{load}}}} + \frac{1}{{J_{\text{ref}}}}\right)}
$$

In robot joints constructed with strain-wave gearing, large reflected inertia coupled with flexspline torsional compliance amplifies high-frequency setpoint harmonics. An admission checker must therefore verify prospective bridges against the joint’s identified, load-dependent vibrational modes, evaluating derivative extrema, peak torques, and drive current limits against empirical structural parameters. The two frequencies reach the checker through the bridge duration. A short bridge can excite these modes, but duration alone does not determine its spectral content or the joint’s response. Identified modal frequencies and damping therefore inform qualified bridge-duration and derivative limits alongside the torque-rate limit. Structural stiffness, resonance modes, and admissible jerk envelopes never transfer between robot platforms without direct system identification.

### Quintic Splines and C² Boundary Matching

^sec-planning-quintic-splines

Closing the position, velocity, and acceleration gap between retiring chunk ${A}$ and incoming chunk ${B}$ over a finite blend duration ${T_{\text{blend}}}$ requires online ${C}^2$ trajectory spline blending. The blend duration is a physical execution interval, distinct from the computational blend deadline ${t_{\text{blend}}}$. Blending synthesizes a dedicated polynomial bridge ${q_{\text{blend}}}({t})$, which must undergo the exact same kinodynamic feasibility checks as the primary chunks rather than acting as a cosmetic filter.[[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/03 Running the Machine/11 Trajectory Planning/V4-11.14 Footnotes#^fn8|⁸]] A localized seam bridge must satisfy position, velocity, and acceleration constraints at both boundary states, which gives six independent boundary conditions. A cubic polynomial has only four degrees of freedom and cannot satisfy them. A fifth-degree quintic polynomial provides the six required coefficients. This localized endpoint-matching formulation differs from fitting a globally continuous cubic spline across an entire offline sequence of knots.

The six boundary conditions are

$$
{q}(0) = {q}_0, \quad {\dot{q}}(0) = {\dot{q}}_0, \quad {\ddot{q}}(0) = {\ddot{q}}_0
$$

$$
{q}({T_{\text{blend}}}) = {q}_1, \quad {\dot{q}}({T_{\text{blend}}}) = {\dot{q}}_1, \quad {\ddot{q}}({T_{\text{blend}}}) = {\ddot{q}}_1
$$

 where $({q}_0, {\dot{q}}_0, {\ddot{q}}_0)$ is the terminal physical state of chunk ${A}$ at the seam instant, and $({q}_1, {\dot{q}}_1, {\ddot{q}}_1)$ is the target state on incoming chunk ${B}$ at ${t} = {T_{\text{blend}}}$. The six conditions fix the six coefficients (${a}_0 \dots {a}_5$) of the fifth-degree polynomial:

$$
{q}({t}) = {a}_0 + {a}_1 {t} + {a}_2 {t}^2 + {a}_3 {t}^3 + {a}_4 {t}^4 + {a}_5 {t}^5
$$

Evaluating boundary conditions at ${t} = 0$ directly yields the first three coefficients: ${a}_0 = {q}_0$, ${a}_1 = {\dot{q}}_0$, and ${a}_2 = \frac{1}{2}{\ddot{q}}_0$. The remaining three coefficients $[{a}_3, {a}_4, {a}_5]^\top$ satisfy the $3 \times 3$ linear system evaluated at duration ${T} = {T_{\text{blend}}}$:

$$
\begin{bmatrix} {T_{\text{blend}}}^3 & {T_{\text{blend}}}^4 & {T_{\text{blend}}}^5 \\ 3{T_{\text{blend}}}^2 & 4{T_{\text{blend}}}^3 & 5{T_{\text{blend}}}^4 \\ 6{T_{\text{blend}}} & 12{T_{\text{blend}}}^2 & 20{T_{\text{blend}}}^3 \end{bmatrix} \begin{bmatrix} {a}_3 \\ {a}_4 \\ {a}_5 \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} {q}_1 - \left({q}_0 + {\dot{q}}_0 {T_{\text{blend}}} + \frac{1}{2}{\ddot{q}}_0 {T_{\text{blend}}}^2\right) \\ {\dot{q}}_1 - \left({\dot{q}}_0 + {\ddot{q}}_0 {T_{\text{blend}}}\right) \\ {\ddot{q}}_1 - {\ddot{q}}_0 \end{bmatrix} \tag{12}
$$

^eq-plan-quintic-spline-matrix

Analytically inverting [[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/03 Running the Machine/11 Trajectory Planning/V4-11.05 Kinodynamic Feasibility#^eq-plan-quintic-spline-matrix|equation 12]] yields closed-form expressions for $[{a}_3, {a}_4, {a}_5]^\top$, given in **[[05 Reference/Volume IV - Physical AI/V4 Control and Dynamics#^sec-appendix-control-splines|Smooth Trajectories and Bumpless Transfer]]**, that require deterministic arithmetic operations per joint. Differentiating ${q}({t})$ three times produces the continuous commanded jerk profile:

$$
{j_{\text{joint}}}({t}) = \dddot{{q}}({t}) = 6 {a}_3 + 24 {a}_4 {t} + 60 {a}_5 {t}^2 \tag{13}
$$

^eq-plan-quintic-jerk

Because ${j_{\text{joint}}}({t})$ in [[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/03 Running the Machine/11 Trajectory Planning/V4-11.05 Kinodynamic Feasibility#^eq-plan-quintic-jerk|equation 13]] is a quadratic function of time, its extrema over the interval $[0, {T}]$ occur exclusively at the domain boundaries (${t} = 0$ or ${t} = {T}$) or at the interior stationary point ${t}^* = -{a}_4 / (5 {a}_5)$. The interior point applies when ${a}_5 \ne 0$ and ${t}^* \in (0, {T})$. If ${a}_5 = 0$, jerk varies linearly or remains constant, and evaluating the boundaries alone suffices. The safety microcontroller can therefore compute exact peak jerk $|{j_{\text{peak}}}|$ analytically in $\mathcal{O}(1)$ operations without executing iterative numerical line searches. An identical principle governs lower derivatives: acceleration extrema occur at the roots of the quadratic jerk, and velocity extrema occur at the roots of cubic acceleration, both solvable in closed form.

Evaluating coupled joint torques across the bridge presents an entirely different challenge. Along a polynomial trajectory ${\mathbf{q}}({t})$, the right-hand side of [[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/03 Running the Machine/11 Trajectory Planning/V4-11.05 Kinodynamic Feasibility#^eq-plan-rigidbody-dynamics|equation 8]] is non-polynomial because the inertia matrix ${\mathbf{M}}({\mathbf{q}})$, Coriolis matrix ${\mathbf{C}}({\mathbf{q}}, {\dot{\mathbf{q}}})$, and gravity vector ${\mathbf{g}}({\mathbf{q}})$ contain transcendental trigonometric functions of joint positions. Consequently, peak torque possesses no closed-form analytic solution. The safety monitor therefore evaluates torque at discrete sample waypoints and bounds inter-sample torque variation using the contraction principles established in [[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/03 Running the Machine/11 Trajectory Planning/V4-11.05 Kinodynamic Feasibility#^sec-planning-intrasample-overshoot|section 11.4.2]].

Bounding trajectory jerk directly protects actuator health, preserves motor inverter voltage headroom, and prevents premature transmission wear. When a trajectory generator outputs time-parameterized splines, it transforms kinematic waypoints into continuous reference functions whose derivatives map directly onto physical electrical and mechanical states. In permanent-magnet synchronous motors operated under field-oriented control, joint torque rate couples to jerk through the time derivative of the manipulator equation: ${\dot{\boldsymbol{\tau}}}({t}) = {\mathbf{M}}({\mathbf{q}}) \dddot{{\mathbf{q}}}({t}) + {\dot{\mathbf{M}}}({\mathbf{q}}) {\ddot{\mathbf{q}}}({t}) + \dots$. For a single joint with constant effective inertia ${J_{\text{eff}}} = {J_{\text{load}}} + {N_{\text{gear}}}^2 {J_{\text{rotor}}}$, neglecting changing gravity, friction, and external-load torques reduces the output torque rate to ${\dot{\tau}} = {J_{\text{eff}}} {j_{\text{joint}}}({t})$. Defining a joint-effective torque constant ${K_t}$ such that output torque satisfies ${\tau} = {K_t} {i_q}$, commanding an output torque rate ${\dot{\tau}}$ demands a proportional quadrature-axis stator current slew rate:

$$
\frac{d{i_q}}{d{t}} = \frac{{J_{\text{eff}}}}{{K_t}} {j_{\text{joint}}}({t})
$$

Here, ${K_t}$ incorporates both the gear reduction ratio and transmission mechanical efficiency; directly substituting a raw motor-side torque constant without gear scaling would incorrectly pair motor torque with output-side inertia. Motor winding inductance ${L_{\text{phase}}}$ and available DC bus voltage impose hard electrical limits on current slew rate. During positive motoring operations with resistive and back-electromotive force (back-EMF) drops, a simplified necessary condition for voltage headroom is:

$$
{V_{\text{bus}}} \ge {L_{\text{phase}}} \frac{d{i_q}}{d{t}} + {R_{\text{phase}}}\, {i_q} + {K_e} {\omega_e} \tag{14}
$$

^eq-plan-inverter-headroom

where ${R_{\text{phase}}}$ represents phase winding resistance, ${K_e}$ is the motor back-EMF constant, and ${\omega_e}$ denotes motor electrical speed. In operational motor drives, space-vector pulse-width modulation limits the maximum usable quadrature-axis voltage to a fraction of the total DC bus voltage. A complete electrical feasibility check must evaluate available voltage against the fully coupled, signed motor equations, including regenerative braking and inductive flyback, rather than treat a scalar approximation as sufficient qualification.

↩︎ Phase inductance, back-EMF, and the current-slew limits they impose are established in **[[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/01 Machine Anatomy/02 The Physical Body/V4-02.05 Actuator Transmission Limits#^sec-body-electrical-time-constants|Electrical time constants, back-EMF, and current clamping]]**.

An acceleration discontinuity in a motion plan demands an instantaneous step in electromagnetic torque and stator current. Because finite bus voltage and nonzero winding inductance prevent physical inverters from supplying infinite current slew rates ($d{i_q}/d{t}$), the physical actuator lags the commanded reference. The resulting tracking error, inverter saturation, and thermal protection response depend on drive gains and mechanical compliance. An unbounded mathematical jerk demand does not instantly cause an overcurrent shutdown, but it induces severe tracking lag, mechanical vibration, and drive stress. A valid bridge must satisfy both continuous mathematical derivative bounds and the physical bandwidth limits of the actuator drive.

Satisfying the six boundary conditions guarantees acceleration continuity at chunk interfaces, but it does not guarantee that interior jerk, torque, or current slew rates remain within actuator ratings. The admission filter evaluates closed-form derivative extrema from [[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/03 Running the Machine/11 Trajectory Planning/V4-11.05 Kinodynamic Feasibility#^eq-plan-quintic-jerk|equation 13]] against the electrical constraints of [[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/03 Running the Machine/11 Trajectory Planning/V4-11.05 Kinodynamic Feasibility#^eq-plan-inverter-headroom|equation 14]], checks sampled multi-body torques against inter-sample contraction bounds, and rejects any bridge violating physical thresholds. Evaluating a fixed-degree polynomial requires bounded arithmetic operations per joint. Spline coefficient synthesis and trajectory extrema verification take place prior to trajectory dispatch; the qualified worst-case execution bound, accounting for memory access latency and register operations, must remain strictly within the configured admission deadline.

While a local quintic bridge resolves seam continuity through explicit boundary matching, global trajectory representations provide alternative verification properties. **Uniform B-splines**[[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/03 Running the Machine/11 Trajectory Planning/V4-11.14 Footnotes#^fn9|⁹]] parameterize joint trajectories as ${\mathbf{q}}({t}) = \sum_{i=0}^{L} {\mathbf{p}}_i {{B}}_{i,{p}}({t})$, where ${\mathbf{p}}_i$ represent geometric control points, ${{B}}_{i,{p}}$ are polynomial basis functions of degree ${p}$, and ${\xi}_i$ denote knot timestamps. Using simple knot sequences, uniform B-splines ensure ${C}^{{p}-1}$ continuity by construction. Furthermore, the **convex hull property** ensures that the continuous trajectory lies entirely within the convex hull formed by its control points, enabling spatial collision checkers to prune entire collision volumes without dense sampling. Differentiating a B-spline yields a derivative spline whose control points are ${\mathbf{v}}_i = \frac{{p}}{{\xi}_{i+{p}+1}-{\xi}_{i+1}}({\mathbf{p}}_{i+1} - {\mathbf{p}}_i)$, allowing difference operations on control points to establish conservative upper bounds on velocity, acceleration, and jerk. These geometric bounds accelerate screening, though they do not replace full dynamic inverse dynamics or swept-volume collision checks. In streaming replanning architectures, the trajectory representation must also support rapid coefficient joining within the real-time admission deadline; [[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/03 Running the Machine/11 Trajectory Planning/V4-11.05 Kinodynamic Feasibility#^tbl-11-trajectory-representations|table 1]] separates the continuity supplied by each representation from the dynamic checks still required before execution.

**Napkin Math 11.2: The seam spike**

**Problem**: At a replanning seam the incoming chunk’s reference runs 0.2 rad/s faster than the active one. Should the joint close that gap in one servo tick or over a ${C}^2$ bridge, and what does each ask of the drive?

**Variables**:

- Joint: a CSG-25-50 strain-wave gear driven by a TQ-RoboDrive ILM 70x18 motor, a Panda-class stand-in because Franka does not publish the Panda’s joint internals (datasheet parts); reflected rotor inertia ${J_{\text{eff}}} = {N_{\text{gear}}}^2 {J_{\text{rotor}}} =$ 0.18 kg·m² (derived; link and payload inertia excluded, so here ${J_{\text{eff}}}$ is the reflected inertia ${J_{\text{ref}}}$ of **[[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/01 Machine Anatomy/02 The Physical Body/V4-02.05 Actuator Transmission Limits#^sec-body-reflected-rotor-inertia|Reflected rotor inertia and optimal inertia matching]]** alone)
- Ratings 51 N·m rated and 127 N·m repeated peak (datasheet); the arm’s torque-rate limit 1,000 N·m/s (datasheet)
- Velocity gap ${\Delta}{\dot{q}} =$ 0.2 rad/s (illustrative) and servo tick ${T_s} =$ 1 ms (chosen)
- Bridge duration ${T_{\text{blend}}} =$ 50 ms (illustrative), zero acceleration at both ends, ending on chunk B’s reference, a position residual ${\Delta}{\dot{q}}\,{T_{\text{blend}}}/2 =$ 0.005 rad (chosen)

**Math**:

1.  **One tick** ([[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/03 Running the Machine/11 Trajectory Planning/V4-11.05 Kinodynamic Feasibility#^eq-plan-seam-torque|equation 11]]): ${J_{\text{eff}}}\,{\Delta}{\dot{q}}/{T_s} =$ 36.7 N·m, below the rating. Reaching it within the tick needs at least 36.7 N·m per 1 ms, or 36,700 N·m/s, 36.7× the torque-rate limit.
2.  **Quintic bridge** ([[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/03 Running the Machine/11 Trajectory Planning/V4-11.05 Kinodynamic Feasibility#^eq-plan-quintic-spline-matrix|equation 12]]): Because a symmetric velocity transition from ${\dot{q}}_0$ to ${\dot{q}}_0 + {\Delta}{\dot{q}}$ averages ${\dot{q}}_0 + \frac{1}{2}{\Delta}{\dot{q}}$ across the bridge, the joint advances by ${\Delta}{\dot{q}}\,{T_{\text{blend}}}/2$ beyond chunk A’s constant-velocity extrapolation; with that position residual and zero boundary accelerations, the system gives ${a}_5 = 0$, ${a}_4=-{\Delta}{\dot{q}}/(2{T_{\text{blend}}}^3)=-800\ \text{rad/s}^4$, and ${a}_3={\Delta}{\dot{q}}/{T_{\text{blend}}}^2=80\ \text{rad/s}^3$.
3.  **Bridge peaks**: Acceleration peaks at mid-bridge at $1.5\,{\Delta}{\dot{q}}/{T_{\text{blend}}} =$ 6 rad/s², a torque of 1.1 N·m. Jerk ([[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/03 Running the Machine/11 Trajectory Planning/V4-11.05 Kinodynamic Feasibility#^eq-plan-quintic-jerk|equation 13]]) peaks at both ends at $6{a}_3 =$ 480 rad/s³, a torque rate ${J_{\text{eff}}}\,{j_{\text{joint}}}$ of 88.1 N·m/s, 8.8 percent of the limit.
4.  **Half the duration**: Peak torque scales as $1/{T_{\text{blend}}}$ and jerk as $1/{T_{\text{blend}}}^2$, so a bridge half as long needs 2.2 N·m, 1,920 rad/s³, and 352.3 N·m/s, still inside the limit.

**Result**: The one-tick step passes a torque check and asks for 36.7× the torque-rate limit; the bridge passes both with a 33.3× smaller torque peak.

**Systems insight**: Evaluating torque magnitude alone is an inadequate feasibility screen at a trajectory seam. Actuator drives fail on the rate of change of torque, which grows quadratically ($1/{T_{\text{blend}}}^2$) as blending duration compresses. When late trajectory admission forces the system to compress bridge duration ${T_{\text{blend}}}$, required jerk and torque slew rates increase fourfold for every twofold reduction in duration. An admission monitor must explicitly re-evaluate the compressed bridge rather than inheriting validity from the uncompressed nominal profile. Furthermore, link inertia, payload mass, gravity, and joint friction compound this inertial baseline, requiring independent peak torque and thermal budget verification. The torque verdict of step 1 depends on that exclusion. With the payload inertia of [[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/03 Running the Machine/11 Trajectory Planning/V4-11.05 Kinodynamic Feasibility#^eq-plan-one-joint-dynamics|equation 7]] included, 1.65 kg·m² in place of 0.18 kg·m², the one-tick request grows by the same ratio and exceeds even the 127 N·m repeated peak, so the one-tick step fails the torque check as well.

High-frequency chatter in learned policy outputs represents an identical physical hazard under a different guise. Stochastic sampling policies, such as diffusion policies or action-chunking transformers, often generate consecutive trajectory chunks where individual waypoints satisfy static position and velocity limits, yet commanded accelerations reverse sign erratically between steps. Each acceleration reversal demands an immediate torque reversal from the actuator, triggering rapid current transitions and severe ${i_q}^2 {R_{\text{phase}}}$ resistive heating in the motor windings. In physical AI systems, engineers must treat this high-frequency variance as a direct cause of thermal rise and transmission fatigue rather than cosmetic jitter. A trajectory candidate that excites structural resonances or exceeds drive thermal dissipation limits is infeasible, regardless of how closely its geometric path resembles demonstration data.

### Time Parameterization Under Torque and Jerk Bounds

^sec-planning-time-parameterization

A collision-free curve through configuration space says where the arm goes. It says nothing about how fast the arm may travel along that curve without saturating a joint’s torque limit on the way up or demanding an impossible torque step when it switches to braking. Geometric search and neural proposal generators determine only the spatial path of motion: a curve ${\mathbf{q}}({s})$ parameterized by scalar path coordinate ${s} \in [0, 1]$. The timing law ${s}({t})$ governs the physical motion, determining every joint velocity, acceleration, and torque required along the trajectory. Establishing a valid timing profile ${s}({t})$ subject to physical actuator limits is the problem of **time parameterization**. Applying the chain rule to the joint coordinates yields:

$$
{\dot{\mathbf{q}}} = {\mathbf{q}'}({s})\,{\dot{s}}, \qquad {\ddot{\mathbf{q}}} = {\mathbf{q}'}({s})\,{\ddot{s}} + {\mathbf{q}''}({s})\,{\dot{s}}^2
$$

where primes denote differentiation with respect to path coordinate ${s}$ (${d}{\mathbf{q}}/d{s}$ and ${d}^2{\mathbf{q}}/d{s}^2$). Substituting these path derivatives into the unconstrained manipulator equation from [[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/03 Running the Machine/11 Trajectory Planning/V4-11.05 Kinodynamic Feasibility#^eq-plan-rigidbody-dynamics|equation 8]] expresses joint torque as a function of three path-dependent terms:

$$
{\boldsymbol{\tau}} = {\mathbf{a}_{\tau}}({s})\,{\ddot{s}} + {\mathbf{b}_{\tau}}({s})\,{\dot{s}}^2 + {\mathbf{g}}({s}) \tag{15}
$$

^eq-plan-path-torque

with vector coefficients ${\mathbf{a}_{\tau}}({s}) = {\mathbf{M}}({\mathbf{q}}){\mathbf{q}'}({s})$, ${\mathbf{b}_{\tau}}({s}) = {\mathbf{M}}({\mathbf{q}}){\mathbf{q}''}({s}) + {\mathbf{C}}({\mathbf{q}},{\mathbf{q}'})\,{\mathbf{q}'}({s})$, and ${\mathbf{g}}({s}) = {\mathbf{g}}({\mathbf{q}})$. Each actuator torque bound $|{\tau}_i| \le {{\tau_{\max}}}_i$ translates into an admissible interval of path acceleration, ${\ddot{s}_{\min}}({s},{\dot{s}}) \le {\ddot{s}} \le {\ddot{s}_{\max}}({s},{\dot{s}})$, at every coordinate of the **phase plane** $({s}, {\dot{s}})$. Actuator velocity and acceleration limits introduce equivalent bounding intervals. As path velocity ${\dot{s}}$ increases, the centrifugal and Coriolis term ${\mathbf{b}_{\tau}}({s}){\dot{s}}^2$ grows quadratically until the admissible acceleration interval closes entirely (${\ddot{s}_{\min}} = {\ddot{s}_{\max}}$). The locus of phase-plane points where this interval collapses defines the maximum velocity curve, representing an upper speed envelope that no feasible parameterization can exceed.

Because total motion duration is ${T} = \int_0^1 \frac{1}{{\dot{s}}}\,d{s}$, the minimum-time profile maintains the highest permissible path velocity ${\dot{s}}$ at every point along the path. Under torque constraints alone, the time-optimal solution takes a bang-bang form ([[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/03 Running the Machine/11 Trajectory Planning/V4-11.13 Source References#^ref-bobrow1985time|Bobrow et al. 1985]]). The system accelerates along the upper bound ${\ddot{s}_{\max}}$ until its phase-plane trajectory encounters a braking curve integrated backward from the goal at lower bound ${\ddot{s}_{\min}}$. It then decelerates along ${\ddot{s}_{\min}}$ to reach the target at rest. Switch points mark the phase-plane coordinates where the trajectory transitions between maximum acceleration and maximum braking. The TOPP-RA algorithm ([[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/03 Running the Machine/11 Trajectory Planning/V4-11.13 Source References#^ref-pham2018new|Pham and Pham 2018]]) solves this phase-plane parameterization efficiently by transforming it into a convex optimization problem. By substituting squared path velocity ${x} = {\dot{s}}^2$ and path acceleration ${u} = {\ddot{s}}$, the torque constraints in [[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/03 Running the Machine/11 Trajectory Planning/V4-11.05 Kinodynamic Feasibility#^eq-plan-path-torque|equation 15]] and the differential step equation ${x}_{i+1} = {x}_i + 2\,{\Delta} {s}\,{u}_i$ become linear across a grid of ${N_{\text{grid}}}$ path waypoints. (This linear step relation follows from $d{x}/d{s} = 2{\dot{s}}\,({d}{\dot{s}}/d{s}) = 2{\ddot{s}}$, matching the kinematic equation ${v}^2 = 2{a} {d}$). A backward integration pass computes the controllable interval of ${x}$ at each grid point from which the terminal state remains reachable, solving a single linear program per point. A subsequent forward pass selects the largest admissible acceleration ${u}$ at each step. The complete pass runs in $\mathcal{O}({N_{\text{grid}}})$ time, and an empty feasible interval at any point provides constructive proof that no parameterization satisfies the bounds on that grid. Kinematic variants that constrain only joint velocity and acceleration compute this timing structure with lower overhead ([[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/03 Running the Machine/11 Trajectory Planning/V4-11.13 Source References#^ref-kunz2012time|Kunz and Stilman 2012]]). The Kunz and Stilman algorithm serves as the default time-parameterization method in MoveIt 2, which can apply a downstream jerk-limiting filter ([[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/03 Running the Machine/11 Trajectory Planning/V4-11.13 Source References#^ref-moveit2timeparam|MoveIt 2 Documentation 2026]]).

**Napkin Math 11.3: Time-optimal swing of one joint**

**Problem**: The shoulder lifts the rated payload at full planar reach through 0.5 rad, from rest to rest. How fast can it swing, and what does the fastest timing ask of the drive?

**Variables**:

- Swing ${\Delta} {q} =$ 0.5 rad (illustrative), with the joint angle itself as the path parameter (${s} = {q}$, measured from the start)
- Inertia ${J_{\text{eff}}} =$ 1.65 kg·m² and payload gravity torque ${\tau_{g}} =$ 20.6 N·m, held at its horizontal value (derived, payload only)
- Joint limits: torque ${\tau_{\max}} =$ 87 N·m, acceleration 7.5 rad/s², velocity 2.175 rad/s, torque rate ${\dot{\tau}_{\max}} =$ 1000 N·m/s (datasheet ([[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/03 Running the Machine/11 Trajectory Planning/V4-11.13 Source References#^ref-franka2023robot|Franka Robotics GmbH 2023]]))

**Math**:

1.  **Binding acceleration**: The torque limit allows $({\tau_{\max}}-{\tau_{g}})/{J_{\text{eff}}} =$ 40.2 rad/s² upward and $({\tau_{\max}}+{\tau_{g}})/{J_{\text{eff}}} =$ 65.1 rad/s² braking, an asymmetry that gravity creates. The published 7.5 rad/s² is tighter than both, so it binds in both directions.
2.  **Time-optimal swing**: The accelerating curve ${\dot{s}}^2 = 2{a_{\max}} {s}$ meets the braking curve at mid-swing, where speed peaks at $\sqrt{{a_{\max}}\,{\Delta} {q}} =$ 1.94 rad/s, below the velocity limit. The swing takes $2\sqrt{{\Delta} {q}/{a_{\max}}} =$ 516.4 ms.
3.  **Switch-point step**: Acceleration reverses from $+{a_{\max}}$ to $-{a_{\max}}$, a torque step $2{J_{\text{eff}}}{}{a_{\max}} =$ 24.8 N·m, which needs at least 24.8 ms at the torque-rate limit. That limit bounds jerk at ${\dot{\tau}_{\max}}/{J_{\text{eff}}} =$ 604.8 rad/s³.
4.  **Jerk-limited swing**: A rest-to-rest double-S profile with no cruise covers ${\Delta} {q} = {a_{\max}}\,{T_{\text{acc}}}\,({T_{\text{acc}}} + {a_{\max}}/{j_{\max}})$ in ${T} = 2({T_{\text{acc}}} + {a_{\max}}/{j_{\max}})$. Because the accelerating half of the swing consists of a constant-acceleration hold framed by two jerk ramps of duration ${a_{\max}}/{j_{\max}}$ (one ramping acceleration up to ${a_{\max}}$ and one ramping it back to zero at mid-swing), ${T_{\text{acc}}} = {T}/2 - {a_{\max}}/{j_{\max}}$ is half the swing less one jerk ramp (the constant-acceleration hold plus one ramp), giving the trapezoidal acceleration pulse an area equal to the peak speed ${a_{\max}} {T_{\text{acc}}}$. With ${a_{\max}}/{j_{\max}} =$ 12.4 ms, the swing needs ${T_{\text{acc}}} =$ 252.1 ms and takes 528.9 ms.

**Systems insight**: Enforcing the torque-rate limit incurs an execution penalty of 12.6 ms, a modest 2.4 percent increase in motion duration, while eliminating the instantaneous torque step entirely. For the payload alone, torque was not the binding constraint; the published kinematic limits governed the trajectory, though identified link inertia would narrow this torque headroom. A valid time parameterization must incorporate every constraint capable of binding in practice. The theoretical time-optimal profile without a jerk bound is an unexecutable mathematical abstraction that physical drives reject.

In the phase plane the two profiles differ only near their corners ([[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/03 Running the Machine/11 Trajectory Planning/V4-11.05 Kinodynamic Feasibility#^fig-11-phase-plane-swing|figure 5]]). The torque limits alone would permit steeper accelerating and braking parabolas, but the published 7.5 rad/s² acceleration limit contracts both arcs, and the bang-bang path rides them to its 1.94 rad/s switch point, well below the velocity limit. The jerk-limited path rounds the start, the apex, and the finish just inside those arcs. That rounding is where its extra 2.4 percent of duration goes, and it is what removes the 24.8 N·m torque step at the switch point.

The time-optimal bang-bang solution produces precisely the derivative discontinuities that safe seam execution forbids. Every transition between maximum acceleration and maximum braking represents a step change in acceleration, demanding an instantaneous step in electromagnetic torque that actuator drives cannot deliver within finite voltage and current slew limits. Incorporating an explicit jerk bound transforms the problem into a third-order trajectory formulation. As the shoulder swing notebook shows, the time cost of bounding jerk is small next to the torque step it removes. Modern motion pipelines therefore parameterize paths under coupled torque and velocity limits and enforce explicit jerk bounds downstream, or constrain jerk directly during parameterization.

An identical jerk-limited profile, synthesized from the robot’s verified state, resolves both seam blending and safe stopping. An **online trajectory generator** receives the instantaneous position, velocity, and acceleration of each joint together with a target boundary state, returning a time-optimal profile that satisfies per-axis velocity, acceleration, and jerk limits ([[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/03 Running the Machine/11 Trajectory Planning/V4-11.13 Source References#^ref-kroger2010online|Kröger and Wahl 2010]]). For example, the Ruckig algorithm ([[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/03 Running the Machine/11 Trajectory Planning/V4-11.13 Source References#^ref-berscheid2021jerk|Berscheid and Kröger 2021]]) evaluates arbitrary initial and target states across seven distinct constant-jerk phases, calculating profile durations analytically for most trajectory classes and using a bounded polynomial root solver for asymmetric edge cases. In published benchmarks, Ruckig achieved an average execution latency of 19.8 μs and a worst-case latency of 123 μs for 7 axes on a desktop processor, making it fast enough to re-solve within a 1 kHz control cycle. This formulation differs structurally from the fixed-duration quintic bridge in [[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/03 Running the Machine/11 Trajectory Planning/V4-11.05 Kinodynamic Feasibility#^sec-planning-quintic-splines|section 11.4.4]]. The quintic approach fixes blending duration ${T_{\text{blend}}}$ upfront and risks exceeding joint limits during aggressive maneuvers, so downstream extrema checks must reject invalid solutions. An online trajectory generator instead solves for minimum duration as an optimization variable, which guarantees that kinematic derivative bounds hold by construction. The S-curve stopping trajectory examined in [[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/03 Running the Machine/11 Trajectory Planning/V4-11.06 Behavior at the Seam#^sec-planning-c2-stopping-suffix|section 11.5.2]] represents this exact profile structure evaluated toward a zero-velocity terminal state.

Kinematic feasibility by construction, however, does not grant permission to move. An online generator guarantees only the mathematical bounds explicitly configured in its parameters. Single-axis velocity, acceleration, and jerk bounds cannot account for multi-joint dynamic coupling in [[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/03 Running the Machine/11 Trajectory Planning/V4-11.05 Kinodynamic Feasibility#^eq-plan-rigidbody-dynamics|equation 8]], spatial swept-volume collisions, or stale perception data. Furthermore, configuration errors or numerical solver faults can directly compromise the guarantees the generator claims to provide. Real-time benchmarks reported on high-performance desktop CPUs also do not represent execution latency on a constrained safety MCU. Online generators produce higher-quality proposals that satisfy kinematic constraints by design, but the deterministic safety controller must independently validate dynamic feasibility, collision margins, and temporal deadlines before granting execution permission. Even a candidate trajectory that passes every internal dynamic and seam continuity check remains unsafe without an admitted contingency. The system must therefore continuously maintain a certified stopping suffix to handle replacement chunks that fail to arrive before the admission deadline.
