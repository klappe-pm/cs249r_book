# V4-11.14 Footnotes

[[Home|Home]] · [[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/Volume 4 Overview|Volume Overview]] · [[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/03 Running the Machine/11 Trajectory Planning/V4-11 Trajectory Planning|V4-11 Trajectory Planning]]

^footnotes

<sup>1</sup> **Action chunking representations**: ACT and Diffusion Policy, the 2023-era chunk policies, carry no boundary-derivative constraints. Real-time chunking ([[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/03 Running the Machine/11 Trajectory Planning/V4-11.13 Source References#^ref-black2025realtime|Black et al. 2025]]) conditions each new chunk on the actions already committed, which makes a consistent join more likely without guaranteeing one, so the planner still proposes a transition bridge across each replanning seam and the permission path checks it before admission.

^fn1

<sup>2</sup> **${C}^2$ continuity at the motor**: In permanent-magnet synchronous motors, electromagnetic torque couples directly to quadrature stator current (${\tau_{\text{motor}}} = {K_t} {i_q}$). Under this model an acceleration step requires a finite step in torque and current, while a velocity step implies an ideal acceleration impulse, which no drive can supply.

^fn2

<sup>3</sup> **Configuration space**: Lozano-Pérez ([[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/03 Running the Machine/11 Trajectory Planning/V4-11.13 Source References#^ref-lozanoperez1983spatial|1983]]) shrank the moving body to a point by growing each obstacle by the body’s shape, and in the same year Schwartz and Sharir ([[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/03 Running the Machine/11 Trajectory Planning/V4-11.13 Source References#^ref-schwartz1983piano|1983]]) treated the “piano movers’ problem” as a search of exactly this space. The reduction turns a collision query into a membership test on ${\mathcal{C}_{\text{obs}}}$, but the space gains a dimension with every joint, which is why collision queries dominate the run time of the sampling planners in [[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/03 Running the Machine/11 Trajectory Planning/V4-11.07 Choosing a Trajectory Generator#^sec-planning-paradigms|section 11.6]].

^fn3

<sup>4</sup> **Manipulator Jacobian**: It is the same object automatic differentiation builds, the matrix of first derivatives of an output with respect to its inputs, here tool position with respect to joint angles. Reverse-mode autograd never forms the matrix; it computes products ${\mathbf{J}}^\top{\mathbf{v}}$, and the manipulator equation uses that same transpose product to map a tool wrench into joint torques ([[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/03 Running the Machine/11 Trajectory Planning/V4-11.05 Kinodynamic Feasibility#^eq-plan-rigidbody-dynamics|equation 8]]). A planner is the unusual consumer that must *invert* ${\mathbf{J}}$, and it is the inverse, not the Jacobian, that fails at a singularity.

^fn4

<sup>5</sup> **Tail at scale**: Dean and Barroso ([[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/03 Running the Machine/11 Trajectory Planning/V4-11.13 Source References#^ref-dean2013tail|2013]]) showed that a distributed request fanned out across many servers is bottlenecked by the slowest tail response, transforming rare single-server delays into frequent user-facing latencies. A continuous mission comprising thousands of sequential handoffs maps this same fan-out onto the time domain. Because every individual handoff must meet its admission deadline to avoid triggering a stop, the complete mission inherits the worst-case tail latency, rendering conventional per-handoff p99 benchmarks insufficient for assessing end-to-end mission reliability.

^fn5

<sup>6</sup> **Smith predictor**: Smith ([[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/03 Running the Machine/11 Trajectory Planning/V4-11.13 Source References#^ref-smith1957closer|1957]]) handled known dead time in feedback control loops by projecting a plant model forward across the delay interval rather than closing feedback against delayed measurements. Future-state conditioning adapts this idea to trajectory planning by anchoring prospective trajectories to the reference state expected at the handoff seam. It also inherits the weakness of any dead-time predictor: external disturbances during the delay interval go uncompensated until physical feedback arrives, which is why the MCU must check measured joint states against strict error bounds at activation.

^fn6

<sup>7</sup> **Strain-wave (harmonic) gearing**: In the mechanism Musser ([[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/03 Running the Machine/11 Trajectory Planning/V4-11.13 Source References#^ref-musser1959strain|1959]]) patented, an elliptical wave generator flexes a thin toothed cup, the flexspline, so that it meshes with a rigid ring carrying two more teeth, and each turn of the generator advances the cup by only that difference. The result is a high reduction in one compact stage with almost no backlash. The flexspline is also the joint’s main compliance, so the same gearing that multiplies rotor inertia by ${N_{\text{gear}}}^2$ supplies the compliant coupling of the two-inertia model below.

^fn7

<sup>8</sup> **Minimum jerk**: Flash and Hogan ([[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/03 Running the Machine/11 Trajectory Planning/V4-11.13 Source References#^ref-flash1985coordination|1985]]) found that unconstrained human reaching follows the trajectory that minimizes integrated squared jerk, which for a rest-to-rest reach is a quintic polynomial in time. The jerk term of [[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/03 Running the Machine/11 Trajectory Planning/V4-11.07 Choosing a Trajectory Generator#^eq-plan-kinodynamic-projection|equation 20]] inherits that smoothness but not a bound, so a minimum-jerk bridge can still exceed the drive’s limits, and its extrema are checked through [[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/03 Running the Machine/11 Trajectory Planning/V4-11.05 Kinodynamic Feasibility#^eq-plan-quintic-jerk|equation 13]].

^fn8

<sup>9</sup> **Spline** (a draftsman’s bending strip): The word named the thin flexible strip that shipwrights and draftsmen bent through fixed points, held in place by lead weights, to draw a fair curve. Schoenberg ([[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/03 Running the Machine/11 Trajectory Planning/V4-11.13 Source References#^ref-schoenberg1946contributions|1946]]) borrowed it for piecewise polynomials, and Boor ([[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/03 Running the Machine/11 Trajectory Planning/V4-11.13 Source References#^ref-deboor1978practical|1978]]) made B-splines practical to compute with a stable recursion. The convex-hull property that construction supplies is what lets a checker bound a whole segment’s derivatives from a few control-point differences.

^fn9

<sup>10</sup> **Inevitable collision states and viability**: Within the free states, the complement of the ICS set is what Aubin ([[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/03 Running the Machine/11 Trajectory Planning/V4-11.13 Source References#^ref-aubin1991viability|1991]]) calls the viability kernel, the states from which some admissible control keeps the system inside its constraints forever. Planning must therefore keep the commitment state inside that kernel, not merely outside the obstacle, and against static obstacles a verified stop to rest is the witness that it is there.

^fn10

<sup>11</sup> **Model predictive control**: MPC began in the process industries of the 1970s, in refineries and chemical plants whose slow dynamics left seconds to minutes for each solve; Qin and Badgwell ([[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/03 Running the Machine/11 Trajectory Planning/V4-11.13 Source References#^ref-qin2003survey|2003]]) trace its industrial lineage from the first IDCOM and dynamic matrix control installations. Moving the same receding-horizon loop onto an arm tracked at 1 kHz shrinks that time to milliseconds, which is why a solver’s deadline, and not its optimum, decides what reaches admission.

^fn11

<sup>12</sup> **Direct collocation defects in real-time solvers**: Direct collocation, introduced for trajectory optimization by Hargraves and Paris ([[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/03 Running the Machine/11 Trajectory Planning/V4-11.13 Source References#^ref-hargraves1987direct|1987]]), turns a continuous-time control problem into a finite nonlinear program by requiring the defect ${\Delta}_k = {\dot{\mathbf{x}}}({t}_k) - {\mathbf{f}}({\mathbf{x}}_k, {\mathbf{u}}_k) = {\mathbf{0}}$ at each collocation point. Contact transitions and unmodeled friction make the problem non-smooth, and the solver then loses its fast local convergence and needs more iterations than a fixed control deadline may allow.

^fn12

<sup>13</sup> **Holonomic** (Greek *holos*, whole, and *nomos*, law): Hertz ([[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/03 Running the Machine/11 Trajectory Planning/V4-11.13 Source References#^ref-hertz1894prinzipien|1894]]) introduced the term for systems whose constraints obey integral laws, relations among positions alone, in contrast to material systems in general, which obey only differential laws. A wheel’s no-slip rule is a differential law that cannot be integrated into such a relation. The distinction decides the planner’s search space, since a holonomic arm can be planned as a path through configuration space while the base must be planned through its controls.

^fn13

<sup>14</sup> **Drive and resonance response**: A sharp command can excite a joint’s *identified* flexible modes, but their frequencies and damping depend on the transmission and load. Current limiting, desaturation detection, DC-bus response, and shutdown depend on the selected driver and its configuration; one vendor’s desaturation-protection design note shows how such trip thresholds are set ([[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/03 Running the Machine/11 Trajectory Planning/V4-11.13 Source References#^ref-chen2025tpsi3133|Chen and Ramachandran 2025]]).

^fn14
