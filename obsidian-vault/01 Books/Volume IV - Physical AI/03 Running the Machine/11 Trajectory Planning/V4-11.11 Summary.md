# V4-11.11 Summary

[[Home|Home]] · [[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/Volume 4 Overview|Volume Overview]] · [[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/03 Running the Machine/11 Trajectory Planning/V4-11 Trajectory Planning|V4-11 Trajectory Planning]]

^sec-planning-summary

A trajectory transforms a high-level goal into timed physical motion. On an active machine, it must resolve three questions that a geometric path leaves unanswered: how long the motion remains valid and executable, how it splices smoothly into the motion already under way, and how the machine stops safely if no replacement arrives. A multi-step task such as the reach for a mug progresses through a continuous sequence of renewed, validated trajectory prefixes without forcing the entire task into a single observation window. Predictive models and planners generate candidate trajectories, but the runtime system grants execution authority only after verifying fresh sensor evidence, admission deadlines, and the machine’s true state. When a replacement trajectory arrives late, the controller falls back to its resident stopping maneuver. Tail latency bounds (${Q_{1-\epsilon}}({L})$) therefore quantify the expected frequency of service degradation to this safe fallback, while the machine’s physical safety rests on the stopping profile itself.

Whether a physical system can track a candidate trajectory is distinct from whether that trajectory avoids obstacles. Kinematic feasibility alone can fail in subtle ways. Near kinematic singularities, the manipulator Jacobian maps modest Cartesian end-effector velocities into unbounded joint rates. Damped least-squares inversion caps these joint steps, but does so by accepting tracking error that can still push joints against their limits. Dynamic feasibility imposes even stricter constraints. The rigid-body manipulator equations translate planned motions into actuator torques, where reflected rotor inertia amplifies any velocity or acceleration discontinuity across trajectory seams into severe torque spikes. A quintic bridge matches position, velocity, and acceleration across the boundary, eliminating these impulsive torque requests. Similarly, time-optimal path parameterization drives actuators along their torque envelopes, but bang-bang acceleration profiles demand instantaneous torque steps that physical drives cannot achieve. Enforcing an explicit jerk bound rounds these transitions at minimal time cost, turning theoretical path timings into executable motor commands. Whether candidates originate from sampling-based search, trajectory optimization, or learned action chunking, the underlying physical obligations remain unchanged. Each planning method generates a prospective motion proposal, but never a license to execute without validation.

Real-time execution relies on strict timing contracts between the planner and the low-level controller. A 60 ms lease halts a stalled proposal stream using a state-matched stopping response, reserving an illustrative 80.5 mm safety buffer to absorb sensing delay and model uncertainty. In an alternate longer-lease configuration, replacement admission closes strictly by 278 ms so that physical deceleration can initiate no later than 300 ms. Both regimes enforce the identical systems invariant: every active trajectory must reserve sufficient travel to accommodate perception-action latency and the entire stopping maneuver. The base faces the same arithmetic. Its jerk-limited S-curve, the shortest stop under the stipulated load bounds, raises the partial stopping budget at the drive limit to 1,123.84 mm (row 6 of **[[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/03 Running the Machine/12 Safety Enforcement/V4-12.04 Stopping Envelopes#^tbl-enforcement-stopping-budget|The warehouse mobile manipulator's stopping budget]]**). Because this exceeds the available 1.10 m rack-end clearance, the runtime controller must proactively restrict operating speed. **Key Takeaways: Timed motion and independent stopping**

- **Prediction is not execution authority**: A long predicted path can guide motion, but the controller executes only short, validated prefixes. While renewal cadence dictates how frequently new plans are requested, target evidence retains its own capture-based timestamp, and its evidence horizon bounds how long it stays valid.
- **Reserve a stopping response from the current state**: A stopping suffix is mathematically valid only for its declared entry state. If an execution lease or sensor observation expires, the machine must possess a pre-validated fallback trajectory, sufficient reserve energy, and enough physical clearance to absorb both response latency and full deceleration.
- **Admission precedes physical commitment**: The alternate arm schedule closes replacement admission strictly by 278 ms to ensure a physical stop can initiate at 300 ms. A late planner proposal cannot spend or delay this reserved response window.
- **A collision-free path is not inherently executable**: Near singularities, Cartesian end-effector speeds demand infeasible joint velocities. Damped least squares bounds the inverse-kinematics step only by introducing tracking error. Similarly, time-optimal path timing produces discontinuous torque demands that trigger actuator saturation. Adding quintic seams and jerk bounds smooths transitions, but interior joint limits, inverter voltage headroom, and clearance remain binding constraints.
- **Trajectory smoothness dictates stopping distance**: Finite jerk bounds make smooth stopping profiles significantly longer than ideal constant-deceleration braking. An S-curve profile pays the least, the equivalent of cruising for half of one jerk ramp, yet that overhead still sets operating speed limits near rack ends and obstacles.
- **Candidate rejection does not require immediate stopping**: The running trajectory continues executing normally as long as its state, sensor evidence, lease deadline, and fallback trajectory remain valid. Rejecting an invalid replacement proposal simply leaves those original expiration boundaries unchanged.
- **Admission and runtime enforcement require separate system budgets**: Evaluating a candidate plan requires an asynchronous, bounded scan over the entire trajectory chunk, whereas per-tick enforcement performs strict real-time checks against current state and local sensor data. System designers must isolate their CPU time, memory footprints, and bus interference to prevent high-level planning from disrupting the low-level control loop.

^callout-takeaways--11-2-f4e02963

A plan stays valid only while the stop it carries can still be executed. When a timing, seam, or clearance check fails, the remedy is to shorten the lease, slow the motion, or lengthen the bridge until every check passes with the resident stop still inside the clearance, never to wait for a better proposal. The stall runway shows which term to work on. If the travel before stop onset rivals the stop itself, the lease is the term to shorten. If the stop dominates, the deceleration or jerk bound the load tolerates is the term to measure.

For motion, the third law, proposal is not permission (principle [[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/03 Running the Machine/V4 Running the Machine#^pri-vol4-proposal-permission|3]]), reduces to a clearance test. However smooth, collision-free, and timely a candidate is, the MCU admits it only with an independently executable stop that fits the clearance that remains, and that test does not depend on how the candidate was generated. Admission is not a waiver either. The conditions certified at admission remain obligations on every control tick, so the machine must keep checking live state against the clearance it has left.
