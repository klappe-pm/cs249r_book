# V4-11.12 Historical Perspective and Further Reading

[[Home|Home]] · [[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/Volume 4 Overview|Volume Overview]] · [[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/03 Running the Machine/11 Trajectory Planning/V4-11 Trajectory Planning|V4-11 Trajectory Planning]]

^sec-planning-history

Motion planning became a concrete computational discipline when Lozano-Pérez ([[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/03 Running the Machine/11 Trajectory Planning/V4-11.13 Source References#^ref-lozanoperez1983spatial|1983]]) mapped geometric collision avoidance into configuration-space search, shrinking the geometry of a complex moving body to a single point. In the same year, Schwartz and Sharir ([[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/03 Running the Machine/11 Trajectory Planning/V4-11.13 Source References#^ref-schwartz1983piano|1983]]) formulated the piano movers’ problem through exact algebraic decomposition. While theoretically complete, exact algebraic methods scale doubly exponentially with degrees of freedom, rendering them intractable for multi-joint manipulators. In the 1990s, randomized sampling bypassed this geometric bottleneck by trading exact completeness for probabilistic guarantees. Probabilistic roadmaps ([[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/03 Running the Machine/11 Trajectory Planning/V4-11.13 Source References#^ref-kavraki1996probabilistic|Kavraki et al. 1996]]) introduced multi-query graph construction across free configuration space, while rapidly-exploring random trees ([[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/03 Running the Machine/11 Trajectory Planning/V4-11.13 Source References#^ref-lavalle1998rapidly|LaValle 1998]]) solved single-query kinodynamic problems by incrementally biasing search trees toward unexplored regions. Later, RRT\* ([[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/03 Running the Machine/11 Trajectory Planning/V4-11.13 Source References#^ref-karaman2011sampling|Karaman and Frazzoli 2011]]) proved that standard random trees almost surely fail to find optimal paths, introducing local edge rewiring to guarantee asymptotic convergence toward the optimum. Together, these foundational algorithms resolve where a robot may safely pass, which represents the geometric half of motion planning.

The second half, timing and dynamics, followed an independent line of work. Bobrow et al. ([[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/03 Running the Machine/11 Trajectory Planning/V4-11.13 Source References#^ref-bobrow1985time|1985]]) showed that the fastest traversal along a pre-specified path under actuator torque limits follows a bang-bang structure in the phase plane, switching between maximum acceleration and maximum deceleration. Decades later, TOPP-RA ([[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/03 Running the Machine/11 Trajectory Planning/V4-11.13 Source References#^ref-pham2018new|Pham and Pham 2018]]) reformulated time-optimal path parameterization through reachability analysis, casting the search into a sequence of linear programs that execute in time linear in the number of path waypoints. Pure time-optimal trajectories command discontinuous accelerations, however, and the resulting infinite jerk excites mechanical resonances that degrade physical drives. To produce smoother execution, roboticists drew inspiration from biology: Flash and Hogan ([[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/03 Running the Machine/11 Trajectory Planning/V4-11.13 Source References#^ref-flash1985coordination|1985]]) showed that human reaching movements naturally minimize jerk. In robotic manipulation, Kröger and Wahl ([[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/03 Running the Machine/11 Trajectory Planning/V4-11.13 Source References#^ref-kroger2010online|2010]]) demonstrated that complete, jerk-limited trajectory profiles could be synthesized deterministically from an arbitrary initial state within a single millisecond-scale control cycle. Ruckig ([[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/03 Running the Machine/11 Trajectory Planning/V4-11.13 Source References#^ref-berscheid2021jerk|Berscheid and Kröger 2021]]) generalized this formulation to arbitrary target kinematic states and asymmetric limits, establishing the fast, closed-form generator that produces this chapter’s stopping profiles. Trajectory optimization reached robotics along a parallel route. Direct collocation ([[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/03 Running the Machine/11 Trajectory Planning/V4-11.13 Source References#^ref-hargraves1987direct|Hargraves and Paris 1987]]) originated in aerospace trajectory design to transcribe continuous optimal control into sparse nonlinear programs. Receding-horizon control matured in the process industries ([[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/03 Running the Machine/11 Trajectory Planning/V4-11.13 Source References#^ref-qin2003survey|Qin and Badgwell 2003]]), where slow chemical plant dynamics afforded ample time for numerical convergence. Later, algorithms like CHOMP ([[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/03 Running the Machine/11 Trajectory Planning/V4-11.13 Source References#^ref-ratliff2009chomp|Ratliff et al. 2009]]) brought functional gradient optimization directly to robotic arms, optimizing trajectory smoothness and obstacle clearance simultaneously.

The commitment problem, deciding when a planned motion becomes irrevocable and keeping execution safe after that point, is the most recent to mature. Because physical systems possess inertia, safety cannot be judged solely by examining whether the current state is collision-free. Fraichard and Asama ([[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/03 Running the Machine/11 Trajectory Planning/V4-11.13 Source References#^ref-fraichard2004inevitable|2004]]) formalized inevitable collision states, establishing that a planner must verify that a system never enters a state from which every future control trajectory culminates in a crash. This formulation gave a robotic form to the viability kernels that Aubin ([[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/03 Running the Machine/11 Trajectory Planning/V4-11.13 Source References#^ref-aubin1991viability|1991]]) had introduced in control theory. Modern learning-based methods have transformed how candidate motions are generated. Action chunking ([[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/03 Running the Machine/11 Trajectory Planning/V4-11.13 Source References#^ref-zhao2023learning|Zhao et al. 2023]]) and diffusion policies ([[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/03 Running the Machine/11 Trajectory Planning/V4-11.13 Source References#^ref-chi2024diffusionpolicy|Chi et al. 2025]]) predict multi-step action trajectories directly from visual observations, capturing dexterous behaviors without explicit geometric reconstruction. To bridge the latency gap between neural network inference and high-frequency actuation, real-time chunking ([[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/03 Running the Machine/11 Trajectory Planning/V4-11.13 Source References#^ref-black2025realtime|Black et al. 2025]]) conditions incoming predictions on the action sequence already committed to the hardware controller. Regardless of how candidate motions are generated, the execution runtime must enforce physical safety. The trajectory record binds the admitted trajectory to its initial seam state, enforces hard commitment deadlines, and guarantees a deterministically executable stopping suffix whenever the policy stalls.

For further reading, six works trace the lineage from classical planning to modern learned proposals:

- Qin and Badgwell ([[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/03 Running the Machine/11 Trajectory Planning/V4-11.13 Source References#^ref-qin2003survey|2003]]) provides a comprehensive survey of industrial model predictive control, charting its progression from early petrochemical applications to commercial implementations and illustrating how receding-horizon optimization operates on physical systems whose long time constants tolerate multi-second solve times.
- Fraichard and Asama ([[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/03 Running the Machine/11 Trajectory Planning/V4-11.13 Source References#^ref-fraichard2004inevitable|2004]]) formalizes inevitable collision states and defines the dynamic conditions required for provable motion safety under actuator limits, establishing the theoretical foundation for the commitment deadlines analyzed in [[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/03 Running the Machine/11 Trajectory Planning/V4-11.06 Behavior at the Seam#^sec-planning-inevitable-collision-states|section 11.5.3]].
- Biagiotti and Melchiorri ([[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/03 Running the Machine/11 Trajectory Planning/V4-11.13 Source References#^ref-biagiotti2008trajectory|2008]]) provides an exhaustive reference on trajectory generation profiles across robotics and industrial automation, detailing the analytical formulation of polynomial splines, filter-based smoothers, and the double-S profile used for the ${C}^2$ stopping suffix in [[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/03 Running the Machine/11 Trajectory Planning/V4-11.06 Behavior at the Seam#^sec-planning-c2-stopping-suffix|section 11.5.2]].
- Kröger and Wahl ([[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/03 Running the Machine/11 Trajectory Planning/V4-11.13 Source References#^ref-kroger2010online|2010]]) develops the mathematical framework for online trajectory generation, deriving algorithms that compute kinematically feasible, jerk-limited motion profiles from arbitrary initial states within single-cycle real-time deadlines.
- Lynch and Park ([[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/03 Running the Machine/11 Trajectory Planning/V4-11.13 Source References#^ref-lynch2017modern|2017]]) offers a unified, geometrically rigorous treatment of configuration space, kinematics, rigid-body dynamics, time scaling, and motion planning, serving as a comprehensive companion to **[[05 Reference/Volume IV - Physical AI/V4 Control and Dynamics#^sec-appendix-control-kinematics|Forward kinematics and singularities]]**.
- Chi et al. ([[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/03 Running the Machine/11 Trajectory Planning/V4-11.13 Source References#^ref-chi2024diffusionpolicy|2025]]) details diffusion policies for robotic manipulation, examining receding-horizon execution of predicted action sequences and presenting the modern learned proposers whose seams, deadlines, and fallback stopping profiles this chapter evaluates.

**What’s Next: From admitted plans to per-tick permission**

Admission judges a trajectory against the state the planner predicted, but the machine executes it against the state it actually reaches. Between the two, the body lags its reference, a person can step into the aisle, and an actuator can run short of torque, and the stopping budget assembled here has not yet charged the first of these. On the base the gap is already decisive, because the jerk-limited stop carries the partial budget to 1,123.84 mm against 1.10 m of rack-end clearance at the drive limit before the body’s departure from its reference is priced. **[[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/03 Running the Machine/12 Safety Enforcement/V4-12 Safety Enforcement#^sec-enforcement|Safety Enforcement]]** begins with the per-tick gatekeeper, the permission-path check that runs on every control tick against live state. It prices tracking error as an inset on the stopping distance and derives a candidate aisle speed whose timing and braking premises still require qualification. When a check fails, it selects among fallback responses that include the resident stopping suffix latched at admission.
