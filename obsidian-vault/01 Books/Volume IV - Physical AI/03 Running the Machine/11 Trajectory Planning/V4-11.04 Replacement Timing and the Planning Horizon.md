# V4-11.04 Replacement Timing and the Planning Horizon

[[Home|Home]] · [[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/Volume 4 Overview|Volume Overview]] · [[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/03 Running the Machine/11 Trajectory Planning/V4-11 Trajectory Planning|V4-11 Trajectory Planning]]

^sec-planning-goal-to-trajectory

On the mug reach, the arm needs an admitted replacement every 50 ms and loses tracking authority 60 ms after the last one. Whether a replacement can arrive that often depends on how long it takes to produce one, and producing one is more than a single inference pass. The observation behind the candidate must be captured, the proposer must run, and the candidate must cross to the safety MCU and pass validation before it is admitted. Every generator, from numerical trajectory optimization to an autoregressive policy, pays for that pipeline. The timing questions therefore come before the choice of generator: how long a replacement takes, how deep into its latency tail the design must look, how much motion one record must cover, and from which state the next candidate must be planned.

### Action Chunk Horizons and Reaction Lag

^sec-planning-horizon-tradeoff

If a replacement chunk takes longer from camera capture to MCU admission than the active chunk’s lease allows, the arm drops into its stopping fallback before the new plan arrives. If the executed prefix is stretched to hide that latency, the arm reacts too slowly when the mug or an obstacle shifts. Sizing the prediction horizon ${H}$ therefore starts from an end-to-end latency budget for replacement, one that spans host accelerator memory, the inter-processor link, and real-time microcontroller firmware.

The scheduling metric that governs every handoff is **replacement latency** ${L}$: the total elapsed wall-clock time from the initial physical capture of the sensor observation on which a candidate trajectory proposal rests to the formal admission of that proposal into the real-time execution queue. Formulated in the event taxonomy of **[[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/01 Machine Anatomy/04 The Nervous System/V4-04.05 Chunk Lease Sizing from the Stopping Budget#^tbl-04-event-timing|The capture-to-brake-onset timing budget of the running machine]]**, ${L}$ spans the interval from capture to admission. It is the direct sum of two intervals. The first is the capture-to-dispatch perception age ${t_{\text{age}}}$ established in **[[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/03 Running the Machine/08 Sensor Perception/V4-08.05 Bounding Observation Age#^sec-perception-latency-waterfall|The latency waterfall]]**. The second is the dispatch-to-admission interval ${\Delta_{\text{da}}}$, a design allocation whose stages are listed next and which **[[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/03 Running the Machine/13 Workload Placement/V4-13.07 Silicon Isolation Decisions#^sec-placement-dispatch-admission|The board-link budget and the dispatch-to-admission interval]]** later tests against the hardware:

$$
{L} = \underbrace{{t_{\text{age}}}}_{\text{capture}\,\to\,\text{dispatch}} + \underbrace{{\Delta_{\text{da}}}}_{\text{dispatch}\,\to\,\text{admission}}, \qquad {\Delta_{\text{da}}} = {t_{\text{infer}}} + {t_{\text{link}}} + {t_{\text{deser}}} + {t_{\text{validate}}} + {t_{\text{activate}}} + {t_{\text{wait}}} \tag{3}
$$

 On the physical machine, sensor data is already 51.6 ms old when planning dispatches. The subsequent dispatch-to-admission allocation ${\Delta_{\text{da}}}$ of 63.3 ms accounts for the chunk policy’s full 40 ms neural network forward pass and 18 ms of whole-plan kinematic validation. The balance is a few milliseconds of inter-board link transport, message deserialization, activation staging, and synchronization wait until the next discrete permission tick. **[[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/03 Running the Machine/13 Workload Placement/V4-13.07 Silicon Isolation Decisions#^sec-placement-dispatch-admission|The board-link budget and the dispatch-to-admission interval]]** itemizes this balance when it tests the allocation on the selected hardware. These stages combine to yield an overall replacement latency ${L}$ of 114.9 ms. ${L}$ is a latency, not a period. It exceeds both the 50 ms renewal period and the 60 ms lease, so the renewal cadence can be sustained only by overlapping requests, several in flight at once, a throughput condition the timeline below returns to. Charging the entire neural inference pass after dispatch intentionally bills the visual encoding stage twice, since that stage is already inside ${t_{\text{age}}}$, which makes the budget deliberately conservative. When pipelined stages execute concurrently or contend for shared memory buses, the sum of independent stage percentiles does not equal the percentile of the sum (**[[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/01 Machine Anatomy/02 The Physical Body/V4-02.03 Measurement Freshness#^sec-body-latency-pipeline|The end-to-end sense-to-actuation latency pipeline]]**). Sizing the planning horizon therefore demands evaluating the empirical end-to-end distribution of ${L}$, measured directly between physical sensor capture interrupts and microcontroller admission timestamps.

Trajectory validation represents the motion planner’s dedicated compute contribution within this latency budget. The 18 ms validation allowance covers the combined elapsed contribution of host geometry validation and bounded local MCU admission, including scheduling delays, rather than overhead silently absorbed into the millisecond actuator tick. Its split between processors remains to be qualified. Formal admission requires bounded staging memory, candidate spline and stopping coefficients, and qualified local clearance evidence; the host processor’s full, multi-gigabyte scene representation need not reside on the embedded MCU. The real-time scheduler must preserve deterministic per-tick enforcement while background admission executes, refreshing the seam boundary check immediately prior to commitment. System architects must allocate and qualify this compute and memory footprint under worst-case shared-bus interference. Because each term in [[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/03 Running the Machine/11 Trajectory Planning/V4-11.04 Replacement Timing and the Planning Horizon#^eq-plan-replacement-latency|equation 3]] represents an illustrative design allocation, neither ${L}$ nor its constituent stages serve as unverified static bounds; both intervals remain explicit qualification obligations on the physical hardware.

Prediction length and execution duration are separate architectural parameters. While the prediction horizon ${H}{\Delta} {t}$ specifies how far into the future the sequence proposer forecasts, the **executed prefix** represents only the initial slice dispatched to actuators before an admitted replacement plan supersedes it. Expanding the prediction horizon supplies candidate continuity well past the next planned handoff, but does not grant the robot authority to execute that trajectory unchecked. The production controller targets an admitted renewal every 50 ms, enforcing hard lease expiry at 60 ms following the most recent admission. The evidence horizon of the upstream target observation can be shorter still.

Compute amortization depends strictly on inference dispatch frequency rather than the raw number of future setpoints generated in a forward pass. Executing a longer trajectory prefix lowers invocation frequency and amortizes accelerator workload, yet it simultaneously inflates closed-loop reaction lag, delaying task corrections when the environment drifts. Conversely, predicting farther into the future while maintaining a high renewal rate increases proposer compute without reducing inference duty. Emergency protective stopping remains governed by an independent hardware permission path and its certified reaction latency. Sizing the prediction horizon therefore balances closed-loop reactivity, pipeline renewal throughput, sensor evidence freshness, and fallback transition rates under realistic system loads; no universal optimal horizon emerges from trajectory buffer capacity alone.

↩︎ Action chunking as a supply lever is established in **[[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/01 Machine Anatomy/03 The Cognitive Brain/V4-03.06 Chunk Supply and Evidence Validity#^sec-brain-action-chunking|Action chunking]]**.

### Tail Latency Budgeting Across a Mission

^sec-planning-tail-latency-budgeting

When the resident fallback works as certified, a late replacement costs availability rather than safety. A missed replanning deadline becomes a collision hazard only if the resident stop is kinematically infeasible, corrupted in memory, or delayed beyond its budgeted stopping distance. Over a long mission, however, the number of handoffs makes tail latency decide whether the mission completes,[[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/03 Running the Machine/11 Trajectory Planning/V4-11.14 Footnotes#^fn5|⁵]] even when a stopping profile safely absorbs every individual miss. The analysis below therefore bounds how often handoffs miss their deadlines, not how often the machine collides.

The component breakdown in [[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/03 Running the Machine/11 Trajectory Planning/V4-11.04 Replacement Timing and the Planning Horizon#^eq-plan-replacement-latency|equation 3]] defines the latency of a single replacement cycle. Because an autonomous mission executes this handoff cycle repeatedly, the tail of the latency distribution dictates system behavior. A mission requiring ${K}$ successive plan handoffs fails its continuous execution target if even one handoff misses its deadline. Partitioning a cumulative mission exceedance budget ${\epsilon_{\text{mission}}}$ uniformly across all ${K}$ handoffs yields an upper bound on allowable per-handoff failure probability via the union bound:

$$
{\epsilon} \le \frac{{\epsilon_{\text{mission}}}}{{K}} \tag{4}
$$

^eq-plan-union-bound

**Napkin Math 11.1: Handoff deadlines across a mission**

**Problem**: A generic replanner, slower than this machine’s chunk renewal, meets a p99 replacement budget on each handoff. Over a ten-minute mission, how likely is it that every handoff arrives on time, and what per-handoff tail must the design target instead?

**Variables**:

- Mission length 600 s and replanning rate 5 Hz (illustrative)
- Per-handoff exceedance of the p99 budget, ${p}=0.01$ (definitional)
- Mission exceedance target ${\epsilon_{\text{mission}}}=1 \times 10^{-3}$ (chosen)
- Independent misses (assumption)

**Math**:

1.  **Handoff count**: 600 s $\times$ 5 Hz gives ${K}=3000$, and ${K}{p}=3000\times0.01=30$ handoffs are expected late.
2.  **Every handoff on time**: $(0.99)^{3000}\approx8 \times 10^{-14}$.
3.  **Design tail** ([[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/03 Running the Machine/11 Trajectory Planning/V4-11.04 Replacement Timing and the Planning Horizon#^eq-plan-union-bound|equation 4]]): ${\epsilon}\le{\epsilon_{\text{mission}}}/{K}\approx3.3 \times 10^{-7}$, the $\text{p99.99997}$ quantile, deeper than the p99 budget by $0.01/(3.3 \times 10^{-7})\approx3 \times 10^{4}$.

**Result**: A mission almost never completes with every handoff inside a p99 budget, and the tail a mission target implies lies more than four orders of magnitude deeper.

**Systems insight**: The quantile a design must target is set by how many handoffs a mission makes, not by the per-handoff budget, and it lies beyond what a test campaign of practical size observes. A late handoff must degrade service availability, not safety clearance. Preserving ${d_{\text{stop}}} \le {D_{\text{clear}}}$ relies on the resident stop profile (the pre-validated stopping suffix latched in MCU memory at admission and triggered without the host), not on optimistic assumptions about latency tails.

This per-handoff quantile functions as a **design quantile target**, a risk allocation across the software stack rather than an observed tail latency. In practice, resource contention across shared memory, PCIe buses, and hardware accelerators introduces temporal correlation, clustering deadline misses and violating independent-failure assumptions. Hardware-in-the-loop stress testing under maximum bus and memory saturation can expose deadline misses, but establishing this deep quantile requires a stated statistical model, sufficient observations, and evidence that the operating conditions match. The mission architecture must tolerate fallback frequencies that the available evidence cannot rule out without requiring human intervention.

![[90 Assets/Volume IV/11 Planning/Margin Handoff Exceedance Ladder 6c94a54122.svg]]

*Three bars measured from probability 1 on a log scale of exceedance probability, so a rarer target draws a longer bar: the p99 per-handoff budget at 0.01, the whole-mission target at 0.001, and the per-handoff design target, 0.001 divided across 3000 handoffs, at 3.3 times 10 to the minus 7, about four and a half orders of magnitude below p99.*

*Spread over 3000 handoffs, a mission target demands a $\text{p99.99997}$ deadline, far past p99.*

To maintain continuous tracking, the client dispatches the next planning request at an offset no later than ${T_{\text{request}}}$ after the active chunk begins executing. Plan admission must complete before a hard decision cutoff that precedes physical commitment ${t_{\text{commit}}}$ by the actuator response allowance, rather than running up against the final nominal setpoint. Given a replacement-latency quantile target ${Q_{1-\epsilon}}({L})$ and a safety reserve ${T_{\text{reserve}}}$, a sufficient conservative timing requirement is ${t_{\text{commit}}}-{t_{\text{start}}}\ge {T_{\text{request}}}+{Q_{1-\epsilon}}({L})+{T_{\text{reserve}}}$. This requirement charges the complete capture-to-admission quantile after request dispatch, conservatively charging the preceding capture-to-dispatch age again. That extra age charge is separate from the visual-encoding overlap in [[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/03 Running the Machine/11 Trajectory Planning/V4-11.04 Replacement Timing and the Planning Horizon#^eq-plan-replacement-latency|equation 3]]. A tighter dispatch-relative requirement would use an independently characterized dispatch-to-admission distribution; subtracting marginal latency quantiles would not establish it. The reserve parameter ${T_{\text{reserve}}}$ includes both scheduling slack before preferred admission and the physical decision-to-onset allowance. Furthermore, executing the resident stop requires an additional time window ${T_{\text{stop}}}$ under dedicated MCU authority. Consequently, the time horizon ${T_{\text{record}}}$ that an admitted trajectory record must cover, whether stored as dense setpoint arrays or polynomial splines, satisfies the corresponding conservative design requirement:

$$
{T_{\text{record}}}\ge {T_{\text{request}}}+{Q_{1-\epsilon}}({L})+{T_{\text{reserve}}}+{T_{\text{stop}}} \tag{5}
$$

^eq-plan-action-chunk-horizon

In the baseline warehouse configuration, the chunk policy outputs a discrete setpoint every 25 ms across a 16-step horizon, producing 400 ms of nominal forward trajectory. Under nominal operation, an admitted replacement supersedes this trajectory after 50 ms. If renewal fails, the active lease lapses at 60 ms past the preceding admission tick. Unexecuted setpoints beyond that cutoff serve as candidate references for continuous handoffs rather than extended tracking authority.

On this running schedule the second tier, the record’s commitment deadline, never acts, because a stalled proposer lets the 60 ms chunk lease lapse long before commitment arrives. To show how a record’s own deadlines coordinate nominal motion with its stop, the worked timeline therefore uses an **alternate longer-lease schedule** for the same arm, one whose chunk lease is long enough not to lapse before the commitment cutoff. The commitment tier can then be seen on its own. On this schedule the planner issues its request 60 ms after the active chunk begins (later than the running machine’s 50 ms renewal, which only a longer lease permits). It budgets 160 ms as its design-tail replacement latency ${Q_{1-\epsilon}}({L})$ above the 114.9 ms nominal sum, as a chosen stand-in for the deep quantile that [[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/03 Running the Machine/11 Trajectory Planning/V4-11.04 Replacement Timing and the Planning Horizon#^eq-plan-union-bound|equation 4]] implies. It then adds 20 ms of scheduling slack to set a preferred admission deadline at 240 ms. Adding the 22 ms decision-to-onset response allowance to the scheduling slack gives the 42 ms full reserve of [[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/03 Running the Machine/11 Trajectory Planning/V4-11.04 Replacement Timing and the Planning Horizon#^eq-plan-action-chunk-horizon|equation 5]] and a conservative minimum stop onset of 262 ms. Selecting a slightly later physical stopping onset at 300 ms leaves 60 ms between preferred admission and commitment. Replacement admission can therefore close as late as 278 ms (one 22 ms decision-to-onset delay before commitment). The pre-validated 275 ms stopping suffix ([[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/03 Running the Machine/11 Trajectory Planning/V4-11.06 Behavior at the Seam#^sec-planning-c2-stopping-suffix|section 11.5.2]]) then brings the arm to rest at 575 ms, provided clearance holds through that entire window.

This alternate profile serves to contrast buffer structure with the production policy. Its window spans 23 setpoint periods, yet it stays compact in memory because the stop is stored as an analytical profile (jerk limits, acceleration limits, and phase durations) rather than as a dense array of setpoints. Set on one time axis, the two schedules spend clearance differently ([[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/03 Running the Machine/11 Trajectory Planning/V4-11.04 Replacement Timing and the Planning Horizon#^fig-11-seam-timeline-fallback|figure 2]]). On the running lease, the lease lapses at 60 ms, the tick, bus, and onset allowance delays the resident stop to 82 ms, and the arm uses 219.5 mm of the 300 mm clearance, leaving 80.5 mm. On the alternate schedule, admission must close by 278 ms, after the preferred 240 ms, so that the matched stop begins at the 300 ms commitment and reaches rest at 575 ms with 102.5 mm to spare. The nominal setpoints that run on to 400 ms are never executed.

Sustaining the continuous 50 ms renewal cadence of the running lease requires verifying pipeline throughput alongside latency. Because the illustrative 114.9 ms replacement latency exceeds the 50 ms renewal period, the system must pipeline consecutive planning requests. Pipelining maintains real-time throughput only when intermediate hardware stages provide non-blocking queue capacity and bounded bus arbitration delay; pipelined concurrency cannot be established from latency sums alone. Until comprehensive load testing validates multi-request pipelining under peak bus contention, the specified resident fallback responds to missed renewal only under its stated state, clearance, sensing, energy, drive-response, deceleration, and jerk assumptions.

Both lease architectures remain bounded by the validity lifespan of upstream perceptual evidence. Even when employing a qualified local object tracker, the tracked evidence horizon derived in **[[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/03 Running the Machine/10 Grounded Intent/V4-10.06 Expiring Goals and Temporal Validity Leases#^sec-intent-goals-expire|Expiring Goals and Temporal Validity Leases]]** is shorter than the alternate commitment duration, so a single vision observation of a target object cannot justify that entire duration. Instead, the manipulator uses a staged perceptual approach. It follows an admitted prefix through a certified clear corridor, triggers fresh visual acquisition from a wrist-mounted camera at closer proximity, and re-anchors subsequent trajectory segments to updated state estimates. Embedded firmware then admits the terminal grasp maneuver only when the complete motion profile satisfies current target tolerances within an active validity envelope. Although an overall reaching motion may span several observation cycles, every executed trajectory slice requires fresh evidentiary support. Generating a new trajectory proposal cannot refresh stale sensor evidence, and losing perceptual tracking immediately transitions the robot into a state-matched stopping fallback.

On the Push-T manipulation benchmark ([[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/03 Running the Machine/11 Trajectory Planning/V4-11.13 Source References#^ref-chi2024diffusionpolicy|Chi et al. 2025]]), frequent closed-loop replanning lets an articulated planar arm recover from live disturbances as it guides a T-shaped block toward a designated pose. A diffusion policy generates ${H} = 16$ future steps and executes an initial prefix of ${T_a} = 6$ steps before replanning. It recovers dynamically from transient camera occlusions, external block displacements during approach, and perturbations near the terminal pose. However, these empirical trials demonstrate task-level reactive correction rather than certified derivative continuity or deterministic stopping under compute starvation. A safety-critical production system still requires an independently executable stopping suffix verified against known kinematic clearance bounds.

### Conditioning on the State at Splice Time

^sec-planning-future-state-conditioning

While a joint rotates at 0.50 rad/s during an illustrative 80 ms compute and transport delay (somewhat longer than the 63.3 ms dispatch-to-admission allocation), a joint pose sampled when the replacement request was dispatched lags the advancing reference by 0.040 rad (2.29°). Mapped through the arm’s forward kinematics ([[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/03 Running the Machine/11 Trajectory Planning/V4-11.03 The Planning Handoff and the Trajectory Record#^eq-plan-two-link-kinematics|equation 1]]) at its 0.70 m maximum planar reach, that angular lag displaces the tool center point by up to 28 mm. This is more than the entire lateral swept-volume clearance budgeted for a reach past the conveyor guard ([[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/03 Running the Machine/11 Trajectory Planning/V4-11.08 Trajectory Planning Failure Modes and Infeasible Seams#^sec-planning-runtime-monitors|section 11.7.2]]). That clearance is a sideways allowance, distinct from the 300 mm of free path ahead of the tool that a stop consumes. Conditioning a motion plan on sensor feedback captured when inference begins therefore induces the **stale-state paradox**. By the time the generative policy completes its forward pass, the physical arm has already advanced along its prior trajectory. Initializing the new plan from the request-time state then commands the actuators to step backward across the distance traversed during computation, which injects step discontinuities into commanded position, velocity, and acceleration. To eliminate boundary discontinuities, the sequence proposer must instead condition its action generation on the forward-projected reference state[[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/03 Running the Machine/11 Trajectory Planning/V4-11.14 Footnotes#^fn6|⁶]] of the active trajectory at the designated replanning seam ${t_{\text{seam}}}$:

$$
{\Delta} {q_{\text{reference}}}=\int_{t_0}^{{t_{\text{seam}}}}{\dot{q}_A}({t})\,d{t},\qquad {\hat{\mathbf{x}}_{\text{seam}}}={\mathbf{x}_A}({t_{\text{seam}}}) \tag{6}
$$

^eq-plan-execution-delay-gap

The left-hand integral is the joint-space gap that a stale anchor would leave, the 0.040 rad of the example, while the right-hand state is the anchor the proposer uses instead. Because the projected seam state ${\hat{\mathbf{x}}_{\text{seam}}}$ in [[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/03 Running the Machine/11 Trajectory Planning/V4-11.04 Replacement Timing and the Planning Horizon#^eq-plan-execution-delay-gap|equation 6]] exists strictly in reference trajectory space, external contact forces, mechanical compliance, and closed-loop tracking errors invariably displace the physical plant from this nominal point. Rather than presuming an unobservable future measurement, admission verification prior to the seam evaluates a bounded reachable-state envelope, confirming that feasible polynomial bridges and certified stopping profiles exist across all reachable states within that set. At activation, embedded MCU firmware reads fresh optical encoder measurements and state-estimator error bounds to validate the actual boundary discrepancy $({\Delta} {q}, {\Delta}{\dot{q}}, {\Delta}{\ddot{q}})$ against that certified envelope. It then instantiates the bounded fifth-degree ${C}^2$ bridge of [[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/03 Running the Machine/11 Trajectory Planning/V4-11.05 Kinodynamic Feasibility#^sec-planning-quintic-splines|section 11.4.4]] and checks its joint-space derivatives against actuator velocity, torque, and jerk limits within the reserved real-time tick before dispatching motor commands. If measured tracking errors or bridge derivatives exceed admitted bounds, the MCU rejects the replacement and maintains the active plan only while its tracking error, target evidence, lease duration, and stopping clearances satisfy safety invariants; otherwise, it triggers the pre-latched fallback profile. Matching two *reference* profiles guarantees derivative continuity across reference commands, but it never eliminates physical tracking errors in the underlying mechanical hardware.

Recent generative imitation learning architectures incorporate delay compensation directly into their sampling algorithms. Real-time chunking ([[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/03 Running the Machine/11 Trajectory Planning/V4-11.13 Source References#^ref-black2025realtime|Black et al. 2025]]) computes upcoming action sequences concurrently with active chunk execution. Because setpoints scheduled to run during the inference delay window are already committed to the actuator queue, the model fixes those initial steps to their committed values and samples subsequent actions conditioned on this prefix, applying decaying consistency weights across the overlap window. Although empirical evaluations show that control throughput remains stable under artificial latency injection, the formulation provides no formal continuity or stability guarantees. In contrast, bidirectional decoding ([[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/03 Running the Machine/11 Trajectory Planning/V4-11.13 Source References#^ref-liu2024bidirectional|Liu et al. 2024]]) samples a diverse candidate batch of trajectory chunks, scoring and selecting the proposal whose trajectory matches the previously executed actions most smoothly. Conditioning generation on the committed trajectory prefix promotes smooth kinematic transitions and reduces boundary tracking discrepancies, yet it cannot substitute for formal safety verification. Embedded microcontroller logic must still evaluate actual physical states against the admitted safety envelope before committing any new chunk to execution.

**Definition 11.2: Future-state conditioning**

***Future-state conditioning*** is the practice of generating candidate motion plans anchored to the anticipated state at execution time rather than the stale state measured at plan initiation.

1.  **Significance**: Offsets computational and transport latency ${\Delta} {t_{\text{plan}}}$, eliminating boundary step discontinuities at trajectory handoffs without forcing zero-velocity pauses between consecutive chunks.
2.  **Distinction**: Differs from state estimation or Kalman filtering; it is a forward reference-planning technique that propagates an admitted nominal trajectory rather than filtering sensor noise.
3.  **Common pitfall**: Conflating forward-projected reference states with actual physical plant positions. If external contact forces or tracking lag displace the mechanism during planning, activation remains contingent upon the MCU verifying actual encoder feedback and bridge feasibility at the handoff seam.

Trajectory replacement therefore comes down to three sizing decisions. The prediction horizon must be long enough to carry kinematically feasible motion together with a certified stopping profile, the executed prefix short enough to keep closed-loop responses to a changing environment fast, and the renewal cadence one that the pipelined compute can sustain deterministically. Perceptual evidence freshness dictates the maximum lifespan over which any target estimate justifies physical motion. Even when replacement plans arrive strictly within latency budgets, timely delivery alone cannot guarantee that physical joint actuators can track the synthesized trajectory or bridge seamlessly from their measured state.
