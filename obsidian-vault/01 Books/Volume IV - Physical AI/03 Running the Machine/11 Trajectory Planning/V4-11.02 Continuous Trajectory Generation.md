# V4-11.02 Continuous Trajectory Generation

[[Home|Home]] · [[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/Volume 4 Overview|Volume Overview]] · [[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/03 Running the Machine/11 Trajectory Planning/V4-11 Trajectory Planning|V4-11 Trajectory Planning]]

^sec-planning-continuous-trajectories

The arm of the warehouse mobile manipulator is reaching for a mug that drifts along a moving conveyor when an inference pass is delayed by operating-system scheduling contention. The intent lease still names the mug as a valid target, but a lease grants a target, never a path. A target coordinate paired with an expiration timestamp specifies neither velocity nor acceleration, so it cannot tell the arm what to do while the next pass is late. The arm cannot freeze abruptly, and it cannot extrapolate blind setpoints into space no check has covered. Planning must supply a concrete, time-parameterized path, an admissible transition from the motion already under way, and a precomputed stop that runs even if the next inference pass never returns.

↩︎ The intent lease, whose expiry bounds the validity window of every trajectory, is established in **[[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/03 Running the Machine/10 Grounded Intent/V4-10.09 The Intent Lease Specification and Contract#^sec-intent-lease|The Intent Lease Specification and Contract]]**.

Classical robotics split this problem into two stages. A search first finds a collision-free geometric path through a configuration space, and a time parameterization then assigns timing that respects kinematic and torque limits. Learned sensorimotor policies change both stages. Instead of searching a static, pre-mapped scene, a policy emits short chunks of future setpoints conditioned directly on live camera observations, and because it replans while the machine moves, each new trajectory must splice into motion already under way rather than start from rest. Neural inference also has variable, heavy-tailed latency, and the hardware keeps moving along its previous trajectory while it waits. Geometric clearance therefore does not settle a plan. A plan must also establish how long the proposed motion remains executable, how it joins the retiring trajectory, and how the machine comes to a controlled stop if later inference passes stall.

On this machine, the arm’s low-level joint tracking loop evaluates feedback and setpoints at 1 kHz (datasheet; see the Reader Guide); however, this high-rate servo clock does not specify the physical stopping response. Above this loop sits the Brain, where learned policies such as Action Chunking with Transformers (ACT) ([[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/03 Running the Machine/11 Trajectory Planning/V4-11.13 Source References#^ref-zhao2023learning|Zhao et al. 2023]]) or Diffusion Policy ([[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/03 Running the Machine/11 Trajectory Planning/V4-11.13 Source References#^ref-chi2024diffusionpolicy|Chi et al. 2025]]) process sensory observations on the host application processor to synthesize future reference paths.[[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/03 Running the Machine/11 Trajectory Planning/V4-11.14 Footnotes#^fn1|¹]] The machine’s chunk policy operates with an illustrative budget of 40 ms per pass at p99, vision included. Even so, memory contention, direct memory access (DMA) traffic, or thread preemption can stretch tail latency beyond 150 ms. The real-time controller therefore cannot block synchronously on policy execution; it requires both buffered nominal motion and an immediately dispatchable, pre-validated stop.

↩︎ The kinetic energy a trajectory commits, and the stopping distance that energy demands, are established in **[[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/01 Machine Anatomy/02 The Physical Body/V4-02.04 Kinetic Momentum#^sec-body-stopping-distance-prebrake|Kinematic stopping distance and pre-brake delay]]**.

Whether candidate motions originate from sampling-based search, numerical trajectory optimization, or learned action chunking ([[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/03 Running the Machine/11 Trajectory Planning/V4-11.07 Choosing a Trajectory Generator#^sec-planning-paradigms|section 11.6]]), the generator serves strictly as an unprivileged proposer. Time parameterization exposes instantaneous velocity, acceleration, and jerk to dynamic validation, and the independent safety permission path must verify that motor windings can generate the required torques under dynamic load and that sufficient clearance exists along the arm’s swept stopping volume before any motion is admitted.

↪ The runtime gatekeeper that checks every admitted setpoint against live state is developed in **[[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/03 Running the Machine/12 Safety Enforcement/V4-12.02 The Per-Tick Gatekeeper#^sec-enforcement-deterministic-gatekeeper|The Per-Tick Gatekeeper]]**.

A multi-step action chunk delivers a finite horizon of future setpoints that the low-level tracking controller consumes sequentially while the proposer computes the next chunk. Splicing successive chunks introduces an **execution seam**: the temporal and kinematic boundary where the retiring setpoints hand off control to the incoming plan. Matching position alone across this seam leaves a discontinuous velocity step, and matching velocity while neglecting acceleration leaves an instantaneous acceleration step. Because physical links and payloads carry inertia, discontinuous accelerations demand instantaneous steps in motor torque, exciting mechanical vibrations and risking overcurrent faults. The trajectory generator must therefore enforce continuity through position, velocity, and acceleration (the ${C}^2$ continuity condition) while verifying that dynamic torques and swept clearance throughout the transition segment remain within safe physical envelopes.[[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/03 Running the Machine/11 Trajectory Planning/V4-11.14 Footnotes#^fn2|²]]

Decoupling the execution rate from the inference rate allows the low-level tracking controller to service motor loops on a rigid real-time clock while neural inference runs asynchronously. Setpoint buffering absorbs latency jitter, but buffered motion cannot remain valid indefinitely. Sensor observations age, dynamic obstacles shift, and localization drift accumulates over time. Consequently, the planner’s replacement deadline must account for the worst-case inference tail, guaranteeing sufficient lead time to commit to a feasible, controlled stop before either the buffered trajectory drains or the supporting environmental evidence expires.

Every admitted proposal must therefore arrive as a self-contained bundle: the active trajectory segment continuous through acceleration (${C}^2$), its explicit validity deadlines, and a pre-verified stopping suffix whose required deceleration distance fits within verified obstacle clearance (${d_{\text{stop}}} \le {D_{\text{clear}}}$). An isolated safety microcontroller (MCU) inspects and validates this bundle across the proposal boundary before arming actuator outputs, caching the emergency stopping continuation in local real-time memory so it can be autonomously dispatched if communications falter.

The machine meets these obligations in a fixed order. What the planner hands to the tracking controller and the permission path comes first, together with the map from tool coordinates to the joint limits the drives enforce. Replacement timing is then sized against the inference tail, a timed candidate and its seams are checked against what the actuators can follow, and the jerk-limited stop that every admitted plan must carry is sized. The generators that propose candidates, from sampling-based search to learned action chunks, can be compared only after those obligations are fixed.
