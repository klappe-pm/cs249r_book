# V4-11.06 Behavior at the Seam

[[Home|Home]] · [[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/Volume 4 Overview|Volume Overview]] · [[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/03 Running the Machine/11 Trajectory Planning/V4-11 Trajectory Planning|V4-11 Trajectory Planning]]

^sec-planning-behavior-seam

When the replacement does not arrive, because inference stalls on scheduling interference, a bus fault, or an unhandled exception, the arm reaches the end of its authority with no successor in hand. The runtime must then bring it to rest without violating the limits derived above or exhausting its stopping clearance. Three things decide the outcome: which deadline has passed, which stop the MCU runs, and how much clearance that stop consumes.

### Replacement Deadlines and Commitment Cutoffs

^sec-planning-two-tier-deadlines

A single timeout cannot protect both a fast control loop against a hung proposer and a moving arm against overrunning its obstacle clearance. A lease short enough to catch a dropped packet fires long before the geometric point of no return. A cutoff placed at the point of no return leaves a stalled policy coasting blind in the meantime. The two tiers named with the trajectory record in [[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/03 Running the Machine/11 Trajectory Planning/V4-11.03 The Planning Handoff and the Trajectory Record#^sec-planning-handoff|section 11.2]], the periodic chunk lease and the commitment cutoff, therefore answer different questions: whether a replacement has renewed tracking authority recently enough, and whether the reserved stop can still begin in time. The planner also uses a soft *blend deadline* (${t_{\text{blend}}}$), a scheduling target for a smooth replacement. Missing that target need not end tracking if the current lease and evidence remain valid and a checked replacement can still arrive before the admission cutoff that precedes physical commitment (${t_{\text{commit}}}$). The earlier admission cutoff leaves the response interval needed for the matched stopping suffix to begin at commitment, before its reserved clearance is spent.

The two deadlines call for two different stops:

- **Matched stop suffix (planned termination at ${t} = {t_{\text{commit}}}$):** A pre-computed, jerk-limited ${C}^2$ slowdown along the admitted path, anchored specifically at the nominal state expected at ${t_{\text{commit}}}$, checked at admission to satisfy ${d_{\text{stop}}} \le {D_{\text{clear}}}$. If no successor is admitted before the earlier admission cutoff, the controller dispatches the stop early enough that the body smoothly transfers to this pre-latched suffix at commitment, decelerating to rest without requiring runtime replanning.
- **State-matched fallback (reactive termination at ${t} < {t_{\text{commit}}}$):** If the high-rate chunk lease lapses, the parent intent expires, or a runtime safety monitor detects an unmodeled obstacle or torque fault *before* reaching ${t_{\text{commit}}}$, the robot cannot use the matched stop suffix (which is anchored at the future state ${t_{\text{commit}}}$). Instead, the MCU selects or constructs a validated state-matched fallback from current state estimates $({q}, {\dot{q}}, {\ddot{q}})$ within reserved clearance. Encoder-derived velocity and acceleration have their own error bounds; bounded polynomial evaluation does not eliminate sensing, dispatch, or actuator response delay.

Both are the stopping suffix that every admitted trajectory carries (definition [11.1](https://mlsysbook.ai/vol4/11_planning/11_planning.html#dfn-planning-stopping-suffix)), differing only in the entry state from which they start. For the running mug reach, a stalled proposer expires the shared lease 60 ms after the last admission. The MCU then requests its state-matched fallback; physical onset follows the separately budgeted response delay. All timeout checks use the MCU monotonic clock. The longer-lease comparison isolates the geometric limit: even with tracking evidence continually renewed, the controller must dispatch the matched stop early enough to begin at commitment. A host progress report changes neither cutoff. Both stops also inherit the seam’s continuity requirement, and that requirement fixes their shape and therefore their length.

### The C² Stopping Suffix and Jerk-Limited Braking

^sec-planning-c2-stopping-suffix

A moving robot carries continuous momentum. If an emergency brake uses an idealized constant-deceleration profile (${\ddot{q}_{\text{stop}}}({t}) = -{a_{\text{brake}}}$), the commanded acceleration steps discontinuously from zero to $-{a_{\text{brake}}}$ at ${t_{\text{commit}}}$, and steps discontinuously back to zero when velocity reaches zero. This instantaneous switch demands an infinite jerk impulse ($\dddot{{q_{\text{stop}}}} = \infty$) and an instantaneous step jump in actuator braking torque (${\Delta}{\tau} = {J_{\text{eff}}} {a_{\text{brake}}}$). A real drive can only approximate that step. Whether the response loses traction, disturbs the payload, or exceeds structural limits depends on the load and controller, so the worked design chooses an explicitly smooth profile.

Two bounds describe what the load and the drive tolerate during that stop: a peak deceleration ${a_{\max}}$ and a peak jerk ${j_{\max}}$, the rate at which deceleration may change. A ${C}^2$ stop keeps position, velocity, and acceleration continuous at both ends, so it must ramp its deceleration up from zero and back down to zero, and the jerk bound limits how quickly it can do either. Within those bounds, the shape of the profile decides how far the machine travels. Consider a cruise entry with position ${q_{\text{stop}}}({t_{\text{commit}}}) = {q_{c}}$, cruise speed ${v_{c}} > 0$, and zero entry acceleration. One smooth candidate is the lowest-order polynomial that matches position, velocity, and acceleration at both ends, written in the normalized time ${u} = ({t} - {t_{\text{commit}}}) / {T_{\text{stop}}} \in [0, 1]$:

$$
{q_{\text{stop}}}({u}) = {q_{c}} + {v_{c}} {T_{\text{stop}}} \left({u} - {u}^3 + \frac{1}{2} {u}^4\right), \qquad {\ddot{q}_{\text{stop}}}({u}) = -\frac{6 {v_{c}}}{{T_{\text{stop}}}} {u}(1 - {u}), \qquad \dddot{{q_{\text{stop}}}}({u}) = -\frac{6 {v_{c}}}{{T_{\text{stop}}}^2} (1 - 2{u}) \tag{16}
$$

^eq-plan-c2-polynomial-stop

Its deceleration is an inverted parabola that peaks at the midpoint, ${a_{\text{peak}}} = 1.5\,{v_{c}}/{T_{\text{stop}}}$, and its jerk is largest at the two ends, $6{v_{c}}/{T_{\text{stop}}}^2$. Holding the peak at ${a_{\max}}$ gives ${T_{\text{stop}}} = 1.5\,{v_{c}}/{a_{\max}}$ and a distance of $\frac{1}{2}{v_{c}} {T_{\text{stop}}} = 0.75\,{v_{c}}^2/{a_{\max}}$, which is $1.5$ times the constant-deceleration distance ${v_{c}}^2/2{a_{\max}}$. That factor is a property of this polynomial, not of smooth stopping. The polynomial touches its peak deceleration only at the midpoint and spends half the stop below three quarters of it, so it gives away distance that its own bounds would allow it to use.

The profile that uses those bounds fully is the **jerk-limited S-curve** (trapezoidal acceleration, the “double S” velocity profile) ([[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/03 Running the Machine/11 Trajectory Planning/V4-11.13 Source References#^ref-biagiotti2008trajectory|Biagiotti and Melchiorri 2008]]). It ramps deceleration from zero to ${a_{\max}}$ at the jerk limit, holds ${a_{\max}}$, and ramps back to zero at the jerk limit just as velocity reaches zero. Each ramp lasts ${a_{\max}}/{j_{\max}}$. The velocity profile is point-symmetric about its midpoint, so the distance is half the entry speed times the duration:

$$
{d_{\text{stop}}}({v}) = \begin{cases} \dfrac{{v}^2}{2{a_{\max}}} + \dfrac{{v}\,{a_{\max}}}{2{j_{\max}}}, & {v} \ge {a_{\max}}^2/{j_{\max}} \\[1.2ex] {v}\sqrt{{v}/{j_{\max}}}, & {v} < {a_{\max}}^2/{j_{\max}} \end{cases} \qquad {T_{\text{stop}}}({v}) = \begin{cases} \dfrac{{v}}{{a_{\max}}} + \dfrac{{a_{\max}}}{{j_{\max}}}, & {v} \ge {a_{\max}}^2/{j_{\max}} \\[1.2ex] 2\sqrt{{v}/{j_{\max}}}, & {v} < {a_{\max}}^2/{j_{\max}} \end{cases} \tag{17}
$$

^eq-plan-c2-stopping-suffix

Above the threshold speed ${a_{\max}}^2/{j_{\max}}$, the stop costs constant-deceleration braking at ${a_{\max}}$ plus cruise over half of one jerk ramp, ${a_{\max}}/2{j_{\max}}$, so a finite jerk limit acts like extra reaction time. Below the threshold the stop is too short to reach ${a_{\max}}$, and its deceleration peaks at $\sqrt{{v}\,{j_{\max}}}$. Among stops that respect both bounds and never reverse, this profile reaches rest in the least time and the least distance. The shape of the stop, not physics, sets the distance beyond ${v}^2/2{a_{\max}}$, and for given deceleration and jerk bounds the S-curve spends the least. A slower profile remains admissible, but it must be charged at its own distance.

The jerk bound belongs to the load. The loaded tote rack and the mug tolerate a deceleration only if it is reached gradually, and the registry does not yet hold a measured jerk tolerance for either. The worked design therefore sets each body’s ${j_{\max}}$ to the peak jerk of [[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/03 Running the Machine/11 Trajectory Planning/V4-11.06 Behavior at the Seam#^eq-plan-c2-polynomial-stop|equation 16]] at that body’s peak deceleration and top speed, a jerk the polynomial already asked the load to accept. A loaded stopping trial that measures the tolerable jerk would replace this stipulation, and [[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/03 Running the Machine/11 Trajectory Planning/V4-11.06 Behavior at the Seam#^eq-plan-c2-stopping-suffix|equation 17]] would then give the distance directly.

The stop is also a slowdown along the admitted path, not a new path. The planner fixes the geometry ${\mathbf{q}}({s})$ it checked for clearance, and the S-curve governs only the speed ${\dot{s}}({t})$ along that geometry. The stop therefore sweeps no volume outside the corridor already checked, and the one-dimensional comparison ${d_{\text{stop}}} \le {D_{\text{clear}}}$ applies along the path. A stop computed separately for each joint would leave that path and would need its own swept-volume check. A nonzero measured entry acceleration requires a profile matched to the actual $({q},{\dot{q}},{\ddot{q}})$ at entry with zero terminal velocity and acceleration; the permission path checks its interior speed, acceleration, jerk, torque, and clearance.

In the alternate longer-lease arm case, the blend deadline is 240 ms, matched commitment 300 ms, nominal setpoint end 400 ms, and stop end 575 ms, so the stop lasts 275 ms. At the arm’s 1 m/s TCP speed limit, the S-curve suffix with a 5 m/s² deceleration bound and a 66.7 m/s³ jerk bound travels 137.5 mm, compared with 150 mm for the polynomial of [[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/03 Running the Machine/11 Trajectory Planning/V4-11.06 Behavior at the Seam#^eq-plan-c2-polynomial-stop|equation 16]] with the same peaks. Because the blend deadline is the preferred admission instant on this schedule, the last scheduled validation and clearance evidence finish at 240 ms. The 60 mm of cruise between 240 ms and physical brake onset at 300 ms must therefore be charged against that clearance alongside the stop. Together they consume 197.5 mm in all, leaving 102.5 mm of the 300 mm obstacle clearance before sensing and model error are deducted. Because 300 ms is **physical suffix onset**, the MCU must close replacement admission and pretrigger any communication or actuator delay ${\delta}$ by that instant minus ${\delta}$. Otherwise, the arm travels an extra ${v_{c}}{\delta}$, shifts the stop end later, and shrinks the 102.5 mm margin. Nominal setpoints end at 400 ms while MCU STOP continues to 575 ms, assuming fresh target evidence keeps TRACK valid until stop dispatch ([[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/03 Running the Machine/11 Trajectory Planning/V4-11.04 Replacement Timing and the Planning Horizon#^sec-planning-goal-to-trajectory|section 11.3]]).

On the running machine, as the alternate schedule’s introduction noted, a stall never reaches commitment because the chunk lease lapses first, so its stop is the state-matched fallback. From zero-acceleration cruise the S-curve matches the arm at whatever instant it starts, and it slows the tool along the path already checked, so the state-matched fallback travels the same distance as the matched stop suffix. On the arm, as on the base, the chunk lease is a term of the stopping budget, the rule established in **[[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/01 Machine Anatomy/04 The Nervous System/V4-04.05 Chunk Lease Sizing from the Stopping Budget#^sec-nervous-lease-sizing|Chunk Lease Sizing from the Stopping Budget]]**; the arm’s own speed, stop, and clearance set what that term costs.

**Napkin Math 11.4: The stall runway**

**Problem**: The proposer stalls while the arm reaches at its TCP limit, and no replacement is admitted. How much of the obstacle clearance does the arm use between its last admission and rest, and how much remains?

**Variables**:

- Chunk lease ${T_{\text{lease}}} =$ 60 ms (chosen); permission tick 1 ms (derived) and bus cycle 1 ms (illustrative); onset 20 ms (illustrative, the base’s registered brake onset charged conservatively to the arm)
- Speed ${v} =$ 1 m/s (chosen, TCP limit)
- Deceleration bound ${a_{\max}} =$ 5 m/s² (illustrative) and jerk bound ${j_{\max}} =$ 66.7 m/s³ (derived, the peak jerk of [[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/03 Running the Machine/11 Trajectory Planning/V4-11.06 Behavior at the Seam#^eq-plan-c2-polynomial-stop|equation 16]] at the same peak)
- Clearance ${D_{\text{clear}}} =$ 300 mm (illustrative)

**Math**:

1.  **Response path**: 60 ms $+$ 1 ms $+$ 1 ms $+$ 20 ms $=$ 82 ms from last admission to stop onset, which is 82 mm at ${v}$.
2.  **Branch test** ([[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/03 Running the Machine/11 Trajectory Planning/V4-11.06 Behavior at the Seam#^eq-plan-c2-stopping-suffix|equation 17]]): ${a_{\max}}^2/{j_{\max}} =$ 0.375 m/s, below ${v}$, so the stop reaches ${a_{\max}}$.
3.  **Stop**: ${v}^2/2{a_{\max}} + {v}\,{a_{\max}}/2{j_{\max}} =$ 100 mm $+$ 37.5 mm $=$ 137.5 mm, in ${v}/{a_{\max}} + {a_{\max}}/{j_{\max}} =$ 200 ms $+$ 75 ms $=$ 275 ms.
4.  **Clearance**: 82 mm $+$ 137.5 mm $=$ 219.5 mm of 300 mm.

**Result**: The stall leaves 80.5 mm of geometric margin, before sensing and model reserves.

**Systems insight**: The travel before the stop begins is 59.6 percent of the stop itself, and at this speed every millisecond of lease costs a millimeter. The remaining margin equals only 80.5 ms of further lease, so the lease length is a term of ${d_{\text{stop}}} \le {D_{\text{clear}}}$ exactly as the deceleration bound is.

While hardware qualification must establish the arm’s actual response in place of the charged onset allowance, the two schedules leave different geometric margins because they date their clearance evidence differently ([[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/03 Running the Machine/11 Trajectory Planning/V4-11.04 Replacement Timing and the Planning Horizon#^fig-11-seam-timeline-fallback|figure 2]]). The running lease charges travel from the last admission (82 mm) plus the 137.5 mm stop, which keeps 80.5 mm. The alternate schedule charges only the cruise from its blend deadline, when clearance was last checked, to commitment (60 mm) plus the same stop, which keeps 102.5 mm. The alternate schedule’s 278 ms admission cutoff is therefore an offset on its own time axis rather than a lease length that the running clearance could support. Any lease, whatever its length, must fit its own evidence age, response travel, stop, and uncertainty reserve, just as the running lease does in the notebook above.

Although the free-space deceleration bound suits the empty-gripper reach toward the mug, it is too aggressive once the arm carries an open mug of liquid away from the conveyor, because container acceleration tilts the free surface and excites sloshing ([[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/03 Running the Machine/11 Trajectory Planning/V4-11.13 Source References#^ref-mayer2012walking|Mayer and Krechetnikov 2012]]). Under a steady deceleration ${a}$, the free surface settles perpendicular to the effective gravity, tilting by $\tan{\theta_{\text{tilt}}} = {a}/{g}$ and raising the liquid at the wall by ${R}\,{a}/{g}$ for inner radius ${R}$. In an illustrative mug of 40 mm inner radius filled to 10 mm below the rim, the free-space stop’s 5 m/s² deceleration raises the liquid 20.4 mm at the wall, well past the rim. Keeping even the steady tilt inside the freeboard ${h_{f}}$ (a necessary condition, since dynamic sloshing overshoots the steady tilt) limits deceleration to ${g}\,{h_{f}}/{R}$, or 2.45 m/s². At that gentler bound, with the jerk the polynomial reaches at the same peak, the stop from the 1 m/s TCP limit lengthens to 280.4 mm and, together with the running lease path, demands 362.4 mm of the 300 mm clearance. The loaded carry must therefore run slower than the empty reach. Before sensing and model reserves are deducted, the highest speed whose lease path and stop fit that clearance is 0.89 m/s. The trajectory record’s assumption sidecar must declare which payload regime and measured slosh limit (or lid) the record assumes.

The commitment deadline forbids waiting. In the alternate arm schedule, commitment preserves the 102.5 mm margin computed from clearance dated at 240 ms. Missing the 278 ms admission cutoff and delaying stop dispatch by 200 ms would delay physical stop onset that far past commitment. Cruise at the TCP limit would add 200 mm before the same stop and breach the 300 mm clearance by 97.5 mm. A later stop must still match its actual entry state; this counterfactual holds cruise speed constant only to show the lost clearance. An arm that waits that long may already occupy a state from which no admissible control avoids the obstacle.

### Inevitable Collision States

^sec-planning-inevitable-collision-states

An arm or base that is still clear of every obstacle at the current millisecond can already be doomed to hit one if its speed and remaining distance leave no control input inside its drive and traction limits that can bring it to rest in time. Classical path planning misses this trap when it treats obstacle avoidance as a static geometric query that asks only whether the robot’s configuration overlaps an obstacle volume at the current instant. Because a mobile manipulator moving with momentum and finite braking torque cannot halt instantaneously, its dynamic state (position, velocity, and acceleration, under its traction limits) can cross a boundary beyond which every admissible control trajectory leads to collision long before geometric contact occurs. Such states are inevitable collision states (ICS)[[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/03 Running the Machine/11 Trajectory Planning/V4-11.14 Footnotes#^fn10|¹⁰]] ([[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/03 Running the Machine/11 Trajectory Planning/V4-11.13 Source References#^ref-fraichard2004inevitable|Fraichard and Asama 2004]]).

**Definition 11.4: Inevitable collision state**

***Inevitable collision state*** (ICS) is any dynamic plant state ${\mathbf{x}}({t}) \in {\mathcal{X}}$ from which every physically admissible future control trajectory ${\mathbf{u}_{[t, \infty)}} \in {\mathcal{U}_{\text{adm}}}$ inevitably produces collision with an obstacle or constraint boundary:

$$
{\mathbf{x}} \in \text{ICS}({\mathcal{X}_{\text{obs}}}) \iff \forall {\mathbf{u}}(\cdot) \in {\mathcal{U}_{\text{adm}}}, \; \exists {t'} \ge {t} \text{ such that } {\mathbf{x}}({t'}) \in {\mathcal{X}_{\text{obs}}}
$$

 where ${\mathcal{U}_{\text{adm}}}$ denotes the admissible control input set bounded by actuator torque, traction, and deceleration limits.

1.  **Significance**: Sets the commitment deadline ${t_{\text{commit}}}$ in receding-horizon planning; continuing nominal motion past it without an admitted successor can carry the plant into an ICS, and no later replanning can bring it back out.
2.  **Distinction**: Unlike static geometric collision checks that evaluate only instantaneous spatial overlap (${\mathbf{x}} \in {\mathcal{X}_{\text{obs}}}$), an ICS identifies dynamically trapped states where current momentum and finite braking torque make future boundary breach physically unavoidable.
3.  **Common pitfall**: Delaying emergency braking in the expectation that an overdue neural planner will complete an evasive path, ignoring that moving mass enters an ICS long before geometric contact occurs.

The commitment deadline ${t_{\text{commit}}}$ and the admission-checked stopping suffix are co-designed precisely so that the machine initiates braking *before* its dynamic state enters an inevitable collision state, $\text{ICS}({\mathcal{X}_{\text{obs}}})$. If an action chunk’s stopping suffix requires distance ${d_{\text{stop}}} > {D_{\text{clear}}}$, that suffix cannot justify admitting the chunk. Failure of this particular stop does not establish that every admissible maneuver collides, as the definition of an inevitable collision state requires. The architecture nevertheless refuses the proposal because it lacks the independently executable stop on which its permission depends. The safety microcontroller therefore enforces the hard admission invariant:

$$
{d_{\text{stop}}}({t_{\text{commit}}}) \le {D_{\text{clear}}}({t_{\text{commit}}}) \tag{18}
$$

 If the controller delays braking past commitment in the hope that an overdue neural replanner will deliver an evasive path, physical momentum can carry the machine into an ICS from which no admissible control input can prevent impact. The commitment deadline is the deadline form of this boundary, fixed at planning time. Its set form, the recoverable set of plant states from which the resident stop still succeeds, is the stopping envelope that **[[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/03 Running the Machine/12 Safety Enforcement/V4-12.04 Stopping Envelopes#^sec-enforcement-stopping-envelopes|Stopping Envelopes]]** develops for the runtime check.

^eq-plan-commitment-invariant

These deadlines and the stop they protect are fixed at admission, before motion begins. The candidate declares payload, contact, effort, and obstacle assumptions. The MCU first validates its fixed header and lineage. It then checks the complete bounded trajectory and suffix, including the seam $({q},{\dot{q}},{\ddot{q}})$ against the reachable state envelope from its current estimate, interior extrema, clearance, stopping distance, and a feasible response from every state reachable through physical stop onset. A scan over ${N}$ setpoints is $\mathcal{O}({N})$, even with a fixed maximum ${N}$ and measured worst-case execution time; after admission, a distinct $\mathcal{O}(1)$ per-tick check reads the current state and local reference. A replacement received after the blend deadline passes the same check with a compressed bridge, whose higher acceleration and torque its extrema must still clear. If the check cannot finish before the earlier admission cutoff, the MCU retains the current record only while its own lease, evidence, and fallback remain valid. A candidate received after commitment cannot restore the spent runway.

The planner proposes the suffix. The MCU latches the validated payload and stop profile into protected memory **before** enabling TRACK, and it dispatches that resident path before commitment without reading host DRAM. A host crash, bus stall, or corrupted shared packet therefore cannot erase the stop. If the measured state falls outside the latched suffix’s entry region, the MCU uses the state-matched fallback. Torque disable or a spring brake is not a universal substitute for a controlled stop, especially with a suspended load (**[[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/03 Running the Machine/12 Safety Enforcement/V4-12.10 The Fallback Ladder#^sec-enforcement-fallback-ladder|The Fallback Ladder]]**).

### Base Stopping Budget Under Jerk-Limited Deceleration

^sec-planning-base-stopping-budget

On the mobile base approaching the rack end at its 1.5 m/s drive limit, the loaded tote rack tolerates the machine’s credible 2 m/s² deceleration only when that deceleration is ramped gradually rather than applied as an abrupt step. The base’s stop is therefore the S-curve of [[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/03 Running the Machine/11 Trajectory Planning/V4-11.06 Behavior at the Seam#^eq-plan-c2-stopping-suffix|equation 17]] with ${a_{\max}}$ at 2 m/s² and a jerk bound of 7.11 m/s³. That jerk bound is the peak jerk of the polynomial stop from the 1.5 m/s drive limit and the stop jerk bound already listed on the machine card (**[[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/00 Introduction/01 The Causal Boundary/V4-01.05 Defining the Causal Boundary#^tbl-01-machine-card|The running machine's card]]**). Under it, every speed above 0.5625 m/s reaches the deceleration plateau. At the drive limit, this suffix lasts 1,031.25 ms and travels 773.44 mm, compared with 562.5 mm for constant-deceleration braking in **[[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/01 Machine Anatomy/02 The Physical Body/V4-02.04 Kinetic Momentum#^eq-body-stopping-distance|equation 2.2]]** and 843.75 mm for the polynomial with the same peaks. Because the jerk ramps cost the equivalent of 140.6 ms of cruise, the suffix adds 210.94 mm to the budget that **[[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/03 Running the Machine/08 Sensor Perception/V4-08.05 Bounding Observation Age#^sec-perception-latency-waterfall|The latency waterfall]]** left at 912.9 mm. This raises the running total to 1,123.84 mm and exceeds the 1.10 m rack-end clear distance by 23.84 mm. Because this partial total still omits the unqualified dispatch-to-admission interval, which can only add travel, the 23.84 mm overrun is a conditional design deficit rather than a complete measured response bound.

Plotted as velocity against time, the area under each curve is its stopping distance, and the jerk ramps appear as the rounded start and finish that stretch the S-curve and the polynomial past the constant-deceleration line ([[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/03 Running the Machine/11 Trajectory Planning/V4-11.06 Behavior at the Seam#^fig-11-stopping-profiles|figure 6]]). Stacked onto the budget’s other terms, only the constant-deceleration stop, which no drive can execute, ends inside the 1.10 m rack-end clearance.

Even the shortest stop under the stipulated bounds leaves a 23.84 mm deficit at the 1.5 m/s drive limit, so the robot cannot cruise at that limit in this aisle under the stated deceleration and jerk bounds. The deficit is narrow and it rests on a stipulated jerk bound, so a measured jerk tolerance could move it in either direction. The machine must either derate its operating speed or detect the boundary with more usable clearance ahead; a longer prediction or evidence lifetime alone creates no stopping room. This is the first law’s stopping condition (principle [[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/01 Machine Anatomy/V4 The Machine Anatomy#^pri-vol4-irreversibility|1]]) with one more term, because a stop the load can tolerate is a longer stop and the distance it adds comes out of the same clear distance. This calculation concerns the fixed rack-end boundary. A person entering the aisle requires separately qualified moving-actor bounds and crossing control or an operating restriction; this static clearance calculation does not establish that protection. Under the selected body and aisle constraints, reducing speed can recover clearance. **[[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/03 Running the Machine/12 Safety Enforcement/V4-12.04 Stopping Envelopes#^sec-enforcement-stopping-envelopes|Stopping Envelopes]]** adds the tracking allowance and derives a candidate speed; permission to operate still requires complete capture-to-admission timing, positive stopping reserve, and qualification under the declared conditions.

↩︎ The intrusion distance an approaching pedestrian adds to the stopping budget, and the site controls it demands, are established in **[[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/03 Running the Machine/09 Spatial Memory/V4-09.08 Forward State Propagation and Occlusion Decay#^sec-memory-belief-through-occlusion|Forward State Propagation and Occlusion Decay]]**.

For either body, the admission and fallback decisions in [[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/03 Running the Machine/11 Trajectory Planning/V4-11.06 Behavior at the Seam#^tbl-11-seam-fallback-hierarchy|table 2]] are distinct from an emergency power cut. The mug reach and the aisle approach now have a complete execution argument: a timed prefix, a checked seam, and a stop reserved under stated conditions. With those obligations fixed, trajectory generators can be compared by the candidate work they perform rather than by treating their outputs as permission.

| TRACK, ordinary handoff    | Replacement passes the complete admission check before the admission cutoff preceding commitment. | Latch the new nominal path and its own stop, then transfer at a checked ${C}^2$ seam.                                                                        |
| TRACK, late candidate      | Candidate arrives after the blend deadline but before the admission cutoff preceding commitment.  | Admit only if the compressed bridge, full path, stop, and timing still fit; otherwise retain the current record only while its own permission remains valid. |
| STOP, matched suffix       | No successor admitted by the earlier cutoff.                                                      | MCU follows its protected suffix from the validated entry state; nominal tracking may end while STOP continues.                                              |
| STOP, earlier invalidation | Lease lapse, intent expiry, contact, state mismatch, or fault before physical stop onset.         | MCU selects the state-matched fallback with reserved clearance.                                                                                              |
| Fault response             | Controlled stop cannot be assured from measured state.                                            | Use the machine-specific risk response (which may include drive inhibit, brake, or compliance) only under its validated load and timing assumptions.         |

Table 2: **Tracking and fallback decisions**: A host crash does not remove a latched stop. TRACK is the nominal mode; STOP is the stop rung of the fallback ladder in **[[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/03 Running the Machine/12 Safety Enforcement/V4-12.10 The Fallback Ladder#^sec-enforcement-four-rungs|The four rungs of the fallback ladder]]**.

**Checkpoint 11.3: Commitment and full stopping clearance**

Before committing an autonomous machine to an action chunk, verify your understanding of commitment deadlines, stopping budgets, and trajectory continuity:

**Deadline distinction**: Distinguish nominal setpoint end, physical stop completion, and the end of the evidence horizon.

**Continuous entry**: Why would a constant-deceleration switch fail ${C}^2$ matching at zero-acceleration cruise entry?

**Clearance and latency**: The arm’s 102.5 mm margin already charges cruise from the blend deadline to commitment. Where must an unreserved delay ${\delta}$ beyond it be charged, and how much of that margin does ${v_{c}}{\delta}$ consume?
