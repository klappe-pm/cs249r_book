# V4-11.10 Fallacies and Pitfalls

[[Home|Home]] · [[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/Volume 4 Overview|Volume Overview]] · [[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/03 Running the Machine/11 Trajectory Planning/V4-11 Trajectory Planning|V4-11 Trajectory Planning]]

^sec-planning-fallacies-pitfalls

A planned trajectory remains physically feasible only as long as its environmental, dynamic, and computational assumptions hold true. In embodied systems, failures rarely stem from minor mathematical errors along the nominal interior of a path. Instead, they emerge at operational and physical seams: when dynamic obstacles intrude into an active swept volume, when concatenated action chunks excite mechanical resonances, when host inference pipelines miss scheduling deadlines, or when controllers attempt to stop faster than actuator dynamics permit. Both the trajectory admission gate and the real-time execution monitor must defend against these five failure modes.

**Fallacy**: *Static admission checks protect a machine against obstacle displacements during active execution.*

**Static geometry confirms clear space only at admission time; dynamic workspaces demand continuous runtime monitoring.** Consider a robotic manipulator reaching toward an assembly target. When the trajectory is first admitted, the swept volume between the end-effector and the target is completely clear. Halfway through execution, however, an operator slides a storage tote into the arm’s path. Although the admission check was mathematically correct when computed, the physical workspace has changed. Static pre-screening guarantees only that a trajectory was safe in the past; it cannot guarantee that the path remains clear as time elapses. Execution monitoring must continuously track obstacle clearances and initiate deceleration while adequate stopping distance remains. Systems architects must explicitly budget sensor bus latency and vision pipeline transit times, reserving enough spatial stopping margin to ensure the machine can halt before an encroaching obstacle breaches its swept volume.

**Architect’s countermeasure**: Pair high-level spatial screening on the host with low-latency local clearance sensing processed directly by the MCU. Explicitly allocate the sensing refresh rate, bus transit latency, and MCU memory footprint. If an unmodeled obstacle breaches the reserved spatial safety margin ${\delta_{\text{margin}}}$, immediately abort nominal tracking and engage the pre-validated, state-matched fallback trajectory.

**Pitfall**: *Checking each action chunk alone without examining the motion the chunks compose.*

**Individual chunk feasibility does not guarantee smooth motion across seams; stochastic neural chatter can overstress a mechanical transmission.** When generative sequence policies (such as diffusion models or action-chunking transformers) are sampled stochastically, successive chunks frequently disagree in direction or mismatch acceleration at their boundary. While every individual chunk satisfies kinematic position and velocity bounds in isolation, concatenating them without boundary constraints induces rapid torque reversals ${\Delta}{\tau} = {J_{\text{eff}}} {\Delta}{\ddot{q}}$. Amplified by the reflected rotor inertia (${N_{\text{gear}}}^2 {J_{\text{rotor}}}$), this high-frequency chatter excites flexible structural modes, accelerates gearbox wear, and causes severe Joule heating (${i_q}^2 {R_{\text{phase}}}$) in the stator windings. The admission pipeline must evaluate derivative continuity across every chunk boundary seam, enforce ${C}^2$ quintic smoothing, and track recent motion history to reject oscillating command sequences before they reach the motor drives.

**Architect’s countermeasure**: Enforce multi-chunk frequency and reversal monitoring across a sliding execution window. Reject any trajectory candidate whose frequency spectrum or repeated effort exceeds the motor’s identified mechanical or thermal envelope. If smoothing filters alter a candidate chunk, re-run the full kinematic, seam continuity, and terminal stop checks on the modified trajectory prior to admission.

**Pitfall**: *Delaying fallback transition past the hard stopping horizon in the hope that delayed compute finishes.*

**An overdue compute cycle never justifies extending a physical commitment deadline.** In an alternate schedule operating with an admission cutoff of 278 ms, the active trajectory record supplies nominal setpoints that expire at 400 ms. The record carries a 275 ms matched stopping suffix that must finish deceleration by 575 ms. To guarantee physical braking onset by 300 ms, the MCU must dispatch the fallback trajectory no later than the 278 ms cutoff unless a verified replacement plan has already arrived. If the host application processor reports that neural inference is nearly complete but has not yet delivered a validated plan, delaying the fallback transition consumes the runway reserved for stopping. The MCU must dispatch the resident stopping suffix by the earlier cutoff so its physical onset occurs at commitment. Progress reports cannot extend a mechanical stopping budget; only an admitted, physically verifiable trajectory justifies continued forward motion.

**Architect’s countermeasure**: Bind the physical commitment deadline ${t_{\text{commit}}}$ strictly to a monotonic hardware timer on the MCU. When the timer reaches the admission cutoff, close the admission window and dispatch the resident stopping suffix immediately, disregarding any speculative progress updates from the host processor.

**Pitfall**: *Relying solely on feedforward dynamic models when physical contact or payload mass can change abruptly.*

**Feedforward model inversion without closed-loop disturbance bounds can drive an unloaded arm beyond its admitted trajectory.** Consider an arm lifting a heavy workpiece near its 3 kg payload limit. To track the aggressive trajectory, the controller injects feedforward torque computed from an inverse dynamics model of the fully loaded arm. If the workpiece slips from the gripper mid-lift, the drives continue applying the large lifting torque intended for the heavy load to an arm that is now empty. Without a timely feedback correction or torque clamp, the excess torque accelerates the arm above its planned rate. While the admitted trajectory has not changed, the physical mass properties that justified those torque commands have vanished. High-bandwidth feedback monitoring must detect the resulting tracking error and clamp torque commands before the arm strikes a mechanical joint stop or triggers inverter thermal derating. Feedforward dynamic models significantly reduce tracking lag under nominal conditions, but they must never run without closed-loop supervisory bounds.

**Architect’s countermeasure**: Implement real-time torque limiters and tracking-error monitoring ($\|{\mathbf{p}_{\text{meas}}} - {\mathbf{r}}\| \le {\epsilon_{\text{track}}}$) directly inside the 1 kHz servo loop. If an abrupt payload drop or unexpected contact causes acceleration to spike, clamp motor currents immediately and transition the system to compliance mode or an emergency stop.

**Fallacy**: *A trajectory can omit an achievable terminal stopping suffix if replacement plans are expected to arrive in time.*

**A trajectory without a pre-latched stopping continuation is an unhedged bet on infinite compute availability.** Traveling at its drive limit of 1.5 m/s, a mobile manipulator’s base requires 1,031.25 ms for even the shortest deceleration profile its kinematic bounds permit. That minimum stop follows an S-curve profile ${T}={v_{c}}/{a_{\max}}+{a_{\max}}/{j_{\max}}$ evaluated at a credible braking acceleration 2 m/s² and jerk limit 7.11 m/s³. Sizing a proposed stopping suffix using the idealized constant-deceleration formula ${v_{c}}/{a_{\text{brake}}}$ yields 750 ms. However, no physical actuator can ramp deceleration instantaneously without infinite jerk. Forcing a stop into that truncated duration inevitably violates the acceleration bound, the jerk limit, or both, exceeding what the chassis and its payload can tolerate. Expecting the next plan to arrive on time does not make an unfeasible fallback executable. The admission gate must verify that the terminal stopping suffix strictly respects ${a_{\max}}$ and ${j_{\max}}$ throughout its duration. With an S-curve, this requires validating ${T_{\text{stop}}}$ against [[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/03 Running the Machine/11 Trajectory Planning/V4-11.06 Behavior at the Seam#^eq-plan-c2-stopping-suffix|equation 17]] and confirming that the stopping distance satisfies ${d_{\text{stop}}} \le {D_{\text{clear}}}$ within verified clearance. The admission gate must reject any record that fails this test. Every admitted trajectory requires a physically executable termination path to protect the machine when upstream inference stalls.

**Architect’s countermeasure**: Require every candidate trajectory record to incorporate a complete, closed-form ${C}^2$ stopping profile along its admitted corridor. Deceleration and jerk along this profile must remain strictly within vehicle and payload stability limits, while the stopping distance must satisfy ${d_{\text{stop}}} \le {D_{\text{clear}}}$ within verified clear space. Reject any record at the admission gate that lacks this independently executable stopping guarantee.
