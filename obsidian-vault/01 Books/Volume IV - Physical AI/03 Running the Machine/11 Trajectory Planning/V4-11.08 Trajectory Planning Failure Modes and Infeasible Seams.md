# V4-11.08 Trajectory Planning Failure Modes and Infeasible Seams

[[Home|Home]] · [[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/Volume 4 Overview|Volume Overview]] · [[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/03 Running the Machine/11 Trajectory Planning/V4-11 Trajectory Planning|V4-11 Trajectory Planning]]

^sec-planning-failure-modes

On the mug reach, a replacement trajectory can fail in three distinct regimes: within the candidate itself, along the boundary where it joins the executing motion, or during execution when the environment changes. The point where a failure first manifests dictates which subsystem has the sensory evidence to reject the trajectory, as well as how much time and clearance remain to bring the machine to a safe stop. Pre-execution candidate and boundary checks intercept the first two failure modes before setpoints can reach actuator registers, whereas runtime monitoring guards against the third.

### Checking Limits and Boundaries Before Execution

^sec-planning-pre-execution-checkers

If an unvalidated chunk reaches actuator registers directly, a single out-of-bounds waypoint or an unblended velocity step at the splice instant will fault the drive before feedback monitoring can react. Before any proposed trajectory setpoint can influence actuator registers, a deterministic safety MCU therefore screens the candidate record through two independent admission gates within a measured worst-case execution budget. Fixed-degree seam calculations require bounded compute per axis, whereas checking a complete setpoint sequence scales with the candidate horizon length:

1.  **Pre-execution Bound Checker:** Evaluates the proposed neural action chunk in isolation across the full candidate horizon $[{t_{\text{start}}}, {t_{\text{plan\_exp}}}]$ against intrinsic kinematic, dynamic, and stopping invariants. The checker evaluates margin functions ${M}({t}) = {L_{\text{limit}}} - |{x}({t})|$ across the signals it checks. Joint positions ${q}({t})$ are checked against their limits. Joint velocities are checked against contracted limits $|{\dot{q}}({t})| \le {v_{\max}} - \frac{1}{2} {a_{\max}} {T_s}$ ([[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/03 Running the Machine/11 Trajectory Planning/V4-11.05 Kinodynamic Feasibility#^eq-plan-contracted-velocity-limit|equation 10]], or the $\frac{1}{4}$ reserve under the equal-velocity, zero-acceleration endpoint conditions and jerk bound of [[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/03 Running the Machine/11 Trajectory Planning/V4-11.05 Kinodynamic Feasibility#^sec-planning-intrasample-overshoot|section 11.4.2]]). Free-space inverse-dynamics joint torques are sampled with a bound on their inter-sample variation and checked against the rated torque, $|{\boldsymbol{\tau}}({t})| = |{\mathbf{M}}({\mathbf{q}}){\ddot{\mathbf{q}}} + {\mathbf{C}}({\mathbf{q}}, {\dot{\mathbf{q}}}){\dot{\mathbf{q}}} + {\mathbf{g}}({\mathbf{q}})| \le {\boldsymbol{\tau}_{\text{rated}}}$. The checker also enforces the binding stopping invariant: the candidate’s matched stopping suffix must satisfy ${d_{\text{stop}}} \le {D_{\text{clear}}}$ throughout observed free space. If any knot point or interior interval yields ${M}({t}) < 0$ or causes the stopping runway to breach available clearance, the gate immediately rejects the candidate and logs a diagnostic fault record containing the joint index, timestamp, and negative margin. Gate rejection causes neither mechanical shudder nor physical pause, because the low-level tracking controller continues along the previously admitted trajectory as long as that trajectory’s state, sensor evidence, lease, and stopping reserve remain valid. A rejected replacement simply fails to renew that lease.
2.  **Boundary Checker:** Evaluates the transition seam between the currently executing trajectory ${A}$ and the candidate replacement ${B}$ at transition time ${t_{\text{seam}}}$. Because unconstrained sequence decoders predict open-loop action tokens without terminal derivative matching, concatenating raw chunks frequently produces sharp acceleration steps. The boundary checker addresses this in two stages. During early admission, it screens the reachable seam-state envelope projected from trajectory ${A}$. At activation, it incorporates real-time encoder estimates $({\mathbf{q}_{\text{meas}}}, {\dot{\mathbf{q}}_{\text{meas}}}, {\ddot{\mathbf{q}}_{\text{meas}}})$ and their error bounds to calculate the physical seam gap $({\Delta}{\mathbf{q}}, {\Delta}{\dot{\mathbf{q}}}, {\Delta}{\ddot{\mathbf{q}}})$. It then evaluates whether an analytical ${C}^2$ quintic bridge ([[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/03 Running the Machine/11 Trajectory Planning/V4-11.05 Kinodynamic Feasibility#^eq-plan-quintic-spline-matrix|equation 12]]) can reconcile the boundary without violating torque limits or the motor drive’s voltage and current-slew constraints. A fast one-tick estimate, ${\tau_{\text{request}}} = {J_{\text{eff}}} \frac{{\Delta}{\dot{q}}}{{T_s}}$, diagnoses the demand of an unblended one-tick transition. Failure of that estimate cannot reject a finite bridge by itself; admission checks the bridge at its actual duration, with its endpoint states, interior derivatives, torque and electrical bounds, and swept clearance.

For the CSG-25-50 joint in the seam analysis, an idealized calculation accounts solely for its 0.18 kg·m² reflected rotor inertia over a single 1 ms tick and allocates the entire 127 N·m repeated-peak rating purely to acceleration. It yields an *idealized ceiling* of 0.69 rad/s on that hypothetical one-tick step ${\Delta}{\dot{q}}$, not on a finite-duration bridge. In practice, the available torque margin is substantially smaller once link and payload inertia, gravity, friction, feedforward torque commands, coil current rise times, and thermal or DC-bus limits are deducted. The admission path must evaluate the finite bridge against this state-dependent torque margin and the drive’s measured step response. The gate nevertheless rejects a step velocity discontinuity outright, even one whose idealized single-tick torque falls below that ceiling, because continuous physical motion requires a smooth, bounded ${C}^2$ bridge that satisfies its own interior limit checks.[[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/03 Running the Machine/11 Trajectory Planning/V4-11.14 Footnotes#^fn14|¹⁴]]

### Runtime Tracking Monitors and Unexpected Contact

^sec-planning-runtime-monitors

↪ The cyclic checking that enforces these trips on every tick, and the independence the monitor needs from the proposer, are developed in **[[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/03 Running the Machine/12 Safety Enforcement/V4-12.09 Admission, Cyclic Checking, and Monitor Independence#^sec-enforcement-where-enforcer-sits|Admission, Cyclic Checking, and Monitor Independence]]**.

When the arm reaches past the conveyor guard with 0.020 m of budgeted swept-volume clearance, a slipping payload or an unexpected contact after admission can invalidate the dynamic and geometric assumptions under which the plan was cleared. Independent **runtime monitors** must detect this divergence while remaining obstacle clearance still allows a controlled stop. They track four primary signals on every tick: joint state divergence, tracking error velocity, unexpected contact wrenches, and violations of the geometric assumptions declared in the trajectory record’s sidecar ([[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/03 Running the Machine/11 Trajectory Planning/V4-11.09 The Trajectory Record#^sec-planning-trajectory-contract|section 11.8]]). This monitoring relies on **spatial margin budgeting**, in which geometric obstacle clearance is not simply awarded to control tracking error. Mechanical compliance under load, structural deflection, and drivetrain backlash consume physical clearance before any feedback tracking error becomes observable at the encoders. The tracking error allowance ${\epsilon_{\text{pos}}}$ therefore equals the total swept-volume clearance minus the structural deflection and gearbox compliance allowance ${\delta_{\text{deflect}}}$.

On the conveyor-guard reach, once payload deflection and gearbox compliance claim an allowance ${\delta_{\text{deflect}}}$ of 0.005 m, the nominal tracking allowance ${\epsilon_{\text{pos}}}$ drops from 0.020 m to 0.015 m. This remainder represents an upper ceiling on allocatable margin rather than a direct trip threshold, because latencies across detection, dispatch, and actuator response, along with state-estimation uncertainty and the swept motion of the fallback maneuver, all require dedicated clearance reserves. The arm tracker’s validated tracking bound, the arm’s counterpart to the base tracking bound ${\epsilon_{\text{track}}}$ that **[[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/03 Running the Machine/12 Safety Enforcement/V4-12.03 The Tracker Handoff and Dynamic Reference Envelopes#^sec-enforcement-handoff|The Tracker Handoff and Dynamic Reference Envelopes]]** derives, must fit within these 0.015 m and is checked by the real-time safety loop on every control tick. If the measured Cartesian tool-position error $\|{\mathbf{p}_{\text{meas}}}({t})-{\mathbf{r}}({t})\|$ exceeds ${\epsilon_{\text{track}}}$, or if a wrist-mounted six-axis force-torque sensor detects an unmodeled 10 N contact during free-space transit, the monitor immediately revokes nominal tracking and commands the validated state-matched fallback. Active physical deceleration begins once the measured detection, communication, and actuator-onset delays elapse.

A trajectory plan can also exhaust its nominal setpoints before a subsequent candidate passes admission. The safety MCU cannot simply allow the execution pointer to overrun the trajectory buffer, nor can it treat a stationary position-hold setpoint as an active, verified stop. Instead, the two deadline tiers of [[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/03 Running the Machine/11 Trajectory Planning/V4-11.06 Behavior at the Seam#^sec-planning-two-tier-deadlines|section 11.5.1]] end the window of validity on the MCU’s monotonic clock, whichever fires first. Furthermore, computing stopping clearance requires accounting for the full physical chain: sensing and detection delays, dispatch latency, actuator torque rise times, and the swept volume across the deceleration profile. Elementary calculations assuming instantaneous or constant deceleration fail to model the true physical trajectory of a smooth ${C}^2$ stopping suffix.

Every checker and monitor described here depends on an explicit execution contract between the upstream trajectory generator and the downstream real-time controller. If a planner delivers only a raw sequence of Cartesian or joint waypoints, without its validity lease, clearance margins, boundary derivative bounds, and certified stopping suffix, the low-level permission path has no principled way to distinguish safe nominal motion from dangerous dynamic drift. Safe autonomous execution requires that geometry, dynamics, and timing be verified together before setpoints ever reach actuator registers.
