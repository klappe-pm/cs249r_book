# V4-12.10 The Fallback Ladder

[[Home|Home]] · [[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/Volume 4 Overview|Volume Overview]] · [[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/03 Running the Machine/12 Safety Enforcement/V4-12 Safety Enforcement|V4-12 Safety Enforcement]]

^sec-enforcement-fallback-ladder

A safety filter must select its fallback before the admissible control set becomes empty. Once the chosen filter has an empty feasible intersection, it cannot maintain its modeled invariant. A qualified alternate response may mitigate damage or support its own narrower containment claim, but selecting it does not restore the lost guarantee. Furthermore, physical systems cannot rely on a single, binary kill switch. Abruptly severing DC bus power whenever a control proposal approaches a barrier boundary induces uncontrolled deceleration, mechanical shock, and incessant false-positive mission aborts. Conversely, complex software optimization is futile during a hardware fault such as an inverter shoot-through, because the fault destroys gate-driver field-effect transistors (FETs) milliseconds before an interrupt handler can even dispatch.

To resolve these competing physical realities, the permission path maintains a deterministic ladder of resident protective responses. The runtime arbiter evaluates plant kinematics, remaining sensor validity, and hardware integrity on every control cycle, selecting deterministically among minimally invasive projection, closed-loop position holding, controlled deceleration, or complete torque removal. The name records an ordering by how much control authority each response retains, from a corrected proposal down to unpowered restraint, not a sequence to descend. The arbiter never climbs down the rungs one at a time; it jumps directly to the one whose prerequisites still hold.

**Definition 12.3: Dynamic fallback ladder**

***Dynamic fallback ladder*** is the set of resident protective responses (${\mathbf{u}} \in \{{\mathcal{P}_{\mathcal{C}}}({\mathbf{u}_{\text{nom}}}), {\mathbf{u}_{\text{hold}}}, {\mathbf{u}_{\text{stop}}}, {\mathbf{u}_{\text{cutoff}}}\}$). A deterministic arbiter selects one of them on every tick according to the check that failed (an invariant violation, a timing overrun, or a hardware fault) and the measured state of the plant. One trigger can therefore select different rungs in motion and at standstill.

1.  **Significance**: Physical AI systems cannot treat all faults identically. Cutting actuator power on dynamic, underactuated, or high-momentum plants (for example, legged robots mid-stride or multirotors in flight) precipitates immediate catastrophic falls or tumbling. These platforms require state-matched active deceleration before executing terminal torque removal.
2.  **Distinction**: Unlike software exception handlers that unwind the call stack or terminate a user-space thread, a dynamic fallback ladder operates against physical momentum. The arbiter matches each rung to the remaining valid sensor channels, surviving control authority, and available stopping clearance.
3.  **Common pitfall**: Treating the rungs as an escalation sequence where lower rungs, such as safe torque off (STO), inherently provide faster or safer arrest. Each rung possesses distinct entry prerequisites; invoking an unpowered torque inhibit on an airborne drone or an unbraked mobile base allows the machine to coast freely without arresting motion.

### The Four Rungs of the Fallback Ladder

^sec-enforcement-four-rungs

Walking down protective responses one step at a time like a software exception cascade fails on physical hardware: commanding a standstill position hold while the base is rolling at 1.3 m/s saturates the drives, while cutting drive torque before active braking completes lets the vehicle coast into the obstacle. The dynamic fallback architecture therefore organizes protection across four resident responses (`PROJECT`, `HOLD`, `STOP`, and `INHIBIT`). The deterministic arbiter samples measured plant state (velocity, position, payload loading) and fault severity on every millisecond tick to dispatch the response whose compute, sensing, and actuation prerequisites still hold:

1.  `PROJECT`, minimal-intervention projection in MCU firmware. The primary safety filter executes on every control cycle while the feasible control set is nonempty (${\mathcal{U}_{\text{safe}}} \neq \emptyset$), the proposal lease is fresh, and tracking error remains within bounds ($\|{e}({t})\| \le {\epsilon_{\text{track}}}$). It computes the minimal-intervention torque ${\mathbf{u}^*} = \arg\min_{{\mathbf{u}}} \|{\mathbf{u}} - {\mathbf{u}_{\text{nom}}}\|^2$ subject to active barrier half-spaces within its 150 ${\text{µ}}\text{s}$ cycle allocation. It makes the smallest feasible effort correction under the stated invariance assumptions; the planner must still account for any changed task-space path.
2.  `HOLD`, powered hold at standstill in MCU firmware. When the machine is stationary, the arbiter selects an energized position hold, analogous to an IEC 60204-1 Category 2 stop: safe stop 2 (SS2) followed by safe operating stop (SOS), the drive function that monitors standstill.[[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/03 Running the Machine/12 Safety Enforcement/V4-12.17 Footnotes#^fn15|¹⁵]] Its protective validity stems from that certified standstill monitor rather than the position loop gain. Because IEC 60204-1 permits only Category 0 or Category 1 for an emergency stop, HOLD can never serve as an E-stop response ([[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/03 Running the Machine/12 Safety Enforcement/V4-12.16 Source References#^ref-iec60204|International Electrotechnical Commission 2016a]]). The drives maintain coordinate ${\mathbf{x}_{\text{hold}}}$ under closed-loop position control, modulating inverter phase currents against gravity and static loads. While this rung incurs continuous Joule heating in motor windings, it eliminates friction brake wear and preserves kinematic calibration, enabling instantaneous task resumption when a valid trajectory proposal arrives. On the warehouse mobile manipulator, this rung handles lapsed leases or lost host heartbeats specifically when the platform is already at standstill.
3.  `STOP`, controlled dynamic stop in MCU firmware. When a machine in active transit loses proposal authority, tracking error diverges, or an obstacle breaches the stopping envelope, the response is a resident, state-matched controlled stop, analogous to an IEC 60204-1 Category 1 stop (safe stop 1, SS1). Its certified counterpart is the ramp-monitored variant (SS1-r), which checks deceleration against a bounded profile throughout the arrest; the unmonitored, time-controlled variant (SS1-t) merely removes torque after a fixed delay without verifying that the planned ramp actually decelerated the load ([[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/03 Running the Machine/12 Safety Enforcement/V4-12.16 Source References#^ref-iec61800part52|International Electrotechnical Commission 2016b]]). The resident jerk-limited S-curve stop suffix of [[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/03 Running the Machine/12 Safety Enforcement/V4-12.04 Stopping Envelopes#^sec-enforcement-stopping-envelopes|section 12.2]] serves this role provided the entry state falls within its validated domain. Executed autonomously by bare-metal MCU firmware directly from local SRAM, this stop peaks at the credible deceleration ${a_{\text{brake}}}$, returning kinetic energy through motor inverters to the DC bus for absorption by the qualified regeneration path. Power removal and mechanical brake clamping occur only after confirmed zero velocity.
4.  `INHIBIT`, torque inhibit and spring-brake clamping by hardware interlocks. When the safety microcontroller suffers a brownout, clock loss, or watchdog timeout, all software decision logic ceases. Dedicated, out-of-band hardware interlocks trigger STO to de-energize gate drive power supplies and drop hold current to electromechanical spring-applied brakes. This terminal rung removes motor torque without software intervention; the mechanical brakes provide only their independently qualified holding or dynamic stopping capacity.

When a machine in motion loses proposal authority or violates a tracking bound, the arbiter engages a controlled dynamic stop, operating analogously to an IEC 60204-1 Category 1 stop. On the warehouse mobile manipulator base, this rung executes the resident stop suffix pre-admitted alongside the motion trajectory, rather than attempting to synthesize a stopping trajectory on the fly during a fault. In steady-state cruising with zero acceleration, this pre-computed profile matches the operating state at fault onset directly; nonzero acceleration states require their own qualified state-matched suffixes. From an aisle cruise velocity of 1.3 m/s, the jerk-limited S-curve suffix sustains a credible peak deceleration of 2 m/s² between its jerk transitions. The entire maneuver spans 931.25 ms and traverses 605.31 mm, the stop-suffix travel the stopping budget of [[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/03 Running the Machine/12 Safety Enforcement/V4-12.04 Stopping Envelopes#^sec-enforcement-stopping-envelopes|section 12.2]] reserves at this speed. Because the deceleration phase channels kinetic energy back through the inverter into the DC bus, bus capacitance and braking choppers must absorb this regenerative surge without triggering a bus overvoltage fault. Complete electrical power removal, if mandated by protocol, occurs only after sensors verify standstill.

Beyond firmware-driven stops, a separate out-of-band circuit can inhibit motor torque generation entirely. Drive STO cuts gate-drive bias to isolate inverter FETs, but it does not disconnect the DC bus, energize mechanical friction brakes, or actively decelerate a moving rotor; an unbraked motor will simply coast under its existing momentum. In robust architectures, an external safety controller simultaneously drives a separately rated contactor and spring-applied mechanical brakes. Engineers must evaluate the dynamic stopping distance of this combined pathway as an integrated system, because dropping drive torque while an arm is under gravitational load or while a vehicle is at speed can exacerbate the hazard. For the warehouse mobile manipulator, this pathway, STO combined with nine spring brakes that engage between 30 ms and 80 ms after coil flux decay, constitutes the unbudgeted INHIBIT rung. It serves exclusively as a hardware backstop for MCU cycle failure, and the primary stopping envelope claims do not rely on it.

An undervoltage fault on the shared drive bus can invoke the STOP rung only while inverter gate drives, angular encoders, and control logic retain adequate voltage for closed-loop deceleration. The isolated permission rail maintains power to logic circuits, transceivers, and brake coils for 2 s, but it does not supply the motor currents required for braking torque. Consequently, timing guarantees for powered stopping assume a surviving drive bus. Under this condition, dynamic braking begins within 22 ms of the fault flag; the hold-up hypothetical uses the pre-dispatch inspected-floor ceiling of 1.23 m/s, rather than an admitted operating configuration, and its stop requires 1211.4 ms. Mechanical brake engagement at standstill adds at most 80 ms. This entire sequence completes within 1313.43 ms, preserving a 686.57 ms buffer of control logic hold-up.

↩︎ The isolated permission rail and its capacitive hold-up are established in **[[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/01 Machine Anatomy/02 The Physical Body/V4-02.09 Which Physical Budget Binds First#^sec-body-permission-rail-isolation|Cross-budget coupling and permission rail isolation]]**.

This analysis confirms the control-supply duration budget, whereas the electrical drive-energy budget requires separate qualification. Systems engineers must prove through empirical testing that the DC bus capacitance, inverter power stages, and regenerative dump resistors sustain controlled deceleration under worst-case electrical fault conditions. If the drive stage cannot guarantee this power integrity, the system must either adopt an unpowered dynamic braking configuration or restrict its operational safety envelope; INHIBIT cannot be credited with standard Category 1 stopping performance. Similarly, at standstill, an undervoltage fault must trigger verified mechanical brake engagement before the logic supply decays, rather than attempting to maintain an active HOLD indefinitely on finite capacitance.

Physical AI systems engineers must distinguish between **fail-safe** and **fail-operational** plants when configuring terminal fallbacks. In a machine already at a mechanically restrained standstill, removing drive torque is safe once mechanical engagement is verified. On underactuated, open-loop unstable, or high-momentum platforms, however, abruptly removing actuator torque directly initiates a catastrophic failure mode:

1.  **Aerial platforms (multirotors and eVTOLs)**: Cutting motor torque drops aerodynamic thrust to zero instantly, transforming an airborne vehicle into an uncontrolled ballistic projectile.
2.  **Dynamic legged systems (bipedal humanoids)**: During dynamic locomotion, the robot’s center of mass frequently falls outside the support polygon; stability depends on active ground-reaction forces and momentum exchange. Suddenly removing joint torques causes an unrecoverable fall, with impact severity governed by gait phase and available compliance.
3.  **High-speed autonomous vehicles**: De-energizing steering actuators or electric traction motors at highway speeds strips away the control authority required to execute a coordinated deceleration, leaving the vehicle at the mercy of passive road friction.

The physical channel that dispatches a hardware torque-removal request is the out-of-band interlock that **[[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/01 Machine Anatomy/04 The Nervous System/V4-04.07 Actuation Authority#^sec-nervous-actuation-authority|Actuation Authority]]** traced (**[[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/01 Machine Anatomy/04 The Nervous System/V4-04.07 Actuation Authority#^fig-real-estop|Illustrative out-of-band interlock and STO path]]**). In it, dual linked emergency-stop contacts, two force-guided relays whose external device monitoring (EDM) contacts block a restart after a weld, and two STO inputs on the drive leave no single fault able to defeat the trip. What the INHIBIT rung needs from that circuit is its third stage, drawn again here with the brake in its own loop ([[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/03 Running the Machine/12 Safety Enforcement/V4-12.10 The Fallback Ladder#^fig-12-estop-interlock|figure 9]]), because removing drive torque (${\tau} = 0$) and arresting physical momentum are distinct functions that need separate validation:

1.  **Torque removal (Stage 3, drive side):** De-energizing the safety relays drops the drive’s STO inputs, which de-energize the gate drive power supply for the three-phase inverter bridge, rendering the power MOSFETs or insulated-gate bipolar transistors (IGBTs) high-impedance, so the motor can no longer produce electromagnetic torque. STO alone does not apply mechanical braking. An unbraked motor carries momentum and may coast freely.
2.  **Mechanical restraint (Stage 3, brake side):** A separate coil circuit powers the spring-applied electromechanical friction brake. When power is removed, coil magnetic flux decays, allowing internal springs to physically clamp friction pads against the motor shaft, applying the brake without electrical power. A holding-brake rating does not establish the ability to arrest a moving load; dynamic stopping requires separate energy, torque, and response qualification.

That separation between software control and hardwired, out-of-band trip circuits is not a stylistic preference. When the processor running the control loop stalls or corrupts its own state, only a physically independent path can still remove drive enable and clamp the brakes. The precedent that the lineage table of **[[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/00 Introduction/01 The Causal Boundary/V4-01.11 The Four Bedrock Laws#^sec-boundary-bedrock-laws|The Four Bedrock Laws]]** attaches to the third law shows the cost of removing that hardware path and trusting software logic to gate a high-energy actuator on its own (war story [12.1](https://mlsysbook.ai/vol4/12_enforcement/12_enforcement.html#ws-enforcement-therac-25-software-interlock-elimination)).

**War Story 12.1: Therac-25 software interlock elimination**

**Context**: Between 1985 and 1987, the Therac-25 medical linear accelerator administered massive radiation overdoses to six cancer patients after system designers eliminated the electromechanical interlocks used in previous models, assigning safety-critical beam containment entirely to software ([[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/03 Running the Machine/12 Safety Enforcement/V4-12.16 Source References#^ref-leveson1993investigating|Leveson and Turner 1993]]).

**Mechanism**: Software race conditions and shared memory corruption in the operator interface allowed the machine to fire its high-current electron beam (25 MeV) without rotating the tungsten target and beam-flattening filter into position. Because the physical microswitches that previously interlocked beam activation had been removed, the underlying hardware possessed no independent means to detect that the operational mode conflicted with the turntable state.

**Impact**: Six patients received radiation doses estimated between 4,000 and 20,000 rads (up to 100 times the therapeutic level), causing severe tissue necrosis and three direct fatalities ([[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/03 Running the Machine/12 Safety Enforcement/V4-12.16 Source References#^ref-leveson1993investigating|Leveson and Turner 1993]]).

**Response**: Investigators mandated the restoration of independent hardware interlocks, requiring positive electromechanical verification of turntable configuration prior to beam generation, alongside formal software safety lifecycle standards.

**Systems lesson**: A software-based protection layer cannot serve as its own independent backstop. A runtime barrier filter may exercise binding authority over nominal control proposals, but the architecture still requires an out-of-band, physically independent trip mechanism to survive processor lockups, memory corruption, and firmware bugs. Moreover, because torque removal alone does not guarantee deceleration, the surviving physical response must match the plant’s dynamic envelope.

The four rungs run on two paths ([[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/03 Running the Machine/12 Safety Enforcement/V4-12.10 The Fallback Ladder#^fig-12-fallback-ladder|figure 10]]). `PROJECT`, `HOLD`, and `STOP` run in safety MCU firmware at the 1 kHz tick and reach the drives through one arbiter, which selects a single actuation command ${\mathbf{u}_{\text{act}}}$; `INHIBIT` bypasses the firmware on a hardware path that removes gate-drive supply (STO) and drops spring-brake holding current when the processor itself fails. The rungs trade response latency, control authority, and recovery cost, and the arbiter selects directly among those whose entry prerequisites still hold. A controlled stop demands verified drive electronics and sufficient stopping clearance; losing drive integrity forces unpowered mitigation with strictly weaker physical guarantees. Selection is also only as sound as the state evidence behind it. The 2023 Cruise pullover, which **[[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/00 Introduction/01 The Causal Boundary/V4-01.09 When the Responsibilities Fail Together#^sec-boundary-when-fails|When the Responsibilities Fail Together]]** set beside Tempe as a failure after impact, selected a motion fallback on the strength of a missing track (war story [12.2](https://mlsysbook.ai/vol4/12_enforcement/12_enforcement.html#ws-enforcement-cruise-fallback-dragging)).

**War Story 12.2: Cruise post-impact fallback (2023)**

**Context**: In October 2023, an autonomous Cruise robotaxi in San Francisco encountered a pedestrian who had been struck by an adjacent human-driven vehicle and propelled into the robotaxi’s travel lane ([[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/03 Running the Machine/12 Safety Enforcement/V4-12.16 Source References#^ref-exponent2024cruise|Exponent, Inc. 2024]]).

**Mechanism**: The autonomous vehicle initiated an immediate emergency stop upon detecting the impact. However, the collision-classification subsystem erroneously localized the pedestrian to the side of the vehicle rather than under the chassis, categorizing the event as a side collision. The operational design domain for side collisions specified a secondary pullover maneuver to clear traffic. Because the perception pipeline had tracked the pedestrian only intermittently before the collision, the tracker marked the lane ahead as clear, prompting the vehicle to accelerate from standstill into the pullover ([[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/03 Running the Machine/12 Safety Enforcement/V4-12.16 Source References#^ref-exponent2024cruise|Exponent, Inc. 2024]]).

**Impact**: Executing the pullover dragged the pedestrian, who remained pinned beneath the vehicle’s floorpan, approximately 20 ft (6.1 m) at speeds reaching 7.7 mph (3.4 m/s), causing severe trauma ([[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/03 Running the Machine/12 Safety Enforcement/V4-12.16 Source References#^ref-quinnemanuel2024cruise|Quinn Emanuel Urquhart and Sullivan, LLP 2024]]). The California DMV subsequently suspended Cruise’s autonomous testing and deployment permits, prompting Cruise to halt fleet operations nationwide and recall software across 950 vehicles ([[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/03 Running the Machine/12 Safety Enforcement/V4-12.16 Source References#^ref-cadmv2023cruise|California Department of Motor Vehicles 2023]]; [[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/03 Running the Machine/12 Safety Enforcement/V4-12.16 Source References#^ref-exponent2024cruise|Exponent, Inc. 2024]]).

**Response**: A post-collision fallback must prohibit renewed motion whenever state uncertainty persists. In this scenario, retaining a locked standstill and mandating physical inspection prevents the system from treating missing perceptual tracks as free space. Teleoperation feeds cannot substitute for verified local sensor coverage.

**Systems lesson**: An embodied fallback cannot infer free space from the absence of sensor tracks. Every transition across fallback states requires positive evidence verifying that assumed physical invariants hold, paired with failsafe physical restraints while state ambiguity remains unresolved.

### Industrial Stop Categories and the Safe Torque Off Trap

^sec-enforcement-stop-categories

On a moving mobile base or a gravity-loaded arm, Safe Torque Off (STO) is not an active brake: removing drive torque immediately leaves the base coasting on wheel momentum and lets the arm drop during the milliseconds before its mechanical friction brakes clamp. Decelerating the mobile manipulator safely instead uses closed-loop motor torque during the active braking phase and engages mechanical holding brakes only once the plant reaches confirmed standstill. Industrial standards distinguish machine-level stop categories from drive-level safety functions across this sequence. IEC 60204-1 classifies whether electrical power remains connected during and after deceleration. Functional safety standards such as IEC 61800-5-2 define the drive functions, including STO, SS1, and SS2, that execute individual stages of a response, and the `HOLD` and `STOP` rungs borrowed their names from them. [[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/03 Running the Machine/12 Safety Enforcement/V4-12.10 The Fallback Ladder#^tbl-12-iec60204-stopping-categories|Table 3]] maps these formal classifications to their concrete physical obligations.

### Certified Safety Functions Beneath the Permission Path

^sec-enforcement-certified-functions

Citing an unrated numerical QP solver or software evidence-age check as the primary hazard control in a safety case fails certification audit immediately, because complex solver code without formal tool qualification and hardware diagnostic coverage cannot carry a safety integrity claim. In an embodied deployment, not all firmware executing the fallback ladder carries formal functional safety certification, so a certification engineer partitions software claims by the integrity of the underlying hardware substrate. On a production machine, formal guarantees rest primarily on a second, lower layer: dedicated safety functions in the motor drives, the optical safety scanner, and the arm’s safety controller, each architected and validated to a Performance Level (PL) under ISO 13849-1 ([[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/03 Running the Machine/12 Safety Enforcement/V4-12.16 Source References#^ref-iso13849part1|International Organization for Standardization 2023a]]). A Performance Level grades a safety function rather than a component, by the architecture category of its channels, their mean time to dangerous failure, their diagnostic coverage, and their defenses against common-cause failure (**[[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/01 Machine Anatomy/04 The Nervous System/V4-04.03 The Independence Requirement and the Two-Processor Hypothesis#^sec-nervous-silicon-partitioning|The Independence Requirement and the Two-Processor Hypothesis]]**); IEC 61508 grades the same functions by Safety Integrity Level (SIL) ([[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/03 Running the Machine/12 Safety Enforcement/V4-12.16 Source References#^ref-iec61508|International Electrotechnical Commission 2010]]). These hardened functions substantiate the certified protective envelope. Above them, the MCU permission path operates primarily as an unrated performance layer designed to keep the learned policy inside the admissible set, preventing unnecessary trips of the hard safety floor. Only one narrow component of this MCU layer carries a safety rating, the watchdog that revokes authority when the chunk lease expires or the host heartbeat falls silent.

[[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/03 Running the Machine/12 Safety Enforcement/V4-12.10 The Fallback Ladder#^tbl-12-certified-functions|Table 4]] maps each response and speed channel onto its certified industrial counterpart. The core drive functions derive from IEC 61800-5-2 ([[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/03 Running the Machine/12 Safety Enforcement/V4-12.16 Source References#^ref-iec61800part52|International Electrotechnical Commission 2016b]]): **safe torque off (STO)**, **safe stop 1 (SS1)**, **safe stop 2 (SS2)**, **safe operating stop (SOS)** (which monitors standstill), **safely limited speed (SLS)**, and **safe brake control (SBC)** (which drives the mechanical brake coil through a monitored output). For the mobile base, ISO 3691-4 assigns required performance level (PLr) d to braking system control and personnel detection within the travel path, while assigning PLr b to the electromechanical parking brake ([[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/03 Running the Machine/12 Safety Enforcement/V4-12.16 Source References#^ref-iso3691part4|International Organization for Standardization 2023b]]; [[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/03 Running the Machine/12 Safety Enforcement/V4-12.16 Source References#^ref-tuv2021iso3691|TÜV Rheinland 2021]]). For robotic manipulators, ISO 10218-1:2025 classifies systems into Class I (manipulator mass under 10 kg with strictly limited force and velocity) and Class II, mandating PL d (or SIL 2) across all safety functions on Class II arms ([[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/03 Running the Machine/12 Safety Enforcement/V4-12.16 Source References#^ref-iso10218part1|International Organization for Standardization 2025a]]; [[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/03 Running the Machine/12 Safety Enforcement/V4-12.16 Source References#^ref-hartmann2026evolution|Hartmann et al. 2026]]). Because the warehouse arm weighs 18 kg, it is governed by Class II mandates.

Table 4: **The permission path above its certified floor**: Each response and speed channel of this chapter beside the safety-rated function that carries the certified claim on a production machine, the requirement that governs it, and the performance level that requirement sets for this machine. Only the first row has no certified counterpart; its checks are the performance layer. The second row is the one rated function on the MCU.

^tbl-12-certified-functions

As the first row of [[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/03 Running the Machine/12 Safety Enforcement/V4-12.10 The Fallback Ladder#^tbl-12-certified-functions|table 4]] indicates, the online CBF-QP, feasibility tests, and sensor evidence-age checks operate without formal safety ratings. Consequently, safety cases cannot cite these complex numerical filters as certified protective measures; instead, the enforcement record treats their correct execution as an operational premise. System architects face two choices when structuring this boundary. An engineering team can treat all MCU-level admission checks strictly as an unrated performance layer, relying entirely on the certified hardware floor to prevent harm. Alternatively, the team can certify the entire MCU firmware stack to functional safety standards (such as IEC 61508 or ISO 26262), subjecting matrix solvers and barrier algorithms to exhaustive formal methods, tool qualification, and deterministic execution bounds.

The running machine assumes the hybrid path. Its qualified watchdog, the table’s second row, independently commands STO or SS1 at the drive level when the chunk lease expires or the host heartbeat falls silent. Its bounded scope reduces the qualification burden, while isolation, failure behavior, timing, and lifecycle evidence remain necessary. Meanwhile, the complex admission checks remain unrated, leaving the certified optical scanner and drive-integrated safety functions to handle primary hazard mitigation. This split architecture demands strict physical and temporal isolation between the rated watchdog and unrated solver routines to eliminate common-cause faults.

↪ How the release case treats the certified watchdog as a rated safety function and the unrated admission checks as empirical premises is developed in **[[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/04 Governing the Machine/16 Deployment Release/V4-16.04 Safety Cases and Claims#^sec-release-standards-map|The standards map for the running machine]]**.

The robotic arm introduces collaborative operational modes beyond those required by the mobile base. **Speed and separation monitoring (SSM)** continuously modulates or halts arm velocity to maintain a protective clearance envelope around human workers, while **power and force limiting (PFL)** caps transient and quasi-static contact forces during intentional physical collaboration. Standard ISO 10218-2:2025 incorporates the collaborative requirements previously defined in ISO/TS 15066 ([[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/03 Running the Machine/12 Safety Enforcement/V4-12.16 Source References#^ref-iso10218part2|International Organization for Standardization 2025b]], [[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/03 Running the Machine/12 Safety Enforcement/V4-12.16 Source References#^ref-isots15066|2016]]). The 2025 standard formalizes SSM within section 5.14.5 and normative Annex L, and specifies PFL limits across anatomical regions in section 5.14.6 and informative Annex M ([[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/03 Running the Machine/12 Safety Enforcement/V4-12.16 Source References#^ref-hartmann2026evolution|Hartmann et al. 2026]]). The protective separation distance is governed by:

$$
{S_p} = {S_h} + {S_r} + {S_s} + {C} + {Z_d} + {Z_r},
$$

 where ${S_h} = {v_h} ({T_r} + {T_{\text{stop}}})$ represents worker displacement during robot reaction latency ${T_r}$ and stopping duration ${T_{\text{stop}}}$. Next, ${S_r} = {v_r} {T_r}$ is robot displacement prior to brake onset, and ${S_s}$ is the physical braking distance. The term ${C}$ is the penetration distance permitted by sensor resolution. Finally, ${Z_d}$ and ${Z_r}$ denote positional uncertainties for the human and robot, respectively ([[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/03 Running the Machine/12 Safety Enforcement/V4-12.16 Source References#^ref-marvel2017implementing|Marvel and Norcross 2017]]).

Evaluating this expression for the descending arm highlights the relative weight of human vs. robot motion. The ISO 13855 human approach speed is ${v_h} =$ 1.6 m/s ([[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/03 Running the Machine/12 Safety Enforcement/V4-12.16 Source References#^ref-iso13855|International Organization for Standardization 2024]]). The vision-path latency is ${T_r} =$ 133.6 ms, and the tool-center-point velocity is ${v_r} =$ 0.96 m/s. The gravity-biased joint deceleration from [[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/03 Running the Machine/12 Safety Enforcement/V4-12.05 Authority Exhaustion and Emergency Fallback#^sec-enforcement-exhausted-authority|section 12.3]] completes in ${T_{\text{stop}}} =$ 166 ms across ${S_s} =$ 79 mm. With these values, the human displacement term is ${S_h} =$ 479 mm and the robot reaction displacement is ${S_r} =$ 128 mm. Together, these preliminary dynamic components total 686 mm before accounting for sensor intrusion margin ${C}$ and spatial uncertainties ${Z_d}$ and ${Z_r}$.

Human movement accounts for 70 percent of this cumulative margin. Just as in the walking-person stopping budget of [[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/03 Running the Machine/12 Safety Enforcement/V4-12.04 Stopping Envelopes#^sec-enforcement-composite-budget|section 12.2.1]], human velocity during the combined reaction and deceleration phases dominates the required clearance. Against an approaching human, total stopping duration imposes a far heavier spatial tax than the robot’s physical braking distance alone. Because standard vision pipelines lack functional safety ratings, a certified SSM implementation must use dedicated, safety-qualified sensors to measure separation and bound ${T_r}$.

The standards distinguish how they treat obstacle velocity. Standard ISO 3691-4 specifies detection assuming a stationary person standing in the travel path ([[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/03 Running the Machine/12 Safety Enforcement/V4-12.16 Source References#^ref-tuv2021iso3691|TÜV Rheinland 2021]]); consequently, vehicle testing protocols evaluate protective envelopes against the static-obstacle formulation of [[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/03 Running the Machine/12 Safety Enforcement/V4-12.04 Stopping Envelopes#^eq-enforce-stopping-envelope|equation 1]]. If a worker continues walking toward the vehicle throughout the braking sequence, the relative closing velocity exceeds the standard’s baseline assumptions. In contrast, SSM explicitly models dynamic human approach velocity (${v_h}$), explaining why mobile base navigation requires an explicit aisle-crossing protocol whereas robotic manipulation requires an active separation or contact-limiting channel. For collaborative handoffs, the enforcement record adopts the standardized biomechanical model of the human hand, distinguishing between unconstrained (transient) and clamped (quasi-static) contact scenarios. While simplified models often approximate PFL as a single lumped elastic collision with a fixed force threshold, ISO 10218-2 specifies distinct permissible force and pressure ceilings across individual anatomical regions. A complete architectural map correlating each subsystem, governing regulatory standard, and validation artifact is synthesized in the release decision framework (**[[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/04 Governing the Machine/16 Deployment Release/V4-16.04 Safety Cases and Claims#^tbl-16-standards-map|Standards map for the warehouse mobile manipulator (as of 2026)]]**).

**Checkpoint 12.3: Choosing the physical fallback**

Before selecting a defensive fallback strategy, review the requirements governing state-dependent stopping modes, safety functions, and timing reserves:

**State-dependent stop**: Why can powered braking be safer than STO for a moving autonomous mobile robot (AMR), while drive torque inhibit may be appropriate after a separately verified restraint holds a stationary arm?

**Independent functions**: What does STO inhibit, and which separate functions would remove bus power or apply a spring brake?

**Timing and clearance**: Which measured detection, dispatch, drive, and brake delays belong in the stop reservation?

**Lost viability**: What can a fallback still accomplish when the admissible torque set is empty?
