# V4-12.03 The Tracker Handoff and Dynamic Reference Envelopes

[[Home|Home]] · [[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/Volume 4 Overview|Volume Overview]] · [[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/03 Running the Machine/12 Safety Enforcement/V4-12 Safety Enforcement|V4-12 Safety Enforcement]]

^sec-enforcement-handoff

Even when communication latency is zero and the permission path admits every reference command without delay, physical robots follow reference trajectories imperfectly. Mechanical compliance, inertia, and non-ideal motor dynamics produce tracking discrepancies. The feedback tracker, the closed-loop controller that turns admitted references into actuator effort, must bound this deviation across the entire maneuver. The bound is a **worst-case tracking error tube** (${\epsilon_{\text{track}}}$), an upper bound on the spatial departure of the tracked position output from its nominal reference. For Cartesian point tracking, ${\mathbf{p}}({t})$ and ${\mathbf{r}}({t})$ denote the actual and reference positions evaluated in the same spatial frame. For whole-body systems such as mobile manipulators, the execution stack defines a declared output map or metric weighting that bounds the maximum physical swept-volume displacement in meters. The safety enforcer depends directly on this bound. Any spatial clearance reserved around a planned reference path must guarantee physical containment of the actual robot body.

↩︎ The admitted setpoints and feedforward torques that the tracker turns into actuator effort are established in **[[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/03 Running the Machine/11 Trajectory Planning/V4-11.03 The Planning Handoff and the Trajectory Record#^sec-planning-handoff|The Planning Handoff and the Trajectory Record]]**.

Provided that the physical system begins within an initial qualified error region and its operating parameters (reference velocity, command acceleration, payload mass, external disturbances, and actuator temperature) stay within their validated operating envelope, the feedback controller guarantees the transient position-error bound:

$$
\|{\mathbf{p}}({t}) - {\mathbf{r}}({t})\| \le {\epsilon_{\text{track}}} \quad \forall {t} \ge 0.
$$

 This guarantee forms a software contract between the tracking layer and the safety enforcer. Rather than re-analyzing raw plant dynamics or reconstructing low-level controller state on every millisecond control cycle, the enforcer consumes this certified error bound as an invariant. However, this contract confines physical motion to the ${\epsilon_{\text{track}}}$ tube only while the operational conditions used to establish the certificate remain valid.

The magnitude of ${\epsilon_{\text{track}}}$ is a physical distance dictated by hardware properties: reflected rotor inertia (${N_{\text{gear}}}^2 {J_{\text{rotor}}}$), gearbox backlash, structural compliance, and actuator torque saturation. While a steady-state stiffness calculation balances constant external disturbances against closed-loop restoring force, steady-state metrics fail to capture transient overshoot, phase lag, or settling oscillations excited during aggressive maneuvers. Consequently, engineering teams cannot rely on static linear approximations; they must support the tracking tube bound ${\epsilon_{\text{track}}}$ with a bounded plant and controller model, defined operating limits, and full-envelope dynamic qualification tests. Tests can expose an invalid bound; the tested trajectories alone do not prove containment for every admissible maneuver.

↩︎ Reflected rotor inertia, ${N_{\text{gear}}}^2 {J_{\text{rotor}}}$, is established in **[[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/01 Machine Anatomy/02 The Physical Body/V4-02.05 Actuator Transmission Limits#^sec-body-reflected-rotor-inertia|Reflected rotor inertia and optimal inertia matching]]**.

For the baseline warehouse mobile manipulator, the illustrative dynamic qualification target is ${\epsilon_{\text{track}}} =$ 40 mm (illustrative; see the Reader Guide). The tracking controller sustains this bound only while three coupled operational constraints remain satisfied. First, reference trajectories must not demand accelerations exceeding drive torque limits. Second, external disturbance forces must remain below ${d_{\max}}$, the qualified bound on external disturbance force. Last, actuator coils must operate below their continuous thermal derating thresholds and DC bus voltage limits. A reference trajectory demanding acceleration beyond peak torque drives the actuators into saturation, causing the physical chassis to lag behind the command. Without aggressive anti-windup clamping, the integrator state accumulates severe windup, prolonging recovery long after the reference demand subsides. Outside this qualified operating envelope, the tracking certificate is void, and the system loses its guaranteed error bound.

↩︎ Continuous thermal derating of motor windings is established in **[[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/01 Machine Anatomy/02 The Physical Body/V4-02.06 Thermal Duty Cycles#^sec-body-thermal-duty-cycles|Thermal Duty Cycles]]**.

Because the physical robot can deviate from its planned reference by up to ${\epsilon_{\text{track}}}$ in any direction, the safety architecture must subtract this uncertainty margin directly from the admissible workspace. Let ${h}$ measure the spatial clearance remaining around a reference. To guarantee that the physical robot maintains non-negative clearance, the enforcer applies an inset contraction:

$$
{h_{\text{contracted}}}({\mathbf{r}}) = {h}({\mathbf{r}}) - {\epsilon_{\text{track}}} \ge 0.
$$

 A reference trajectory that maintains nominal clearance ${c}$ from a warehouse rack upright guarantees the physical chassis only ${c} - {\epsilon_{\text{track}}}$ of actual clearance. Consequently, the safety enforcer evaluates reference commands against a contracted boundary that adds 40 mm to the nominal buffer. As detailed in [[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/03 Running the Machine/12 Safety Enforcement/V4-12.04 Stopping Envelopes#^sec-enforcement-stopping-envelopes|section 12.2]], this tracking inset compounds directly with stopping distance budgets, where localization uncertainty and transport delay consume additional clearance. Mechanical wear poses an insidious operational risk here. If worn drive bearings, tire degradation, or altered motor parameters double the true tracking error to 80 mm without updating the enforcer’s parameter registry, the physical base consumes an unbudgeted 40 mm of clearance that the enforcer assumes remains available. An unmodeled tracking degradation silently undermines every higher-level safety margin.

↩︎ Clearance insets that absorb spatial sensing error are established in **[[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/03 Running the Machine/08 Sensor Perception/V4-08.07 Spatial Error and Clearance Bounds#^sec-perception-error-clearance|Spatial Error and Clearance Bounds]]**.

To maintain safety guarantees in production, the runtime monitoring stack must treat ${\epsilon_{\text{track}}}$ as an actively verified contract rather than a static design assumption. The underlying real-time middleware publishes ${\epsilon_{\text{track}}}$ alongside its validity envelope, while hardware monitors estimate the instantaneous tracking error ${e}({t}) = \|{\mathbf{p}}({t}) - {\mathbf{r}}({t})\|$ on every 1 ms controller tick. Agreement with encoder-reported position is a runtime proxy, not independent evidence of physical localization; the localization allowance must contain drift that these channels cannot expose. If an observable disturbance or actuator thermal throttling causes the monitored ${e}({t})$ to exceed ${\epsilon_{\text{track}}}$, the tracking contract is broken. Upon detecting this violation, the safety enforcer revokes the admitted command and activates a state-matched emergency fallback maneuver. Maintaining physical containment during this transition requires that the fallback braking trajectory remains dynamically feasible even after the tracking bound is lost.

**Systems Perspective 12.1: The inset margin invariant**

A spatial stopping boundary must reserve margin for the tracking loop’s qualified error bound. The runtime stopping envelope ([[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/03 Running the Machine/12 Safety Enforcement/V4-12.04 Stopping Envelopes#^eq-enforce-stopping-envelope|equation 1]]) incorporates this geometric inset alongside displacement accumulated during transport lag before admitting reference trajectories. When the safety barrier certificates of [[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/03 Running the Machine/12 Safety Enforcement/V4-12.06 Safe Sets as Conditional Permission#^sec-enforcement-safe-sets-conditional-permission|section 12.4]] are formulated in non-spatial coordinates (such as energy or momentum limits), the safety layer must map the tracking tube into a corresponding conservative bound on barrier variation, rather than subtracting a scalar distance. Evaluating barrier certificates against nominal trajectory geometry alone risks admitting references whose physical tracking tubes intersect obstacle boundaries.

Control theory provides rich methodologies for synthesizing tracking controllers, state observers, and disturbance rejection algorithms ([[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/03 Running the Machine/12 Safety Enforcement/V4-12.16 Source References#^ref-slotine1991applied|Slotine and Li 1991]]; [[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/03 Running the Machine/12 Safety Enforcement/V4-12.16 Source References#^ref-astrom2021feedback|Åström and Murray 2021]]). In embedded industrial robotics, systems frequently deploy a **cascaded control hierarchy**, where outer position loops feed intermediate velocity setpoints down to high-rate current loops. Alternatively, a **tracking Model Predictive Controller (MPC)** solves an online optimization problem over a receding horizon to generate optimal corrective control actions while respecting actuator bounds. From the perspective of the safety enforcer, the specific internal architecture of the tracking controller is abstracted away; the safety filter requires only that the chosen implementation certifies and guarantees a concrete tracking bound ${\epsilon_{\text{track}}}$ under specified operational conditions.

A tracking controller may establish convergence to the reference path under its qualified model and operating conditions. That result supplies no collision-avoidance guarantee. A well-tuned tracker will follow an erroneous trajectory directly into a warehouse support column with high precision. Control Lyapunov functions[[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/03 Running the Machine/12 Safety Enforcement/V4-12.17 Footnotes#^fn2|²]] mathematically formalize this asymptotic convergence toward reference targets; **[[05 Reference/Volume IV - Physical AI/V4 Control and Dynamics#^sec-appendix-control-clf|Control Lyapunov functions and stabilization]]** details their theoretical foundation and how optimization pipelines unify stabilizing Lyapunov objectives with the safety barrier certificates this chapter develops in [[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/03 Running the Machine/12 Safety Enforcement/V4-12.06 Safe Sets as Conditional Permission#^sec-enforcement-safe-sets-conditional-permission|section 12.4]].

This division of responsibilities cleanly separates trajectory tracking from safety enforcement. The low-level tracking controller focuses on driving the physical plant toward reference states, while the safety enforcer determines whether those references can be executed without violating invariant boundaries. The tracking error bound ${\epsilon_{\text{track}}}$ bridges these subsystems by quantifying the spatial divergence between admitted commands and physical execution. Because environmental disturbances, unmodeled payload shifts, or thermal current derating can rapidly widen this error tube, safety architectures must continuously verify physical performance against contracted assumptions. The downstream stopping envelope accounts for this tracking tube alongside sensor latency, braking distance, and localization drift, ensuring that the physical robot remains safely buffered from surrounding hazards.
