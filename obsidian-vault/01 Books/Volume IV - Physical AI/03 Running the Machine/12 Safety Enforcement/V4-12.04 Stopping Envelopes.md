# V4-12.04 Stopping Envelopes

[[Home|Home]] · [[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/Volume 4 Overview|Volume Overview]] · [[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/03 Running the Machine/12 Safety Enforcement/V4-12 Safety Enforcement|V4-12 Safety Enforcement]]

^sec-enforcement-stopping-envelopes

If the warehouse mobile manipulator approaches a rack end at its 1.5 m/s mechanical drive limit when a coworker steps into the aisle, the delay before braking begins and the jerk-limited deceleration curve carry the base past the 1.10 m clearance even before tracking error is counted. That rack-end crossing is the scene in which nearly every chapter since **[[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/01 Machine Anatomy/02 The Physical Body/V4-02 The Physical Body#^sec-body|The Physical Body]]** has priced one stopping term: the body supplied braking and its fixed allowances, the Nervous System the lease path, perception the observation age, and planning the jerk-limited stop suffix that exceeded the rack-end clearance at the drive limit (**[[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/03 Running the Machine/11 Trajectory Planning/V4-11.06 Behavior at the Seam#^sec-planning-base-stopping-budget|Base stopping budget under jerk-limited deceleration]]**). The tracking bound of **[[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/03 Running the Machine/12 Safety Enforcement/V4-12.03 The Tracker Handoff and Dynamic Reference Envelopes#^sec-enforcement-handoff|The Tracker Handoff and Dynamic Reference Envelopes]]** is the last spatial term. Every one of these terms serves the test that the first law, irreversibility (principle [[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/01 Machine Anatomy/V4 The Machine Anatomy#^pri-vol4-irreversibility|1]]), sets, ${d_{\text{stop}}} \le {D_{\text{clear}}}$. The enforcer applies that test not as one calculation but as a set of states.

**Definition 12.1: Stopping envelope**

***Stopping envelope*** is the set of plant states, parameterized by clearance and velocity along each degree of freedom, from which the resident emergency braking response, initiated after accounting for all worst-case delays, halts motion before reaching an obstacle boundary while preserving all localization, protective, and tracking margins.

1.  **Significance**: The envelope translates the abstract irreversibility requirement ${d_{\text{stop}}} \le {D_{\text{clear}}}$ into an actionable runtime velocity ceiling. When approaching a rack end, the warehouse mobile manipulator’s envelope restricts forward velocity, with the stated dispatch allocation, to at most 1.35 m/s, substantially below its mechanical drive limit of 1.5 m/s.
2.  **Distinction**: Unlike a static geometric keep-out zone that depends solely on position, the stopping envelope is a dynamic construct governed by speed, system latencies, and braking authority. A given physical clearance safely admits a slow approach while strictly forbidding a high-speed transit.
3.  **Common pitfall**: Treating the stopping envelope as a static, precomputed boundary. In practice, carried payload mass, surface traction coefficients, and motor thermal limits continuously reshape this boundary; calculating safety margins from ideal datasheet decelerations severely overestimates stopping authority when the machine is heavily loaded.

The stopping envelope is the machine’s recoverable set, the states from which the resident stop still succeeds. **[[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/03 Running the Machine/11 Trajectory Planning/V4-11.06 Behavior at the Seam#^sec-planning-inevitable-collision-states|Inevitable collision states]]** fixes the same boundary in time, as the commitment deadline past which momentum can carry the machine into an inevitable collision state. Velocity enters the recoverable set twice. Delay travel grows linearly with speed and braking travel quadratically, so a clearance that admits a slow approach forbids a fast one at the same position, and the enforcer must keep the machine inside the envelope on every tick, before a fault or an unmodeled disturbance can carry it out. The running calculation uses representative engineering limits; deployment requires each term to be qualified on the actual hardware.

The pre-brake delay ${\tau_{\text{delay}}}$ of **[[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/01 Machine Anatomy/02 The Physical Body/V4-02.04 Kinetic Momentum#^sec-body-stopping-distance-prebrake|Kinematic stopping distance and pre-brake delay]]** already contains the enforcer’s permission cycle, budgeted alongside the lease communication path in **[[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/01 Machine Anatomy/04 The Nervous System/V4-04.05 Chunk Lease Sizing from the Stopping Budget#^sec-nervous-lease-sizing|Chunk Lease Sizing from the Stopping Budget]]**. One interval stays out of the spatial sum: the dispatch-to-admission delay ${\Delta_{\text{da}}}$, which **[[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/01 Machine Anatomy/04 The Nervous System/V4-04.05 Chunk Lease Sizing from the Stopping Budget#^tbl-04-event-timing|The capture-to-brake-onset timing budget of the running machine]]** places between proposal dispatch and admission. The budget charges it instead against the timing allowance that the remaining clearance leaves ([[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/03 Running the Machine/12 Safety Enforcement/V4-12.04 Stopping Envelopes#^sec-enforcement-composite-budget|section 12.2.1]]). What the spatial sum still lacks is the tracking error between the commanded reference and the actual plant.

The enforcer accounts for that error with the tracking bound ${\epsilon_{\text{track}}}$, offsetting the baseline stopping distance of **[[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/01 Machine Anatomy/02 The Physical Body/V4-02.04 Kinetic Momentum#^eq-body-stopping-distance|equation 2.2]]**. It can admit a candidate reference trajectory only if the plant, which may lag that reference by up to ${\epsilon_{\text{track}}}$, can still brake to a standstill within the available free distance ${D_{\text{clear}}}$. Combining physical clearance, latency, and velocity yields the stopping subtotal before dispatch-to-admission travel is charged:

$$
{v}\,{\tau_{\text{delay}}} + {d_{\text{suffix}}}({v}) + {\delta_{\text{loc}}} + {\delta_{\text{margin}}} + {\epsilon_{\text{track}}} \le {D_{\text{clear}}} \tag{1}
$$

 Here, ${d_{\text{suffix}}}({v})$ represents the physical distance traversed during the braking maneuver from an initial speed ${v}$. For idealized constant-deceleration braking, this term simplifies to ${v}^2 / (2{a_{\text{brake}}})$. Under realistic jerk-limited trajectory profiles, ${d_{\text{suffix}}}({v})$ is governed by **[[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/03 Running the Machine/11 Trajectory Planning/V4-11.06 Behavior at the Seam#^eq-plan-c2-stopping-suffix|equation 11.17]]**, which incorporates the additional displacement accrued during finite jerk transitions. The term ${\delta_{\text{loc}}}$ denotes the localization uncertainty bound, while ${\delta_{\text{margin}}}$ provides an explicit protective safety cushion. The left-hand side sums the baseline stopping distance ${d_{\text{stop}}}({v})$ from **[[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/01 Machine Anatomy/02 The Physical Body/V4-02.04 Kinetic Momentum#^eq-body-stopping-distance|equation 2.2]]** with the tracking inset ${\epsilon_{\text{track}}}$.

^eq-enforce-stopping-envelope

↪ The full stopping-distance model, tracking inset included, is developed in **[[05 Reference/Volume IV - Physical AI/V4 Systems and Hardware#^sec-appendix-systems-stopping|Kinematic Stopping Envelopes and Information Age Lag]]**.

For complete static-obstacle admission, the delay term uses ${\tau_{\text{delay}}} + {\Delta_{\text{da}}}$ in place of ${\tau_{\text{delay}}}$; the resident stop and fixed allowances are unchanged. This inequality defines a conditional stopping subtotal in state space. Within this set, the accounted reaction latency and braking profile fit within the measured obstacle clearance; complete admission must also charge dispatch-to-admission travel. Operating outside this boundary violates the stopping reservation under the qualified model assumptions, though conservative engineering margins do not imply that an instantaneous collision is certain under all conceivable emergency maneuvers. Per-tick enforcement must maintain this stopping reservation over the entire control epoch until the next validated command can take effect, rather than merely verifying instantaneous feasibility at the sample instant.

Whenever a proposed trajectory requires a stopping distance in [[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/03 Running the Machine/12 Safety Enforcement/V4-12.04 Stopping Envelopes#^eq-enforce-stopping-envelope|equation 1]] that exceeds the measured clearance along the current heading, the enforcer rejects the command. Because the robot moves directionally, the stopping envelope is anisotropic. Heading dictates which obstacles threaten forward travel and consume braking clearance, while localization uncertainty and the robot’s swept geometric volume demand clearance margins around the entire perimeter.

### Sizing the Composite Stopping Budget

^sec-enforcement-composite-budget

Omitting any single interval during which the base keeps moving before or during a stop, or evaluating those intervals under mismatched operating conditions, admits transit speeds that overrun the physical boundary when all delays coincide. The composite stopping budget therefore reserves clearance for every interval in the arrest sequence: sensor transduction lag, compute scheduling delays, actuator engagement, the deceleration profile, and tracking divergence, each evaluated under the same worst-case operating conditions.

For the warehouse mobile manipulator, the tracking bound of 40 mm is the final spatial term. At the 1.5 m/s drive limit, the matched stop suffix had already pushed the cumulative requirement to 1,123.84 mm, past the 1.10 m rack-end clearance; that is the deficit **[[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/03 Running the Machine/11 Trajectory Planning/V4-11.06 Behavior at the Seam#^sec-planning-base-stopping-budget|Base stopping budget under jerk-limited deceleration]]** left open. The tracking inset carries the total to 1,163.84 mm, an overshoot of 63.84 mm beyond the physical boundary.

The ${C}^2$ stop suffix replaces idealized constant deceleration (${v}^2 / 2{a_{\text{brake}}}$) with the jerk-limited S-curve of **[[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/03 Running the Machine/11 Trajectory Planning/V4-11.06 Behavior at the Seam#^sec-planning-c2-stopping-suffix|The C^2 stopping suffix and jerk-limited braking]]**, governed by the peak jerk limit ${j_{\max}} =$ 7.11 m/s³. Planning stipulated that bound from the reference ${C}^2$ polynomial rather than measuring it; the S-curve is the stop the MCU actually runs, and the polynomial supplies only its jerk bound. Because both operating velocities reach the peak deceleration threshold, **[[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/03 Running the Machine/11 Trajectory Planning/V4-11.06 Behavior at the Seam#^eq-plan-c2-stopping-suffix|equation 11.17]]** models this maneuver by adding the displacement of half a jerk ramp:

$$
{d_{\text{suffix}}}({v}) = \frac{{v}^2}{2 {a_{\text{brake}}}} + {v}\,\frac{{a_{\text{brake}}}}{2 {j_{\max}}}.
$$

 Because this smoothly shaped stop consumes more clearance than discontinuous full-torque braking, the admissible transit speed must be read from the finished budget rather than from the constant-deceleration estimate.

Velocity remains the single operational variable the control architecture can dynamically modulate. Above the characteristic velocity threshold ${a_{\text{brake}}}^2/{j_{\max}} =$ 0.56 m/s, the finite jerk ramps function equivalently to an added delay of ${a_{\text{brake}}}/(2{j_{\max}})$ prior to braking at constant deceleration ${a_{\text{brake}}}$. Under these conditions, the pre-dispatch **speed ceiling** keeps the form of **[[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/01 Machine Anatomy/02 The Physical Body/V4-02.04 Kinetic Momentum#^eq-body-velocity-ceiling|equation 2.3]]**, the positive root of the stopping inequality at equality. The jerk ramps lengthen the pre-brake delay to an effective delay, and the tracking bound comes off the available braking distance:

$$
{v_{\max}} = -{a_{\text{brake}}}{}{T_{\text{eff}}} + \sqrt{({a_{\text{brake}}}{}{T_{\text{eff}}})^2 + 2{a_{\text{brake}}}{}{D_{\text{avail}}}}, \qquad {T_{\text{eff}}} = {\tau_{\text{delay}}} + \frac{{a_{\text{brake}}}}{2{j_{\max}}}, \qquad {D_{\text{avail}}} = {D_{\text{clear}}} - {\delta_{\text{loc}}} - {\delta_{\text{margin}}} - {\epsilon_{\text{track}}} \tag{2}
$$

 Below ${a_{\text{brake}}}^2/{j_{\max}}$, the deceleration trajectory never reaches the peak limit ${a_{\text{brake}}}$, rendering stopping distance non-quadratic in velocity; in this low-speed regime, MLSysIM evaluates [[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/03 Running the Machine/12 Safety Enforcement/V4-12.04 Stopping Envelopes#^eq-enforce-stopping-envelope|equation 1]] via numerical root-finding. Notebook [12.1](https://mlsysbook.ai/vol4/12_enforcement/12_enforcement.html#nbk-enforcement-speed-ceiling) computes this ceiling for the mobile manipulator approaching the aisle rack end.

**Napkin Math 12.1: The ceiling the budget sets**

**Problem**: The warehouse mobile manipulator approaches the rack end with rows 1 to 7 of [[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/03 Running the Machine/12 Safety Enforcement/V4-12.04 Stopping Envelopes#^tbl-enforcement-stopping-budget|table 1]] accounted, before dispatch-to-admission travel. What is the highest speed at which the jerk-limited stop still fits the clear distance, and how much clearance does a candidate aisle speed of 1.3 m/s leave?

**Variables**:

- ${D_{\text{clear}}} =$ 1.10 m (Illustrative; clear distance at the rack end).
- ${\delta_{\text{loc}}} + {\delta_{\text{margin}}} =$ 50 mm $+$ 100 mm (Illustrative localization bound; chosen protective clearance), and ${\epsilon_{\text{track}}} =$ 40 mm (Illustrative; the qualification target of **[[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/03 Running the Machine/12 Safety Enforcement/V4-12.03 The Tracker Handoff and Dynamic Reference Envelopes#^sec-enforcement-handoff|The Tracker Handoff and Dynamic Reference Envelopes]]**).
- ${a_{\text{brake}}} =$ 2 m/s² (Illustrative; credible loaded deceleration on a dry floor) and ${j_{\max}} =$ 7.11 m/s³ (Derived; the peak jerk of the reference ${C}^2$ polynomial).
- ${\tau_{\text{delay}}} =$ 133.6 ms (Derived; observation age plus lease path plus brake onset, excluding ${\Delta_{\text{da}}}$).
- Drive limit 1.5 m/s (Illustrative).

**Math**: The fixed allowances leave ${D_{\text{avail}}} =$ 1,100 mm $-$ 190 mm $=$ 910 mm. The jerk ramps lengthen the delay to ${T_{\text{eff}}} =$ 133.6 ms $+$ 140.6 ms $=$ 274.2 ms, so ${a_{\text{brake}}}{}{T_{\text{eff}}} =$ 0.5485 m/s. Under the square root of [[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/03 Running the Machine/12 Safety Enforcement/V4-12.04 Stopping Envelopes#^eq-enforce-speed-ceiling|equation 2]], $({a_{\text{brake}}}{}{T_{\text{eff}}})^2 =$ 0.3008 and $2{a_{\text{brake}}}{}{D_{\text{avail}}} =$ 3.64 (both in m²/s²), whose sum has root 1.9851 m/s; subtracting 0.5485 m/s gives the ceiling. At 1.3 m/s the terms of [[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/03 Running the Machine/12 Safety Enforcement/V4-12.04 Stopping Envelopes#^tbl-enforcement-stopping-budget|table 1]] sum to 969.0 mm, and the remainder divided by the speed is the timing allowance.

**Result**: The pre-dispatch ceiling is ${v_{\max}} =$ 1.44 m/s, below the 1.5 m/s drive limit. The candidate aisle speed leaves 131.0 mm of spare, equivalent to 100.8 ms of unallocated delay.

**Systems insight**: The irreversibility requirement ${d_{\text{stop}}} \le {D_{\text{clear}}}$, rather than motor electrical capability, dictates the operating speed. Running at the nominal drive limit violates the verified spatial budget, and the speed ceiling shifts strictly through the parameters governing the quadratic root and effective latency ${T_{\text{eff}}}$: available clearance, qualified braking deceleration, fixed margins, and end-to-end transport lag. Any latency introduced by downstream processing directly consumes the 100.8 ms timing allowance, which cannot be arbitrarily replenished.

The candidate transit speed, 1.3 m/s, sits below that ceiling, and its 131.0 mm margin is the only clearance left to absorb unmodeled pipeline delays or to serve as a reserve. That margin is narrow relative to the failure modes it must absorb. If chunk lease enforcement were entrusted to a non-real-time host operating system, an upstream planning stall lasting 200 ms instead of the nominal 60 ms lease would inject 182 mm of unbraked displacement, causing the base to overrun the rack clearance by 50.99 mm. This hazard shows why lease termination must be autonomously enforced by dedicated microcontroller hardware directly gating motor power ([[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/03 Running the Machine/12 Safety Enforcement/V4-12.07 Refusing Proposals and Enforcing State Invariants#^sec-enforcement-authority-refuse|section 12.5]]).

The observation-age term ends at dispatch, and the chunk lease begins at admission, so the interval between them, ${\Delta_{\text{da}}}$, belongs to neither. The complete operational delay therefore expands to ${T_{\text{true}}} = {\tau_{\text{delay}}} + {\Delta_{\text{da}}}$, requiring an additional spatial reservation of ${v}{\Delta_{\text{da}}}$. This phase encompasses any remaining neural inference, safety envelope projection, and inter-process communication; pipelined or overlapping stages must be budgeted carefully to avoid double-counting. A newly arrived sensor observation updates this budget only when the hardware pipeline delivers and validates it prior to actuation. Laid out on one time axis ([[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/03 Running the Machine/12 Safety Enforcement/V4-12.04 Stopping Envelopes#^fig-12-capture-to-onset|figure 2]]), the observation age ends at dispatch, the dispatch-to-admission interval follows, and the lease, one tick, one bus cycle, and drive onset bring the base to brake onset. A travel row beneath converts each interval into millimeters at the candidate aisle speed.

At this candidate speed, dividing the 131.0 mm spare distance by the speed yields 100.8 ms of unallocated timing allowance. Added to the 51.6 ms observation age, that allowance places the latest admission after capture at 152.4 ms. Added to the 133.6 ms pre-brake delay, it places the latest brake onset after capture at 234.4 ms. The dispatch-to-admission interval spends part of that allowance and leaves whatever remains as the reserve; because operating right at these boundaries spends all available clearance, the figures are qualification constraints rather than performance claims. The budget carries forward the allocation that the placement chapter makes for this interval (**[[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/03 Running the Machine/13 Workload Placement/V4-13.07 Silicon Isolation Decisions#^sec-placement-dispatch-admission|The board-link budget and the dispatch-to-admission interval]]**), ${\Delta_{\text{da}}} =$ 63.3 ms for the partitioned compute pipeline. That allocation consumes 82.3 mm of the spatial margin, leaves a cushion of 37.5 ms (48.7 mm of distance), and puts total latency from capture to brake onset at 196.9 ms. Because that allocation is an engineering budget rather than a measurement, an instrumented campaign with hardware timestamps must confirm it before the vehicle can be certified at this speed. This baseline stopping model evaluates a stationary obstacle situated at distance ${D_{\text{clear}}}$, such as a dropped warehouse tote or a pedestrian who has stepped out from an aisle rack and stopped. Under this assumption, the hazard remains fixed from the instant braking begins. If, instead, a pedestrian continues walking toward the vehicle throughout the reaction and braking phases, the obstacle actively closes the safety gap from the opposing direction. Before dispatch allocation, an ongoing pedestrian approach restricts the mobile base to a subtotal ceiling of just 0.26 m/s at this rack end.

This drastic velocity reduction occurs because an active hazard penalizes the stopping budget by the *duration* of the stop rather than merely its displacement. Assuming an ISO 13855 approach velocity ${v_h} =$ 1.6 m/s across the entire reaction and stopping interval ${\tau_{\text{delay}}} + {T_{\text{stop}}}({v})$, the spatial budget acquires an additional closure penalty ${v_h}\,[{\tau_{\text{delay}}} + {T_{\text{stop}}}({v})]$ absent from static calculations. When operating below the velocity threshold ${a_{\text{brake}}}^2/{j_{\max}} =$ 0.56 m/s, the jerk-limited braking profile cannot achieve peak deceleration ${a_{\text{brake}}}$; it ramps deceleration up and immediately down in a triangular profile lasting $2\sqrt{{v}/{j_{\max}}}$. This duration diminishes far more slowly than the linear scaling ${v}/{a_{\text{brake}}}$ as velocity decreases. Initiating a jerk-limited stop from 0.26 m/s requires 382 ms, compared to only 130 ms for idealized step deceleration. While the mobile base travels only 50 mm during braking, the pedestrian advances 612 mm toward the robot over the same window. Achieving peak deceleration from such low speeds would demand a jerk of 41 m/s³, several times the stipulated bound the load was assumed to tolerate, whereas unconstrained step deceleration would permit 0.64 m/s. That contrast arises because the jerk bound costs differently in the two regimes. Above ${a_{\text{brake}}}^2/{j_{\max}}$ it adds a fixed delay of ${a_{\text{brake}}}/(2{j_{\max}})$ whose travel grows with speed and forms the row-6 term pushing the drive limit past the clear distance. Below that threshold it stretches the stop’s duration. Against a person who keeps walking, duration is what the budget pays for, so reducing operating speed yields diminishing safety returns.

↩︎ The pedestrian intrusion distance, evaluated for a constant-deceleration stop at the drive limit, is established in **[[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/03 Running the Machine/09 Spatial Memory/V4-09.08 Forward State Propagation and Occlusion Decay#^sec-memory-belief-through-occlusion|Forward State Propagation and Occlusion Decay]]**.

Throttling nominal aisle velocity to the walking-person ceiling would severely degrade warehouse transport throughput for an event that facility layout and procedural crossing rules can prevent. The baseline operational design therefore mandates enforced physical or optical crossing restrictions at aisle ends, while the residual-claims register in **[[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/04 Governing the Machine/18 Engineering Physical AI/V4-18.04 The Epistemic Frontier#^sec-frontier-residual-claims-register|A residual-claims register]]** tracks the governing premise that pedestrian incursions into the stopping zone are procedurally prevented. Operating at the candidate aisle velocity remains contingent on empirical validation of these access restrictions. The derivation bounds stopping against a static obstruction; protecting against an unconstrained approaching pedestrian requires certified physical interlocks or external safety scanners.

Three operating regimes therefore set three ceilings ([[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/03 Running the Machine/12 Safety Enforcement/V4-12.04 Stopping Envelopes#^fig-12-stopping-vs-speed|figure 3]]). On a dry floor, fixed allowances, linear delay travel ${v}{\tau_{\text{delay}}}$, quadratic braking travel ${v}^2/(2{a_{\text{brake}}})$, and jerk-ramp travel sum to a total that meets the 1.10 m clear distance at 1.44 m/s. Even an optimistic all-weight traction screen requires a floor friction coefficient of at least 0.204, because ${a} = \min({a_{\text{brake}}}, {\mu} {g})$ assumes all weight contributes braking traction. The running machine also requires qualified driven-wheel load share, torque, and stability (**[[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/01 Machine Anatomy/02 The Physical Body/V4-02.04 Kinetic Momentum#^sec-body-kinetic-momentum|Kinetic Momentum]]**). On a sealed floor inspected to ${\mu} =$ 0.12, the ceiling falls to 1.23 m/s. A pedestrian walking toward the base at 1.6 m/s charges the stop by its duration and contracts the ceiling to 0.26 m/s. Because these three roots are subtotal ceilings before dispatch allocation, charging the 63.3 ms allocation lowers each of them. The dry-floor root falls to 1.35 m/s by adding the base’s travel through dispatch. The optimistic inspected-floor root falls to 1.17 m/s in the same way. The walking-person root falls to 0.19 m/s by adding both the base’s travel and the person’s closure across that interval. Because operating at any maximum root spends all clearance and leaves zero reserve, the remaining reserve must be recomputed for the selected speed, floor, and hazard motion. The 37.5 ms timing reserve belongs specifically to the dry, static-obstacle 1.3 m/s candidate, and an authority transition cannot claim its original 100.8 ms margin a second time.

↪ The authority transitions that must fit inside this remaining reserve are developed in **[[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/04 Governing the Machine/14 Supervisory Intervention/V4-14.04 Authority Transitions#^sec-intervention-authority-transitions|Authority Transitions]]**.

An independent protective safety scanner operates under its own hardware-to-actuation timing budget. When an obstacle breaches a safety-rated zone, the scanner’s internal optical sampling, fail-safe output switching, safety controller logic, and drive disconnect delays entirely bypass the perception dispatch and software lease infrastructure. However, the physical response must still reserve the same underlying deceleration distance, mechanical tracking error, localization tolerance, and protective safety cushion; only computational delays bypassed by the safety bus are eliminated from the sum. Industrial mobile bases deploy such protective fields under ISO 3691-4 ([[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/03 Running the Machine/12 Safety Enforcement/V4-12.16 Source References#^ref-iso3691part4|International Organization for Standardization 2023b]]) for driverless industrial trucks and ISO 13849-1 ([[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/03 Running the Machine/12 Safety Enforcement/V4-12.16 Source References#^ref-iso13849part1|International Organization for Standardization 2023a]]) for safety-related control circuits, using a safety-rated planar scanner of the kind in [[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/03 Running the Machine/12 Safety Enforcement/V4-12.04 Stopping Envelopes#^fig-12-real-laser-scanner|figure 4]], which evaluates programmable warning and protective fields. The physical dimensions of this protective field must be calculated from the dedicated scanner path’s verified hardware timing budget, rather than inheriting the software lease and vision latencies of the neural planner. The safety-rated optical scanner is a component of a separately qualified protective function, distinct from the primary perception LiDAR that generates non-safety-rated point clouds for neural proposal generation. Its component rating does not certify the complete sensor-to-stop path. In the running architecture, that path must establish the detection coverage, response time, and physical stopping performance required for protection at rack intersections. ### Phase Portraits and Speed Ceilings

^sec-enforcement-phase-portraits

Checking velocity against a fixed scalar limit fails as soon as obstacle distance shrinks: a speed that is recoverable two meters from the rack end becomes an inevitable collision eighty centimeters closer. Drawn in the distance–velocity $({D_{\text{clear}}}, {v})$ phase plane ([[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/03 Running the Machine/12 Safety Enforcement/V4-12.04 Stopping Envelopes#^fig-12-stopping-envelope-phase-plane|figure 5]]), the plotted pre-dispatch envelope separates states whose accounted subtotal fits from states whose subtotal already exceeds clearance. Complete admission contracts that boundary further by charging dispatch travel. Braking alone traces the outer parabola. The inset boundary pulls the viable set, the recoverable set drawn in this plane, inward by the pre-brake travel ${v}{\tau_{\text{delay}}}$, the jerk ramps of the matched stop, and the fixed localization, protective, and tracking allowances. At the rack-end clear distance the height of that plotted boundary is the subtotal speed ceiling, with the candidate aisle speed beneath it. The complete dry-floor ceiling is 1.35 m/s; the governor must use the fully charged boundary. Under the verified delay and deceleration bounds, the envelope is therefore a velocity ceiling that varies with clearance, and a state outside it violates the stopping reservation even if an emergency maneuver might avoid a collision in an edge case.

Evaluated on every enforcer tick, the envelope becomes a speed governor that runs in two stages, and their order matters:

1.  **Current-state feasibility check**: The enforcer first verifies whether the robot’s current operational state $({D_{\text{clear}}}, {v})$ remains strictly inside the envelope. It tests whether a full braking response from the current velocity ${v}$, with all transport lags, jerk transitions, and margin allowances, fits within the currently measured clearance ${D_{\text{clear}}}$. If this condition fails, no newly proposed command setpoint can restore the lost stopping reservation, because reducing the target acceleration cannot retroactively erase the kinetic energy or displacement accrued from the existing physical velocity. A violation at this step immediately selects `STOP`, the resident controlled stop that [[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/03 Running the Machine/12 Safety Enforcement/V4-12.10 The Fallback Ladder#^sec-enforcement-fallback-ladder|section 12.8]] defines among the enforcer’s fallback responses.
2.  **Candidate command projection**: If and only if the current state passes validation, the enforcer samples the speed ceiling ${v_{\max}}({D_{\text{clear}}})$ from the fully charged counterpart of the pre-dispatch boundary shown in [[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/03 Running the Machine/12 Safety Enforcement/V4-12.04 Stopping Envelopes#^fig-12-stopping-envelope-phase-plane|figure 5]]. It then clamps the candidate velocity proposal ${v_{\text{cand}}}$ to the admissible ceiling, admitting ${v^*} = \min\bigl({v_{\text{cand}}}, \max(0, {v_{\max}})\bigr)$. Rather than discarding an over-speed proposal entirely, this governor projects the command onto the boundary of the safe set.

If obstacle clearance shrinks, computational jitter expands end-to-end latency, or floor friction degrades, the envelope boundary shifts inward, throttling admissible velocity on the subsequent control cycle when qualified sensing or configuration evidence exposes the change. A reference clamp alone does not establish that actual velocity remains inside the envelope between checks. This one-dimensional velocity governor is the scalar prototype for multi-dimensional safety filters; in [[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/03 Running the Machine/12 Safety Enforcement/V4-12.06 Safe Sets as Conditional Permission#^sec-enforcement-safe-sets-conditional-permission|section 12.4]], control barrier functions extend this exact principle to enforce forward invariance over full state spaces.

↩︎ The clearance-parameterized velocity ceiling is established as a kinematic constraint in **[[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/01 Machine Anatomy/02 The Physical Body/V4-02.04 Kinetic Momentum#^sec-body-dynamic-velocity-ceilings|Dynamic velocity ceilings and clearance budgeting]]**.

The stopping envelope is the kinematic roofline of **[[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/00 Introduction/01 The Causal Boundary/V4-01.05 Defining the Causal Boundary#^sec-boundary-kinematic-roofline|Delay consumes stopping clearance]]** carried through the finished budget. Physical clearance (${D_{\text{clear}}}$) sets a ceiling on transit speed for the qualified braking, margin, and timing bounds. A faster inference frame rate raises that ceiling only if it reduces a delay charged to the stopping budget. Model size and inference optimizations affect the ceiling through their share of the sensor-to-actuator delay ${\tau_{\text{delay}}}$. On the warehouse mobile manipulator, every 1 ms of added pipeline delay consumes 1.3 mm of spatial clearance at nominal aisle velocity, and an unbudgeted 10 ms latency increase depresses the allowable speed ceiling by approximately 14 mm/s. Consequently, deploying an oversized vision-language model that increases latency directly lowers maximum transit speed, whereas an inference improvement that does not change the scheduled delivery tick may leave the charged delay unchanged. The speed ceiling shifts materially only when altering the physical parameters of the plant: verified deceleration authority ${a_{\text{brake}}}$, end-to-end transport lag ${\tau_{\text{delay}}}$, tracking error bound ${\epsilon_{\text{track}}}$, and localization uncertainty ${\delta_{\text{loc}}}$.

None of these governing parameters remain stationary during real-world operation. Manipulating a heavy payload in the end-effector increases arm inertia, diminishing joint deceleration limits within peak motor torque bounds. Industrial floor contaminants such as oil or water severely degrade tire traction beneath the assumed braking limit ${a_{\text{brake}}}$, while aggressive operational cycles heat motor windings and drive inverters into thermal derating. A safety enforcer that treats ${a_{\text{brake}}}$ as an immutable datasheet constant will dangerously underestimate stopping distance under heavy workloads. Runtime safety monitors must update braking bounds as observable, qualified payload and thermal states evolve. Instantaneous wheel-floor friction is not measured on this machine; an unobserved floor change invalidates the inspection premise and requires an operating restriction rather than automatic adaptation. In either case, relying on unvalidated, optimistic estimates invalidates the formal safety contract.

Dynamic adaptation remains effective only as long as state estimators remain uncorrupted and physical actuators retain control authority. If severe surface contamination reduces friction toward zero, or if sustained high-torque demands push drive inverters beyond their thermal trip thresholds, physical braking authority collapses. When required stopping torque exceeds what the physical hardware can exert against the environment, software can still detect the boundary violation, but it can no longer prevent an impact.
