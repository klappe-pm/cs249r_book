# V4-12.07 Refusing Proposals and Enforcing State Invariants

[[Home|Home]] · [[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/Volume 4 Overview|Volume Overview]] · [[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/03 Running the Machine/12 Safety Enforcement/V4-12 Safety Enforcement|V4-12 Safety Enforcement]]

^sec-enforcement-authority-refuse

A trajectory chunk arrives from the application processor on schedule, properly framed, and bearing a fresh evidence timestamp, yet one of its constituent joint setpoints would accelerate the arm directly into a steel rack upright. Nothing in the incoming packet payload marks that setpoint as erroneous. The permission path therefore grants zero executive authority to an incoming learned command until that proposal passes the configured state, input, freshness, and stopping-clearance checks. This is the third law, **proposal is not permission** (principle [[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/03 Running the Machine/V4 Running the Machine#^pri-vol4-proposal-permission|3]]), executed deterministically one 1 ms tick at a time. Rather than assuming that a learned proposal satisfies stability criteria, actuator limits, or stopping guarantees, the permission path treats every incoming vector strictly as an advisory candidate.

The resident controller and output selector translate this principle of refusal into deterministic execution. The resident controller maintains a fallback response qualified for the current machine state, while the selector determines whether to admit an incoming candidate, apply a feasible correction, or transfer authority directly to that resident response. Rejecting an incoming candidate does not instantaneously abort the command currently driving the plant. Instead, the active command continues running so long as its own evidence timestamp, lease deadline, tracking error bound, and stopping reservation remain valid.

Under this architecture, the upstream neural policy never commands the physical plant directly. Its proposals reach the safety MCU through the board link and seqlock mailbox, with no addressable memory shared between the two compute domains. Each admitted chunk drives the plant only under a chunk lease, a hardware-enforced dead-man permission[[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/03 Running the Machine/12 Safety Enforcement/V4-12.17 Footnotes#^fn10|¹⁰]] that lapses unless a fresh, validated proposal renews it. A trajectory chunk arrives as a horizon of ${H}$ waypoints, and the admission filter inspects all ${H}$ points against reach, velocity, and stopping-reservation constraints before permitting the first waypoint to execute. On each subsequent control tick, the enforcer re-evaluates the specific setpoint scheduled for execution against live sensor telemetry. If the enforcer projects a setpoint on a single tick, it does not rewrite the chunk’s downstream waypoints. Instead, the safety MCU reports the intervention upstream, requiring the policy to replan from the actual, corrected state rather than resuming execution along a trajectory computed for a state the machine never reached.

↩︎ The seqlock mailbox that hands proposals to the permission path is established in **[[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/01 Machine Anatomy/04 The Nervous System/V4-04.04 Multi-Rate Cadences#^sec-nervous-mailbox-exchanges|Lock-free and wait-free mailbox exchanges]]**.

If the proposal remains fresh, the physical machine state resides within the qualified stopping region, and the tracking error satisfies $\|{e}({t})\| \le {\epsilon_{\text{track}}}$, the output selector permits the proposal (or its minimally projected variant ${\mathbf{u}^*}$) to drive the actuators. Conversely, if the chunk lease expires without renewal, tracking error breaches the tolerance bound, no admissible command satisfies instantaneous physical constraints, or the active command endangers physical boundaries beyond the corrective capacity of projection, the output selector revokes permission and unilaterally engages the baseline safety controller. The upstream neural policy is not consulted, cannot negotiate, and possesses no hardware mechanism to override the selector’s decision.

When a proposal conflicts with physical constraints, the enforcer chooses between filtering and vetoing the command. Filtering applies when the proposal is structurally sound and a nearby admissible control action preserves the policy’s underlying intent. Consider the manipulator arm approaching a spring-latched cage door when the chunk policy proposes approaching the strike plate at 0.10 m/s. The door zone admits only the guarded approach speed of 0.03 m/s defined by the target operational design domain (ODD), the velocity envelope validated across empirical trials and demonstration datasets. The faster proposal therefore fails on both evidential and physical grounds. It fails on evidence, because no trial or demonstration sampled that speed. It also fails on physics, because the contact analysis of [[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/03 Running the Machine/12 Safety Enforcement/V4-12.08 The Minimal Intervention Principle and Optimization Filters#^sec-enforcement-intentional-contact|section 12.6.2]] shows that at that speed the force on the plate at brake onset nearly reaches the latch limit, leaving a stop that demands far harder arm deceleration than the guarded approach needs. Consequently, the enforcer preserves the approach trajectory’s target and spatial direction, projecting only its scalar speed down to the 0.03 m/s limit. The least-change quadratic program formulated in [[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/03 Running the Machine/12 Safety Enforcement/V4-12.08 The Minimal Intervention Principle and Optimization Filters#^sec-enforcement-minimal-intervention|section 12.6]] solves this projection in real time alongside the half-space constraints derived by a control barrier function.

↩︎ The evaluation record’s target ODD boundary, sampled at the guarded approach speed, is established in **[[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/02 Teaching the Machine/07 Closed-Loop Evaluation/V4-07.09 The Evaluation Record#^sec-evaluation-logs|The Evaluation Record]]**.

Filtering must account for momentum alongside position, ensuring that the actuators retain sufficient control authority to bring the machine to a complete stop before available clearance vanishes ([[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/03 Running the Machine/12 Safety Enforcement/V4-12.06 Safe Sets as Conditional Permission#^sec-enforcement-safe-sets-conditional-permission|section 12.4]]). In the latch example, scalar speed projection preserves both the goal and the approach direction. In a coupled joint-torque filter, however, a correction can alter the end-effector trajectory through task space; such modification remains permissible only when the revised motion satisfies dynamic continuity constraints and remains task-admissible. Conversely, a structurally invalid proposal (a discontinuous jump in commanded position, an acceleration profile beyond peak motor torque, or a trajectory pointed directly into an immovable obstacle) indicates that perception or inference has entered an unmodeled failure mode. Projecting an ungrounded candidate onto the nearest admissible boundary would execute arbitrary motion unrelated to any coherent task intent. Under these conditions, the enforcer exercises an outright veto. The selector permits the active command to finish only if that command continues to satisfy its own validity checks; otherwise, it immediately transfers authority to a prequalified local fallback.

Both filtering and veto operate strictly at runtime; offline training cannot substitute for either mechanism. Penalty terms, barrier losses, and reward shaping bias the policy distribution during training, but they cannot mathematically bound what the deployed neural network will emit in edge cases. The **statistical safety fallacy** conflates a bound on expected violation cost with an absolute guarantee across every execution trace. A constrained reinforcement learning objective such as $\mathbb{E}[\text{cost}] \le {\delta}$ constrains only the ensemble mean, leaving open the possibility of catastrophic tail events during individual rollouts. Formal neural-network verification can establish provable output bounds over bounded input sets ([[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/03 Running the Machine/12 Safety Enforcement/V4-12.16 Source References#^ref-katz2017reluplex|Katz et al. 2017]]), and closed-loop verification can combine those bounds with plant models. Formal proofs nevertheless remain vulnerable to reality gaps: unmodeled friction, sensor latency, non-deterministic scheduling, or actuator saturation outside the proof’s operational assumptions will invalidate the guarantee. The permission path therefore verifies every candidate control vector at the hardware actuation boundary, regardless of the optimization objective used during training. When an incoming proposal is safe, the verification check is transparent, allowing learned sensorimotor competence to pass directly to the motor drives without alteration.

↩︎ Why training-time penalties cannot bound the actions a deployed policy emits is established in **[[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/00 Introduction/01 The Causal Boundary/V4-01.13 Fallacies and Pitfalls#^sec-boundary-fallacies-pitfalls|Fallacies and Pitfalls]]**.

The chunk lease, 60 ms on this machine, bounds upstream execution authority during a host stall or scheduling delay, provided the local timer, selector, sensing, and actuation paths survive. In addition to the lease, a dedicated 100 $\text{Hz}$ host heartbeat monitors basic link viability with a 30 ms silence timeout. The safety MCU executes its deterministic 1 kHz control loop, servicing a local 5 ms hardware watchdog timer only after successfully completing an entire cycle; an independent hardware trip circuit supervises MCU power and clock integrity. If either the host heartbeat or the chunk lease expires, the safety MCU revokes proposal permissions and engages a deterministic, state-matched fallback from the responses that [[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/03 Running the Machine/12 Safety Enforcement/V4-12.10 The Fallback Ladder#^sec-enforcement-fallback-ladder|section 12.8]] defines, assuming reserved clearance and actuator braking capacities remain sufficient. On the warehouse mobile manipulator, if a proposer renews its lease and subsequently stalls, mechanical braking begins within 82 ms of the last valid renewal. This lease-expiry scenario assumes the underlying heartbeat remains active even as valid proposals cease; a total host operating-system freeze trips the 30 ms heartbeat timeout even earlier. If a worker steps out from behind a warehouse rack, the sensor capture latency of the camera frame that first revealed the obstacle adds directly to this renewal-to-onset delay, and [[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/03 Running the Machine/12 Safety Enforcement/V4-12.04 Stopping Envelopes#^sec-enforcement-stopping-envelopes|section 12.2]] carries this cumulative latency directly into the admission check.

↩︎ The lease, heartbeat, and self-watchdog timers, and why a heartbeat cannot renew a lease, are established in **[[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/01 Machine Anatomy/04 The Nervous System/V4-04.05 Chunk Lease Sizing from the Stopping Budget#^sec-nervous-supervisory-timers|Supervisory timers and proposal wire contracts]]**.

Severing upstream authority requires an unambiguously defined physical response at the lowest hardware interface. The zero-command floor defines the default physical state enforced when active software control terminates, but commanding ‘zero’ produces drastically different dynamics depending on the actuator topology. Injecting zero current into a low-friction, direct-drive actuator allows a gravity-loaded manipulator arm to drop freely unless a mechanical brake or counterweight holds the link. Conversely, commanding zero velocity to a stiff position-controlled servo can demand infinite acceleration and trigger violent inertial transients. As detailed in **[[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/01 Machine Anatomy/02 The Physical Body/V4-02.07 Electrical Power Integrity#^sec-body-unpowered-states|Unpowered states and unmanaged fallback dynamics]]**, an unpowered drive drops into one of three hardware states: unconstrained coasting, phase-short dynamic braking, or mechanical locking via spring-applied brakes. The system architect must ensure that the commanded fallback matches both the instantaneous kinetic state and the physical capabilities of the surviving hardware.

Proposal rejection is a continuous, steady-state operating decision rather than an exceptional emergency trip. The enforcer must evaluate every invariant and execute its selected fallback deterministically even if the upstream proposer crashes completely, provided local sensing, braking authority, and stopping clearance remain intact. When those physical invariants hold, runtime filtering smoothly reconciles learned proposals with safety boundaries, preserving admissible task progress within the qualified physical envelope.
