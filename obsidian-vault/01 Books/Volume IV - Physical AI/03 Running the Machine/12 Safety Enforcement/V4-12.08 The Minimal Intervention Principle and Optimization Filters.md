# V4-12.08 The Minimal Intervention Principle and Optimization Filters

[[Home|Home]] · [[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/Volume 4 Overview|Volume Overview]] · [[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/03 Running the Machine/12 Safety Enforcement/V4-12 Safety Enforcement|V4-12 Safety Enforcement]]

^sec-enforcement-minimal-intervention

When the arm approaches the cage-door latch too quickly while still proposing a useful target and direction, rejecting every such proposal would discard useful learned behavior, whereas accepting it unchecked would exceed the permitted approach speed. The **minimal intervention principle** resolves this trade-off by passing an admissible proposal untouched and choosing the smallest feasible correction, one that satisfies physical constraints and finishes before the permission deadline, whenever a proposal crosses a boundary. The resulting **safety shield**[[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/03 Running the Machine/12 Safety Enforcement/V4-12.17 Footnotes#^fn11|¹¹]] preserves useful task progress within the physical limits. For the torque forbidden by [[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/03 Running the Machine/12 Safety Enforcement/V4-12.06 Safe Sets as Conditional Permission#^eq-enforce-kinetic-torque|equation 6]], the correction should remove only the constraint violation; replacing every near-boundary command with a stop would discard admissible learned behavior.

For the already-derived torque half-space, the enforcer solves a constrained least-change problem, the **CBF-QP safety filter** ([[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/03 Running the Machine/12 Safety Enforcement/V4-12.16 Source References#^ref-ames2016control|Ames et al. 2017]]). The tracker supplies the nominal effort; the barrier and actuator limits constrain the result. The combined program of **[[05 Reference/Volume IV - Physical AI/V4 Control and Dynamics#^sec-appendix-control-clf|Control Lyapunov functions and stabilization]]**, which optimizes convergence and safety together and relaxes convergence when they conflict, is not needed here. The tracker of **[[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/03 Running the Machine/12 Safety Enforcement/V4-12.03 The Tracker Handoff and Dynamic Reference Envelopes#^sec-enforcement-handoff|The Tracker Handoff and Dynamic Reference Envelopes]]** already supplies the nominal effort, and the filter has only to keep it inside the barrier:

$$
\min_{{\mathbf{u}} \in \mathbb{R}^{m}} \frac{1}{2}\|{\mathbf{u}} - {\mathbf{u}_{\text{nom}}}\|^2 \quad \text{s.t.} \quad {\mathbf{a}_{\text{cbf}}}({\mathbf{x}})^\top {\mathbf{u}} \le {b_{\text{cbf}}}({\mathbf{x}}), \quad {\mathbf{u}_{\min}} \le {\mathbf{u}} \le {\mathbf{u}_{\max}} \tag{7}
$$

 where the half-space normal and threshold are defined by the directional derivatives ${\mathbf{a}_{\text{cbf}}}({\mathbf{x}})^\top = -{L_{\mathbf{g}} h}({\mathbf{x}})$ and ${b_{\text{cbf}}}({\mathbf{x}}) = {L_{\mathbf{f}} h}({\mathbf{x}}) + {\kappa}({h}({\mathbf{x}}))$ derived in [[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/03 Running the Machine/12 Safety Enforcement/V4-12.06 Safe Sets as Conditional Permission#^sec-enforcement-kinetic-barrier-certificates|section 12.4.1]], matching the single-axis torque constraint of [[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/03 Running the Machine/12 Safety Enforcement/V4-12.06 Safe Sets as Conditional Permission#^eq-enforce-kinetic-torque|equation 6]], and $[{\mathbf{u}_{\min}}, {\mathbf{u}_{\max}}]$ represents the verified actuator torque envelope. The selected implementation allocates 150 ${\text{µ}}\text{s}$ to this solve. Dedicated memory and a fixed iteration cap support that target; qualification must establish completion or a timely fallback under the admitted constraint set.

### Closed-Form Quadratic Programming and Torque Projection

^sec-enforcement-qp-projection

Clamping each joint axis independently, or projecting a command onto a barrier and then clipping it to motor torque limits afterward, can leave the coupled stopping constraint violated while skewing the direction of motion across task space. A single active barrier admits a closed-form projection when the projected command also lies inside the actuator bounds; if a bound becomes active, the enforcer must solve for the intersection rather than project onto the barrier and clamp afterward.

For a nonzero constraint normal and inactive actuator bounds, the single barrier constraint ${\mathbf{a}_{\text{cbf}}}^\top {\mathbf{u}} \le {b_{\text{cbf}}}$ in [[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/03 Running the Machine/12 Safety Enforcement/V4-12.08 The Minimal Intervention Principle and Optimization Filters#^eq-enforce-cbf-qp|equation 7]] admits the closed-form projection derived in **[[05 Reference/Volume IV - Physical AI/V4 Control and Dynamics#^sec-appendix-control-cbf-qp|Minimal-intervention quadratic program]]**:

$$
{\mathbf{u}^*} = {\mathbf{u}_{\text{nom}}} - {\lambda}^* {\mathbf{a}_{\text{cbf}}}, \qquad {\lambda}^* = \max\left(0, \frac{{\mathbf{a}_{\text{cbf}}}^\top {\mathbf{u}_{\text{nom}}} - {b_{\text{cbf}}}}{\|{\mathbf{a}_{\text{cbf}}}\|^2}\right) \tag{8}
$$

 where the optimal dual multiplier ${\lambda}^* \ge 0$ is zero whenever the nominal proposal is safe (${\mathbf{a}_{\text{cbf}}}^\top {\mathbf{u}_{\text{nom}}} \le {b_{\text{cbf}}}$), leaving ${\mathbf{u}^*} = {\mathbf{u}_{\text{nom}}}$. It is strictly positive when the proposal breaches the boundary (${\mathbf{a}_{\text{cbf}}}^\top {\mathbf{u}_{\text{nom}}} > {b_{\text{cbf}}}$). In that case it scales the minimal intervention vector ${\Delta} {\mathbf{u}} = -{\lambda}^* {\mathbf{a}_{\text{cbf}}}$, which projects ${\mathbf{u}^*}$ orthogonally onto the safe half-space boundary ${\mathbf{a}_{\text{cbf}}}^\top {\mathbf{u}} = {b_{\text{cbf}}}$.

^eq-enforce-cbf-projection

On a two-axis manipulator with illustrative values, suppose the tracker following setpoints from an unconstrained neural policy computes nominal joint torques ${\mathbf{u}_{\text{nom}}} = [$ 3.5, 3 $]^\top\text{ N}\cdot\text{m}$ against a validated safety half-space ${\mathbf{a}_{\text{cbf}}}^\top {\mathbf{u}} \le {b_{\text{cbf}}}$. Its parameters are ${\mathbf{a}_{\text{cbf}}} = [$ 2, 1 $]^\top$ and ${b_{\text{cbf}}} =$ 5 $\text{N}\cdot\text{m}$. The per-joint actuator limits are ±5 $\text{N}\cdot\text{m}$. Because the candidate gives ${\mathbf{a}_{\text{cbf}}}^\top {\mathbf{u}_{\text{nom}}} =$ 10 $\text{N}\cdot\text{m}$ and breaches the boundary by 5 $\text{N}\cdot\text{m}$, [[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/03 Running the Machine/12 Safety Enforcement/V4-12.08 The Minimal Intervention Principle and Optimization Filters#^eq-enforce-cbf-projection|equation 8]] yields ${\lambda}^* =$ 1 and projects the command to ${\mathbf{u}^*} = [$ 1.5, 2 $]^\top\text{ N}\cdot\text{m}$. This meets the half-space exactly while keeping both joints within their limits at an intervention norm of 2.236 $\text{N}\cdot\text{m}$. By contrast, an independent per-axis clamp that ignores the coupling and cuts only the joint with the larger normal component holds Joint 1 to 2 $\text{N}\cdot\text{m}$ while passing Joint 2 at 3 $\text{N}\cdot\text{m}$. That clamp gives ${\mathbf{a}_{\text{cbf}}}^\top {\mathbf{u}_{\text{clip}}} =$ 7 $\text{N}\cdot\text{m}$, still 2 $\text{N}\cdot\text{m}$ outside the constraint, while skewing the torque vector from 40.6$^\circ$ to 56.3$^\circ$. The per-axis clamp both violates the coupled constraint and distorts the command. Orthogonal projection satisfies the constraint with the smallest possible torque change, though it too rotates the torque vector, so the resulting end-effector path still requires checking against the coupled plant model.

This geometric behavior exposes the distinction between **torque-space and task-space minimal intervention**: an orthogonal projection ${\mathbf{u}^*} = {\mathbf{u}_{\text{nom}}} + {\Delta} {\mathbf{u}}$ minimizes joint effort deviation $\|{\mathbf{u}} - {\mathbf{u}_{\text{nom}}}\|^2$, which redistributes multi-axis joint torques and rotates the net Cartesian wrench. While a feasible projection can preserve the safe set ${\mathcal{C}}$ under the stated model and timing assumptions, it deflects the end-effector trajectory and may arrest task progress. If the upstream neural policy continues emitting unprojected nominal setpoints without awareness of the intervention, the robot can chatter or stall at the boundary. The downstream enforcer must therefore stream the intervention vector ${\Delta} {\mathbf{u}}$ back to the application processor as a telemetry signal, notifying the high-level planner to re-plan around the newly active physical constraint.

The same correction has a picture in state space and one in torque space ([[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/03 Running the Machine/12 Safety Enforcement/V4-12.08 The Minimal Intervention Principle and Optimization Filters#^fig-12-cbf-safety-filter|figure 7]]). In state space, a trajectory that would breach the velocity-dependent boundary ${\partial \mathcal{C}}$ is turned along it; in the torque plane, the projection places ${\mathbf{u}^*}$ on the half-space boundary ${\mathbf{a}_{\text{cbf}}}^\top {\mathbf{u}} = {b_{\text{cbf}}}$, while the per-axis clamp leaves ${\mathbf{u}_{\text{clip}}}$ outside it and skews its direction.

Filter choice joins a mathematical condition to a measured execution budget and the machine’s available braking authority. As summarized in [[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/03 Running the Machine/12 Safety Enforcement/V4-12.08 The Minimal Intervention Principle and Optimization Filters#^tbl-12-safety-filter-architectures|table 2]], architectural choices differ in their handling of relative degree, solver worst-case execution time (WCET), forward invariance guarantees, and directional fidelity. Independent clamping can miss coupled constraints; a CBF-QP can enforce a derived half-space if it is feasible and finishes within the verified cycle. Predictive filters ([[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/03 Running the Machine/12 Safety Enforcement/V4-12.16 Source References#^ref-wabersich2021predictive|Wabersich and Zeilinger 2021]]) trade a longer horizon for configuration-dependent compute cost.

The rows of [[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/03 Running the Machine/12 Safety Enforcement/V4-12.08 The Minimal Intervention Principle and Optimization Filters#^tbl-12-safety-filter-architectures|table 2]] belong to one unified family rather than rival techniques ([[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/03 Running the Machine/12 Safety Enforcement/V4-12.16 Source References#^ref-hsu2024safetyfilter|Hsu et al. 2024]]): each filter monitors a candidate against a **fallback policy** known to keep the machine safe and intervenes whenever the candidate would leave the set from which that fallback still works, differing only in how the intervention occurs. Whereas a **least-restrictive** filter passes the proposal untouched up to the boundary and then switches abruptly to the fallback (which is simple to implement but chatters along the boundary), a smooth filter such as the CBF-QP blends in a correction before the boundary is reached. This chapter’s enforcer combines both mechanisms: admitting a trajectory only with a matched stop suffix and holding a state-matched fallback for every reachable state forms a **backup-set filter** that checks safety by rolling the fallback forward from the proposed state ([[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/03 Running the Machine/12 Safety Enforcement/V4-12.16 Source References#^ref-gurriet2018online|Gurriet et al. 2018]]; [[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/03 Running the Machine/12 Safety Enforcement/V4-12.16 Source References#^ref-chen2021backup|Chen et al. 2021]]). The per-tick CBF-QP acts as the smooth local filter layered on top of it, and HJ reachability ([[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/03 Running the Machine/12 Safety Enforcement/V4-12.16 Source References#^ref-fisac2019general|Fisac et al. 2019]]) supplies the value function when a precomputed surface replaces a closed-form barrier.

^tbl-12-safety-filter-architectures

Operating near an active safe-set boundary can produce chattering. If a learned policy repeatedly proposes an unsafe command, sampled enforcement may switch the active constraint across successive 1 ms ticks. Depending on the plant and controller, rapid torque changes can excite a structural mode or increase motor heating and transmission wear. A tested boundary layer or hysteresis can reduce switching, but its added tracking error must fit inside the validated stopping margin.

The intervention vector ${\Delta} {\mathbf{u}} = {\mathbf{u}^*} - {\mathbf{u}_{\text{nom}}}$ also measures upstream policy health. A rising intervention frequency or magnitude can indicate proposal drift, a changed task, or a changed plant. Normal filtering can also preserve successful whole-machine service while reducing unassisted proposer autonomy; the log must distinguish these outcomes rather than count every correction as a task failure. Summed over an illustrative rolling window of ${W} =$ 500 ms as $\sum_{k} \|{\mathbf{u}^*}_k - {{\mathbf{u}_{\text{nom}}}}_k\| {\Delta} {t}$, the metric lets the permission path flag divergence for logging or operator notification while the per-tick limits still protect the plant.

### Intentional Contact and In-Contact Energy Barriers

^sec-enforcement-intentional-contact

Once the manipulator arm reaches the cage-door strike plate (${\theta_{\text{clear}}} = 0$), the spatial kinetic barrier ([[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/03 Running the Machine/12 Safety Enforcement/V4-12.06 Safe Sets as Conditional Permission#^eq-enforce-kinetic-barrier|equation 5]]) reduces to ${h}(0, {\omega}) = -{\omega}^2 / (2{\alpha_{\text{net}}}) \ge 0$, which admits only zero speed (${\omega} = 0$) and freezes the gripper before it can depress the latch. A spatial kinetic barrier operates under the premise that the robot’s objective is to avoid collision (${\theta_{\text{clear}}} > 0$), whereas manipulation, grasping, and assembly require intentional physical contact (${\theta_{\text{clear}}} = 0$). While the spatial barrier can admit arrival at contact with zero approach speed, it does not govern the subsequent force-controlled interaction where geometric separation no longer supplies the relevant margin. Once the warehouse mobile manipulator’s gripper meets the strike plate at the guarded approach, the arm must push against the latch spring, and the margin that matters is the energy and force it delivers there.

Enforcing safety during intentional contact requires transitioning from a **spatial clearance barrier** to an **energetic passivity barrier**. When physical clearance is zero (${D_{\text{clear}}} = 0$), safety cannot mean zero velocity; it means bounding mechanical energy exchange and normal contact force ${f_{k, \perp}}$. A controller in physical contact interacts with an unmodeled, potentially stiff environment. If tracking errors or neural policy oscillations pump unmeasured energy into the interface, the closed-loop system undergoes contact chattering, limit cycles, or high-force impact spikes that damage both the workpiece and the robot’s transmission.

To bound active energy exchange, the enforcer augments the controller state with an **energy tank** ${E_{\text{tank}}}({t})$[[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/03 Running the Machine/12 Safety Enforcement/V4-12.17 Footnotes#^fn12|¹²]] ([[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/03 Running the Machine/12 Safety Enforcement/V4-12.16 Source References#^ref-ferraguti2013tank|Ferraguti et al. 2013]]; [[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/03 Running the Machine/12 Safety Enforcement/V4-12.16 Source References#^ref-hogan1985impedance|Hogan 1985]]) grounded in passivity. A passive system can return no more energy than its initial stored energy plus the energy it absorbs, so a power-preserving interconnection with a passive environment retains a passive energy balance (while a stability claim additionally requires the appropriate dissipation and state-observability conditions). The tank enforces that property by bookkeeping, acting as a software tally of work rather than a physical store of energy. Every joule that virtual damping removes from the arm’s motion is credited to the tank, and the controller may spend actively only what the tank holds, namely its accumulated credits plus a fixed initial reserve. In this contact formulation, ${\mathbf{u}_{\text{active}}}$ denotes the active torque component excluding damping, so the total controller torque is ${\mathbf{u}_{\text{active}}}-{\mathbf{D}_{\text{virt}}}{\dot{\mathbf{q}}}$. The damping term can credit the tank only to the extent that the actuator actually realizes the corresponding dissipative work, and using total torque in the debit while also crediting damping would count the same dissipation twice. With those components separated, the modeled balance is:

$$
{\dot{E}_{\text{tank}}} = {\dot{\mathbf{q}}}^\top {\mathbf{D}_{\text{virt}}}\,{\dot{\mathbf{q}}} - {\mathbf{u}_{\text{active}}}^\top {\dot{\mathbf{q}}} \tag{9}
$$

 where ${\dot{\mathbf{q}}}^\top {\mathbf{D}_{\text{virt}}}\,{\dot{\mathbf{q}}} \ge 0$ is the dissipative power credited under qualified torque tracking and ${\mathbf{u}_{\text{active}}}^\top {\dot{\mathbf{q}}}$ is active mechanical power. Saturation, delay, or tracking error requires conservative bounds on realized work rather than credit for an unrealized damping request. The enforcer then defines the energy-tank barrier certificate of [[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/03 Running the Machine/12 Safety Enforcement/V4-12.08 The Minimal Intervention Principle and Optimization Filters#^eq-enforce-energy-cbf|equation 10]]:

$$
{h_{\text{energy}}}({\mathbf{x}}, {E_{\text{tank}}}) = {E_{\text{tank}}} - {E_{\text{min}}} \ge 0 \tag{10}
$$

 where ${E_{\text{min}}} > 0$ is a reserved baseline. Combined with a hard contact-force limit ${h_{\text{force}}}({\mathbf{F}_{\text{contact}}}) = {F_{\text{limit}}} - \|{\mathbf{F}_{\text{contact}}}\| \ge 0$, the enforcer establishes the in-contact viable set ${\mathcal{C}_{\text{contact}}}$. While ${E_{\text{tank}}} > {E_{\text{min}}}$ and $\|{\mathbf{F}_{\text{contact}}}\| < {F_{\text{limit}}}$, candidate neural actions are admitted to perform manipulation. If the task consumes the allocated energy budget (${E_{\text{tank}}} \to {E_{\text{min}}}$) or contact force approaches ${F_{\text{limit}}}$, the barrier filter clamps active energy injection (${\mathbf{u}_{\text{active}}}^\top {\dot{\mathbf{q}}} \le 0$), restricting active work under the modeled power balance. This energy constraint does not by itself bound contact force, because a passive interaction with a stiff surface can still produce a large force. The force channel needs its own sensing-error, response-delay, and actuator bounds before the combined constraints can justify continued contact.

^eq-enforce-energy-tank

For a contact command, integrating the work balance in [[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/03 Running the Machine/12 Safety Enforcement/V4-12.08 The Minimal Intervention Principle and Optimization Filters#^eq-enforce-energy-tank|equation 9]] over one control interval ${\Delta} {t}$ supplies the energy allowance for the same least-change decision. Suppose the current tank level is ${{E_{\text{tank}}}}_0\ge {E_{\text{min}}}$, a conservative lower bound on damping power actually realized is ${P_d}$, and a bounded contact-direction speed ${v_c}>0$ is held over this simplified interval. The spendable work is then ${{E_{\text{tank}}}}_0-{E_{\text{min}}}+{P_d}{\Delta} {t}$. Because a proposed constant force ${F_{\text{nom}}} \ge 0$ debits ${F_{\text{nom}}} {v_c} {\Delta} {t}$, the energy allowance caps force at ${F_E}=({{E_{\text{tank}}}}_0-{E_{\text{min}}}+{P_d}{\Delta} {t})/({v_c}{\Delta} {t})$. Letting ${F_{\text{cap}}}$ denote the smaller of the deliverable force and the contact-force ceiling after sensing and delay allowances, the admitted command under these stipulated scalar conditions is ${F^*} = \min({F_{\text{nom}}}, {F_E}, {F_{\text{cap}}})$, whose debit leaves ${{E_{\text{tank}}}}_1={{E_{\text{tank}}}}_0+{P_d}{\Delta} {t}-{F^*} {v_c}{\Delta} {t}\ge {E_{\text{min}}}$ while keeping force below the independent cap. The controller can therefore continue useful contact with a reduced force instead of abandoning the task. At the reserve with no credited damping, however, no positive active work is admitted. Zero speed requires a separate force check rather than division by speed, and delayed or uncertain power requires conservative work bounds over the interval.

The force channel’s own bounds can be computed for the running machine’s two contacts: the cage door, which the arm pushes on purpose, and the coworker’s hand, which it must never press too hard. Each calculation turns a force limit into a speed that the enforcement record carries as a channel ceiling, and each rests on premises that only a measurement can settle. They are derived here, where the channel is enforced; the release decision consumes the results as premises.

↪ Contact force and speed ceilings are enforced as release premises in **[[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/04 Governing the Machine/16 Deployment Release/V4-16.03 Physical Criteria for Release#^sec-release-question|Physical Criteria for Release]]**.

At the door the quantity that matters is how fast force rises. Against a strike plate of stiffness ${k_{\text{latch}}}$, an arm approaching at ${v_{\text{latch}}}$ raises the contact force at $d{f_{k, \perp}}/d{t} = {k_{\text{latch}}}\,{v_{\text{latch}}}$, which at the guarded 0.03 m/s against the 4.0 × 10⁵ N/m plate is 12 N/ms. The contact tripwire fires at ${F_{\text{trip}}} =$ 15 N, but the contact loop needs ${T_c} =$ 2 ms for detection, command delivery, and torque rise, and the plate keeps compressing by ${v_{\text{latch}}} {T_c}$ before deceleration begins. The force at physical braking onset is therefore

$$
{F_{\text{cmd}}} = {F_{\text{trip}}} + {k_{\text{latch}}}\,{v_{\text{latch}}}\,{T_c} \tag{11}
$$

 which evaluates to 39 N, well inside the 100 N latch limit. The limit still binds the stop that follows, because the plate goes on compressing while the arm sheds its remaining kinetic energy:

$$
{x_{\max}} = \frac{{F_{\text{cmd}}}}{{k_{\text{latch}}}} + \frac{{v_{\text{latch}}}^2}{2{a_{\text{arm}}}} \le \frac{{F_{\text{lim}}}}{{k_{\text{latch}}}} \tag{12}
$$

 Against time since first contact ([[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/03 Running the Machine/12 Safety Enforcement/V4-12.08 The Minimal Intervention Principle and Optimization Filters#^fig-10-latch-force-budget|figure 8]]), force climbs through the tripwire and across the loop-response window to 39 N at brake onset; the commanded stop then bends the curve over to finish at the 100 N latch limit, while an unbraked run crosses the limit within a few milliseconds. The braking term ignores the plate’s spring work, which would help decelerate the arm, so the bound is conservative. Below the limit the plate can compress only 0.15 mm further, and stopping within that distance requires about 3 m/s², an illustrative stop of about 10 ms.

The same two equations show why the approach speed is an enforced channel rather than a preference. At the chunk policy’s proposed 0.10 m/s ([[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/03 Running the Machine/12 Safety Enforcement/V4-12.07 Refusing Proposals and Enforcing State Invariants#^sec-enforcement-authority-refuse|section 12.5]]), [[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/03 Running the Machine/12 Safety Enforcement/V4-12.08 The Minimal Intervention Principle and Optimization Filters#^eq-release-trip-indentation|equation 11]] already gives 95 N at braking onset, and [[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/03 Running the Machine/12 Safety Enforcement/V4-12.08 The Minimal Intervention Principle and Optimization Filters#^eq-release-coupled-indentation|equation 12]] then demands an arm deceleration of 400 m/s². The faster approach spends the response interval on force that the stop can no longer absorb. This is the physical half of the refusal in [[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/03 Running the Machine/12 Safety Enforcement/V4-12.07 Refusing Proposals and Enforcing State Invariants#^sec-enforcement-authority-refuse|section 12.5]]; the evidential half is that no trial sampled this speed. Both results treat the arm as rigid and the plate as the whole contact stiffness. Gripper, wrist-sensor, and joint compliance in series lower the effective stiffness and slow the rise, while a geared joint reflects rotor inertia into the moving mass. Whether this arm achieves the required deceleration within a fraction of a millimeter depends on joint torque, drive rise time, and structural stiffness that only arm stopping trials establish. A compliant element placed in series on purpose turns that softening into passive protection. Springs in series add their compliances, so the combined stiffness falls below that of the softer element and the force rises more slowly, a protection that holds even when the enforcer cannot stop in time. The stiffness of that element is therefore part of the contact margin, and **[[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/04 Governing the Machine/18 Engineering Physical AI/V4-18.04 The Epistemic Frontier#^sec-frontier-passive-compliance-containment|Responses when the state remains hidden]]** sizes it for this latch.

The coworker’s hand is the opposite case, a contact the task tolerates only up to a bounded peak force. The force depends first on which contact occurs. In a *transient* contact the hand is free and recoils, so both bodies move and the collision acts through their reduced mass ${m_{\text{red}}}$. In a *clamped* (quasi-static) contact the hand is trapped against a rack upright or the robot’s own body, cannot recoil, and receives the full robot-side effective mass at the tool center point, ${m_{\text{eff}}} =$ 12 kg. The biomechanical body model of ISO/TS 15066:2016, whose collaborative-application requirements ISO 10218-2:2025 now incorporates ([[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/03 Running the Machine/12 Safety Enforcement/V4-12.10 The Fallback Ladder#^sec-enforcement-certified-functions|section 12.8.3]]), assigns the hand and fingers an effective mass ${m_H}$ of 0.6 kg and an effective spring constant ${k_H}$ of 75 N/mm ([[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/03 Running the Machine/12 Safety Enforcement/V4-12.16 Source References#^ref-isots15066|International Organization for Standardization 2016]], [[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/03 Running the Machine/12 Safety Enforcement/V4-12.16 Source References#^ref-iso10218part2|2025b]]; [[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/03 Running the Machine/12 Safety Enforcement/V4-12.16 Source References#^ref-kirschner2022iso15066|Kirschner et al. 2022]]). This worked example estimates the robot-side mass as half the arm’s moving mass plus its payload capacity; it does not compute the configuration- and direction-dependent mass reflected through the contact Jacobian. In an unbraked elastic contact the peak force is

$$
{F_{\text{unbraked}}} = {v_{\text{approach}}}\,\sqrt{{k_H}\,{m_{\text{red}}}}, \qquad {m_{\text{red}}} = \left(\frac{1}{{m_H}} + \frac{1}{{m_{\text{eff}}}}\right)^{-1} \tag{13}
$$

 This is the spring-mass bound of **[[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/02 Teaching the Machine/06 Policy Training/V4-06.05 The Simulator as a System Component#^eq-training-impact-peak|equation 6.7]]**, which equated a moving mass’s kinetic energy to the peak elastic energy of the contact, with one change: two bodies move. Momentum conservation keeps the pair’s common (center-of-mass) motion unchanged through the contact, so only the kinetic energy of their relative motion, $\frac{1}{2}{m_{\text{red}}} {v_{\text{approach}}}^2$ for a closing speed ${v_{\text{approach}}}$, can compress the hand, and the reduced mass takes the place of the single moving mass. For this hand and arm, ${m_{\text{red}}}$ is 0.57 kg, because the light hand dominates the sum; a clamped hand is the limit ${m_H} \to \infty$, in which ${m_{\text{red}}}$ becomes ${m_{\text{eff}}}$. Solved for speed at a force criterion ${F_h}$, the equation gives the channel ceiling ${v_{\text{approach}}} = {F_h}/\sqrt{{k_H}\,{m_{\text{red}}}}$. The peak follows the assumed masses and stiffness; the model establishes neither an impact duration nor a pressure or injury limit.

^eq-release-unbraked-force

↩︎ The single-mass spring-impact bound that this reduced-mass form extends is established in **[[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/02 Teaching the Machine/06 Policy Training/V4-06.05 The Simulator as a System Component#^sec-training-contact-exploit|The sim-to-real contact exploit]]**.

At the arm’s 1 m/s free-space limit even the transient model predicts 207 N, or 4.1× the 50 N criterion, so the arm cannot enter an unseparated contact at that speed. At the handover speed the two contact cases bound the approach very differently (notebook [12.3](https://mlsysbook.ai/vol4/12_enforcement/12_enforcement.html#nbk-enforcement-clamped-hand-penalty)).

**Napkin Math 12.3: The clamped-hand penalty**

**Problem**: The arm approaches the coworker’s hand at the handover speed. What peak force does [[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/03 Running the Machine/12 Safety Enforcement/V4-12.08 The Minimal Intervention Principle and Optimization Filters#^eq-release-unbraked-force|equation 13]] predict for a free hand that recoils and for a hand clamped against a rack upright, and what approach speed does the force criterion allow in each case?

**Variables**:

- ${m_{\text{eff}}} =$ 12 kg (Derived; the simplified half-arm-mass plus payload-capacity estimate used above).
- ${m_H} =$ 0.6 kg and ${k_H} =$ 75 N/mm (Datasheet; the ISO/TS 15066 hand body model).
- ${v_{\text{approach}}} =$ 0.10 m/s (Chosen; the handover approach while the coworker holds the `accept_item` grant that **[[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/04 Governing the Machine/14 Supervisory Intervention/V4-14.03 Human Authority, Reaction Time, and Clearance#^sec-intervention-human-authority|Human Authority, Reaction Time, and Clearance]]** defines).
- ${F_h} =$ 50 N (Chosen; the handover force criterion).

**Math**: For a free hand, ${m_{\text{red}}} =$ (1/0.6 + 1/12)$^{-1} =$ 0.571 kg, and with ${k_H} =$ 75,000 N/m the stiffness term is $\sqrt{{k_H}{m_{\text{red}}}} =$ 207 N per m/s. The peak force is ${v_{\text{approach}}}\sqrt{{k_H}{m_{\text{red}}}} =$ 0.10 $\times$ 207 $=$ 20.7 N, and the criterion admits ${F_h}/\sqrt{{k_H}{m_{\text{red}}}} =$ 50 $/$ 207, or 0.24 m/s. A clamped hand cannot recoil, so ${m_{\text{red}}}$ becomes ${m_{\text{eff}}}$ and $\sqrt{{k_H} {m_{\text{eff}}}} =$ 949 N per m/s. The same approach then delivers 0.10 $\times$ 949 $=$ 94.9 N, and the criterion admits only 50 $/$ 949, or 0.053 m/s.

**Result**: The free hand sees 20.7 N, inside the criterion; the clamped hand sees 94.9 N, which fails it.

**Systems insight**: Clamping multiplies the force at any speed by $\sqrt{{m_{\text{eff}}}/{m_{\text{red}}}} =$ 4.6, because the whole arm-side mass, not the light hand, sets the collision. A speed ceiling derived for a free hand is therefore a geometric premise as much as a kinematic one.

![[90 Assets/Volume IV/12 Enforcement/Margin Handover Clamp Force Envelope 8fe5556e6d.svg]]

*Two horizontal bars of peak contact force at the handover speed: a free hand at 20.7 newtons, below a red dashed line at the 50 newton criterion, and a clamped hand at 94.9 newtons, in red, past it.*

*At one handover speed a free hand passes the force criterion and a clamped hand fails it.*

The handover channel is therefore a transient-contact ceiling. It holds only while the handover geometry excludes clamping, with the handover point in free space and no rack upright, tote edge, or link of the arm within reach of the hand along the approach. Both contacts end the same way. The arithmetic is exact; what it cannot supply is the premise it rests on, whether an achievable arm deceleration, a contact stiffness, or a clamp-free geometry. The enforcement record keeps each ceiling beside that premise, and the same discipline governs every barrier in this chapter.

### The Conditions Forward Invariance Rests On

^sec-enforcement-invariance-conditions

Unmodeled friction loss, actuator saturation, solver overruns, or inter-sample drift under a held torque command can each push a physical plant across a barrier boundary even when the continuous-time inequality is satisfied at the sample instant. The containment claim provided by both spatial and energetic barrier calculations is therefore strictly conditional. A forward-invariance claim requires a feasible barrier and the following bounded conditions; the list is necessary for this design, not an exhaustive if-and-only-if test:

1.  **Model Fidelity:** The nominal plant dynamics ${\mathbf{f}}({\mathbf{x}})$ and ${\mathbf{g}}({\mathbf{x}})$ must capture the true system dynamics within a validated parameter error bound.
2.  **Disturbance Bounds:** External forces and unmodeled friction acting on the mass must remain strictly within the modeled disturbance envelope $|{d}({t})| \le {d_{\max}}$.
3.  **Actuation Authority:** Physical actuators must possess sufficient voltage, current, and thermal headroom to realize the commanded corrective force within a qualified delivered-effort error and response-delay envelope, as well as without saturation (${u}^* \in [{u_{\min}}, {u_{\max}}]$).
4.  **Solver Determinism:** The optimization solver or projection algorithm must terminate strictly within its allocated execution budget (${T_{\text{solve}}} \le {T_{\text{budget}}} < {T_{\text{tick}}}$).
5.  **Discretization Invariance:** The discrete sampling interval ${T_{\text{tick}}}$ must be sufficiently small that inter-sample state drift does not breach the continuous-time invariance boundary.

The approach-acceleration upper bound is a separate premise from the lower bound on available braking, and equality of their illustrative magnitudes does not identify their roles. Each of the five conditions maps to a dedicated engineering budget: model fidelity and disturbance bounds come from system identification and bench load testing, actuation authority from dynamometer curves and thermal limits, solver determinism from static worst-case execution time analysis on the MCU, and discretization from the loop period itself. Because the drive holds the last admitted command between samples (a zero-order hold[[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/03 Running the Machine/12 Safety Enforcement/V4-12.17 Footnotes#^fn13|¹³]]), the plant drifts for one full period before the next check can act on it. The drift covers at most ${\Delta} {d_{\text{ZOH}}} = {v} {T_{\text{tick}}} + \frac{1}{2} {a_{\max}} {T_{\text{tick}}}^2$ at speed ${v}$, where the worst case is the highest speed the base can reach rather than the ceiling ${v_{\max}}$ that admission enforces. At the 1.5 m/s drive limit, an independently stipulated 2 m/s² upper bound on approach acceleration, and ${T_{\text{tick}}} =$ 1 ms, that drift is 1.501 mm, a small fraction of ${\delta_{\text{margin}}}$. Tightening a raw-clearance check by this displacement preserves geometric separation over the hold interval under the stated motion bounds, whereas a kinetic or energy barrier requires a bound on its own evolution throughout the interval.

A condition that no detector watches is an assumption, so the permission path attaches one to each. A **disturbance observer** compares predicted with measured acceleration on every cycle. Inverter status registers and current shunts report actuator saturation. A hardware watchdog and cycle counter time the solver against its deadline. Timestamp counters on the sensor bus catch jitter and missed samples. When a detector invalidates a premise, the enforcer cannot restore permission by substituting optimistic parameters. It selects a response whose sensing, effort, and clearance premises survive, as developed in [[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/03 Running the Machine/12 Safety Enforcement/V4-12.10 The Fallback Ladder#^sec-enforcement-fallback-ladder|section 12.8]]. Both the free-space projection and contact decision must finish before the physical margin reserved for computation is spent.

### Execution Timing in the Sub-Millisecond Loop

^sec-enforcement-loop-budget

A correct barrier calculation delivered after the permission deadline cannot protect the margin reserved for that deadline. The whole cycle must therefore have a bounded execution time ([[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/03 Running the Machine/12 Safety Enforcement/V4-12.16 Source References#^ref-wilhelm2008worst|Wilhelm et al. 2008]]), including state acquisition and the final drive transfer. A real-time operating system can reduce scheduling delays, but its name does not establish a bound on shared-memory or peripheral interference. The permission path’s implementation must be qualified under the competing workloads that can delay it, so the declared cycle allocation below is a timing requirement carried into loaded placement tests rather than a measured trace or a platform-independent solver guarantee.

In this heterogeneous design, the shield executes inside a 1 kHz timer interrupt routine on the MCU. An unloaded cycle of acquisition, validation, and a typical active-set solve completes in about 135 ${\text{µ}}\text{s}$, an illustrative estimate rather than a bound. The candidate path instead declares a worst-case execution-time requirement of 250 ${\text{µ}}\text{s}$ for the whole cycle, divided among the stages below, and the whole sequence must finish inside the 400 ${\text{µ}}\text{s}$ deadline:

![[90 Assets/Volume IV/12 Enforcement/Margin Cbf Qp Cycle Budget Envelope 2059fa4535.svg]]

*One horizontal bar of the 250 microsecond declared WCET, split into the QP solve at 150 in orange, the other stages in dark slate, and the 30 microsecond reserve in light slate, with empty headroom out to a red dashed line at the 400 microsecond deadline. A small tick below the bar marks the unloaded run at 135.*

*The QP solve takes 150 of the 250 ${\text{µ}}\text{s}$ declared cycle, inside a 400 ${\text{µ}}\text{s}$ deadline.*

1.  **Interrupt Entry & State Acquisition (30 ${\text{µ}}\text{s}$):** The hardware timer triggers the interrupt service routine (ISR), and firmware latches the wheel and joint encoder registers and the latest inertial measurement unit (IMU) sample.
2.  **Proposal & Lease Verification (15 ${\text{µ}}\text{s}$):** The MCU makes one bounded read of its local mailbox, which the link receiver fills outside this routine, validates the checksum, and confirms sequence order before checking evidence age, the active chunk lease, and the host heartbeat timer. In the evidence-age check, the evidence’s age at dispatch is the difference between the source frame’s evidence timestamp and the host dispatch timestamp in the proposal header (**[[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/01 Machine Anatomy/04 The Nervous System/V4-04.05 Chunk Lease Sizing from the Stopping Budget#^sec-nervous-supervisory-timers|Supervisory timers and proposal wire contracts]]**). That age may not exceed 51.8 ms, which combines the 51.6 ms observation age charged in row 5 of [[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/03 Running the Machine/12 Safety Enforcement/V4-12.04 Stopping Envelopes#^tbl-enforcement-stopping-budget|table 1]] with 0.2 ms of clock uncertainty (**[[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/03 Running the Machine/08 Sensor Perception/V4-08.09 The Observation Contract#^sec-perception-observation-contract|The Observation Contract]]**). Passing that test qualifies the evidence only at dispatch, so the MCU also reconstructs the evidence’s current age from its source epoch and elapsed time and checks it against the evidence’s own validity deadline and the remaining physical margin. Inference, admission, waiting, and transfer after dispatch must already be reserved once in that margin through the dispatch-to-admission allocation inside the timing allowance. Otherwise fresh evidence is required before activation, because a new proposal timestamp cannot renew old evidence. The MCU then verifies that elapsed time since valid admission or renewal has not exceeded the 60 ms active chunk lease and reads the host heartbeat timer, which marks the host silent once no heartbeat has arrived within the 30 ms timeout.
3.  **Tracking Contract Verification (10 ${\text{µ}}\text{s}$):** Firmware evaluates instantaneous tracking error norm $\|{e}({t})\| = \|{\mathbf{p}}({t}) - {\mathbf{r}}({t})\|$ against the qualified bound ${\epsilon_{\text{track}}}$.
4.  **CBF-QP Safety Shield Solve (150 ${\text{µ}}\text{s}$):** An active-set QP solver[[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/03 Running the Machine/12 Safety Enforcement/V4-12.17 Footnotes#^fn14|¹⁴]] running in pre-allocated static SRAM, the MCU’s tightly coupled data memory, evaluates active barrier inequalities and computes ${\mathbf{u}^*} = \arg\min_{{\mathbf{u}} \in \mathbb{R}^{m}} \frac{1}{2}\|{\mathbf{u}} - {\mathbf{u}_{\text{nom}}}\|^2$. A fixed iteration cap limits the work count; meeting the allocation also requires bounded per-iteration arithmetic, memory access, interrupt interference, and output service, including ill-conditioned constraint geometry. Dynamic memory allocation is forbidden on this path so that heap service is excluded from that timing obligation. In this two-processor design no memory is shared with the host, so camera direct memory access (DMA) streams cannot reach the memory this cycle uses. The couplings that remain are the link receiver, whose DMA writes into the MCU’s own memory, and any shared supply rail or clock; **[[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/03 Running the Machine/13 Workload Placement/V4-13.09 The Placement Record#^sec-placement-hardware-allocation|The Placement Record]]** measures that residual interference and tests whether the isolation holds under load.
5.  **Output Selection & Admitted Command Staging (15 ${\text{µ}}\text{s}$):** If the QP is feasible and constraints hold, the enforcer emits the **admitted command**. The drives close the velocity and current loops. Because industrial servo drives running in position mode choose torque direction internally from position tracking error while an unsigned torque limit clips only the magnitude of each joint’s effort symmetrically, a position setpoint plus an unsigned torque limit does not by itself realize the signed braking effort or coupled multi-axis half-space the QP requires. This interface needs a qualified mapping from the staged command to delivered effort, with bounded realization error and response delay. The filter tightens each commanded half-space by the largest possible projection of that effort error along its normal, so every effort the drive may realize still satisfies the delivered-effort constraint. Near full braking, that allowance may remove feasibility and require earlier intervention. The drive configuration and qualification evidence must establish these bounds; this example supplies no measured error or delay. During the response interval, a previously held command must remain valid and the intersample/response allowance must cover its effect, charging each delay once. If the position/limit interface cannot satisfy this contract, the design needs a qualified effort interface or a stricter qualified operating envelope and fallback. The next position setpoint and per-axis torque limit travel in the next EtherCAT frame only under that realization contract. Drive-reported torque on the following tick can detect a breach but cannot retroactively certify the preceding interval; an invalid replacement leaves previously admitted setpoints in force only while their own evidence, lease, state, and stopping reservation remain valid.
6.  **Completion & Reserve (30 ${\text{µ}}\text{s}$):** The MCU checks the register write and services its own watchdog only after the cycle succeeds. The reserve absorbs variation in the stages above; a cycle that has not completed by the deadline requires a qualified preempting timer or an independent mechanism to select the resident fallback before the next tick. The overrunning ISR cannot be assumed to detect and recover from its own stall. Only with that bounded transfer does a missed tick cost the base 1.3 mm of travel at the aisle speed. **[[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/03 Running the Machine/13 Workload Placement/V4-13.08 Empirical Verification of Silicon Isolation#^sec-placement-testing-separation|Empirical Verification of Silicon Isolation]]** tests whether the declared WCET holds under load, so that ticks are not missed.

↪ The schedulability analysis that a declared WCET and deadline feed is developed in **[[05 Reference/Volume IV - Physical AI/V4 Systems and Hardware#^sec-appendix-systems-timers|Real-Time Timers, Watchdogs, and Schedulability]]**.

Stage 5 is also where refusal takes effect. Any of several conditions triggers it. The chunk lease may have expired, the host heartbeat may have fallen silent, the shared drive bus may report undervoltage, tracking error $\|{e}({t})\|$ may exceed ${\epsilon_{\text{track}}}$, actuator saturation may render the feasible control set empty (${\mathcal{U}_{\text{safe}}} = \emptyset$), or an operating-contract detector such as the disturbance observer or the solver deadline may trip. In any of these cases, the output selector withholds the admitted command, revokes policy authority, and selects the state-matched stop. At standstill, lease or heartbeat expiry selects HOLD, the powered standstill hold that [[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/03 Running the Machine/12 Safety Enforcement/V4-12.10 The Fallback Ladder#^sec-enforcement-fallback-ladder|section 12.8]] defines, only while powered support remains available. Undervoltage instead requires a qualified transition to mechanical restraint before that support expires. In motion, STOP under undervoltage remains conditional on drive survival.

**Checkpoint 12.2: Barrier, projection, and cycle budget**

The barrier fixes which commands preserve the stop; the filter and its cycle decide whether the chosen command arrives in time.

**Relative degree**: Why does a barrier on raw distance give torque no control authority at the boundary, and what does the kinetic barrier of [[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/03 Running the Machine/12 Safety Enforcement/V4-12.06 Safe Sets as Conditional Permission#^eq-enforce-kinetic-barrier|equation 5]] add to restore it?

**Projection vs. clamp**: Why can clamping each joint separately leave a coupled half-space violated, while [[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/03 Running the Machine/12 Safety Enforcement/V4-12.08 The Minimal Intervention Principle and Optimization Filters#^eq-enforce-cbf-projection|equation 8]] satisfies it with the smallest torque change?

**Projection estimate**: Treat the latch approach as a one-dimensional speed command whose half-space is the 0.03 m/s target-ODD ceiling. What are ${\lambda}^*$ and the admitted speed when the chunk policy proposes 0.10 m/s?

**Timing premise**: Which of the five forward-invariance conditions do the declared 250 ${\text{µ}}\text{s}$ WCET and the 400 ${\text{µ}}\text{s}$ deadline defend, and what must select the fallback when a cycle overruns?

Every stage of this cycle assumes that the solve returns a feasible command. When saturation empties the admissible torque set, the lease lapses, or the cycle overruns its deadline, projection has nothing to return, and the permission path must already hold a response it can execute without the proposer. Executing that check deterministically while surviving proposer faults requires placing the enforcer where the host processor cannot preempt or bypass it.
