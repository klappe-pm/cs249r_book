# V4-12.06 Safe Sets as Conditional Permission

[[Home|Home]] · [[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/Volume 4 Overview|Volume Overview]] · [[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/03 Running the Machine/12 Safety Enforcement/V4-12 Safety Enforcement|V4-12 Safety Enforcement]]

^sec-enforcement-safe-sets-conditional-permission

Once gravitational bias torque is counted, the descending arm analyzed in [[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/03 Running the Machine/12 Safety Enforcement/V4-12.05 Authority Exhaustion and Emergency Fallback#^sec-enforcement-exhausted-authority|section 12.3]] requires 79.45 mm of tool travel to brake to a complete stop, and this stopping distance scales quadratically with approach speed. A clearance margin that guarantees safety at one velocity fails completely at a higher velocity. The runtime safety enforcer therefore requires a joint predicate over position and velocity that it can evaluate synchronously on every tick of the control clock.

While a stopping budget establishes a static physical margin, a control barrier function[[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/03 Running the Machine/12 Safety Enforcement/V4-12.17 Footnotes#^fn4|⁴]] transforms that spatial margin into an instantaneous constraint on the next actuator command ([[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/03 Running the Machine/12 Safety Enforcement/V4-12.16 Source References#^ref-ames2014control|Ames et al. 2014]]; [[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/03 Running the Machine/12 Safety Enforcement/V4-12.16 Source References#^ref-ames2019control|Ames et al. 2019]]). This formulation belongs to the broader family of **safety filters** that monitor candidate action proposals against certified fallbacks, intervening only when necessary ([[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/03 Running the Machine/12 Safety Enforcement/V4-12.16 Source References#^ref-hsu2024safetyfilter|Hsu et al. 2024]]) so that the machine can execute aggressive tasks while guaranteeing sufficient braking authority to halt.

A **safe set** ${\mathcal{C}}$ collects all system states for which a scalar certificate ${h}({\mathbf{x}})$ remains nonnegative. Its boundary represents zero margin, whereas its interior provides positive margin. For the manipulator arm, both position and velocity must enter this certificate because a physical clearance that safely accommodates a slow approach must reject a high-speed approach at the identical position:

$$
{\mathcal{C}} = \{{\mathbf{x}} \in {\mathcal{X}} \mid {h}({\mathbf{x}}) \ge 0\}, \quad {\partial \mathcal{C}} = \{{\mathbf{x}} \in {\mathcal{X}} \mid {h}({\mathbf{x}}) = 0\}, \quad \text{Int}({\mathcal{C}}) = \{{\mathbf{x}} \in {\mathcal{X}} \mid {h}({\mathbf{x}}) > 0\}.
$$

 Ensuring that trajectories remain inside ${\mathcal{C}}$ is known as forward invariance, the physical counterpart to an **inductive loop invariant** in formal software verification:

$$
{h}({\mathbf{x}}_0) \ge 0 \quad \land \quad {\dot{h}}({\mathbf{x}}, {\mathbf{u}}) \ge -{\kappa}({h}({\mathbf{x}})) \quad \implies \quad {h}({\mathbf{x}}_t) \ge 0 \quad \forall {t} \ge 0. \tag{4}
$$

 This implication holds in continuous time under valid plant models and actuator feasibility. Nagumo’s theorem[[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/03 Running the Machine/12 Safety Enforcement/V4-12.17 Footnotes#^fn5|⁵]] ([[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/03 Running the Machine/12 Safety Enforcement/V4-12.16 Source References#^ref-blanchini1999set|Blanchini 1999]]) provides the geometric boundary interpretation: the closed-loop vector field at the boundary ${\partial \mathcal{C}}$ must point tangent to or back into the set. The comparison function ${\kappa}(\cdot)$, chosen as an extended class-${\mathcal{K}}$ function, strictly increases with safety margin and vanishes at zero. This allows rapid approach motions deep inside the interior of ${\mathcal{C}}$ while enforcing a nondecreasing safety margin along the perimeter. In an embedded implementation, discrete-time sampling, state estimation noise, and torque delivery latency demand additional safety buffers before continuous-time invariance translates into physical reliability.

^eq-enforce-forward-invariance

**Definition 12.2: Control barrier function**

A ***control barrier function*** is a continuously differentiable scalar function ${h}({\mathbf{x}})$ of the plant state whose 0-superlevel set defines the safe set ${\mathcal{C}}$, and whose directional derivative can be directly influenced by the control input. Restricting actuation to control inputs that satisfy ${\dot{h}}({\mathbf{x}}, {\mathbf{u}}) \ge -{\kappa}({h}({\mathbf{x}}))$ guarantees that any trajectory initialized within ${\mathcal{C}}$ remains inside ${\mathcal{C}}$ for all time (forward invariance, [[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/03 Running the Machine/12 Safety Enforcement/V4-12.06 Safe Sets as Conditional Permission#^eq-enforce-forward-invariance|equation 4]]), provided the plant model, actuator limits, and timing assumptions remain valid.

1.  **Significance**: The function translates an empirical stopping margin into an admissible half-space constraint on the next control cycle. For the descending arm of [[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/03 Running the Machine/12 Safety Enforcement/V4-12.05 Authority Exhaustion and Emergency Fallback#^sec-enforcement-exhausted-authority|section 12.3]], its zero level set sits exactly where remaining tool clearance equals the biased stopping distance of 79.45 mm; closer than this boundary, no admissible torque command can keep the system within ${\mathcal{C}}$.
2.  **Distinction**: Whereas a Lyapunov function certifies asymptotic convergence toward an equilibrium state, a control barrier function certifies set invariance, proving that system trajectories never exit an admissible operating region. The barrier function provides the mathematical certificate; the least-change quadratic program filter of [[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/03 Running the Machine/12 Safety Enforcement/V4-12.08 The Minimal Intervention Principle and Optimization Filters#^sec-enforcement-minimal-intervention|section 12.6]] provides the runtime mechanism to enforce it.
3.  **Common pitfall**: Formulating ${h}$ strictly as raw geometric distance. Because distance has relative degree two with respect to torque (commanded torque produces acceleration, which integrates to velocity, which in turn integrates to position), the control input has zero direct authority over ${\dot{h}}$ at the boundary. A filter monitoring position alone reacts only after physical momentum has made collision unavoidable. An effective barrier must incorporate approach velocity and net braking deceleration, as shown in [[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/03 Running the Machine/12 Safety Enforcement/V4-12.06 Safe Sets as Conditional Permission#^sec-enforcement-kinetic-barrier-certificates|section 12.4.1]].

### Kinetic Barrier Certificates and Relative Degree

^sec-enforcement-kinetic-barrier-certificates

On the descending arm, a barrier function written purely in terms of remaining spatial distance ${\theta_{\text{clear}}}$ leaves commanded motor torque out of its first time derivative, so the safety filter remains blind to closing velocity until the arm reaches the boundary with momentum it can no longer arrest. Across the causal boundary, high-level software proposals meet continuous physical plant dynamics governed by a control-affine differential equation:

$$
{\dot{\mathbf{x}}} = {\mathbf{f}}({\mathbf{x}}) + {\mathbf{g}}({\mathbf{x}}){\mathbf{u}}
$$

 where ${\mathbf{f}}({\mathbf{x}})$ captures unforced physical drift (including inertia, Coriolis forces, gravitational bias, and passive friction), ${\mathbf{g}}({\mathbf{x}})$ maps control inputs to generalized accelerations, and ${\mathbf{u}}$ represents delivered joint torques. On this robot, embedded motor drives close the internal current and velocity loops, meaning the MCU does not write raw voltages to the coils; the per-tick cycle described in [[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/03 Running the Machine/12 Safety Enforcement/V4-12.08 The Minimal Intervention Principle and Optimization Filters#^sec-enforcement-loop-budget|section 12.6.4]] converts a torque-space decision into the setpoint and torque limit the drive accepts under the qualified delivered-effort realization contract developed there.

Evaluating how a scalar barrier certificate ${h}({\mathbf{x}})$ evolves along state trajectories requires computing its directional time derivative. By the multivariable chain rule, the total time derivative ${\dot{h}}$ expands across the state velocity ${\dot{\mathbf{x}}}$:

$$
{\dot{h}}({\mathbf{x}}, {\mathbf{u}}) = \frac{d{h}}{d{t}} = \frac{\partial {h}}{\partial {\mathbf{x}}} {\dot{\mathbf{x}}} = \nabla {h}({\mathbf{x}}) \cdot {\dot{\mathbf{x}}} = \underbrace{\nabla {h}({\mathbf{x}}) \cdot {\mathbf{f}}({\mathbf{x}})}_{{L_{\mathbf{f}} h}({\mathbf{x}})} + \underbrace{\left(\nabla {h}({\mathbf{x}}) \cdot {\mathbf{g}}({\mathbf{x}})\right)}_{{L_{\mathbf{g}} h}({\mathbf{x}})} {\mathbf{u}}
$$

 Nonlinear control theory designates ${L_{\mathbf{f}} h}$ and ${L_{\mathbf{g}} h}$ as Lie derivatives.[[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/03 Running the Machine/12 Safety Enforcement/V4-12.17 Footnotes#^fn6|⁶]] In modern computational systems, these directional derivatives are standard **Jacobian-vector products (JVPs)** supported natively by autodiff frameworks such as JAX and PyTorch:

$$
{L_{\mathbf{f}} h}({\mathbf{x}}) = \nabla {h}({\mathbf{x}})^\top {\mathbf{f}}({\mathbf{x}}) = \text{JVP}({h}, {\mathbf{x}}, {\mathbf{f}}({\mathbf{x}})), \qquad {L_{\mathbf{g}} h}({\mathbf{x}}) = \nabla {h}({\mathbf{x}})^\top {\mathbf{g}}({\mathbf{x}}) = \text{JVP}({h}, {\mathbf{x}}, {\mathbf{g}}({\mathbf{x}})).
$$

 In forward-mode automatic differentiation, a JVP evaluates the derivative along a single tangent vector. When the control matrix ${\mathbf{g}}({\mathbf{x}})$ contains multiple input columns ${\mathbf{g}}_j({\mathbf{x}})$, the notation $\operatorname{JVP}({h}, {\mathbf{x}}, {\mathbf{g}})$ denotes the row vector of individual channel products $\operatorname{JVP}({h}, {\mathbf{x}}, {\mathbf{g}}_j)$. The drift term and the control-channel terms capture distinct physical dynamics:

1.  ${L_{\mathbf{f}} h}({\mathbf{x}}) = \text{JVP}({h}, {\mathbf{x}}, {\mathbf{f}}({\mathbf{x}}))$ is the **system drift JVP**, measuring the rate at which safety margin changes due solely to momentum, gravitational load, and passive dynamics.
2.  ${L_{\mathbf{g}} h}({\mathbf{x}}) = \text{JVP}({h}, {\mathbf{x}}, {\mathbf{g}}({\mathbf{x}}))$ is the **control authority JVP**, measuring the instantaneous sensitivity of the barrier rate of change to commanded actuator torque across the causal boundary.

Formulating an effective barrier certificate requires avoiding the **relative degree trap**. In computational graph terms, relative degree is the graph distance, counted in time-integrator nodes, between the commanded control input ${\mathbf{u}}$ and the barrier certificate ${h}({\mathbf{x}})$. If an engineer defines a safety certificate purely as geometric clearance, ${h}({\mathbf{x}}) = {x_{\text{obstacle}}} - {x_{\text{robot}}} \ge 0$, the commanded actuation is separated from position by two successive integrations:

$$
\text{Actuation } {u} \xrightarrow{\int \frac{1}{{m}} d{t}} \text{Velocity } {v_{\text{robot}}} \xrightarrow{\int d{t}} \text{Position } {x_{\text{robot}}}
$$

 This two-stage integration cascade establishes relative degree ${r} = 2$. Taking the first time derivative of clearance yields the closing velocity, ${\dot{h}} = -{v_{\text{robot}}}$, which contains no control input whatsoever (${L_{\mathbf{g}} h}({\mathbf{x}}) = \text{JVP}({h}, {\mathbf{x}}, {\mathbf{g}}({\mathbf{x}})) = {\mathbf{0}}$). Because the control authority JVP evaluates to zero, motor torque has zero instantaneous authority over the rate of barrier decay. At the boundary of the safe set (${h} = 0$), enforcing forward invariance ${\dot{h}} \ge -{\kappa}(0) = 0$ requires $-{v_{\text{robot}}} \ge 0 \implies {v_{\text{robot}}} \le 0$. Halting a moving system instantaneously would require infinite deceleration and unbounded torque (${u} = -\infty$). Applying this first-derivative condition to position alone provides no torque constraint before the geometric boundary. Higher-order barrier formulations can retain a position constraint and differentiate until the input appears; this chapter instead incorporates the stopping distance explicitly.

To restore nonzero control authority (${L_{\mathbf{g}} h} \neq {\mathbf{0}}$), the barrier certificate must explicitly incorporate kinetic energy and braking limits. For an approaching arm joint, let ${\theta_{\text{clear}}} = {d_{\text{tool}}} / {r_{\text{arm}}}$ represent the remaining angular clearance, ${\omega} > 0$ the approach speed, ${J_{\text{eff}}} {\dot{\omega}} = {\tau} + {\tau_{\text{bias}}}$ the joint dynamics, and ${\alpha_{\text{net}}}$ a validated lower bound on net available angular braking deceleration. Adopting the linear class-${\mathcal{K}}$ function ${\kappa}({h}) = {\gamma_{\text{cbf}}} {h}$, where ${\gamma_{\text{cbf}}} > 0$ is a constant approach-rate gain, the **kinetic barrier certificate** is:

$$
{h}({\theta_{\text{clear}}},{\omega})={\theta_{\text{clear}}}-\frac{{\omega}^2}{2{\alpha_{\text{net}}}}. \tag{5}
$$

 Differentiating ${h}({\theta_{\text{clear}}},{\omega})$ along the joint dynamics yields ${\dot{h}} = {L_{\mathbf{f}} h} + {L_{\mathbf{g}} h}\,{\tau}$, where ${L_{\mathbf{g}} h} = -\frac{{\omega}}{{J_{\text{eff}}}{\alpha_{\text{net}}}} \ne 0$ for any nonzero approach speed ${\omega} > 0$. Because commanded torque ${\tau}$ appears directly in this first time derivative with a nonzero coefficient, the kinetic barrier achieves **relative degree one** (${r} = 1$). Actuator commands now exert immediate control authority across the causal boundary. Enforcing the forward-invariance condition ${\dot{h}} + {\gamma_{\text{cbf}}} {h} \ge 0$ isolates the admissible torque into an explicit affine half-space constraint:[[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/03 Running the Machine/12 Safety Enforcement/V4-12.17 Footnotes#^fn7|⁷]]

$$
{\tau}\le-{\tau_{\text{bias}}}+{J_{\text{eff}}}{\alpha_{\text{net}}}\left(\frac{{\gamma_{\text{cbf}}} {h}}{{\omega}}-1\right),\qquad -{\tau_{\max}}\le{\tau}\le{\tau_{\max}}. \tag{6}
$$

 In this sign convention, positive angular velocity ${\omega}$, positive gravitational bias ${\tau_{\text{bias}}}$, and positive motor torque ${\tau}$ all act downward toward the workstation so that clearance shrinks at rate ${\dot{\theta}_{\text{clear}}} = -{\omega}$, and a negative upper bound on ${\tau}$ in [[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/03 Running the Machine/12 Safety Enforcement/V4-12.06 Safe Sets as Conditional Permission#^eq-enforce-kinetic-torque|equation 6]] therefore sets a minimum magnitude on the upward braking torque $-{\tau}$ that the motor must apply against gravity. This condition defines the linear half-space ${\mathbf{a}_{\text{cbf}}}({\mathbf{x}})^\top {\mathbf{u}} \le {b_{\text{cbf}}}({\mathbf{x}})$ solved by the control barrier function quadratic program (CBF-QP) filter developed next, with the half-space normal and scalar threshold determined directly by the Jacobian-vector products: ${\mathbf{a}_{\text{cbf}}}({\mathbf{x}})^\top = -{L_{\mathbf{g}} h}({\mathbf{x}})$ and ${b_{\text{cbf}}}({\mathbf{x}}) = {L_{\mathbf{f}} h}({\mathbf{x}}) + {\gamma_{\text{cbf}}} {h}({\mathbf{x}})$. At standstill (${\omega} = 0$), the microcontroller firmware applies a separate static acceleration and position-hold check to prevent division by zero.[[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/03 Running the Machine/12 Safety Enforcement/V4-12.17 Footnotes#^fn8|⁸]]

Applying this kinetic barrier to the descending-arm parameters from [[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/03 Running the Machine/12 Safety Enforcement/V4-12.05 Authority Exhaustion and Emergency Fallback#^sec-enforcement-exhausted-authority|section 12.3]] sets concrete torque ceilings across the approach. The moment of inertia is ${J_{\text{eff}}} =$ 3 kg·m². The gravitational bias torque is ${\tau_{\text{bias}}} =$ 65.25 N·m, directed toward the workstation. The actuator saturation limit is $|{\tau}| \le$ 87 N·m, so the net angular braking deceleration is ${\alpha_{\text{net}}} = ({\tau_{\max}} - {\tau_{\text{bias}}}) / {J_{\text{eff}}} =$ 7.25 $\text{rad/s}^2$. The link lever arm is ${r_{\text{arm}}} =$ 0.80 m, the approach speed is ${\omega} =$ 1.2 $\text{rad/s}$, and the barrier gain is ${\gamma_{\text{cbf}}} =$ 10 $\text{s}^{-1}$. These are stipulated joint-level dynamics that deployment must calibrate on the actual arm. At 110 mm of tool clearance from the workstation surface, the angular clearance is ${\theta_{\text{clear}}} =$ 0.1375 $\text{rad}$ and the barrier certificate evaluates to ${h} =$ 0.0382 $\text{rad}$, so substituting into [[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/03 Running the Machine/12 Safety Enforcement/V4-12.06 Safe Sets as Conditional Permission#^eq-enforce-kinetic-torque|equation 6]] requires the joint to deliver at least 80.1 N·m of counteracting braking torque. Most of that demand is not deceleration at all, because the first 65.25 N·m merely cancels gravity to keep the joint from speeding up and only the remainder slows it, which is why a joint still outside its zero set already asks for most of its peak torque. At 79.45 mm, the system reaches the zero set (${h} = 0$) where the barrier demands 87 N·m, the motor’s full peak torque capacity. If clearance degrades further to 70 mm, the inequality demands 89.1 N·m, which exceeds peak actuator limits and places the state outside the control-admissible viable set. There, under these scalar dynamics, fixed bias, and instantaneous maximum-braking assumptions, no controller initiated at that point can prevent contact.

Physical robotic hardware provides neither perfectly continuous state feedback nor instantaneous torque response. Stator current rise times, fieldbus jitter, and drivetrain mechanical compliance delay and soften corrective torque delivery. To guarantee safety under these real-world non-idealities, the runtime enforcer evaluates the barrier certificate against an inset boundary that explicitly accounts for the worst-case stopping deficit induced by hardware latency and compliant dynamics. This initiates corrective filtering earlier along the trajectory.

This kinetic barrier construction follows directly from rigid-body braking mechanics. For the stipulated scalar approach with exact dynamics, fixed bias, instantaneous effort, and no omitted margins, its zero level set represents the exact boundary of states from which maximum allowable braking stops the joint before collision: the **viability kernel** ([[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/03 Running the Machine/12 Safety Enforcement/V4-12.16 Source References#^ref-aubin1991viability|Aubin 1991]]) of a double integrator subject to actuator saturation. Among collision-free states, the kernel’s complement is the set of inevitable collision states defined in **[[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/03 Running the Machine/11 Trajectory Planning/V4-11.06 Behavior at the Seam#^sec-planning-inevitable-collision-states|Inevitable collision states]]**. With a conservative available lower bound instead of exact maximum braking, the same construction supplies a sufficient stopping set rather than the exact kernel. The resulting torque requirement remains compatible with the stipulated actuator limits. At ${h} = 0$, the inequality in [[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/03 Running the Machine/12 Safety Enforcement/V4-12.06 Safe Sets as Conditional Permission#^eq-enforce-kinetic-torque|equation 6]] demands exactly $-{\tau_{\max}}$, and for all ${h} > 0$ the demand is strictly less restrictive, ensuring that at least one admissible control command exists everywhere within the safe set. Actuator feasibility across the entire set, not merely the calculus of the derivative, is what qualifies ${h}({\theta_{\text{clear}}}, {\omega})$ as a valid barrier certificate under input constraints.

This identical principle governs autonomous base navigation. The mobile base certificate ${h_{\text{base}}}({D_{\text{clear}}}, {v}) = {D_{\text{clear}}} - [{v}\,{\tau_{\text{delay}}} + {d_{\text{suffix}}}({v}) + {\delta_{\text{loc}}} + {\delta_{\text{margin}}} + {\epsilon_{\text{track}}}]$ is nonnegative precisely when the stopping subtotal in [[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/03 Running the Machine/12 Safety Enforcement/V4-12.04 Stopping Envelopes#^eq-enforce-stopping-envelope|equation 1]] fits the clearance. The operational certificate must additionally subtract the dispatch-to-admission travel before granting permission. Both constructions reserve the motion needed to stop. A nonnegative stopping reservation alone does not establish forward invariance; the sampled implementation must also preserve that reservation under its qualified state, effort, and timing bounds. The single-joint arm derivation omits latency and localization margins temporarily only to isolate the mechanics of relative degree.

When complex nonlinear dynamics prevent closed-form analytical solutions for the viability kernel, **Hamilton-Jacobi (HJ) reachability**[[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/03 Running the Machine/12 Safety Enforcement/V4-12.17 Footnotes#^fn9|⁹]] computes the boundary numerically as the zero level set of an optimal control value function ([[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/03 Running the Machine/12 Safety Enforcement/V4-12.16 Source References#^ref-mitchell2005time|Mitchell et al. 2005]]; [[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/03 Running the Machine/12 Safety Enforcement/V4-12.16 Source References#^ref-bansal2017hamilton|Bansal et al. 2017]]). The resulting tabulated lookup surface serves as the runtime barrier certificate, with its spatial gradient defining the half-space normal for real-time filtering. Because state-space grid discretization scales exponentially with dimensionality, HJ reachability lookup tables are best suited for decoupled, low-dimensional subsystems such as a single revolute joint or the longitudinal tracking channel of a mobile base.

↪ The Hamilton-Jacobi value function and its differential-game formulation are developed in **[[05 Reference/Volume IV - Physical AI/V4 Control and Dynamics#^sec-appendix-control-hj-reachability|Hamilton-Jacobi reachability and worst-case differential games]]**.

A barrier certificate specifies which actuator commands preserve forward invariance, but it does not specify how the runtime safety path should handle an uncertified proposal. When a candidate command violates the half-space constraint, the enforcer must choose whether to project the command onto the boundary of admissible inputs, reject it in favor of a precomputed emergency braking trajectory, or maintain the previously certified setpoint. This intervention policy determines how mathematical barrier constraints translate into real-time authority over the motor drives.
