# V4-12.05 Authority Exhaustion and Emergency Fallback

[[Home|Home]] · [[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/Volume 4 Overview|Volume Overview]] · [[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/03 Running the Machine/12 Safety Enforcement/V4-12 Safety Enforcement|V4-12 Safety Enforcement]]

^sec-enforcement-exhausted-authority

A loaded arm descending toward the packing station must counter gravity before any torque remains to slow its motion. **Actuator saturation** occurs when requested effort reaches the electrical, mechanical, or thermal limits of the physical body, which can cause downstream integral controllers to accumulate windup.[[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/03 Running the Machine/12 Safety Enforcement/V4-12.17 Footnotes#^fn3|³]] The actuator cannot deliver the corrective control action ${\mathbf{u}^*}$ at its commanded magnitude in any of several cases. Motor phase currents may hit inverter current-chopping limits (${I_{\max}}$). The DC bus may sag under inductive switching transients (${L_{\text{bus}}}\,d{I}/d{t}$). Back-EMF at high rotor speeds may collapse voltage headroom (${V_{\text{rail}}} - {K_e} {\omega_m} \to 0$). Winding temperatures may approach thermal derating thresholds (${T_{\text{winding}}} \to {T_{\text{limit}}}$). No mathematical safety boundary can be defended with control effort the physical body cannot generate.

↩︎ The electrical and transmission limits that bound delivered actuator effort are established in **[[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/01 Machine Anatomy/02 The Physical Body/V4-02.05 Actuator Transmission Limits#^sec-body-actuator-limits|Actuator Transmission Limits]]**.

When required braking effort exceeds physical actuator limits, no safety filter can enforce the promised stopping horizon. An emergency fallback invoked after control authority is lost can mitigate damage, but it cannot restore an invariant guarantee that physical dynamics have already broken. The runtime enforcer must therefore contract its forward reservation *before* actuator effort saturates, preserving a feasible braking margin under worst-case loading.

A stopping envelope based on peak torque alone overstates braking authority when gravity drives the moving load toward a hazard. Under a steady-state bias torque ${\tau_{\text{bias}}}$, the effective deceleration available to arrest angular motion collapses:

$$
{\alpha_{\text{net}}} = \frac{{\tau_{\max}} - {\tau_{\text{bias}}}}{{J_{\text{eff}}}} \tag{3}
$$

 where ${J_{\text{eff}}}$ is mechanism inertia and ${\tau_{\max}}$ is the peak dynamic torque limit. In linear translational systems with mass ${m}$ and bias force ${u_{\text{bias}}}$, [[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/03 Running the Machine/12 Safety Enforcement/V4-12.05 Authority Exhaustion and Emergency Fallback#^eq-enforce-effective-deceleration|equation 3]] takes the equivalent form ${a_{\text{net}}} = ({u_{\max}} - \lvert {u_{\text{bias}}}\rvert) / {m}$. For motion aligned with the bias, a payload consuming 75 percent of peak opposing torque leaves only the remaining quarter for active braking, quadrupling the constant-deceleration stopping distance at that speed.

^eq-enforce-effective-deceleration

Gravitational and dynamic biases are directionally asymmetric. When motion opposes gravity (such as during an upward arm swing), gravity assists motor braking, yielding ${\alpha_{\text{net}}} = ({\tau_{\max}} + {\tau_{\text{bias}}}) / {J_{\text{eff}}}$. Conversely, when motion aligns with the bias (such as a loaded arm descending toward a workstation), gravity directly opposes deceleration, collapsing braking headroom to ${\alpha_{\text{net}}} = ({\tau_{\max}} - {\tau_{\text{bias}}}) / {J_{\text{eff}}}$. Because safety certificates must guarantee forward invariance in the worst-case physical direction, the runtime enforcer must evaluate ${\alpha_{\text{net}}}$ with directional sign awareness rather than treating braking authority as an isotropic scalar. Suppose the warehouse mobile manipulator carries a mug from the takeaway conveyor down toward a coworker at the packing station. Its tool center point (TCP) moves at 0.96 m/s, within its 1 m/s free-space limit (only the final approach to the coworker’s hand runs at the reduced handover speed derived in [[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/03 Running the Machine/12 Safety Enforcement/V4-12.08 The Minimal Intervention Principle and Optimization Filters#^sec-enforcement-intentional-contact|section 12.6.2]]). Gravitational bias during this descent quadruples the tool’s stopping distance (notebook [12.2](https://mlsysbook.ai/vol4/12_enforcement/12_enforcement.html#nbk-enforcement-gravity-braking-tax)).

**Napkin Math 12.2: The gravity braking tax**

**Problem**: The loaded joint descends toward the packing station at ${\omega}_0$. How far does the tool point travel before the joint stops when the whole peak torque brakes, and how far when gravity on the arm and mug holds part of it?

**Variables**:

- ${\omega}_0 =$ 1.2 rad/s and ${r_{\text{arm}}} =$ 0.80 m (Illustrative; the joint speed and effective link lever arm of the scenario, see the Reader Guide).
- ${J_{\text{eff}}} =$ 3 kg·m² (Illustrative; joint inertia with the mug).
- ${\tau_{\max}} =$ 87 N·m (Datasheet; peak torque of the registered arm’s axes 1 to 4).
- ${\tau_{\text{bias}}} =$ 65.25 N·m, 75 percent of peak torque (Illustrative; gravity on the arm and mug in the direction of motion).

**Math**: With no bias, ${\alpha_{\text{nom}}} = {\tau_{\max}}/{J_{\text{eff}}} =$ 29 rad/s², the stopping angle is ${\omega}_0^2/(2{\alpha_{\text{nom}}}) =$ 0.0248 rad (1.42$^\circ$), and the tool point travels ${r_{\text{arm}}}\,{\Delta}{\theta_{\text{joint}}} =$ 19.86 mm. On the descent, [[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/03 Running the Machine/12 Safety Enforcement/V4-12.05 Authority Exhaustion and Emergency Fallback#^eq-enforce-effective-deceleration|equation 3]] leaves ${\tau_{\max}} - {\tau_{\text{bias}}} =$ 21.75 N·m for braking, so ${\alpha_{\text{net}}} =$ 7.25 rad/s², the stopping angle is 0.0993 rad (5.69$^\circ$), and the tool travels 79.45 mm.

**Result**: The descending stop is four times the unbiased one, an unbudgeted overrun of 59.59 mm for an enforcer that assumes the datasheet torque.

**Systems insight**: Stopping distance scales as $1/{\alpha_{\text{net}}}$, so a bias that takes 75 percent of the torque quadruples the braking term of ${d_{\text{stop}}}$ at the same speed. The certificate must use the net authority in the worst direction, not the actuator’s rating.

The asymmetry is direction, not magnitude ([[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/03 Running the Machine/12 Safety Enforcement/V4-12.05 Authority Exhaustion and Emergency Fallback#^fig-12-arm-bias-braking|figure 6]]). On the descent, gravity torque acts with the motion and leaves only 21.75 N·m of the motor’s peak for braking, while the same joint moving up recruits gravity and stops in less distance than the unbiased case. Whether the descending overrun reaches the workstation depends on the clearance left when braking begins, the margin the barrier certificate of [[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/03 Running the Machine/12 Safety Enforcement/V4-12.06 Safe Sets as Conditional Permission#^sec-enforcement-safe-sets-conditional-permission|section 12.4]] evaluates. The coworker’s hand must never fall within the arm’s biased stopping distance of 79.45 mm plus its blind travel during sensing and communication delay before the handover channel engages.

An unprotected integrator downstream of the safety enforcer can introduce severe latency during actuator saturation. If clipping does not halt integration, tracking error continues to accumulate in the controller state. When the safety system subsequently requests maximum braking, the command must pass through this wound-up state rather than bypassing it. The actuator command consequently remains pinned at its forward saturation limit while the integrator unwinds. Take an illustrative case in which tracking error accumulates for 200 ms and the unwinding phase then delays net braking deceleration by ${T_{\text{unwind}}} =$ 150 ms. The two durations are independent assumptions rather than one derived from the other; the unwind time depends on the integrator gain and on how sharply the error reverses, and only a test of the actual drive fixes it. At an aisle speed of 1.3 m/s, this latency adds ${v}\,{T_{\text{unwind}}} =$ 195 mm of unbraked travel. That overrun exceeds the system’s entire 131.0 mm unallocated clearance buffer by 63.99 mm, even before dispatch-to-admission latency is charged. Classical techniques such as integrator clamping, back-calculation, and conditional integration prevent windup in the feedback loop ([[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/03 Running the Machine/12 Safety Enforcement/V4-12.16 Source References#^ref-astrom2006advanced|Åström and Hägglund 2006]]; [[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/03 Running the Machine/12 Safety Enforcement/V4-12.16 Source References#^ref-franklin1998digital|Franklin et al. 1998]]). The enforcement architecture, however, must explicitly quantify any residual unwinding delay and budget the corresponding unbraked distance into the stopping horizon.

Actuator saturation is one of the few hardware limitations with an immediate, deterministic detector inside the permission path. On each 1 ms tick, the permission path compares the commanded effort ${u_{\text{cmd}}}$ against the realizable actuator envelope: ${u_{\text{act}}} = \text{clip}({u_{\text{cmd}}}, {u_{\min}}, {u_{\max}})$. A nonzero residual ${u_{\text{cmd}}} - {u_{\text{act}}} \neq 0$ or an active drive-stage current-limit flag directly reveals that the requested effort has been clipped. This comparison detects saturation at the command interface, however, rather than measuring delivered shaft torque or tire-ground traction. Qualified physical evidence (phase currents, DC bus voltage, thermal derating thresholds, and kinematic state telemetry) must support a conservative lower bound on available directional braking deceleration. An upper actuator-effort ceiling limits possible braking; it does not establish the braking that a stop may credit, and a clipping detector does not measure tire-floor traction.

Because degraded authority immediately lengthens stopping distance, the enforcer updates its braking envelope in real time. Before admitting the next trajectory segment, the filter recomputes the effective deceleration ${a_{\text{net}}} = ({u_{\max}} - \lvert {u_{\text{bias}}}\rvert) / {m}$. Allowable vehicle velocity scales down as authority contracts; if the recalculated stopping distance no longer fits within the confirmed clear distance, the enforcer must command immediate deceleration while sufficient braking headroom remains.

Authority exhaustion must also be communicated upstream to the planner. If a 20 $\text{Hz}$ trajectory generator continues commanding aggressive accelerations while the low-level 1 kHz loop reports saturation, the policy is planning trajectories for a virtual body that the physical robot cannot realize. The permission path treats saturation as an active constraint violation. It revokes the active trajectory lease and forces a replan initialized from the machine’s actual, authority-constrained dynamic state.

**Systems Perspective 12.2: The actuator authority invariant**

A control barrier function, as formulated in **[[05 Reference/Volume IV - Physical AI/V4 Control and Dynamics#^sec-appendix-control-cbf|Safety and Control Barrier Functions]]**, remains mathematically valid only while physical actuators retain sufficient current, voltage, and thermal headroom to deliver the computed corrective effort: ${u}^* \in [{u_{\min}}, {u_{\max}}]$. When steady-state gravity, friction, or payload inertia encroaches on this operating envelope, the runtime enforcer must dynamically contract the safe set before marginal control authority vanishes.

**Checkpoint 12.1: Stopping envelope and actuator headroom**

Consider how the stopping envelope, and the actuator authority beneath it, bound permissible motion:

**Quadratic envelope scaling**: Why does the stopping budget grow faster than speed, so that the finished budget, rather than the drive limit, sets the aisle speed, and why can no planning policy bypass this kinematic floor?

**Transport lag vs. braking estimate**: At the 1.3 m/s aisle speed, what clearance is spent during ${\tau_{\text{delay}}}$ (${v}\,{\tau_{\text{delay}}}$) and during constant-deceleration braking (${v}^2/2{a_{\text{brake}}}$), and what is the speed below which the delay term dominates?

**Effective deceleration collapse**: What is the physical mechanism that causes stopping distance to quadruple when steady-state gravity consumes 75 percent of peak actuator torque?

**Dynamic safe set contraction**: Why must the permission path actively shrink the admissible state space when an actuator approaches thermal or electrical saturation, rather than waiting for tracking error to breach a hard limit?
