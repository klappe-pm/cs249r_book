# V4-09.04 The Spatial Memory Handoff and State Invariants

[[Home|Home]] · [[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/Volume 4 Overview|Volume Overview]] · [[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/03 Running the Machine/09 Spatial Memory/V4-09 Spatial Memory|V4-09 Spatial Memory]]

^sec-memory-handoff

A position vector arriving over an inter-process communication bus cannot identify the entity, physical quantity, or units it describes. The same sequence of floating-point values could denote a workpiece center in meters, a joint angle in radians, or a contact force in newtons. A consumer requires these distinctions before executing a control law; a confusion between meters and millimeters alone scales the interpreted displacement by a factor of one thousand. The belief record therefore explicitly packages entity identity, physical quantity, and measurement units at its foundation, rather than relying on conventions hidden in the producing model or transport wrapper.

Specifying the quantity and units remains incomplete without binding the value to a concrete reference frame. Identical spatial coordinates describe entirely different physical realities depending on the origin and orientation of the coordinate system. A Cartesian position $[0.142, -0.051, 0.812]\text{ m}$ read in the robot base frame names a point about 0.8 m above the chassis origin, in open air over the base. Read in the tool flange frame, the same three numbers name a point 0.812 m out along the flange’s approach axis, wherever the arm happens to point; with the gripper aimed at the cage door, that point lies inside the door rather than above the chassis. Because kinematic links flex, joints move, and coordinate transforms update at varying rates across distributed nodes, a belief record must carry an explicit frame identifier. Attaching the frame allows the receiving node to query the spatial transformation tree and project the stored geometry into the local frame required by the actuator, preventing the controller from acting on geometric misalignments caused by moving kinematic chains.

Geometry and identity describe physical state at a single moment, but spatial memory must also capture the temporal anchor of that state. The record carries the evidence epoch ${t_0}$ of the physical interaction or optical exposure that last constrained the estimate, the instant at which a sensor transduced the evidence. In distributed machine architectures, intermediate nodes often republish data, execute Kalman filter prediction steps, or run forward kinematic propagations to maintain high-rate control streams; none of these computational steps adds physical evidence. Republishing and prediction must preserve the evidence epoch; a measurement update may renew only the components it physically constrains. Consumers therefore measure evidence age against the physical world rather than the scheduling frequency of the local computer.

At the boundary of the multi-sensor state estimator ([[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/03 Running the Machine/09 Spatial Memory/V4-09.03 Estimating State from Dated Measurements#^sec-perception-state-estimation|section 9.2]]), downstream consumers do not need to rerun the filter recursion or repeat the consistency tests of [[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/03 Running the Machine/09 Spatial Memory/V4-09.03 Estimating State from Dated Measurements#^sec-memory-estimator-consistency|section 9.2.1]]; they need only the resulting state, its justified error model, and the physical evidence that constrains each relevant component. Within that recursion, a prediction step advances the estimate epoch, the instant the propagated state describes (formalized in [[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/03 Running the Machine/09 Spatial Memory/V4-09.05 State Epochs and Temporal Anchoring#^sec-memory-state-epoch|section 9.4]]), while its underlying physical evidence continues aging. A fresh measurement, by contrast, renews only the state dimensions it directly constrains. The spatial memory handoff therefore obliges the producer to expose bounded growth through occlusion and withdraw claims whose operating assumptions no longer hold.

Knowing the evidence epoch ${t_0}$ establishes elapsed time ${\Delta} {t} = {t} - {t_0}$, but elapsed time alone cannot determine whether a belief is valid for a given action. Two estimates of identical age diverge at different rates depending on physical constraints. A warehouse rack end bolted to the floor maintains a stationary position; its remembered boundary can remain useful while the machine’s localization bound and the fixed-rack premise hold. In contrast, an occluded person hidden behind that rack can move at walking speed and close the narrow gap beside it before the next camera frame arrives.

The minimum record fields necessary for a consumer to evaluate a stored state can be derived directly from the requirement to bound current error. Let ${E}({\Delta} {t})$ represent the upper bound of the discrepancy between the stored value and true physical state after an elapsed time ${\Delta} {t} = {t} - {t_0}$. If an estimator establishes a state at time ${t_0}$ with an initial uncertainty bound ${e_0}$, and external disturbances or unmodeled velocities cause the true state to diverge at a maximum rate ${r}$, the upper bound on error at current time ${t}$ obeys the linear expansion ${E}({\Delta} {t}) = {e_0} + {r}\,{\Delta} {t}$. At the rack end, the stored spatial claim asserts that no person stands near the machine. Here ${e_0}$ is the machine’s localization error, ${r}$ is the walking speed at which an occluded person can advance toward the vehicle, and the safety tolerance is the depth of observed free space beyond the machine’s clearance envelope. Other disturbances grow error faster. A bounded unmodeled acceleration adds a quadratic term, and random acceleration noise, integrated twice, makes position variance grow with the cube of elapsed time, both derived in [[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/03 Running the Machine/09 Spatial Memory/V4-09.08 Forward State Propagation and Occlusion Decay#^sec-memory-belief-through-occlusion|section 9.7]]. Omitting the initial bound or the dynamic growth terms leaves the consumer unable to compute ${E}({\Delta} {t})$, making an initial bound and an explicit expansion model structurally required fields of the record.

Expressing uncertainty through physical bounds and expansion rates distinguishes a physical AI belief from a probabilistic confidence score. An object detection network might output a class confidence of $0.94$, but that softmax score is not calibrated evidence (**[[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/01 Machine Anatomy/03 The Cognitive Brain/V4-03.07 Cognitive Vulnerabilities#^sec-brain-softmax-illusion|The softmax illusion and out-of-distribution overconfidence]]**), and a downstream position controller cannot convert a unitless probability into a safe actuator displacement margin (that is, how far the arm can physically move before risking a collision). A confidence score of $0.94$ provides no information about whether an obstacle boundary is uncertain by $2.0\text{ mm}$ or by $200\text{ mm}$, nor does it indicate how rapidly that boundary uncertainty grows when occlusion occurs. A physical memory record must express its initial bound ${e_0}$ and expansion rate ${r}$ in concrete physical dimensions such as meters, radians, or newtons per second. These rates are valid only within a declared validity region, such as a maximum assumed acceleration or a verified contact friction coefficient. If physical operating conditions leave that region, the expansion rate no longer holds, and the belief record must be treated as invalid.

At the spring-latched cage door, the gripper finger must enter a narrow latch-release slot that the finger itself hides from the wrist camera during the final approach. Before relying on the remembered slot pose, the consumer compares the belief’s current error with the slot clearance. It converts the evidence epoch into its own clock domain, includes the clock-conversion bound, and evaluates the declared growth law at the time the state will be used. This check is a continuing obligation during execution, not a one-time path-generation check. A new motion proposal whose evidence fails the check is refused without automatically revoking a still-valid active command. If the active motion itself depends on expiring evidence, the controller must begin a feasible fallback maneuver early enough that the mechanism halts within the available clearance. The machine must therefore remain inside its recoverable set, the states from which a qualified stop still fits within the remaining clearance, a boundary that **[[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/03 Running the Machine/12 Safety Enforcement/V4-12.04 Stopping Envelopes#^sec-enforcement-stopping-envelopes|Stopping Envelopes]]** derives from the finished stopping budget. The belief’s expiry is a limit on supported motion, not a promise that an instantaneous stop is physically possible at that deadline.

The fields a consumer needs for this check travel together as the **belief record**, the seventh record in the record chain. At this stage, the record binds an identified physical quantity, with its unit, to a reference frame, an evidence epoch, an initial error bound, an uncertainty-growth law, and the validity region in which that law holds. The sections that follow add the fields each new problem demands (the estimate epoch and clock-conversion bound, a per-consumer query-work limit, the expiry deadline, provenance, and lifecycle status), and [[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/03 Running the Machine/09 Spatial Memory/V4-09.11 The Belief Record Schema#^sec-memory-spatial-state-schema|section 9.10]] assembles the complete record. A classification score, a map publication time, or a transform value alone cannot supply that contract, because none couples physical units and evidence age to an error-growth law. The record makes the claims checkable; qualification must establish that they hold. A consumer cannot wait for an upstream publisher to announce expiry. If that publisher’s thread hangs or its message stream stops, the consumer must still age the evidence autonomously and enforce its own safety deadline.

Evaluating the belief record depends on calculating the elapsed time ${\Delta} {t} = {t_{\text{now}}} - {t_0}$ against physical reality. If the consumer cannot verify the exact physical instant when energy transduction occurred, the calculated error bound ${E}({\Delta} {t})$ becomes an unanchored fiction, mistaking software compute cycles for physical evidence. Protecting that anchor is the job of the next two fields, the estimate epoch and the clock-conversion bound.
