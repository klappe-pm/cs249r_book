# V4-09.03 Estimating State from Dated Measurements

[[Home|Home]] · [[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/Volume 4 Overview|Volume Overview]] · [[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/03 Running the Machine/09 Spatial Memory/V4-09 Spatial Memory|V4-09 Spatial Memory]]

^sec-perception-state-estimation

A single LiDAR return or stereo match localizes the rack-end upright at one isolated instant. A physical safety permission path, however, requires a continuous range estimate that stays current between sweeps, fuses observations across heterogeneous sensors, and produces an uncertainty metric whose statistical confidence matches physical reality. A recursive **state estimator** satisfies all three demands by alternating two complementary operations: a prediction step that projects the state forward through a dynamic motion model, and an update step that incorporates each newly arrived, dated measurement. The discrete Kalman filter[[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/03 Running the Machine/09 Spatial Memory/V4-09.16 Footnotes#^fn2|²]] ([[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/03 Running the Machine/09 Spatial Memory/V4-09.15 Source References#^ref-kalman1960new|Rudolph Emil Kalman 1960]]) provides the classical formulation of this recursion, establishing the mathematical foundation for this chapter ([[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/03 Running the Machine/09 Spatial Memory/V4-09.15 Source References#^ref-thrun2005probabilistic|Thrun et al. 2005]], ch. 3; [[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/03 Running the Machine/09 Spatial Memory/V4-09.15 Source References#^ref-barshalom2001estimation|Bar-Shalom et al. 2001]]). Multi-object tracking and delayed-observation handling apply these two steps directly. The belief decay under occlusion examined in [[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/03 Running the Machine/09 Spatial Memory/V4-09.08 Forward State Propagation and Occlusion Decay#^sec-memory-belief-through-occlusion|section 9.7]] corresponds to the prediction step executing without measurement updates, while the observability limits of [[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/03 Running the Machine/09 Spatial Memory/V4-09.09 Observability Ceilings and Estimation Limits#^sec-memory-state-estimation-bounds|section 9.8]] define the state dimensions that no update stream can reconstruct. The mechanics are clearest on a scalar, the remaining distance ${s}$ from the mobile base to the rack-end upright, so the two operations are worked through on it before the notation extends to full state vectors.

The **predict** step advances the state estimate across time without incorporating new external evidence. Between two successive LiDAR sweeps separated by ${T_{\text{nominal}}}$ = 50 ms, wheel odometry ([[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/03 Running the Machine/09 Spatial Memory/V4-09.06 Coordinate Transform Expiry and Localization Drift#^sec-memory-localization-slam|section 9.5.1]]) indicates that the mobile base closed the distance by ${v}\,{T_{\text{nominal}}}$, yielding the prior prediction ${\hat{s}}^- = {\hat{s}} - {v}\,{T_{\text{nominal}}}$. Odometry, however, carries inherent uncertainty. An illustrative speed disturbance of 0.1 m/s (from minor wheel slip on polished concrete, for example) accumulates 5 mm of standard deviation over that single sweep interval. Because independent errors combine in quadrature, ${\sigma}^2_{-} = {\sigma}^2 + {\sigma_{w}}^2$, an initial 20 mm uncertainty expands to 20.6 mm. Without new measurement constraints, prediction inevitably inflates state uncertainty.

The robot’s inertial measurement unit (IMU) is the other source of high-rate data for the predict step, and its datasheet shows why dead reckoning cannot sustain an accurate estimate on its own. A sensor’s noise density states how much random error it adds per square root of measurement bandwidth, so white noise of density ${N_{\text{IMU}}}$ integrated for ${T}$ seconds spreads by ${N_{\text{IMU}}}\sqrt{{T}}$, while a fixed offset integrates in proportion to ${T}$. The Bosch BMI088 gyroscope has a rate-noise density of 0.014 °/s/$\sqrt{\text{Hz}}$ alongside an uncalibrated zero-rate bias of 1 °/s. Its accelerometer has a noise density of 160 µg/$\sqrt{\text{Hz}}$ and an uncalibrated zero-g offset of 20 mg ([[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/03 Running the Machine/09 Spatial Memory/V4-09.15 Source References#^ref-bosch2024bmi088|Bosch Sensortec 2024]]). Integrating these sensor signals accumulates error, and stochastic noise compounds along a different trajectory than deterministic bias. Broadband white noise integrates in variance, causing orientation uncertainty to grow with the square root of time. Across 1 s without external range correction, it accumulates only 0.014°. In contrast, a constant bias integrates linearly, producing heading drift proportional to time that reaches 1° over that same second. At 1.10 m, this angular error projects into 19.2 mm of lateral displacement for the rack upright. Because acceleration undergoes double integration to give position, its bias scales quadratically with elapsed time ($\tfrac{1}{2} {b_a} {T}^2$). The bias generates 98.1 mm of drift after one second, about twice the 50 mm localization allowance. Integrated accelerometer white noise contributes only 0.9 mm (its position variance grows with the cube of time, a law derived in [[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/03 Running the Machine/09 Spatial Memory/V4-09.08 Forward State Propagation and Occlusion Decay#^sec-memory-belief-through-occlusion|section 9.7]]). In this illustrative uncalibrated case, sensor offsets dominate broadband noise and limit how long the inertial system can coast uncorrected. Practical estimators therefore augment the state vector to estimate these biases online. This works only while the available measurements and executed motion constrain the relevant bias components (the observability condition of [[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/03 Running the Machine/09 Spatial Memory/V4-09.09 Observability Ceilings and Estimation Limits#^sec-memory-state-estimation-bounds|section 9.8]]). The estimators also use the underlying noise densities to parameterize the process noise, the matrix generalization of the odometry variance ${\sigma_{w}}^2$ that the general filter below writes as ${\mathbf{Q}}$. Because these offsets drift with temperature and vary across physical chips, operating parameters must be identified through in-situ stationary calibration on the physical platform rather than taken from static datasheet ratings.

The **update** step incorporates a fresh scalar measurement ${z}$ characterized by variance ${\sigma_z}^2$. The updated posterior is a linear combination, ${\hat{s}} = (1-{g})\,{\hat{s}}^- + {g}\,{z}$. Assuming the prior prediction error and measurement noise remain statistically independent, the posterior variance evaluates to $(1-{g})^2 {\sigma}^2_{-} + {g}^2 {\sigma_z}^2$. Minimizing this variance with respect to the blending weight ${g}$ produces the optimal Kalman gain, which balances each information source inversely against its relative uncertainty:

$$
{g} = \frac{{\sigma}^2_{-}}{{\sigma}^2_{-} + {\sigma_z}^2}, \qquad {\hat{s}} = {\hat{s}}^- + {g}\,({z} - {\hat{s}}^-), \qquad {\sigma}^2 = (1 - {g})\,{\sigma}^2_{-}
$$

 The residual ${z} - {\hat{s}}^-$ constitutes the **innovation**, the new information supplied by the measurement that the forward projection did not capture. Given the Ouster OS1-64’s worst-case datasheet range precision of 30 mm, the computed gain is 0.32. The estimator shifts approximately one-third of the way toward the raw LiDAR reading, contracting the posterior standard deviation to 17.0 mm, a modeled spread tighter than either input alone. Because published precision specifications typically reflect ideal calibration conditions rather than real-time operating modes, this sensor variance must be verified at the configured operational scan rate. Furthermore, the sensor’s separate 25 mm systematic accuracy offset represents a structural bias that zero-mean Gaussian models cannot accommodate; offline calibration must eliminate this offset, or the downstream safety clearance margin must absorb it explicitly.

Fusing a second sensor executes as a sequential update step. The illustrative stereo camera rig analyzed in **[[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/03 Running the Machine/08 Sensor Perception/V4-08.07 Spatial Error and Clearance Bounds#^sec-perception-error-clearance|Spatial Error and Clearance Bounds]]** achieves ${\sigma_z} =$ 3.8 mm at the 1.10 m rack-end distance. Fusing this measurement yields a stereo gain of 0.95, shrinking the final fused standard deviation to 3.7 mm. For statistically independent measurements captured at the same physical instant, update ordering is commutative: each observation contributes an information increment, and their underlying precision matrices (inverse variances) sum directly.

A misdated measurement breaks this estimation algebra silently. Suppose a stereo depth observation is tagged with its host arrival timestamp, which **[[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/03 Running the Machine/08 Sensor Perception/V4-08.04 Dating and Placing the Measurement#^sec-perception-handoff|Dating and Placing the Measurement]]** shows hides exposure, readout, and transport delay, rather than with its physical exposure epoch. The filter erroneously pairs this observation with a robot pose 39.9 mm further along the aisle than where the photons actually struck the sensor. Consequently, the measured range reads systematically long by that spatial displacement, causing the update to drag the state estimate 38.0 mm away from the physical obstacle while reporting the same deceptively tight 3.7 mm standard deviation. The resulting physical error spans approximately 10.3 reported standard deviations in the hazardous direction, yet the filter’s covariance update detects nothing amiss because algebraic variance reduction depends entirely on modeled sensor noise rather than measurement residuals. Corrupt timestamps are therefore far more dangerous than dropped sensor frames. Dropping the stereo frame instead would leave the posterior uncertainty at 17.0 mm, imprecise but statistically honest.

Set side by side on one range-error scale ([[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/03 Running the Machine/09 Spatial Memory/V4-09.03 Estimating State from Dated Measurements#^fig-08-kalman-predict-update|figure 1]]), the stages read as a single story. Prediction widens the bar from 20 mm to 20.6 mm, each update narrows it below both of its inputs to a final 3.7 mm, and the misdated column reports that same tight spread while its center sits 38.0 mm from the true range, the only bar that does not contain the truth.

The recursion generalizes from a scalar distance to an ${n}$-dimensional state vector ${\hat{\mathbf{x}}}$ with error covariance ${\mathbf{P}} \in \mathbb{R}^{{n} \times {n}}$. It parameterizes system dynamics through a state transition matrix ${\mathbf{F}}$, process noise covariance ${\mathbf{Q}}$, an observation matrix ${\mathbf{H}}$ projecting state space into measurement space, and measurement noise covariance ${\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{\text{meas}}}$ ([[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/03 Running the Machine/09 Spatial Memory/V4-09.03 Estimating State from Dated Measurements#^eq-percep-kf-predict|equation 1]] and [[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/03 Running the Machine/09 Spatial Memory/V4-09.03 Estimating State from Dated Measurements#^eq-percep-kf-update|equation 2]]):

$$
{\hat{\mathbf{x}}_{k|k-1}} = {\mathbf{F}}\,{\hat{\mathbf{x}}_{k-1|k-1}}, \qquad {\mathbf{P}_{k|k-1}} = {\mathbf{F}}\,{\mathbf{P}_{k-1|k-1}}{\mathbf{F}}^\top + {\mathbf{Q}} \tag{1}
$$

$$
{\boldsymbol{\nu}}_k = {\mathbf{y}}_k - {\mathbf{H}}{\hat{\mathbf{x}}_{k|k-1}}, \quad {\mathbf{S}}_k = {\mathbf{H}}{\mathbf{P}_{k|k-1}}{\mathbf{H}}^\top + {\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{\text{meas}}}, \quad {\mathbf{G}}_{k} = {\mathbf{P}_{k|k-1}}{\mathbf{H}}^\top{\mathbf{S}}_k^{-1}, \quad {\hat{\mathbf{x}}_{k|k}} = {\hat{\mathbf{x}}_{k|k-1}} + {\mathbf{G}}_{k}{\boldsymbol{\nu}}_k, \quad {\mathbf{P}_{k|k}} = ({\mathbf{I}} - {\mathbf{G}}_{k}{\mathbf{H}})\,{\mathbf{P}_{k|k-1}} \tag{2}
$$

 In this formulation, ${\boldsymbol{\nu}}_k$ represents the innovation vector, ${\mathbf{S}}_k$ denotes innovation covariance, and ${\mathbf{G}}_{k}$ signifies the Kalman gain matrix (designated ${\mathbf{G}}_{k}$ to reserve ${\mathbf{K}}$ for camera intrinsic calibration matrices). The preceding scalar analysis corresponds to the degenerate setting ${\mathbf{F}} = {\mathbf{H}} = 1$, with the odometry displacement $-{v}\,{T_{\text{nominal}}}$ entering as a known control input. [[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/03 Running the Machine/09 Spatial Memory/V4-09.03 Estimating State from Dated Measurements#^eq-percep-kf-predict|Equation 1]] omits that input term for brevity, and the term shifts the mean without changing ${\mathbf{P}}$. Under linear dynamics and additive Gaussian noise, this closed-form update yields the provably optimal minimum-variance unbiased estimate. If nonlinearities or non-Gaussian noise corrupt the process, the update functions merely as an empirical linear estimator whose output covariance requires experimental validation.

Physical robotic sensors are inherently nonlinear functions of platform state. A LiDAR range measurement to the rack upright evaluates to ${r} = \|{\mathbf{p}_{\text{upright}}} - {\mathbf{p}_{\text{base}}}\|$, while a camera records target bearings through the nonlinear pinhole projection detailed in **[[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/03 Running the Machine/08 Sensor Perception/V4-08.03 Capture Physics and Calibration#^eq-percep-pinhole-projection|equation 8.2]]**. The **extended Kalman filter (EKF)** ([[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/03 Running the Machine/09 Spatial Memory/V4-09.15 Source References#^ref-smith1962application|Smith et al. 1962]]) adapts [[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/03 Running the Machine/09 Spatial Memory/V4-09.03 Estimating State from Dated Measurements#^eq-percep-kf-update|equation 2]] to nonlinear regimes by evaluating the measurement function ${h}({\hat{\mathbf{x}}_{k|k-1}})$ directly and substituting the linear projection ${\mathbf{H}}$ with the measurement Jacobian $\nabla_{{\mathbf{x}}} {h}$ evaluated at the current state prediction. For a scalar range reading, this Jacobian with respect to platform base position reduces to the unit line-of-sight vector directed from the upright toward the base. This Taylor-series linearization is a first-order approximation. Substantial curvature across the prior distribution, common when estimating bearings to nearby obstacles or operating under broad initial uncertainty, can therefore cause the EKF to underestimate its true estimation error ([[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/03 Running the Machine/09 Spatial Memory/V4-09.15 Source References#^ref-julier2004unscented|Julier and Uhlmann 2004]]). This failure mode makes the empirical consistency evaluations of [[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/03 Running the Machine/09 Spatial Memory/V4-09.03 Estimating State from Dated Measurements#^sec-memory-estimator-consistency|section 9.2.1]] safety requirements rather than optional checks.

The **unscented Kalman filter (UKF)** developed by Julier and Uhlmann ([[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/03 Running the Machine/09 Spatial Memory/V4-09.15 Source References#^ref-julier1997new|1997]]) bypasses analytical Jacobians through deterministic sampling. For an ${n}$-dimensional state vector, the UKF instantiates $2{n}+1$ sigma points structured around the matrix square root of ${\mathbf{P}}$, propagates each sample point through the true nonlinear observation function, and reconstructs the transformed posterior mean and covariance ([[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/03 Running the Machine/09 Spatial Memory/V4-09.15 Source References#^ref-julier2004unscented|Julier and Uhlmann 2004]]). This transformation captures higher-order curvature effects on the mean that the EKF truncates, requiring $2{n}+1$ functional evaluations per cycle. The UKF nevertheless approximates the posterior as a unimodal Gaussian, leaving it subject to identical qualification and consistency standards.

Both filtering frameworks, and the multi-object tracking and out-of-sequence replay built on them, depend on every sensor measurement being indexed by its true capture epoch. The misdated stereo benchmark illustrates the breakdown when this assumption fails. While reported uncertainty remained artificially compressed, true physical error drifted across ten standard deviations. A reported covariance is therefore an engineering hypothesis that must be systematically validated against real-world data.

### Testing Whether the Covariance Is Honest

^sec-memory-estimator-consistency

Catching an overconfident covariance like the misdated stereo filter’s requires two complementary statistical tests: an offline qualification metric evaluated against ground truth before deployment, and an online runtime monitor evaluated directly against incoming measurement streams during operation. Both formulations use a zero-mean Gaussian error model with the correct nonsingular covariance. Whitening the error under these assumptions produces independent standard normal coordinates; their squared norm, rather than the error vector itself, follows a chi-squared distribution. Matching covariance alone does not establish Gaussian tails.

A filter achieves statistical consistency when its reported covariance matches the dispersion of its actual state errors. When offline ground truth is accessible (a laser-tracker survey of the rack upright, say, with an optical motion-capture reference for the base pose), the normalized estimation error squared (NEES), ${\epsilon}_k = ({\mathbf{x}}_k - {\hat{\mathbf{x}}}_k)^\top {\mathbf{P}}_k^{-1} ({\mathbf{x}}_k - {\hat{\mathbf{x}}}_k)$, evaluates the system. Under that zero-mean Gaussian model with the correct nonsingular covariance, whitening the error of an ${n}$-dimensional state forms a vector of ${n}$ independent standard normal variates. Their sum of squares follows a chi-squared distribution with ${n}$ degrees of freedom and theoretical mean ${n}$. Evaluated across ${N}$ statistically independent experimental trials, the scaled sample mean ${N}{\bar{\epsilon}}$ follows a chi-squared distribution with ${N} {n}$ degrees of freedom ([[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/03 Running the Machine/09 Spatial Memory/V4-09.15 Source References#^ref-barshalom2001estimation|Bar-Shalom et al. 2001]]). For the scalar rack-end distance over 50 experimental trials, the two-sided 95 percent acceptance interval for sample mean NEES ${\bar{\epsilon}}$ spans from 0.65 to 1.43. An empirical NEES above this band flags an overconfident filter, the safety-critical defect, because its reported uncertainty understates the error that clearance buffers must absorb. Conversely, an average falling below the lower bound indicates an underconfident filter whose inflated covariance unnecessarily throttles vehicle operating speed.

While absolute ground truth remains unavailable during live deployment, the estimator can continuously audit its measurement innovations. The normalized innovation squared (NIS), ${\boldsymbol{\nu}}_k^\top{\mathbf{S}}_k^{-1}{\boldsymbol{\nu}}_k$, follows a chi-squared distribution with ${m}$ degrees of freedom for an ${m}$-dimensional measurement vector under valid model assumptions, so its expected value is ${m}$. The same statistic reappears below as the data-association gate. A bias ${b}$ in the innovation adds ${b}^2/{S}$ to that expectation, where ${S}$ is the innovation variance. For the misdated stereo observation in [[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/03 Running the Machine/09 Spatial Memory/V4-09.03 Estimating State from Dated Measurements#^fig-08-kalman-predict-update|figure 1]], ${b}$ is the 39.9 mm arrival-stamp travel, and the expected NIS rises from 1 to 6.25. A single misdated update’s NIS then follows a noncentral chi-squared distribution, the bias setting its offset and the sensor noise its spread. One update therefore breaches the 95 percent validation gate of 3.84 with probability 0.63, and trips the 99 percent threshold of 6.63 only 0.39 of the time. Single-frame residual gating therefore admits a substantial fraction of corrupted measurements. Averaging independent innovations reduces sampling variation and can expose a persistent residual bias. For 50 independent scalar innovations under the valid model, the mean NIS has the same 0.65 to 1.43 acceptance band derived above for NEES. Independent repetitions of the worked misdated update average near 6.25, far above it. A sequential filter can instead absorb a systematic dating error into its state, reducing later innovations; correlated innovations also invalidate this nominal band. A runtime sliding-window NIS supervisor therefore detects dating errors that persist in the innovations, but cannot replace capture-epoch validation. Once an estimator’s covariance and innovations are calibrated on a single landmark, the next challenge is maintaining separate filters across multiple moving objects without confusing their identities.

### Multi-Object Tracking and Data Association

^sec-perception-tracking-association

Maintaining separate filters across multiple moving objects adds an immediate architectural constraint: a perception pipeline cannot withhold an obstacle detection until a multi-frame tracker confirms its persistent identity, or a worker stepping into the aisle remains invisible to collision avoidance during the confirmation window. A fresh occupied-region claim must therefore constrain vehicle motion immediately, before perception systems identify the specific obstacle class or confirm a track. Persistent identity serves a distinct behavioral objective: correlating detections across frames allows the motion planner to estimate target velocity vectors and anticipate dynamic crossing events, while immediate spatial occupancy protects reactive clearance.

The software architecture therefore decouples immediate geometric spatial occupancy from the **tracking-by-detection** hypothesis. The tracking layer associates incoming detections, filters kinematic velocities, and tracks multi-dimensional uncertainty. Track retirement follows the lifecycle below, and how state persists when measurements stop is the subject of [[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/03 Running the Machine/09 Spatial Memory/V4-09.08 Forward State Propagation and Occlusion Decay#^sec-memory-belief-through-occlusion|section 9.7]]. The state equations formulated below govern the kinematic information a confirmed track provides, rather than serving as a prerequisite for reactive collision avoidance.

Each active track ${i}$ maintains an independent state filter tracking a kinematic state vector ${\hat{\mathbf{x}}}_t = [{\hat{\mathbf{p}}}_t^\top, {\hat{\mathbf{v}}}_t^\top]^\top$ with error covariance ${\mathbf{P}}_t \in \mathbb{R}^{6\times6}$. A discrete constant-velocity transition matrix ${\mathbf{F}}$ and process noise covariance ${\mathbf{Q}}$ advance this state between sensory frames through the prediction step of [[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/03 Running the Machine/09 Spatial Memory/V4-09.03 Estimating State from Dated Measurements#^eq-percep-kf-predict|equation 1]], which carries the mean forward and grows the covariance by the process noise. When an incoming perception frame yields ${n_{\text{det}}}$ candidate detections $\{{\mathbf{y}}_{t, j}\}_{j=1}^{{n_{\text{det}}}}$ (the frame index ${t}$ plays the role of the step index ${k}$ in [[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/03 Running the Machine/09 Spatial Memory/V4-09.03 Estimating State from Dated Measurements#^eq-percep-kf-predict|equation 1]]), the tracker must resolve **data association**,[[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/03 Running the Machine/09 Spatial Memory/V4-09.16 Footnotes#^fn3|³]] mapping candidate detections to established state tracks. To prevent spurious sensor noise or distant clutter from corrupting legitimate tracks, the tracker evaluates the squared Mahalanobis distance ${d_M}^2$ between detection ${\mathbf{y}}_{t, j}$ and the projected measurement ${\mathbf{H}}{\hat{\mathbf{x}}_{t|t-1}}$:

$$
{d_M}^2 = ({\mathbf{y}}_{t, j} - {\mathbf{H}}{\hat{\mathbf{x}}_{t|t-1}})^\top {\mathbf{S}}_{ij}^{-1} ({\mathbf{y}}_{t, j} - {\mathbf{H}}{\hat{\mathbf{x}}_{t|t-1}}) \tag{3}
$$

 where ${\mathbf{S}}_{ij} = {\mathbf{H}}{\mathbf{P}_{t|t-1}}{\mathbf{H}}^\top + {\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{\text{meas},j}}$ defines the innovation covariance from [[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/03 Running the Machine/09 Spatial Memory/V4-09.03 Estimating State from Dated Measurements#^eq-percep-kf-update|equation 2]] and ${\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{\text{meas},j}}$ specifies the measurement noise covariance; this validation metric is identical to the NIS metric defined in [[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/03 Running the Machine/09 Spatial Memory/V4-09.03 Estimating State from Dated Measurements#^sec-memory-estimator-consistency|section 9.2.1]]. An ellipsoidal **validation gate** evaluates [[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/03 Running the Machine/09 Spatial Memory/V4-09.03 Estimating State from Dated Measurements#^eq-percep-mahalanobis-gate|equation 3]], rejecting any pairing where ${d_M}^2 > {\gamma_{\text{gate}}}$. The gating threshold ${\chi}_3^2(1 - {\alpha_{\text{sig}}})$ possesses three degrees of freedom corresponding to three-dimensional Cartesian position measurements, retaining its theoretical significance only while residual Gaussian assumptions hold. For all candidate pairings satisfying the gate, the tracker builds an assignment cost matrix with entries ${c}_{ij} = {d_M}^2$ and computes the global minimum-cost one-to-one match. It uses either the Hungarian algorithm ([[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/03 Running the Machine/09 Spatial Memory/V4-09.15 Source References#^ref-kuhn1955hungarian|Kuhn 1955]]), as the Simple Online and Realtime Tracking (SORT) baseline does ([[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/03 Running the Machine/09 Spatial Memory/V4-09.15 Source References#^ref-bewley2016simple|Bewley et al. 2016]]), or the Jonker-Volgenant shortest-augmenting-path solver ([[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/03 Running the Machine/09 Spatial Memory/V4-09.15 Source References#^ref-jonker1987shortest|Jonker and Volgenant 1987]]). The match is optimal only for the costs it was given; it does not guarantee that each pairing is physically correct.

^eq-percep-mahalanobis-gate

The tracking lifecycle organizes target hypotheses through three discrete states:

1.  **Tentative state**: Unassociated detections instantiate tentative candidate tracks. Because sensor artifacts (specular reflections, dust particles, optical lens flare) generate transient false positives, candidate identities remain tentative until satisfying an ${M}$-of-${N_{\text{conf}}}$ frame confirmation policy (associated in at least ${M}$ of the last ${N_{\text{conf}}}$ frames). Raw geometric occupancy claims remain immediately accessible to vehicle collision-avoidance buffers throughout this confirmation window. If a downstream behavioral planner requires confirmed object identity or calibrated velocity, its latency allocation must account for this multi-frame confirmation interval rather than relying solely on the single-frame pipeline latency described in **[[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/03 Running the Machine/08 Sensor Perception/V4-08.05 Bounding Observation Age#^sec-perception-latency-waterfall|The latency waterfall]]**.
2.  **Confirmed state**: Upon passing confirmation criteria, the track registers as confirmed and publishes its complete kinematic state $(\text{ID}_i, {\hat{\mathbf{p}}}_i, {\hat{\mathbf{v}}}_i, {\mathbf{P}}_i)$ to planning and safety subsystems. Each newly associated measurement refines the track via [[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/03 Running the Machine/09 Spatial Memory/V4-09.03 Estimating State from Dated Measurements#^eq-percep-kf-update|equation 2]], correcting the mean vector and contracting the error covariance matrix. Because this update achieves minimum variance only under linear-Gaussian assumptions, tracked covariances must continuously satisfy the consistency criteria established in [[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/03 Running the Machine/09 Spatial Memory/V4-09.03 Estimating State from Dated Measurements#^sec-memory-estimator-consistency|section 9.2.1]].
3.  **Coasting state (occlusion)**: When an obstacle moves behind storage shelving or suffers transient occlusion by another vehicle, object detection fails. Rather than immediately dropping the entity, the tracker transitions the hypothesis into an unobserved coasting state. During coasting, state propagation proceeds strictly through kinematic motion model ${\mathbf{F}}$ in [[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/03 Running the Machine/09 Spatial Memory/V4-09.03 Estimating State from Dated Measurements#^eq-percep-kf-predict|equation 1]], while process noise ${\mathbf{Q}}$ progressively inflates state covariance in the absence of measurement constraints. Downstream path planners must compensate for this increasing uncertainty by applying progressively widening clearance margins. If no matching observation associates within timeout window ${T_{\text{lost}}}$, the active track terminates. Terminating a track reflects the loss of persistent identity rather than the clearance of physical space, so the spatial map must retain an appropriate unknown-space constraint ([[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/03 Running the Machine/09 Spatial Memory/V4-09.07 Certified Spatial Assertions in Scene Memory#^sec-memory-scene-attention-buffers|section 9.6]]). The rate at which coasting uncertainty degrades spatial traversability is the belief expiry problem formulated in [[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/03 Running the Machine/09 Spatial Memory/V4-09.08 Forward State Propagation and Occlusion Decay#^sec-memory-belief-through-occlusion|section 9.7]].

To support real-time planning under motion uncertainty, the tracker continuously serializes an auditable **dynamic track manifest** $[(\text{ID}_k, {\hat{\mathbf{p}}}_k, {\hat{\mathbf{v}}}_k, {\mathbf{P}}_k, {t_{\text{last}}}, \text{state})]$. The intent layer consumes these velocity vectors and covariance ellipses to bound the region each object could occupy over the planning horizon, while real-time safety enforcement systems compute certified stopping clearances against moving dynamic obstacles. Even when a detection associates cleanly with the right track, however, vision inference delay means that camera detections routinely arrive after newer LiDAR sweeps and IMU samples have already advanced the filter.

↪ The track manifest’s velocities and covariances are developed as forward occupancy sets in **[[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/03 Running the Machine/10 Grounded Intent/V4-10.07 Admissible Goal Sets#^sec-intent-dynamic-forecasting|Dynamic obstacle forecasting and forward occupancy]]**.

### Applying a Delayed Observation

^sec-perception-discrete-state-estimation

Fusing such a delayed visual detection directly into the estimator’s current state pulls the position estimate backward toward where the obstacle stood at exposure time and corrupts the velocity vector. The physical capture epoch preserved by the observation contract enables the estimator to integrate these delayed measurements at their historical capture instant instead, provided the system maintains a calibrated state history and dynamic motion model. Rolling the filter back to that capture instant applies the update of [[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/03 Running the Machine/09 Spatial Memory/V4-09.03 Estimating State from Dated Measurements#^eq-percep-kf-update|equation 2]] to the historical state estimate ${\hat{\mathbf{x}}}$, error covariance ${\mathbf{P}}$, observation model ${\mathbf{H}}$, and Kalman gain ${\mathbf{G}}_{k}$.

A delayed visual observation must correct the state at its true historical capture epoch before the estimator can project the correction forward to current wall-clock time. Robust systems implement this mechanism using an **out-of-sequence ring buffer**, a circular array whose slot ${k}$ holds the prediction ${\hat{\mathbf{x}}_{k|k-1}}$ and covariance ${\mathbf{P}_{k|k-1}}$ for IMU sample ${k}$, the IMU input ${\mathbf{u}}_k$ that drove it, and that sample’s capture time. The buffer needs ${N_{\text{buffer}}} = \lceil {T_{\text{buffer}}} / {\Delta} {t_{\text{IMU}}} \rceil$ slots, where ${\Delta} {t_{\text{IMU}}}$ is the IMU sample period and the buffer horizon ${T_{\text{buffer}}}$ is the oldest observation age the estimator will still accept. On the running machine, the IMU samples at 1 kHz (${\Delta} {t_{\text{IMU}}} =$ 1 ms), so covering the navigation camera’s 51.6 ms observation age takes 52 slots. A consumer must either interpolate the buffered state to the observation’s actual capture time or charge the bounded nearest-sample timing discrepancy to the error model. In the equations below, subscripts index IMU samples; ${t_0}$ and ${t_{\text{recv}}}$ name the samples whose capture times are nearest the observation’s capture epoch and its receipt. When an out-of-sequence observation ${\mathbf{y}_{t_0}}$ arrives at receipt sample ${t_{\text{recv}}}$, the estimator rolls back to sample ${t_0}$, retrieves the historical prediction ${\hat{\mathbf{x}}_{t_0|t_0-1}}$, computes the corresponding innovation and Kalman gain, and applies the update of [[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/03 Running the Machine/09 Spatial Memory/V4-09.03 Estimating State from Dated Measurements#^eq-percep-kf-update|equation 2]] at that historical instant ([[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/03 Running the Machine/09 Spatial Memory/V4-09.03 Estimating State from Dated Measurements#^eq-percep-oosm-correction|equation 4]]):

$$
{\hat{\mathbf{x}}_{t_0|t_0}} = {\hat{\mathbf{x}}_{t_0|t_0-1}} + {\mathbf{G}_{t_0}} ({\mathbf{y}_{t_0}} - {\mathbf{H}_{t_0}} {\hat{\mathbf{x}}_{t_0|t_0-1}}), \quad {\mathbf{P}_{t_0|t_0}} = ({\mathbf{I}} - {\mathbf{G}_{t_0}}{\mathbf{H}_{t_0}}) {\mathbf{P}_{t_0|t_0-1}} \tag{4}
$$

 For the forward propagation pass that follows, consider first the baseline case with no intervening updates from other sensors; in general configurations, any intervening sensor updates within the interval must be sequentially re-applied in timestamp order. Because nonlinear motion models and their linearized transition matrices ${\mathbf{F}}_k$ depend on the state trajectory itself, the estimator cannot simply add the historical state shift to the current estimate, and instead executes a rapid replay sweep, re-integrating the buffered IMU inputs ${\mathbf{u}}_k$ from sample ${t_0}$ to sample ${t_{\text{recv}}}$ through the continuous motion model ${\mathbf{f}}({\mathbf{x}}, {\mathbf{u}})$, the state’s rate of change under input ${\mathbf{u}}$, using the corrected state ${\hat{\mathbf{x}}_{t_0|t_0}}$ as its initial condition. This forward integration synchronizes current state estimate ${\hat{\mathbf{x}}_{t_{\text{recv}}}}$ with the newly arrived observation without introducing non-causal state discontinuities ([[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/03 Running the Machine/09 Spatial Memory/V4-09.03 Estimating State from Dated Measurements#^eq-percep-oosm-replay|equation 5]]):

$$
{\hat{\mathbf{x}}_{t_{\text{recv}}}} = {\hat{\mathbf{x}}_{t_0|t_0}} + \sum_{k = {t_0}}^{{t_{\text{recv}}}-1} {\mathbf{f}}({\hat{\mathbf{x}}}_k, {\mathbf{u}}_k) {\Delta} {t_{\text{IMU}}} \tag{5}
$$

 with covariance propagation across the replay interval governed by unrolling the single-step covariance recursion of [[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/03 Running the Machine/09 Spatial Memory/V4-09.03 Estimating State from Dated Measurements#^eq-percep-kf-predict|equation 1]] through the discrete transition matrix product ${\boldsymbol{\Phi}}({t_{\text{recv}}}, {t_0}) = \prod_{k = {t_0}}^{{t_{\text{recv}}}-1} {\mathbf{F}}_k$ to carry the newly contracted historical covariance ${\mathbf{P}_{t_0|t_0}}$ and each intermediate process-noise increment forward:

$$
{\mathbf{P}_{t_{\text{recv}}}} = {\boldsymbol{\Phi}}({t_{\text{recv}}}, {t_0}) {\mathbf{P}_{t_0|t_0}} {\boldsymbol{\Phi}}^\top({t_{\text{recv}}}, {t_0}) + \sum_{k = {t_0}}^{{t_{\text{recv}}}-1} {\boldsymbol{\Phi}}({t_{\text{recv}}}, {k}+1) {\mathbf{Q}}_k {\boldsymbol{\Phi}}^\top({t_{\text{recv}}}, {k}+1) \tag{6}
$$

 The applicability of this rewind-and-replay routine ([[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/03 Running the Machine/09 Spatial Memory/V4-09.03 Estimating State from Dated Measurements#^eq-percep-oosm-cov-replay|equation 6]]) is strictly limited by ring buffer horizon ${T_{\text{buffer}}}$. If scheduling jitter or memory bus contention delays an observation such that ${t_{\text{age}}} > {T_{\text{buffer}}}$, the circular buffer has already overwritten the slot for sample ${t_0}$. In this scenario, the estimator must reject the stale measurement, permit process noise covariance ${\mathbf{Q}}$ to grow the predictive covariance ${\mathbf{P}}$, and immediately notify downstream safety paths that external visual grounding has degraded.

**Checkpoint 9.1: The estimator behind every belief**

Every belief the rest of the chapter ages is the output of this estimator. These items test whether you can run it and whether you can tell when its reported uncertainty is honest.

**Predict vs. update**: In the worked scalar drift model, can you explain why a prediction step widens the reported variance, while an independent measurement update narrows it below both the prediction and the measurement?

**Gain estimate**: If the stereo range’s standard deviation doubled from 3.8 mm, with the LiDAR-updated 17.0 mm prior unchanged, what would the stereo gain and the fused standard deviation be, and which sensor would then dominate the fused estimate?

**Estimator consistency**: Can you explain why a misdated update leaves the reported variance unchanged, when averaging independent innovations can expose a dating error that a per-update gate misses, and why this monitor cannot replace capture-epoch validation?

**Occupancy vs. identity**: Can you explain why a tentative track’s occupied region must reach clearance consumers before confirmation, and what a decision that needs confirmed velocity must add to its detection delay?

**Late measurements**: Can you explain what the estimator must do with a measurement whose age exceeds its ring buffer’s horizon, and why?

The state estimator outputs the core data record that persistent spatial memory maintains: a kinematic state vector, its verified covariance, the physical instant the state describes, and the capture epochs of the raw sensor observations that last constrained it. Forward prediction and rewind-replay passes maintain temporal currency across execution cycles, but only external sensor measurements inject genuine physical evidence, and neither mathematical projection advances the underlying evidence timestamps. Because downstream consumers must independently audit when a stored belief expires once an obstacle slips into occlusion, this state record must carry explicit temporal provenance alongside its numerical estimates. The handoff challenge is defining the exact contract a downstream consumer requires to evaluate stored spatial beliefs when direct line of sight is lost.
