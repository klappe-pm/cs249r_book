# V4-09.14 Historical Perspective and Further Reading

[[Home|Home]] · [[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/Volume 4 Overview|Volume Overview]] · [[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/03 Running the Machine/09 Spatial Memory/V4-09 Spatial Memory|V4-09 Spatial Memory]]

^sec-memory-history

The state estimators at the foundation of modern spatial memory predate the mobile robots that rely on them. Rudolph Emil Kalman ([[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/03 Running the Machine/09 Spatial Memory/V4-09.15 Source References#^ref-kalman1960new|1960]]) formulated the discrete-time recursive linear filter, establishing optimal estimation for linear systems subject to Gaussian noise. Within two years, Stanley Schmidt and his team at NASA Ames linearized the filtering equations about the running state trajectory to guide spacecraft navigation for the Apollo circumlunar mission ([[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/03 Running the Machine/09 Spatial Memory/V4-09.15 Source References#^ref-smith1962application|Smith et al. 1962]]), creating what is now known as the extended Kalman filter (EKF). Because Taylor-series linearizations introduce severe truncation errors when applied to highly nonlinear kinematics, Julier and Uhlmann ([[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/03 Running the Machine/09 Spatial Memory/V4-09.15 Source References#^ref-julier1997new|1997]]) developed the unscented transform, offering a derivative-free mechanism that propagates deterministic sigma points through the true nonlinear dynamics. In parallel, radar tracking research addressed measurement uncertainty in cluttered environments: Bar-Shalom and Tse ([[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/03 Running the Machine/09 Spatial Memory/V4-09.15 Source References#^ref-barshalom1975tracking|1975]]) introduced probabilistic data association (PDA) to resolve ambiguous target-to-observation assignments, while the statistical gating and consistency frameworks compiled by Bar-Shalom et al. ([[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/03 Running the Machine/09 Spatial Memory/V4-09.15 Source References#^ref-barshalom2001estimation|2001]]) provided rigorous methods to detect filter divergence. Control theory supplied the theoretical bridge to autonomy: Åström ([[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/03 Running the Machine/09 Spatial Memory/V4-09.15 Source References#^ref-astrom1965optimal|1965]]) demonstrated that under linear-quadratic-Gaussian assumptions, a controller operating under partial observability can separate state estimation from control law computation and act directly on the conditional state distribution without loss of optimality. This separation principle justifies maintaining an explicit belief record in an embodied system.

Mobile robotics brought these recursive estimation techniques into unstructured environments. Moravec and Elfes ([[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/03 Running the Machine/09 Spatial Memory/V4-09.15 Source References#^ref-moravec1985high|1985]]) and Elfes ([[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/03 Running the Machine/09 Spatial Memory/V4-09.15 Source References#^ref-elfes1989using|1989]]) introduced occupancy grid mapping, projecting wide-beam sonar range returns into discrete spatial cells via probabilistic sensor models. While early robotic systems decoupled map building from self-localization, Smith et al. ([[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/03 Running the Machine/09 Spatial Memory/V4-09.15 Source References#^ref-smith1990estimating|1990]]) proved that errors in estimated robot pose and errors in stationary landmark coordinates are fundamentally correlated, formulating simultaneous localization and mapping (SLAM) as a single high-dimensional estimation problem. Over the following decades, surveyed extensively by Durrant-Whyte and Bailey ([[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/03 Running the Machine/09 Spatial Memory/V4-09.15 Source References#^ref-durrant2006simultaneous|2006]]) and Cadena et al. ([[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/03 Running the Machine/09 Spatial Memory/V4-09.15 Source References#^ref-cadena2016past|2016]]), SLAM matured from filtering frameworks into non-linear least-squares optimization over factor graphs ([[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/03 Running the Machine/09 Spatial Memory/V4-09.15 Source References#^ref-dellaert2017factor|Dellaert and Kaess 2017]]), which decouple front-end feature tracking from back-end trajectory optimization. In parallel, volumetric surface representations migrated from computer graphics into physical AI pipelines. Curless and Levoy ([[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/03 Running the Machine/09 Spatial Memory/V4-09.15 Source References#^ref-curless1996volumetric|1996]]) developed signed distance fields (SDFs) with weighted least-squares fusion to merge multiple range scans into coherent polygon meshes. With the arrival of commodity depth sensors, KinectFusion adapted this approach to run in real time on graphics hardware ([[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/03 Running the Machine/09 Spatial Memory/V4-09.15 Source References#^ref-newcombe2011kinectfusion|Newcombe et al. 2011]]). Autonomous robotics then repurposed these dense geometric structures for motion planning: OctoMap introduced hierarchically clamped octrees for compact volumetric occupancy ([[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/03 Running the Machine/09 Spatial Memory/V4-09.15 Source References#^ref-hornung2013octomap|Hornung et al. 2013]]), Voxblox enabled incremental generation of Euclidean signed distance fields (ESDFs) directly from truncated signed distance fields (TSDFs) ([[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/03 Running the Machine/09 Spatial Memory/V4-09.15 Source References#^ref-oleynikova2017voxblox|Oleynikova et al. 2017]]), and nvblox ported these reconstructions to high-throughput embedded GPU architectures ([[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/03 Running the Machine/09 Spatial Memory/V4-09.15 Source References#^ref-millane2024nvblox|Millane et al. 2024]]). To bridge geometric reconstruction and task-level semantic reasoning, 3D dynamic scene graphs now organize these dense metric representations into hierarchical abstractions spanning object instances, topological rooms, buildings, and moving agents ([[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/03 Running the Machine/09 Spatial Memory/V4-09.15 Source References#^ref-armeni20193d|Armeni et al. 2019]]; [[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/03 Running the Machine/09 Spatial Memory/V4-09.15 Source References#^ref-rosinol20203d|Rosinol et al. 2020]]; [[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/03 Running the Machine/09 Spatial Memory/V4-09.15 Source References#^ref-hughes2022hydra|Hughes et al. 2022]]).

Despite these mathematical and computational advances, classical mapping and estimation architectures leave one systems requirement unresolved, the explicit lifetime and epistemic boundaries of spatial information. Conventional estimators maintain state covariances, grid maps accumulate occupancy log-odds, and scene graphs categorize objects. Those ingredients must also be exposed and enforced as a consumer contract, coupling timestamped evidence, uncertainty growth under occlusion, and the temporal lease that limits each intended use. Downstream control processes, including trajectory generators, speed governors, and hardware safety monitors, require bounded guarantees on spatial validity. Automated driving systems approached this requirement by expanding reachable dynamic sets outward from the boundaries of sensor occlusion fields ([[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/03 Running the Machine/09 Spatial Memory/V4-09.15 Source References#^ref-orzechowski2018tackling|Orzechowski et al. 2018]]). Spatial memory formalizes this coupling by binding evidence epochs, growth dynamics, and explicit validity leases directly to state estimates. By carrying its own temporal boundaries, a belief record enables hardware permission pipelines to revoke actuation authority before an expired belief causes an unsafe intervention or a physical collision.

The following foundational works provide detailed treatments of state estimation, map representations, and statistical consistency:

- Thrun et al. ([[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/03 Running the Machine/09 Spatial Memory/V4-09.15 Source References#^ref-thrun2005probabilistic|2005]]) establishes Bayesian filtering, the Kalman and particle filter families, occupancy grid mapping, and SLAM within a unified probabilistic framework, serving as the foundational reference for recursive state estimation in [[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/03 Running the Machine/09 Spatial Memory/V4-09.03 Estimating State from Dated Measurements#^sec-perception-state-estimation|section 9.2]] and the volumetric map representations in this chapter.
- Bar-Shalom et al. ([[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/03 Running the Machine/09 Spatial Memory/V4-09.15 Source References#^ref-barshalom2001estimation|2001]]) serves as the definitive reference on target tracking and state estimation, establishing the mathematical foundations for statistical gating and the normalized estimation error squared (NEES) and normalized innovation squared (NIS) consistency tests deployed in [[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/03 Running the Machine/09 Spatial Memory/V4-09.03 Estimating State from Dated Measurements#^sec-memory-estimator-consistency|section 9.2.1]] and [[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/03 Running the Machine/09 Spatial Memory/V4-09.03 Estimating State from Dated Measurements#^sec-perception-tracking-association|section 9.2.2]].
- Julier and Uhlmann ([[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/03 Running the Machine/09 Spatial Memory/V4-09.15 Source References#^ref-julier2004unscented|2004]]) provides a rigorous mathematical derivation of sigma-point transformations, demonstrating why first-order Taylor series approximations cause extended Kalman filters to underestimate posterior covariance and become overconfident, which is the operational failure mode that filter consistency checks must catch.
- Cadena et al. ([[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/03 Running the Machine/09 Spatial Memory/V4-09.15 Source References#^ref-cadena2016past|2016]]) surveys the evolution of SLAM systems as they transitioned from probabilistic filtering to factor graph optimization, detailing key open challenges in dynamic scene segmentation, semantic representations, and lifelong map maintenance summarized in [[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/03 Running the Machine/09 Spatial Memory/V4-09.07 Certified Spatial Assertions in Scene Memory#^tbl-09-map-maintenance|table 3]].
- Hornung et al. ([[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/03 Running the Machine/09 Spatial Memory/V4-09.15 Source References#^ref-hornung2013octomap|2013]]) describes the OctoMap framework, formalizing a 3D probabilistic octree that pairs clamped log-odds updates with an explicit distinction between free and unobserved unknown space, a structural requirement for permission-grade spatial safety checks.
- Oleynikova et al. ([[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/03 Running the Machine/09 Spatial Memory/V4-09.15 Source References#^ref-oleynikova2017voxblox|2017]]) presents an incremental algorithm for constructing Euclidean signed distance fields directly from truncated signed distance fields on computationally constrained systems, providing the core representation adopted in this chapter for local metric snapshots.

**What’s Next: From valid beliefs to bounded goals**

What may a valid belief authorize? Memory rules only on validity. It can say that a remembered object lies within a stated bound until a stated deadline, but it cannot say what the machine should do about that object. In the intent layer, a spoken request to take the mug from the conveyor must first be grounded to a feature the gripper can act on, the mug’s handle, in the robot’s own frame and units. Only then does it become an intent lease. The lease consumes the belief record of that handle, carries that belief’s evidence epoch forward as its own evidence time, and caps its expiry at that belief’s horizon. It grants a bounded target with effort limits, never a path, for no longer than the evidence beneath it supports.
