# V4-09.09 Observability Ceilings and Estimation Limits

[[Home|Home]] · [[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/Volume 4 Overview|Volume Overview]] · [[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/03 Running the Machine/09 Spatial Memory/V4-09 Spatial Memory|V4-09 Spatial Memory]]

^sec-memory-state-estimation-bounds

A force sensor can report the exact same reading when mechanical contact force increases as when its analog conditioning circuit drifts thermally. Sampling that stream repeatedly cannot distinguish the two physical causes. The underlying question is observability, whether a system’s available sensor outputs and known physical dynamics suffice to reconstruct the internal states required for control. Consider an instantaneous observation model ${\mathbf{y}} = {h}({\mathbf{x}})$. When distinct physical states ${\mathbf{x}}_1 \neq {\mathbf{x}}_2$ yield identical measurements (${h}({\mathbf{x}}_1) = {h}({\mathbf{x}}_2)$), the sensor mapping alone cannot separate them. In a combined force-and-bias state, equal and opposite shifts in contact force and sensor bias form an insensitive direction in the observation Jacobian ${\mathbf{H}} = \partial {h} / \partial {\mathbf{x}}$. Because this direction lies in the null space of ${\mathbf{H}}$, local state variations along it produce zero change in the measurement vector. The measurement likelihood therefore has zero directional derivative along this unconstrained direction, although it may still vary in directions that change the measured sum. Instantaneous ambiguity does not automatically imply unobservability across time; if subsequent motion or control actions induce dynamics that differentiate the states, temporal filtering can resolve the ambiguity. However, if the ambiguity persists along the entire operating trajectory, no amount of signal processing can recover the lost degrees of freedom. Scaling up neural network parameters or adding recurrent layers expands representational capacity, but computation cannot synthesize physical information that no sensor ever transduced.

Observability depends on both the sensor suite and the executed trajectory. When a physical disturbance drives a required state along an unobserved manifold, an estimator can fall into the **zero-innovation trap**. The innovation vanishes (${\boldsymbol{\nu}} = {\mathbf{y}} - {\mathbf{H}}{\hat{\mathbf{x}}} = {\mathbf{0}}$), so no measurement ever contradicts the model. The covariance update depends on the modeled noise and measurement geometry rather than the observed residual. It can contract uncertainty in measured or model-coupled directions without revealing omitted drift along an unobservable direction. A misspecified filter that treats bias as known can therefore contract its reported force uncertainty while the true force and omitted bias drift apart without changing the measured sum.

Consider an illustrative strain-gauge force sensor mounted at the mobile manipulator’s wrist, delivering a steady reading of 5 N. Once an unknown bias is introduced, this single reading is compatible with an infinite continuum of true contact forces. An estimator or learned policy that reports this contact force with narrow confidence bounds has not demonstrated that the state is **observable**.[[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/03 Running the Machine/09 Spatial Memory/V4-09.16 Footnotes#^fn12|¹²]] When two distinct physical state histories map to the identical sequence of sensor observations, no downstream algorithm, whether an extended Kalman filter or a deep recurrent network, can deduce which trajectory the physical system followed. The digital computing substrate receives identical bitstreams across both realities.

**Definition 9.3: Observability**

***Observability*** is the property that the history of a machine’s sensor outputs over a finite time window, combined with its known control inputs and system dynamics, uniquely determines its initial state, ensuring that distinct physical trajectories produce distinct measurement sequences.

1.  **Significance**: Observability defines the hard physical ceiling on state reconstruction, regardless of model architecture or parameter count. In the strain-gauge example below, a 3 N bias shift over 15 minutes alters the true contact force by 60 percent while the innovation remains zero, because the sensor records only their scalar sum.
2.  **Distinction**: Observability is distinct from predictability. An estimator can project a future state with minimal covariance based entirely on prior dynamical assumptions; only measurements sensitive to that state can verify the prediction against reality.
3.  **Common pitfall**: A vanishing innovation does not validate state accuracy. Along an unobservable manifold, the innovation residual can remain zero while tracking error grows. A misspecified filter can still contract its reported covariance under its model assumptions; the zero residual does not justify that contraction.

To trace the operational impact of this distinction, consider the wrist gripper regulating contact force against a rigid surface. Let ${x}({t})$ denote the true normal contact force, while the strain-gauge conditioning electronics introduce an unknown, slowly drifting bias ${b}({t})$. The raw measurement stream delivered to the estimator at each sample interval is ${y}({t}) = {x}({t}) + {b}({t})$. Suppose the sensor transmits a constant reading ${y}({t}) =$ 5 N. This measurement is satisfied by a true force ${x}({t}) =$ 5 N with zero bias ${b}({t}) =$ 0 N. However, it is equally satisfied by a true force ${x}({t}) =$ 2 N with a sensor bias ${b}({t}) =$ 3 N, or even by ${x}({t}) =$ 0 N with a bias ${b}({t}) =$ 5 N. For any arbitrary offset trajectory ${\Delta}({t})$, the paired state trajectory $({x}({t}) - {\Delta}({t}), {b}({t}) + {\Delta}({t}))$ generates an identical measurement sequence ${y}({t})$. The difference between physical contact force and sensor bias lies within the **unobservable subspace** of the measurement model, the null space in which the two variations cancel exactly in the digitized signal.

Unable to distinguish physical load from electronic drift, an estimator must fall back on prior assumptions about state evolution. If a Kalman filter or recurrent estimator assumes that bias ${b}({t})$ remains static with near-zero process noise, it attributes all measurement variations in ${y}({t})$ directly to changes in contact force ${x}({t})$. As new samples arrive at 1,000 $\text{Hz}$, the estimator repeatedly folds the measurements into its belief state. Because the incoming stream aligns with the forward state propagation, the computed covariance contracts monotonically. The estimator then publishes an aggressively tight uncertainty bound of 5 N $\pm$ 0.05 N at p99. In reality, heat generated by nearby joint actuators warms the mechanical wrist bracket, inducing thermoelastic drift in the strain gauge. Sensor bias ${b}({t})$ creeps at 0.2 $\text{N/min}$. Over 15 $\text{min}$, this thermal drift shifts ${b}({t})$ by 3 N. True physical force on the workpiece drops from 5 N to 2 N, a 60 percent loss of grip force, even as the estimator reports high statistical certainty in its original value.

The downstream controller responds to this false certainty by regulating joint torques to maintain what it believes is an optimal 5 N clamping force. If the workpiece requires substantial friction to resist gravity, the actual 2 N contact load allows it to slip from the jaws, even as telemetry shows the grip force sitting exactly on target. Standard filter monitoring cannot catch this failure. The estimator evaluates its innovation sequence (the residual formulation of [[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/03 Running the Machine/09 Spatial Memory/V4-09.03 Estimating State from Dated Measurements#^sec-perception-state-estimation|section 9.2]]), comparing each incoming measurement ${y}({t})$ against its predicted observation ${\hat{y}}({t})$. Throughout the entire drift sequence, the estimator predicts ${\hat{y}}({t}) = {\hat{x}}({t}) + {\hat{b}}({t}) =$ 5 N, which exactly matches the incoming sensor reading ${y}({t}) =$ 5 N. The innovation ${\nu}({t})$ evaluates to zero at every sample step. An innovation check tests only whether new sensor data conflicts with the model’s forward projection; it cannot expose errors aligned with an unobservable subspace where the sensor output remains entirely invariant to physical state drift.

Restoring observability requires system designers to introduce an external constraint that breaks the symmetry along the unobservable subspace. An engineering architecture accomplishes this in one of three ways. First, independent sensing can introduce a secondary measurement channel governed by distinct failure physics, such as monitoring joint motor drive currents or optical deflection sensors that do not share the strain gauge’s thermoelastic drift. Second, the machine can execute deliberate physical excitation. After securing the workpiece with an external fixture, the arm commands the gripper to break contact briefly for 40 $\text{ms}$, establishing a known zero-force boundary condition (${x}({t}) =$ 0 N) where the raw telemetry directly exposes the sensor bias ${b}({t})$. Third, the machine can rely on periodic external anchoring, bringing the end-effector into contact with a calibrated reference load cell during scheduled tool-change routines to zero the bias estimate. The mobile manipulator’s base already depends on this third remedy. Wheel odometry leaves its longitudinal position along the aisle unobservable, and only the map-based corrections of [[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/03 Running the Machine/09 Spatial Memory/V4-09.06 Coordinate Transform Expiry and Localization Drift#^sec-memory-localization-slam|section 9.5.1]] supply the missing spatial constraint. When no admissible excitation separates two states that call for different responses before the machine must act, and no added channel is available, the states are observationally indistinguishable and no model on that channel can tell them apart. **[[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/04 Governing the Machine/18 Engineering Physical AI/V4-18.04 The Epistemic Frontier#^sec-frontier-observational-indistinguishability-sub|Observational indistinguishability and the action deadline]]** develops this limit and the operating restrictions it forces.

When a physical state cannot be constrained through complementary sensing or active excitation, the estimation layer must treat that state as unobservable and refuse to publish unwarranted precision. The software interface must either mark the affected state dimensions as invalid or inflate their uncertainty bounds based on the hardware’s worst-case physical drift rate, overriding the artificially contracted covariance generated by the filter. Formal observability analysis provides the mathematical foundation for identifying these blind spots. Classical linear state-space formulations originate with Rudolf E. Kalman ([[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/03 Running the Machine/09 Spatial Memory/V4-09.15 Source References#^ref-kalman1960general|1960]]), while nonlinear extensions establish differential observability rank conditions on the Lie derivatives of the observation field ([[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/03 Running the Machine/09 Spatial Memory/V4-09.15 Source References#^ref-hermann1977nonlinear|Hermann and Krener 1977]]). Systems engineers do not recompute these differential rank conditions in real-time control loops. Instead, the design task is to identify during system modeling which operating regimes lose observability rank, ensuring that runtime state estimators never claim statistical convergence along an unconstrained physical dimension.

↪ The Kalman and Hermann-Krener observability rank conditions are developed in **[[05 Reference/Volume IV - Physical AI/V4 Control and Dynamics#^sec-appendix-control-covariance|Continuous Covariance Propagation and Observability]]**.

Deterministic observability establishes whether system dynamics and sensor equations can distinguish trajectories over time, independent of stochastic noise assumptions. Nevertheless, practical estimator convergence and calibrated covariance bounds demand further conditions: accurately identified kinematic parameters, persistent trajectory excitation, and well-characterized sensor noise statistics. A manipulator trajectory can, for instance, leave a required state direction unconstrained by the available measurements; this estimator observability condition must be checked separately from the arm’s kinematic singularities. Robust physical AI systems therefore deploy runtime monitors that track the validity conditions of each estimator. These watchdogs cross-check redundant sensing channels, track kinematic condition numbers, and enforce re-zeroing schedules. While runtime monitors cannot guarantee the truth of every modeling assumption, any detected violation must immediately invalidate the dependent state estimate. When an estimator explicitly revokes its own confidence, downstream controllers can transition cleanly to a validated fallback policy. An estimator that invalidates its own output fails safely and visibly; the dangerous failures in physical AI occur when an estimator reports tight confidence along an unobservable manifold, silently blinding the controller to physical reality.
