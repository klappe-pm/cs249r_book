# V4-08.09 The Observation Contract

[[Home|Home]] · [[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/Volume 4 Overview|Volume Overview]] · [[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/03 Running the Machine/08 Sensor Perception/V4-08 Sensor Perception|V4-08 Sensor Perception]]

^sec-perception-observation-contract

A downstream tracker, policy backbone, or permission path cannot reconstruct exposure timing, mounting calibration, or byte integrity from a raw tensor alone. The perception subsystem therefore wraps each sensory output in an **observation contract**, the sixth record in the book’s sixteen-record chain. In closed-loop physical systems, an observation is unusable without an explicit capture epoch, an identified clock domain, a bounded conversion to the permission path’s clock, and an extrinsic calibration operating within its qualified validity envelope. A safe consumer must reject any incoming record lacking these guarantees before inspecting a single payload byte. The system’s shared clock domain and ${\mathcal{H}}_1$ age budget originate in the nervous-system handoff record of **[[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/01 Machine Anatomy/04 The Nervous System/V4-04.10 The Handoffs Matrix#^sec-nervous-handoffs-matrix|The Handoffs Matrix]]**; perception, in turn, provides the capture-side timestamps and uncertainty bounds that downstream consumers need to verify compliance.

↪ The sixteen-record chain, in which the observation contract is Record 6, is developed in **[[05 Reference/Volume IV - Physical AI/V4 Systems and Hardware#^sec-appendix-systems-record-register|The record register]]**.

Formally, the contract couples either a raw observation tensor or an extracted geometric bounding envelope with the validation metadata that downstream consumers require ([[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/03 Running the Machine/08 Sensor Perception/V4-08.09 The Observation Contract#^eq-percep-observation-contract-tuple|equation 12]]):

$$
\mathcal{O} = \langle {t_{\text{capture}}}, \text{clock\_id}, {\epsilon_{\text{sync}}}, \text{frame\_id}, {\mathbf{T}_{\text{payload}}}, {\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{\text{body}}}, {C_{\text{inset}}}, \text{mask}_{\text{health}}, \text{crc32} \rangle \tag{12}
$$

 In this tuple, ${t_{\text{capture}}}$ records the exposure-midpoint timestamp registered on monotonic clock $\text{clock\_id}$, ${\epsilon_{\text{sync}}}$ bounds the maximum synchronization error when converting to the clock of the safety MCU that hosts the permission path, and $\text{frame\_id}$ identifies the calibrated sensor transform. The payload ${\mathbf{T}_{\text{payload}}}$ contains the raw sensor array or derived geometric bounding envelope, ${\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{\text{body}}}$ characterizes its spatial covariance, and ${C_{\text{inset}}}$ provides the qualified clearance inset. The health mask $\text{mask}_{\text{health}}$ conveys hardware and perceptual health status bits, and the checksum $\text{crc32}$ seals both header and payload against transport corruption. Spatial memory consumes this record to update its world model, while trajectory planning uses ${C_{\text{inset}}}$ to inflate collision boundaries. Both modules evaluate evidence age, verify kinematic coordinate frames, and bound spatial uncertainty before allowing an observation to influence a control decision.

^eq-percep-observation-contract-tuple

The tuple is the same object as the error-qualified claim $({\mathbf{p}_{\text{body}}}, {\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{\text{body}}}, {C_{\text{inset}}}, {t_{\text{age}}})$ that [[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/03 Running the Machine/08 Sensor Perception/V4-08.07 Spatial Error and Clearance Bounds#^sec-perception-error-clearance|section 8.6]] assembled, now wrapped for transport. The claim’s point travels in ${\mathbf{T}_{\text{payload}}}$, its covariance in ${\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{\text{body}}}$, and its inset in ${C_{\text{inset}}}$. Its age is deliberately not stored, because each consumer computes the current evidence age from ${t_{\text{capture}}}$ and ${\epsilon_{\text{sync}}}$ on its own clock.

This contract does not replace binary serialization formats such as Protocol Buffers, FlatBuffers, or ROS 2 IDL; any of these serialization systems can transmit the underlying struct over shared memory or bus fabrics. While transport schemas standardize memory layout and data types, the observation contract establishes verifiable physical invariants: monotonic temporal progression, bounded clock skew, active calibration validity windows, and rigorous spatial uncertainty limits. [[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/03 Running the Machine/08 Sensor Perception/V4-08.09 The Observation Contract#^tbl-08-observation-record-schema|Table 5]] serializes the tuple as named fields, details each one alongside its validation rule, and populates the schema for the mobile manipulator’s navigation camera. It splits ${t_{\text{capture}}}$ and $\text{clock\_id}$ into `capture_timestamp_ns` and `capture_clock_id`, carries the calibration behind $\text{frame\_id}$ in `calibration_metadata`, and adds a `sequence_index` and a `provenance_digest` that the tuple leaves out.

^tbl-08-observation-record-schema

The fields detailed in [[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/03 Running the Machine/08 Sensor Perception/V4-08.09 The Observation Contract#^tbl-08-observation-record-schema|table 5]] anchor each observation in physical time, geometric space, and software revision history. The capture timestamp ${t_{\text{capture}}}$ must refer to a strictly monotonic hardware timer rather than wall-clock time (such as nanoseconds since the UNIX epoch), which remains vulnerable to stepping backward during network time adjustments. Because rolling-shutter sensors read out scan lines sequentially, any spatial claim derived from a rolling-shutter imager must explicitly identify the image row or region corresponding to that capture instant. The transport-level `sequence_index` counter detects frame drops and out-of-order deliveries directly on the bus, independently of clock readings. When the safety MCU operates in a separate clock domain from the camera host, an active synchronization daemon provides a versioned affine mapping ${t_{\text{MCU}}} = {a} {t_{\text{capture}}} + {b_{\text{clk}}}$, an expiration window, and an empirical sync-error bound ${\epsilon_{\text{sync}}}$, characterized as described in [[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/03 Running the Machine/08 Sensor Perception/V4-08.07 Spatial Error and Clearance Bounds#^sec-perception-qualifying-error|section 8.6.2]].

Systems engineering demands precise conventions when accounting for sensor latency. The handoff matrix and the perception latency budget evaluate the sensor’s latency contribution under different row conventions, and the observation contract must explicitly state which convention governs the record. The handoff matrix allots ${\mathcal{H}}_1$ one frame period (16.7 ms at 60 Hz) and references the entire frame by its middle row. Because rolling-shutter rows stagger their exposures at the exact rate they read out, the middle row finishes its own exposure halfway through the frame’s readout window and waits only the remaining ${t_{\text{readout}}}/2$ for the bottom half of the array to clock off the die, so measuring from that middle row’s exposure midpoint to the completion of sensor readout yields a sensor-side latency of ${t_{\text{exp}}}/2 + {t_{\text{readout}}}/2 =$ 16.3 ms, which falls well within the handoff allotment. Conversely, the perception latency waterfall charges for the top row: ${t_{\text{exp}}}/2 + {t_{\text{readout}}} =$ 24.6 ms. Because a physical hazard such as a warehouse rack upright can appear in the very first scan lines, a safety-certified stopping budget must charge for the oldest sensory evidence underpinning the spatial claim. Under this oldest-row convention, the navigation camera exceeds the handoff allotment by 7.9 ms. The two numbers answer different questions, and the stopping budget governs. The ${\mathcal{H}}_1$ allotment is a design budget for the sensing handoff, stated in the middle-row convention, and the camera meets it. The safety charge is row five, which uses this chapter’s oldest-row waterfall. The 7.9 ms is already inside the 51.6 ms that row five charges, so nothing is missing from the stopping budget. What the mismatch does require is a record that says which convention dated it. The capture metadata therefore stamps the reference row index, so that a consumer checking ${\mathcal{H}}_1$ and a consumer checking clearance each read the age they need from the same field.

Upon dispatching an observation, the producer records an upper bound on its observation age at dispatch as ${t_{\text{MCU,dispatch}}} - {t_{\text{MCU,capture}}} + {\epsilon_{\text{sync}}}$. That metric reflects only the latency accumulated up to the producer’s egress boundary. By the time the permission path admits the record, the evidence has aged further by the dispatch-to-admission transit delay ${\Delta_{\text{da}}}$, and it ages further still before actuators execute a command. The permission path must evaluate freshness using its local monotonic clock against the original capture epoch; treating a static dispatch timestamp as if time had frozen violates basic physical causality. Submitting a new trajectory proposal derived from a previously buffered observation does not refresh the underlying sensory evidence.

For the navigation camera, the **dispatch-local** threshold equals the budgeted observation age 51.6 ms plus the 0.2 ms clock-conversion bound, yielding 51.8 ms. The threshold sits exactly at the budget because row five of the stopping budget charges exactly that age; admitting an older frame would spend travel that no row accounts for. A frame that meets every stage budget therefore reaches dispatch with zero spare margin, and any host contention, pipeline queuing, or batching beyond those budgets pushes it past the threshold. The stage values are allocations each stage must meet at its tail, not typical latencies, so a pipeline that meets them refuses only the frames that overrun them. In the vehicle’s stopping budget, the 51.6 ms perception delay maps directly to physical travel distance at cruising velocity. The 0.3 mm displacement associated with the clock-conversion uncertainty bound is absorbed into the localization uncertainty parameter ${\delta_{\text{loc}}}$. Neither accounting choice eliminates the ongoing physical aging that occurs after dispatch.

Admitting an observation into the motion-planning safety pipeline requires an empirically qualified current-age limit that accounts for the dispatch-to-admission bound ${\Delta_{\text{da}}}$ while reserving sufficient execution time for the active motion lease and physical braking response. Because this partial budget has not yet bounded ${\Delta_{\text{da}}}$, the populated record in [[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/03 Running the Machine/08 Sensor Perception/V4-08.09 The Observation Contract#^tbl-08-observation-record-schema|table 5]] illustrates correct structural field construction rather than an admitted full-speed trajectory. Once engineers measure and bound ${\Delta_{\text{da}}}$ across all inter-process channels, they can either close the stopping budget or reduce maximum vehicle velocity to restore safety margins. Latency accounting conserves time. Whether an engineer anchors the timeline at physical capture or measures from the admission gate by summing prior evidence age with the active lease, each elapsed interval appears exactly once in the stopping budget.

The transform identifier `sensor_frame_id` and its calibration revision key the observation into the robot’s kinematic transform tree.[[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/03 Running the Machine/08 Sensor Perception/V4-08.14 Footnotes#^fn14|¹⁴]] The provenance digest records the exact SHA-256 hash of the vision model weights and camera firmware, providing an auditable record of software configuration. Concurrently, `payload_crc32` guards against transmission glitches across shared-memory rings or serial buses. Data integrity is not semantic truth. Checksums and build digests detect accidental bit flips and firmware mismatches, but they cannot verify whether an image is optically uncorrupted, nor do they prevent adversarial tampering without a keyed cryptographic signature.

The `health_bitmask` structures failure reporting across three architectural layers. The lowest bits report hardware-level faults, such as clock synchronization loss, DMA ring buffer overruns, or physical link dropouts. The intermediate bits signal environmental degradation, including sensor saturation from direct sunlight, optical blooming, and aerosol backscatter from dust or fog. The upper bits represent perceptual validity concerns, such as severe field-of-view occlusion or out-of-distribution (OOD) sensory inputs that fall outside the training support tested during system evaluation. When any safety-critical bit is set, downstream motion planners and trackers must immediately reject spatial claims derived from that frame.

An explicit observation schema also creates a systematic interface for automated fault injection. Verification harnesses can deterministically alter capture timestamps, drop sequence numbers, artificially inflate covariance diagonals, or assert occlusion bits, confirming that downstream consumers reject or gracefully degrade unviable observations within their budgeted time limits. These software mutations demonstrate consumer responses for the tested corruptions under the tested budgets; evaluating resilience against physical phenomena such as optical flare, mounting deflection, and actuator dynamics requires hardware-in-the-loop testing regimes.

↪ Hardware-in-the-loop testing of physical phenomena is developed in **[[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/04 Governing the Machine/15 Adversarial Verification/V4-15.09 Fault Injection on Hardware#^sec-verification-hardware-fault-injection|Fault Injection on Hardware]]**.

Learned policies require explicit contract metadata just as much as classical state estimators do. An end-to-end policy ${\mathbf{a}}_{t} = \pi({\mathbf{z}_{t_0}})$ that receives only sensory pixels or point clouds cannot distinguish two identical images consumed under radically different pipeline latencies. Widely used vision-language-action (VLA) models do not receive these quantities. RT-2 and OpenVLA condition on visual tokens and a language prompt ([[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/03 Running the Machine/08 Sensor Perception/V4-08.13 Source References#^ref-brohan2023rt2|Brohan et al. 2023]]; [[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/03 Running the Machine/08 Sensor Perception/V4-08.13 Source References#^ref-kim2024openvla|Kim et al. 2024]]), and $\pi_0$ adds proprioceptive joint state ([[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/03 Running the Machine/08 Sensor Perception/V4-08.13 Source References#^ref-black2024pi0|Black et al. 2024]]), but none of them is given capture age, extrinsic transforms, or measurement covariance as input. To integrate learned models safely into real-time control loops, this book presents an **observation tokenizer** that projects the full observation contract into the token space of a VLA policy or neural estimator. The sensory modality dictates the raw payload encoding (patch tokens from vision transformers, range tokens from lidar arrays, spike tokens from event cameras, or stress fields from tactile arrays), while the tokenizer interface preserves the capture epoch, geometric frame, and measurement uncertainty associated with that payload.

Under this architecture, the observation tokenizer pairs the sensory feature tokens ${\mathbf{z}_{\text{sensor}}}$ with continuous Fourier temporal embeddings (${\mathbf{e}_{\Delta t}} = \text{Fourier}({t_{\text{current}}} - {t}_0)$), coordinate frame prefix embeddings (${\mathbf{e}_{\text{frame}}} = \text{MLP}({\mathbf{T}_{bc}})$), and spatial covariance embeddings (${\mathbf{e}_{\Sigma}} = \text{MLP}(\text{vec}({\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{\text{body}}}))$) ([[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/03 Running the Machine/08 Sensor Perception/V4-08.09 The Observation Contract#^eq-percep-vla-obs-tokenizer|equation 13]]):

$$
{\mathbf{z}_{\text{token}}} = [{\mathbf{z}_{\text{sensor}}}, {\mathbf{e}_{\Delta t}}, {\mathbf{e}_{\text{frame}}}, {\mathbf{e}_{\Sigma}}] \tag{13}
$$

 A forward-propagated state token ${\mathbf{e}_{\text{state}}} = \text{MLP}({\hat{\mathbf{x}}_{t_{\text{current}}}})$, generated by the state estimator that spatial memory maintains, can accompany these tokens so the policy conditions on the robot’s predicted state at the instant of actuation rather than its historical configuration at sensor exposure. Because the observation contract preserves the exact capture epoch, delayed frames do not become worthless garbage. When network congestion or compute preemption delays delivery, a state estimator can ingest an out-of-order observation at the precise historical instant it captured and propagate corrections forward to the current state, a technique that spatial memory develops in **[[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/03 Running the Machine/09 Spatial Memory/V4-09.03 Estimating State from Dated Measurements#^sec-perception-discrete-state-estimation|Applying a delayed observation]]**. While this embedding scheme exposes physical time, frame geometry, and uncertainty directly to transformer attention layers, engineers must verify that the model actually attends to these tokens in closed-loop operation. Demonstrating that a policy uses temporal embeddings requires rigorous ablation experiments that inject artificial latency into held-out evaluation trials and measure resulting closed-loop tracking error. Embedding metadata as neural tokens also does not substitute for deterministic safety gating. The permission path must continue to verify clock bounds, extrinsic calibration envelopes, innovation residuals (the gap between each measurement and the estimator’s prediction of it), and health status bits before any network output is permitted to drive the physical actuators.

**Checkpoint 8.3: Observation contract checks**

A consumer can refuse only what the record lets it check, and it must check before it reads the payload.

**Record triage**: Can you explain why a health bit, checksum, valid clock conversion, and freshness check must precede consumption of a spatial claim?

**Timestamp corruption vs. dropped packets**: Can you describe why receiving a sensory frame with a desynchronized timestamp degrades spatial state estimation and policy grounding far more severely than dropping the packet entirely?

**Deterministic fault injection**: Can you distinguish between physically perturbing optical sensors vs. deterministically mutating fields in the shared-memory observation contract during automated hardware-in-the-loop verification?

**Age on the consumer’s clock**: Can you explain why the permission path must recompute evidence age on its own monotonic clock from the capture epoch, rather than trusting the age stamped at dispatch, and why its 51.8 ms dispatch-local threshold is not the limit it applies at admission?
