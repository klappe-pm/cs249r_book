# V4-08.02 Choosing Evidence for a Spatial Decision

[[Home|Home]] · [[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/Volume 4 Overview|Volume Overview]] · [[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/03 Running the Machine/08 Sensor Perception/V4-08 Sensor Perception|V4-08 Sensor Perception]]

^sec-perception-photons-to-claims

A vision model on the warehouse mobile manipulator can detect a worker stepping past a rack upright with near-perfect benchmark accuracy and still hand the trajectory planner an obsolete position. The world keeps moving while the navigation camera integrates charge, serializes pixel rows across the bus, and runs inference through the vision pipeline. On this machine, 51.6 ms elapses between the midpoint of optical exposure and the dispatch of the processed observation (derived in the latency waterfall of [[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/03 Running the Machine/08 Sensor Perception/V4-08.05 Bounding Observation Age#^sec-perception-latency-waterfall|section 8.4.1]]). Driving at its 1.5 m/s drive limit, the base covers 77.4 mm over that single interval. Benchmark accuracy reveals nothing about whether an observation is still fresh enough to support safe clearance. On a moving machine, every observation carries an expiration date.

Every motion a physical AI system executes rests on a claim about the world derived from earlier sensor readings. Perception converts raw energy incident on a detector (photons on a CMOS sensor array, laser pulses reflected to an avalanche photodiode, or elastomer deformation at a fingertip) into spatial claims: the volume a coworker occupies, the metric range to a rack upright, or the incipient slip of a grasped object. Machine perception has historically been scored by static recognition accuracy on fixed benchmarks, which rewards a correct label regardless of when the exposure occurred or how the camera was moving. For an embodied machine that abstraction collapses. A spatial claim can support motion only if it states three properties: the instant its energy was captured, the coordinate frame in which it was measured, and a bound on its spatial uncertainty. Set against the machine’s current velocity, that qualified uncertainty must fit inside the clearance that remains.

The evaluation record that closed Part II (**[[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/02 Teaching the Machine/07 Closed-Loop Evaluation/V4-07.09 The Evaluation Record#^sec-evaluation-logs|The Evaluation Record]]**) hands the runtime a list of obligations rather than a verdict of trust. It limits the learned proposer to its target operational design domain (ODD) and leaves the rest of the machine to supply the runtime monitors that hold it there (**[[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/02 Teaching the Machine/07 Closed-Loop Evaluation/V4-07.10 Verification Ceilings and the Exposure Wall#^sec-evaluation-limits|Verification Ceilings and the Exposure Wall]]**). Two of those monitors, the input-support monitor and the latency and freshness monitor, can act only on observations that carry their capture time, frame, and uncertainty, so their first obligation falls on perception. Perception delivers this runtime evidence as dated spatial claims across the proposal boundary. It executes within the non-deterministic Brain above that boundary and holds no actuator authority. The deterministic permission path below the boundary evaluates each proposed action against the capture timestamps, coordinate frames, and error qualifications of the perceptual claims behind it. Part III’s governing law, proposal is not permission (principle [[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/03 Running the Machine/V4 Running the Machine#^pri-vol4-proposal-permission|3]]), applies to perception in a specific way. A confident detection does not license motion. It is evidence behind a proposal, and the permission path can refuse that proposal only when the evidence carries an age, a frame, and an error bound it can check.

↩︎ The proposal boundary and permission path are established in **[[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/00 Introduction/01 The Causal Boundary/V4-01.08 The Machine in Five Levels#^sec-boundary-machine-model|The Machine in Five Levels]]**.

Checking a claim against physical margin means following the evidence from capture to qualification. Each sensor type must first match the physical quantity an action requires. Calibration and detector physics then ground the measurement in space and time, the capture-to-dispatch age must be budgeted, and the spatial error, including the silent corruption no driver reports, must be bounded before any downstream check permits motion. The chapter closes by packaging those properties into an observation contract, so that a consumer can check a measurement’s age, frame, and error before reading a single payload byte. The contract is the first record of the runtime strand, which meets the limit record and the evaluation record at the permission decision. Perception ends once it yields a qualified measurement. Fusing such measurements across time into persistent spatial state is the work of state estimation and spatial memory in **[[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/03 Running the Machine/09 Spatial Memory/V4-09 Spatial Memory#^sec-memory|Spatial Memory]]**.

Whether an architecture employs an end-to-end vision-language-action (VLA) policy ([[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/03 Running the Machine/08 Sensor Perception/V4-08.13 Source References#^ref-brohan2023rt2|Brohan et al. 2023]]; [[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/03 Running the Machine/08 Sensor Perception/V4-08.13 Source References#^ref-kim2024openvla|Kim et al. 2024]]), an end-to-end visuomotor model without a language backbone ([[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/03 Running the Machine/08 Sensor Perception/V4-08.13 Source References#^ref-zhao2023learning|Zhao et al. 2023]]), or a modular estimator, it must reconcile the physical scene captured at a past instant with the kinematic state of the robot at execution. Architectural choices alter processing latency and representation formats, but no network topology can bypass photon integration, sensor readout, or bus transport delay. Explicit timestamps and spatial transforms keep this relationship inspectable; a latent representation holds it only implicitly, so checking it requires external instrumentation. Neither approach converts ambiguous sensor signals into ground truth. A specular reflection on a polished warehouse floor remains an optical hazard regardless of model capacity.

Navigating past a blind rack corner requires establishing a bounded occupied volume in the robot’s base coordinate frame before available stopping distance runs out. RGB appearance helps classify an object as a person, but monocular bearing alone cannot supply metric range. The navigation camera therefore requires corroborated depth measurements before its detections can justify motion clearance, and on the running machine the spinning LiDAR introduced below supplies them. At the end-effector, sensing requirements change completely. While external optical cameras guide the initial reaching approach, detecting contact mechanics, shear forces, and incipient slip requires tactile sensing directly at the fingertips. Sensor selection begins by identifying the physical quantity governing each action, followed by analyzing sensor coverage, update rates, and environmental failure modes.

[[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/03 Running the Machine/08 Sensor Perception/V4-08.02 Choosing Evidence for a Spatial Decision#^tbl-08-sensor-modalities|Table 1]] compares the physical evidence each sensing mechanism provides alongside the primary failure modes and safety checks required before fusing claims across an embodied sensor suite. Adding a secondary sensor mitigates a shared failure mode only when its independent spatial coverage, timing guarantees, extrinsic calibration, and correlated failure risks are established.

| Modality         | Direct physical information                                                  | Main limitation                                                                                | Permission-relevant check                             |
|:-----------------|:-----------------------------------------------------------------------------|:-----------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------|:------------------------------------------------------|
| RGB camera       | Bearing, appearance, and image motion                                        | Monocular depth scale is ambiguous; glare, darkness, and occlusion can hide objects            | Fresh capture time and validated metric corroboration |
| Active stereo    | Disparity-derived range with infrared texture projection                     | Sunlight washes out projected pattern; range error grows quadratically with distance (${z}^2$) | Range-dependent residual and bias validation          |
| Structured light | High-density triangulated depth from projected coded patterns                | Ambient solar infrared flooding; multi-robot pattern crosstalk; short range ($<3\,\text{m}$)   | Lighting lux limits and pattern decoding integrity    |
| Time-of-flight   | Metric depth via phase delay (iToF) or photon arrival timing (dToF)          | iToF phase wrapping; multipath reflections in corners; flying pixels on moving edges           | Ambiguity distance and corner multipath flags         |
| Pulsed LiDAR     | Metric time-of-flight range at sampled laser beam directions                 | Near-field aerosol backscatter in fog/rain; retroreflector blooming; specular puddle dropouts  | Qualified range, return quality, and scan timing      |
| FMCW radar       | Metric range and instantaneous radial Doppler velocity of targets            | Multipath ghost returns off ground; lower angular resolution; antenna sidelobe clutter         | Track association and coverage of crossing targets    |
| Event camera     | Asynchronous per-pixel log-intensity contrast events ($\ge {C_{\text{th}}}$) | Unchanging scenes supply little event evidence; noise and flicker can still generate events    | Event-rate health and independent metric depth source |
| Optical tactile  | Contact micro-depth, surface normals, and shear force vectors                | Zero sensing before physical contact; strictly localized to gripper elastomeric pad            | Contact threshold, slip flags, and marker tracking    |

Table 1: **Spatial sensor modality comparison**: Sensor mechanisms provide complementary but fallible claims across physical operating regimes. Numeric range, accuracy, refresh, and link budgets require a named device and mode. For example, [Prophesee EVT2](https://docs.prophesee.ai/stable/data/encoding_formats/evt2.html) encodes an event in 32 bits, so an event camera’s link load scales with its event rate before transport overhead.

On an operational robot, this division of responsibility is explicit and follows established industrial safety standards. Neither the vision model nor the spinning LiDAR owns the certified safety claim regarding an obstacle at the rack end. ISO 3691-4 mandates that an autonomous industrial truck detect persons in its path at Performance Level d (PL d) ([[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/03 Running the Machine/08 Sensor Perception/V4-08.13 Source References#^ref-iso3691part4|International Organization for Standardization 2023b]]; [[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/03 Running the Machine/08 Sensor Perception/V4-08.13 Source References#^ref-tuv2021iso3691|TÜV Rheinland 2021]]), the second highest of the five reliability grades (a through e) defined in ISO 13849-1 ([[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/03 Running the Machine/08 Sensor Perception/V4-08.13 Source References#^ref-iso13849part1|International Organization for Standardization 2023a]]). Mobile bases satisfy this mandate using an industrial safety laser scanner, a dual-channel planar time-of-flight sensor certified as an active opto-electronic protective device under IEC 61496-3 ([[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/03 Running the Machine/08 Sensor Perception/V4-08.13 Source References#^ref-iec61496part3|International Electrotechnical Commission 2025]]; [[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/03 Running the Machine/08 Sensor Perception/V4-08.13 Source References#^ref-keyence2026szv|Keyence Corporation 2026]]). By design and certification standards, the safety scanner performs no semantic classification; it reports solely whether an opaque object has entered its protective optical field, directly triggering the certified emergency stop circuit.

The mobile base’s spinning LiDAR (an Ouster OS1-64) carries no functional safety rating; its datasheet certifies only electrical, laser, and electromagnetic compatibility ([[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/03 Running the Machine/08 Sensor Perception/V4-08.13 Source References#^ref-ouster2025os1|Ouster, Inc. 2025]]). Instead, it provides the dense 3D metric range field that the permission path evaluates against spatial clearance limits. The permission path runs on its own safety microcontroller (MCU), as **[[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/01 Machine Anatomy/04 The Nervous System/V4-04 The Nervous System#^sec-nervous|The Nervous System]]** placed it, but the clearance evaluation it performs there is an unrated admission check, a software layer above the certified safety scanner. Two components therefore guard the rack end, the permission path’s unrated clearance check and the certified scanner beneath it. The permission path’s role is to keep learned proposals smooth and proactive, adjusting trajectories well before the robot approaches the scanner’s protective boundary and causes an abrupt hardware stop.

↪ The certified drive, scanner, and arm safety functions beneath the permission path, and the one rated watchdog on it, are developed in **[[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/03 Running the Machine/12 Safety Enforcement/V4-12.10 The Fallback Ladder#^sec-enforcement-certified-functions|Certified safety functions beneath the permission path]]**.

Meanwhile, the navigation camera enriches the learned proposer with semantic context: distinguishing a coworker from a stationary tote and predicting velocity headings. Because the proposer constructs its trajectory predictions relative to the camera frame, that image frame’s capture timestamp defines the evidence epoch carried by the resulting motion proposal. [[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/03 Running the Machine/08 Sensor Perception/V4-08.05 Bounding Observation Age#^sec-perception-lidar-age|Section 8.4.2]] details how the high-rate LiDAR stream is synchronized and cross-dated against this camera epoch. When operational conditions shift, other sensors fill the gaps: active illumination resolves textureless surfaces, event cameras preserve high-speed edge transitions across wide dynamic ranges, and tactile arrays capture localized contact dynamics that remote cameras cannot see. Before any of these sensors can anchor downstream motion, however, the system must account for how physical capture and mounting geometry distort the raw signal.
