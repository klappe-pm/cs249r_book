# V4-08.03 Capture Physics and Calibration

[[Home|Home]] · [[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/Volume 4 Overview|Volume Overview]] · [[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/03 Running the Machine/08 Sensor Perception/V4-08 Sensor Perception|V4-08 Sensor Perception]]

^sec-perception-transduction-calibration

When photons from a warehouse rack upright strike the navigation camera’s silicon detector, they do not become an instantaneous feature tensor in host memory. Before a neural backbone or state observer ever reads the frame, the photodiode array must accumulate charge over a finite exposure window, serialize rows off the sensor die, and move bytes across the interconnect, while the robot keeps moving through every millisecond of that process. In CMOS active-pixel sensors, incident photons generate electron-hole pairs through the photoelectric effect according to a wavelength-dependent quantum efficiency ${\eta_{\text{QE}}}({\lambda_{\text{wl}}})$. Silicon photodiodes accumulate this photocharge across a finite exposure interval ${t_{\text{exp}}}$, collecting total charge:

$$
{Q} = \int_0^{{t_{\text{exp}}}} \frac{{q} {\eta_{\text{QE}}}({\lambda_{\text{wl}}})}{{h} {\nu}} {P_{\text{opt}}}({t})\,d{t}
$$

 where ${q}$ is the elementary charge, ${h}$ is Planck’s constant, ${\nu}$ is optical frequency, and ${P_{\text{opt}}}({t})$ is incident optical power. Exposure integration, readout serialization, bus transport across PCIe or MIPI CSI, memory staging, preprocessing kernels, and neural backbone inference each consume a slice of the end-to-end latency budget.

Because the photodiode accumulates charge over the finite exposure duration ${t_{\text{exp}}}$, the captured frame integrates scene radiance over time while both the robot and the surrounding environment are in motion. Assuming approximately constant linear velocity ${\mathbf{v}}$ and angular velocity ${\boldsymbol{\omega}}$, with the optical center displaced from the vehicle’s center of rotation by lever arm ${\mathbf{r}_{P/O}}$, a conservative bound on the physical displacement of the camera center during exposure is:

$$
{\Delta x_{\text{motion}}} = \|{\mathbf{v}}\| \, {t_{\text{exp}}} + \|{\boldsymbol{\omega}} \times {\mathbf{r}_{P/O}}\| \, {t_{\text{exp}}}
$$

 This distance measures the physical displacement of the camera center, not the pixel footprint of the blur on the detector plane. Projecting this displacement into image pixels requires scene depth, focal length, viewing direction, and relative attitude changes. Pure rotational motion can smear the image severely even when the camera center remains completely stationary. The resulting **motion blur** acts as a low-pass spatial filter, attenuating image gradients and destroying high-frequency edge information before neural feature extraction or corner detection can run. Downstream estimators can use accurate timestamps and extrinsic calibrations to position the blurred observation at its capture epoch, but no coordinate transformation can synthesize geometric detail lost during optical integration.

The finite integration window also determines which physical instant the resulting image represents. Under an idealized linear sensor response, the pixel value reflects incoming scene radiance ${I}({t})$ integrated across the exposure window:

$$
{I_{\text{pixel}}} = \int_{0}^{{t_{\text{exp}}}} {I}({t})\,d{t}
$$

The exposure midpoint is the reference epoch assigned to the integrated image, exactly ${t_{\text{exp}}}/2$ after the shutter opens and ${t_{\text{exp}}}/2$ before it closes. This convention dates the measurement without making its blurred content an instantaneous sample; changes in illumination or motion within the window still require qualification. For the mobile base running at its 1.5 m/s drive limit with the navigation camera configured to a 16 ms exposure, the frame represents the scene where the robot was 8 ms earlier, while the robot traverses 24 mm of translation during the exposure itself. Exposure time is a configured software parameter rather than an immutable hardware constant. The warehouse mobile manipulator configures this relatively long exposure to operate reliably in dim aisle lighting; higher ambient illumination permits shorter integration times, scaling down both motion blur and midpoint latency proportionally.

### Rolling Shutter Distortion and Motion Compensation

^rolling-shutter-distortion-and-motion-compensation

Assigning a single exposure midpoint to an entire image assumes that every pixel row integrates light over that same window. In a **global shutter** sensor, every photodiode integrates charge during the exact same time window and transfers accumulated electrons to shielded in-pixel storage nodes simultaneously. In contrast, cost and silicon die area constraints often dictate the use of a **rolling shutter** sensor, where rows are reset, exposed, and read out sequentially from top to bottom (${y} = 0$ to ${y} = {H} - 1$). Each successive scanline begins its exposure after an incremental row readout period ${t_{\text{row}}}$, introducing a progressive time skew across the vertical axis:

$$
{t_{\text{scan}}}({y}) = {t_{\text{start}}} + {y} \cdot {t_{\text{row}}}
$$

 where ${t_{\text{start}}}$ denotes the exposure start time of the initial row (${y} = 0$). When the vehicle translates at linear velocity ${\mathbf{v}}$ perpendicular to a vertical object, scanline ${y}$ samples that object at an along-track spatial offset:

$$
{\Delta \mathbf{x}}({y}) = {\mathbf{v}} \cdot {y} \cdot {t_{\text{row}}} \tag{1}
$$

 **[[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/02 Teaching the Machine/05 Physical Data/V4-05.06 Action Label Selection Across the Control Boundary#^sec-data-rolling-shutter-skew|Rolling shutter skew across camera streams]]** met this row timing as a skew that corrupts the pairing of a logged frame with an action. On a moving base the same skew becomes the spatial offset of [[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/03 Running the Machine/08 Sensor Perception/V4-08.03 Capture Physics and Calibration#^eq-percep-rolling-shutter-skew|equation 1]], and it is charged against clearance.

^eq-percep-rolling-shutter-skew

Under angular motion, rolling shutter distortion becomes non-linear. If the camera rotates with angular velocity vector ${\boldsymbol{\omega}} = [{\omega_x}, {\omega_y}, {\omega_z}]^\top$ (for example, rapid yaw or pitch during aggressive vehicle maneuvers), the camera attitude drifts continuously throughout the readout phase. The optical axis points in a slightly different direction for every single scanline. For a row at vertical coordinate ${y}$, the incremental rotation relative to the start of the frame is approximately ${\Delta \boldsymbol{\theta}_{\text{att}}}({y}) \approx {\boldsymbol{\omega}} \cdot {y} \cdot {t_{\text{row}}}$. This line-dependent rotation bends straight structural lines into curves, stretches or compresses vertical scale during pitching motions, and shears vertical surfaces into inclined planes, as photographed on the rotating propeller blades in [[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/03 Running the Machine/08 Sensor Perception/V4-08.03 Capture Physics and Calibration#^fig-rolling-shutter-skew|figure 2]].

Consider the warehouse mobile manipulator navigating past a vertical rack upright at its 1.5 m/s drive limit. A vertical post that spans the full frame height does not appear vertical in the raw pixel array, because the sensor reads the bottom scanline (${y} =$ 3,039) an entire readout cycle after the top scanline (${y} = 0$). This delay induces two distinct geometric errors that consume separate safety margins: along-track translation shears the upright along the direction of travel, while base yaw during a turn shifts the feature laterally. Notebook [8.1](https://mlsysbook.ai/vol4/08_perception/08_perception.html#nbk-perception-readout-shear) quantifies both effects.

**Napkin Math 8.1: The readout shear**

**Problem**: The base passes a rack upright at its drive limit while turning into the aisle. How far apart does the navigation camera record the upright’s top and bottom rows, and what share of the corresponding clearance allowance does each error consume?

**Variables**:

- Rows ${H} =$ 3,040 and readout ${t_{\text{readout}}} =$ 16.6 ms (Datasheet, full-resolution mode).
- Drive limit ${v_{\text{base}}} =$ 1.5 m/s (Illustrative).
- Yaw rate ${\omega_z} =$ 0.5 rad/s while turning into the aisle, at the rack-end range ${z} =$ 1.10 m (Illustrative).
- Localization allowance ${\delta_{\text{loc}}} =$ 50 mm, the along-travel term of the stopping budget (**[[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/01 Machine Anatomy/02 The Physical Body/V4-02.04 Kinetic Momentum#^sec-body-stopping-distance-prebrake|Kinematic stopping distance and pre-brake delay]]**), and side clearance 0.15 m, the gap the base keeps between its flank and the rack faces while it drives the aisle (both Illustrative; **[[05 Reference/Volume IV - Physical AI/V4 Systems and Hardware#^sec-appendix-systems-machine-data-sheet|The machine data sheet]]**).

**Math**:

*Row interval.*

$$
t_{\text{row}} = \frac{t_{\text{readout}}}{H} = \frac{16.6\ \text{ms}}{3040} = 5.46\ \mu\text{s}
$$

*Along-track skew.* At the last row, [[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/03 Running the Machine/08 Sensor Perception/V4-08.03 Capture Physics and Calibration#^eq-percep-rolling-shutter-skew|equation 1]] gives

$$
\Delta x = v\,t_{\text{readout}} = 1.5\ \text{m/s} \times 16.6\ \text{ms} = 24.9\ \text{mm}, \quad \frac{\Delta x}{\delta_{\text{loc}}} = \frac{24.9\ \text{mm}}{50\ \text{mm}} = 0.50
$$

*Lateral skew.* The bearing rotates by ${\omega_z}\,{t_{\text{readout}}}$ during readout, which moves the upright sideways at range ${z}$ by

$$
\Delta y = z\,\omega\,t_{\text{readout}} = 1.10\ \text{m} \times 0.5\ \text{rad/s} \times 16.6\ \text{ms} = 9.1\ \text{mm}, \quad \frac{9.1\ \text{mm}}{150\ \text{mm}} = 0.061
$$

*Sensitivity.* At the aisle’s chosen 1.3 m/s, the along-track skew is still 21.6 mm, 43.2 percent of ${\delta_{\text{loc}}}$.

**Result**: The bottom rows see the upright from a camera position 24.9 mm farther down the aisle than the top rows did, half of ${\delta_{\text{loc}}}$. Yaw adds 9.1 mm sideways, about 6.1 percent of the side clearance.

**Systems insight**: Uncorrected, one readout would spend half of the localization allowance that the stopping budget holds for the whole state estimate, and slowing to aisle speed barely helps. The budget’s ${\delta_{\text{loc}}}$ is therefore valid only if the skew is removed before the estimator sees the frame. The lateral term is small at the rack end, but it grows with range and turn rate, so it belongs in the calibration and timing checks rather than in a budget row.

The along-track error charges directly against the localization allowance ${\delta_{\text{loc}}}$ established in **[[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/01 Machine Anatomy/02 The Physical Body/V4-02.04 Kinetic Momentum#^sec-body-stopping-distance-prebrake|Kinematic stopping distance and pre-brake delay]]**. Treating the image as an instantaneous rigid perspective projection mistakenly models the bottom of the upright as closer along the corridor than the top, directly consuming the along-travel stopping allowance. Both error components scale linearly with total readout time. A global-shutter sensor, such as the wrist camera on the arm, eliminates scanline skew at the expense of larger pixel pitch and higher silicon cost. Selecting a shutter architecture is therefore a primary systems decision whenever a sensor’s measurements inform clearance boundaries. The running machine deliberately carries a rolling-shutter navigation camera on its base, so its perception pipeline must handle this constraint rather than avoid it.

Resolving this spatial artifact requires **rolling-shutter motion compensation**: unwarping image coordinates geometrically using an interpolated camera pose for every scanline. The onboard IMU supplies high-rate (1,000 Hz) angular velocity and specific-force[[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/03 Running the Machine/08 Sensor Perception/V4-08.14 Footnotes#^fn1|¹]] samples rather than direct velocity measurements. A fused state estimator integrates these inertial readings with bias estimates, gravity tracking, and wheel odometry to compute continuous body poses. Transforming these poses through calibrated body-to-camera extrinsics yields an estimated optical pose for each scanline, allowing the perception pipeline to rectify the image before running stereo matching or landmark triangulation. Plotted against row index, the uncompensated skew is a straight line, because each row is read a fixed interval after the one above it ([[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/03 Running the Machine/08 Sensor Perception/V4-08.03 Capture Physics and Calibration#^fig-08-rolling-shutter-rows|figure 1]]). The line ends halfway to the 50 mm allowance, which is why compensation, not a budget row, must absorb it.

### Camera Calibration

^camera-calibration

Unwarping a rolling-shutter image still leaves a pixel without a physical direction or position if the camera’s internal lens parameters or chassis mount have shifted. Under the **pinhole camera model** ([[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/03 Running the Machine/08 Sensor Perception/V4-08.13 Source References#^ref-hartley2003multiple|Hartley and Zisserman 2003]]), the intrinsic matrix ${\mathbf{K}}$ maps a point ${\mathbf{p}_c} = [{X_c}, {Y_c}, {Z_c}]^\top$ in the camera’s optical frame to the pixel $[{u}, {v}]^\top$:

$$
{\tilde{\mathbf{m}}} \sim \frac{1}{{Z_c}} {\mathbf{K}} {\mathbf{p}_c} = \frac{1}{{Z_c}} \begin{bmatrix} {f_x} & 0 & {c_x} \\ 0 & {f_y} & {c_y} \\ 0 & 0 & 1 \end{bmatrix} \begin{bmatrix} {X_c} \\ {Y_c} \\ {Z_c} \end{bmatrix} \tag{2}
$$

 where ${f_x}, {f_y}$ are focal lengths in pixels and $({c_x}, {c_y})$ is the principal point. Inverting the map recovers only a bearing ray, along which every depth is consistent with the pixel:

$$
{\mathbf{r}}({\tilde{\mathbf{m}}}) = {\mathbf{K}}^{-1} {\tilde{\mathbf{m}}} = \begin{bmatrix} \frac{{u} - {c_x}}{{f_x}} \\ \frac{{v} - {c_y}}{{f_y}} \\ 1 \end{bmatrix} \tag{3}
$$

^eq-percep-bearing-ray

^eq-percep-pinhole-projection

This bearing ray emanates from the camera’s optical center at the precise instant of photon capture ${{t_{\text{capture}}}}_0$. If downstream motion planning unprojects ${\mathbf{r}}({\tilde{\mathbf{m}}})$ using the robot’s *current* pose instead of its historical capture pose, the current evidence age at that downstream use, ${t_{\text{now,mono}}} - {{t_{\text{capture}}}}_0$, translates the ray origin by ${\Delta \mathbf{p}} = \int_{{{t_{\text{capture}}}}_0}^{{t_{\text{now,mono}}}} {\mathbf{v}}({t})\,d{t}$. These timing and angular errors scale along entirely different physical axes. At the 1.5 m/s drive limit, each millisecond of unmodeled latency shifts the ray origin by 1.5 mm, making the dispatch-age contribution of 51.6 ms displace the origin by 77.4 mm. Attitude errors, by contrast, scale with range. A single degree of orientation misalignment shifts a target at the 1.10 m rack-end distance by 19.2 mm laterally, expanding proportionally with distance. Unchecked, either discrepancy can push a physical rack upright entirely outside the bounding volume evaluated by the permission path. A single monocular camera cannot recover metric range along its bearing rays; scale must be supplied by stereo disparity, an active ranging sensor, or a learned geometric prior, and each introduces uncertainty that [[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/03 Running the Machine/08 Sensor Perception/V4-08.07 Spatial Error and Clearance Bounds#^sec-perception-error-clearance|section 8.6]] audits against the operating clearance budget. Optical lens distortion is rectified inside the hardware image signal processor (ISP) stage detailed in [[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/03 Running the Machine/08 Sensor Perception/V4-08.05 Bounding Observation Age#^tbl-08-latency-budget|table 3]]. **[[05 Reference/Volume IV - Physical AI/V4 Systems and Hardware#^sec-appendix-systems-vision|Camera Projective Geometry and Covariance Propagation]]** develops the full projection equations, radial distortion models, and Zhang’s planar calibration method ([[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/03 Running the Machine/08 Sensor Perception/V4-08.13 Source References#^ref-zhang2000flexible|Zhang 2000]]).

Spatial extrinsics and temporal synchronization between the camera and the IMU must be calibrated jointly ([[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/03 Running the Machine/08 Sensor Perception/V4-08.13 Source References#^ref-furgale2013unified|Furgale et al. 2013]]). An extrinsic orientation error ${\theta_{\text{ext}}}$ misplaces an observed feature at depth ${z}$ by approximately ${z}\,{\theta_{\text{ext}}}$, while spatial translation between the camera center and the IMU introduces the centripetal and tangential lever-arm accelerations given in **[[05 Reference/Volume IV - Physical AI/V4 Control and Dynamics#^sec-appendix-control-spatial|Spatial coordinate frames and lever-arm kinematics]]**. Similarly, an uncalibrated temporal offset ${\Delta t_{\text{imu}}}$ between image exposure and inertial sampling causes an effective orientation error of ${\omega_z}\,{\Delta t_{\text{imu}}}$ while turning at angular velocity ${\omega_z}$. This timing skew displaces the reconstructed feature by approximately ${z}\,{\omega_z}\,{\Delta t_{\text{imu}}}$, growing with both vehicle yaw rate and target distance. Hardware-latching exposure triggers and IMU clock signals to a shared real-time counter bounds this synchronization offset to sub-microsecond regimes, leaving small residual latencies for online filtering. Because mechanical shocks and thermal expansion degrade calibration over time, every observation packet must record its calibration identifier alongside its empirical operating envelope.

### Choosing Metric Depth for the Aisle

^choosing-metric-depth-for-the-aisle

Because a single bearing ray leaves range undetermined, robotic systems often pair passive cameras with active optical depth cameras to recover scale. In a warehouse aisle, however, ambient lighting, surface material properties, and multi-robot optical interference dictate which depth-sensing principle survives in practice. **Structured light** projects a known pseudo-random or coded infrared pattern onto the scene and triangulates surface deformation using an offset receiver camera. Although this active pattern injects distinct visual texture onto featureless cardboard cartons, direct sunlight from skylights or open loading docks easily washes out the projected infrared pattern, and overlapping projectors from adjacent mobile bases create optical interference that corrupts feature correspondence. Engineering qualification must therefore evaluate the operating environment under peak ambient lux and multi-robot contention rather than treating structured light as an unconditional depth source.

Rather than triangulating a spatial pattern, **indirect time-of-flight (iToF)** calculates distance by measuring the phase shift of continuous-wave amplitude-modulated infrared light. Phase differences yield dense range maps with compact hardware. Modulation periodicity, however, creates phase wrapping and distance ambiguity intervals. Multipath reflections in metallic warehouse corners and industrial shelving also send diffuse photons along several bounces, which bias the depth estimate. Motion between sequential phase exposures also introduces edge artifacts in dynamic scenes. **Direct time-of-flight (dToF)** avoids periodic phase wrapping by measuring the arrival times of discrete photon pulses using single-photon avalanche diodes (SPADs), yet it remains subject to timing jitter in the receiver discriminator, multipath contamination, and optical pile-up from high-reflectance foregrounds.

**Active stereo** combines structured infrared illumination with a calibrated stereo camera pair. Unlike structured light systems that must decode a proprietary pattern, active stereo uses projected speckle purely to synthesize artificial texture on smooth surfaces while computing standard epipolar stereo correspondence. If bright ambient light washes out the infrared pattern, the underlying stereo pipeline continues operating over ambient visible texture; if a smooth surface lacks ambient texture, however, high illumination alone still cannot resolve depth. The range-dependent uncertainty analysis in [[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/03 Running the Machine/08 Sensor Perception/V4-08.07 Spatial Error and Clearance Bounds#^sec-perception-error-clearance|section 8.6]] determines whether active stereo depth uncertainty satisfies the robot’s required stopping clearance.

For the rack end, the running machine carries none of these depth cameras. Each of these active principles has a failure mode the aisle supplies, whether daylight through open dock doors, a neighboring base’s projector, or multipath off metal shelving. The machine’s metric range comes instead from the OS1-64 LiDAR described later, and the navigation camera contributes bearing and class. Stereo returns in [[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/03 Running the Machine/08 Sensor Perception/V4-08.07 Spatial Error and Clearance Bounds#^sec-perception-error-clearance|section 8.6]] only as an illustrative rig that shows where a camera-only design meets a range wall.

### Event Cameras and Asynchronous Contrast

^event-cameras-and-asynchronous-contrast

Conventional CMOS sensors integrate photons over a fixed exposure window, forcing a trade-off between motion blur on fast targets and photon starvation in dim aisles. **Neuromorphic event cameras** (Dynamic Vision Sensors, or DVS)[[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/03 Running the Machine/08 Sensor Perception/V4-08.14 Footnotes#^fn3|³]] avoid frame-rate exposure trade-offs by abandoning discrete frames entirely ([[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/03 Running the Machine/08 Sensor Perception/V4-08.13 Source References#^ref-lichtsteiner2008128|Lichtsteiner et al. 2008]]; [[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/03 Running the Machine/08 Sensor Perception/V4-08.13 Source References#^ref-gallego2022event|Gallego et al. 2022]]). Each pixel on an event sensor array operates autonomously, containing a logarithmic photodiode, an analog amplifier, and two differential voltage comparators. The pixel continuously monitors incoming scene radiance ${I}({u}, {v}, {t})$. Whenever the temporal change in logarithmic intensity exceeds a configurable contrast threshold ${C_{\text{th}}}$, the pixel emits an asynchronous digital event packet ${e}_k$:

$$
|{\Delta} \ln {I}({u}, {v}, {t})| = |\ln {I}({u}, {v}, {t}) - \ln {I}({u}, {v}, {t_{\text{last}}})| \ge {C_{\text{th}}}
$$

$$
{e}_k = \langle {u}_k, {v}_k, {t}_k, {p}_k \rangle
$$

 where $({u}_k, {v}_k)$ is the pixel address, ${t}_k$ is the event timestamp latched with microsecond resolution ($1\text{--}10\,{\text{µ}}\text{s}$), and ${p}_k \in \{-1, +1\}$ is the polarity indicating whether radiance increased or decreased.

Event sensing preserves the temporal fidelity of high-speed edge transitions without waiting for a complete exposure cycle and serial scanline readout, providing high temporal resolution for rapid maneuvers. The systems trade-off lies in the nature of temporal contrast. Static obstacles produce zero events when the camera is stationary, so an unmoving obstacle is undetectable without ego-motion. Furthermore, high-frequency fluorescent lighting flicker and thermal noise generate spurious event bursts. The bandwidth demand across the physical sensor interface is therefore dynamic and scene-dependent rather than deterministic and fixed.

Downstream estimation algorithms must convert asynchronous coordinate streams in $({u}, {v}, {t})$ space into representations suitable for neural networks or state observers. Accumulating events into fixed time slices allows the use of standard convolutional backbones but reintroduces latency; processing individual event timestamps maintains sub-millisecond temporal responsiveness at the cost of specialized asynchronous graph or spiking architectures. Neither representation provides absolute metric depth without stereo baseline pairing or active sensor fusion. For the rack-end decision, an event camera would add edge timing but no range, and it would lose a worker who stops at the corner, so the running machine does not carry one.

### LiDAR and Radar Ranging

^lidar-and-radar-ranging

Passive cameras cannot measure metric distance along a single viewing ray, and short-range depth cameras fail when dock sunlight or metal shelving corrupts their patterns. Active ranging sensors, principally Light Detection and Ranging (**LiDAR**) and Radio Detection and Ranging (**radar**), overcome these limits by emitting structured electromagnetic energy and timing its return to measure metric range and radial velocity directly. Pulsed time-of-flight LiDAR calculates radial distance ${R}$ by measuring the round-trip flight duration ${\Delta t}$ of an emitted laser pulse traveling at the speed of light ${c}$:

$$
{R} = \frac{{c} \cdot {\Delta t}}{2}
$$

 In three-dimensional space, optical scanners steer each pulse along azimuth ${\theta_{\text{az}}}$ and elevation ${\phi}$, casting an emitted ray along unit direction vector ${\mathbf{d}}({\theta_{\text{az}}}, {\phi}) = [\cos{\phi}\cos{\theta_{\text{az}}}, \; \cos{\phi}\sin{\theta_{\text{az}}}, \; \sin{\phi}]^\top$. When reflected energy trips the receiver threshold after flight time ${\Delta t}$, the sensor unprojects the return into a 3D point in the sensor frame ${\mathcal{F}_{\text{lidar}}}$ via ray casting ([[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/03 Running the Machine/08 Sensor Perception/V4-08.03 Capture Physics and Calibration#^eq-percep-lidar-ray|equation 4]]):

$$
{\mathbf{p}_{\text{lidar}}} = {R} \cdot {\mathbf{d}}({\theta_{\text{az}}}, {\phi}) = \frac{{c} \cdot {\Delta t}}{2} \begin{bmatrix} \cos{\phi} \cos{\theta_{\text{az}}} \\ \cos{\phi} \sin{\theta_{\text{az}}} \\ \sin{\phi} \end{bmatrix} \tag{4}
$$

^eq-percep-lidar-ray

Spatial uncertainty in this unprojected 3D point decomposes into two distinct physical mechanisms:

1.  **Radial uncertainty (${\sigma_R}$)**: Governed by receiver discriminator timing jitter ${\sigma_{\Delta t}}$ and pulse rise time, ${\sigma_R} = \frac{{c}}{2} {\sigma_{\Delta t}}$ remains approximately constant across operating distances until return signal attenuation drops photon counts near the detection threshold. The manufacturer datasheet for the robot’s OS1-64 specifies a one-standard-deviation precision between 5 mm and 30 mm beyond 1 m. It also specifies a systematic accuracy bias of 25 mm on diffuse targets in the specified 1024×10 Hz mode ([[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/03 Running the Machine/08 Sensor Perception/V4-08.13 Source References#^ref-ouster2025os1|Ouster, Inc. 2025]]). These are datasheet scales, not qualified bounds for the running machine’s selected scan mode, surfaces, and motion. The accuracy figure describes the bias of the mean, a calibration offset that temporal averaging cannot remove.
2.  **Transverse footprint**: Governed by optical beam divergence ${\delta\theta_{\text{div}}}$; internal angular encoder quantization contributes a separate bearing error. The laser pulse expands into a narrow cone rather than propagating along an infinitesimal line. Taking ${\delta\theta_{\text{div}}}$ as the full-angle divergence, the transverse spot diameter scales as range times divergence. At ${R} =$ 30 m, the illustrative 2 mrad divergence gives a 60 mm spot diameter. This geometric footprint is not a standard deviation of position error. When it strikes a surface boundary, the detector receives mixed returns from foreground and background, blurring geometric edges; the resulting error distribution must be measured.

Most terrestrial systems operate either at near-infrared wavelengths below $1{,}000\,\text{nm}$, where silicon detectors respond, or at an eye-safe $1{,}550\,\text{nm}$ (permitting higher pulse energies under IEC 60825-1 Class 1 laser regulations).[[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/03 Running the Machine/08 Sensor Perception/V4-08.14 Footnotes#^fn4|⁴]] The machine’s OS1-64 is of the first kind; it emits at 865 nm as a Class 1 laser product ([[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/03 Running the Machine/08 Sensor Perception/V4-08.13 Source References#^ref-ouster2025os1|Ouster, Inc. 2025]]). Mechanical spinning LiDAR systems rotate an array of 16 to 128 laser diodes and avalanche photodiode (APD) detectors across an internal spindle at $10\text{--}20\,\text{Hz}$ to sweep a $360^\circ$ panoramic point cloud.

Much like rolling-shutter cameras expose scanlines in sequence, a spinning mechanical LiDAR does not record an instantaneous 3D snapshot. Completing a full $360^\circ$ azimuth sweep requires ${T_{\text{sweep}}} = 1/{f_{\text{scan}}}$, corresponding to 50 ms on the machine’s OS1-64 configured at 20 Hz. If the mobile base translates at velocity ${\mathbf{v}}$ and rotates at angular velocity ${\boldsymbol{\omega}}$ while the sensor spins, pulses fired at the start of the sweep (${\theta_{\text{az}}} = 0^\circ$) and near completion (${\theta_{\text{az}}} = 360^\circ$) originate from physically distinct vehicle poses. At the 1.5 m/s drive limit and an illustrative 0.5 rad/s turn rate, the robot travels 75 mm and rotates 25 mrad (1.4°) during a single scan. Without motion correction, this sampling lag produces an apparent geometric tear where the end of the revolution meets the beginning, while shearing vertical structures across the corridor.

Restoring rigid geometric fidelity requires **continuous-time LiDAR de-skewing**. The sensor hardware latches each point return ${\mathbf{p}}_i$ with an exact firing timestamp ${t}_i \in [{t_{\text{sweep}}}, {t_{\text{sweep}}} + {T_{\text{sweep}}}]$. A rigid pose transformation ${\mathbf{T}} \in \text{SE}(3)$ encapsulates orientation and translation. In the two-subscript convention, the first subscript names the destination frame and the second names the source: `T_ab` maps coordinates from frame b to frame a, and its inverse maps back. A homogeneous point appends a final coordinate of 1 so that the matrix applies translation as well as rotation. **[[05 Reference/Volume IV - Physical AI/V4 Control and Dynamics#^sec-appendix-control-spatial|Spatial coordinate frames and lever-arm kinematics]]** develops this representation and composition; [[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/03 Running the Machine/08 Sensor Perception/V4-08.04 Dating and Placing the Measurement#^sec-perception-coordinate-chain|section 8.3.1]] applies it to the machine’s mounting chain. Combining high-frequency (1,000 Hz) inertial data with wheel odometry allows the state estimator to compute the continuous trajectory of the vehicle body frame, ${\mathbf{T}_{wb}}({t}_i)$. Applying the calibrated mounting extrinsic ${\mathbf{T}_{bl}}$ transforms coordinates from the LiDAR frame to the body frame, yielding the world-frame sensor pose ${\mathbf{T}_{wl}}({t}_i) = {\mathbf{T}_{wb}}({t}_i){\mathbf{T}_{bl}}$. Each individual homogeneous point return is then projected back into the LiDAR’s coordinate frame at the frame reference epoch ${t_{\text{sweep}}}$:

$$
{\mathbf{p}}_i({t_{\text{sweep}}}) = {\mathbf{T}_{wl}}^{-1}({t_{\text{sweep}}}) \cdot {\mathbf{T}_{wl}}({t}_i) \cdot {\mathbf{p}}_i({t}_i)
$$

 De-skewing is what makes an accumulated LiDAR map usable. The returns registered in [[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/03 Running the Machine/08 Sensor Perception/V4-08.03 Capture Physics and Calibration#^fig-08-lidar-pointcloud|figure 3]], gathered over about 18 s of driving through a city intersection, keep crisp building facades, road striping, and parked vehicles only because each return was placed with the vehicle pose at its own firing epoch ${t}_i$. Independently moving obstacles in the environment require separate velocity estimation and clustering. Flash LiDAR architectures avoid sweep distortion altogether by illuminating the entire field of view with a single laser pulse to capture an instantaneous 3D range matrix. Distributing finite optical pulse energy across a wide solid angle, however, sharply limits maximum operational range compared to concentrated, steerable beams.

Millimeter-wave radar offers an orthogonal active sensing channel, operating in the $76\text{--}81\,\text{GHz}$ band (${\lambda} \approx 3.9\,\text{mm}$). Rather than emitting short, high-energy pulses, **Frequency-Modulated Continuous-Wave (FMCW) radar** transmits linear frequency chirps whose instantaneous carrier frequency sweeps across bandwidth $\text{BW}_{\text{chirp}}$ over chirp duration ${T_c}$ with slope ${S} = \text{BW}_{\text{chirp}} / {T_c}$. When the emitted waveform reflects from an obstacle at range ${R}$, the echo returns delayed by round-trip flight time ${t_{\text{rt}}} = 2{R}/{c}$. Mixing the received echo with a replica of the transmitted signal generates an intermediate frequency (IF) beat tone at frequency ${f_b}$:

$$
{f_b} = {S} \cdot {t_{\text{rt}}} = \frac{2 {R} \, \text{BW}_{\text{chirp}}}{{c} \, {T_c}}
$$

 Computing a fast Fourier transform across the digitized fast-time samples of each chirp (the **Range-FFT**) extracts metric range ${R}$ directly, without requiring optical contrast or ambient scene illumination. Physical range resolution depends strictly on total sweep bandwidth:

$$
{\Delta R} = \frac{{c}}{2\,\text{BW}_{\text{chirp}}}
$$

 For a standard 4 GHz radar sweep ($77\text{--}81\,\text{GHz}$), FMCW radar achieves a fine range resolution of ${\Delta R} \approx$ 3.75 cm. The transmitted chirps simultaneously encode radial velocity and bearing. A moving target shifts the phase of successive chirp reflections proportionally to its Doppler velocity; evaluating a second Fourier transform across chirps (the Doppler-FFT) extracts this radial velocity within a single coherent processing interval, avoiding the latency and noise of multi-frame position differencing. Array processing across multiple receive antennas resolves target azimuth and elevation, albeit at substantially coarser angular resolution than optical LiDAR. The running machine does not mount radar, but millimeter-wave sensing is a primary modality on outdoor autonomous platforms. Its millimeter wavelength penetrates the dust, fog, and steam that scatter optical wavelengths ([[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/03 Running the Machine/08 Sensor Perception/V4-08.08 Perception Failure Modes and Sensor Degradation#^sec-perception-failure-modes|section 8.7]]). However, radar returns remain sensitive to specular scattering, surface orientation, and multi-path ghost targets, providing complementary evidence rather than an infallible, all-weather guarantee.

### Tactile Contact Sensing

^sec-perception-tactile-grounding

At the end-effector interface, external cameras and range finders cannot measure contact pressure or verify friction adhesion during physical manipulation; indeed, the gripper structure often occludes the contact patch entirely. An optical tactile sensor[[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/03 Running the Machine/08 Sensor Perception/V4-08.14 Footnotes#^fn5|⁵]] addresses this perceptual blind spot by mounting a compliant, internally illuminated elastomer membrane on the fingertip and imaging its deformation with an internal camera. Photometric stereo algorithms translate surface shading into calibrated local contact topography, while tracking arrays of embedded fiducial markers yields direct measurements of tangential shear strain and incipient slip. Converting these raw optical deformations into a calibrated contact wrench requires a validated finite-element or empirical model that accounts for elastomer hysteresis, dynamic loading, and mechanical wear over time.

Tactile sensing thus governs the terminal contact phase of robotic grasping, whereas external cameras and range sensors guide the initial approach trajectory. Across both regimes, downstream estimators cannot treat sensor outputs as timeless, unframed tensors. Every tactile frame and spatial point cloud carries a finite physical age, a mounting transform, and a calibration uncertainty that must be attached to the measurement before any consumer can use it.
