# V4-08.05 Bounding Observation Age

[[Home|Home]] · [[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/Volume 4 Overview|Volume Overview]] · [[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/03 Running the Machine/08 Sensor Perception/V4-08 Sensor Perception|V4-08 Sensor Perception]]

^sec-perception-age-budget

Even when a rack-end observation is dated at capture and placed in a calibrated coordinate frame, the scene it describes belongs to the past. Delivering that measurement across an embodied runtime adds acquisition, bus transport, queueing, and neural inference delay while the robot continues to move. Any delivery deadline for perception must therefore track the full physical interval from energy integration at the detector through dispatch, bounding the observation’s age before downstream actuators act.

**Definition 8.1: Observation age**

***Observation age*** (${t_{\text{age}}}$), also called spatial information age, is the physical time elapsed between the exposure midpoint at the transducer and the moment perception dispatches the resulting observation. It is the capture-to-dispatch part of the measurement age that **[[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/01 Machine Anatomy/02 The Physical Body/V4-02.03 Measurement Freshness#^sec-body-measurement-freshness|Measurement Freshness]]** counts from transduction. The observation keeps aging after dispatch, and the age a consumer computes on its own clock when it admits or reuses the observation is its **current evidence age**, which equals ${t_{\text{age}}}$ plus the time elapsed since dispatch.

1.  **Significance**: Because the robot continues moving while the compute pipeline runs, observation age converts directly into physical displacement ([[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/03 Running the Machine/08 Sensor Perception/V4-08.05 Bounding Observation Age#^eq-percep-spatial-displacement|equation 5]]) and expands the stopping budget through the pre-brake delay ${\tau_{\text{delay}}}$. On the warehouse mobile manipulator, the navigation-camera observation is already 51.6 ms old at dispatch, which is 77.4 mm of blind travel at the 1.5 m/s drive limit before any downstream consumer has inspected the frame.
2.  **Distinction**: Conventional benchmark latency measures only the software execution time of a forward pass. In contrast, observation age spans the entire physical journey from photon arrival to dispatch: the exposure midpoint, sensor rolling readout, serialized bus transport, direct memory access (DMA) transfers, driver queueing, image signal processor (ISP) conversions, model inference, and inter-process dispatch. Scheduling delay after dispatch belongs to the current evidence age, not to ${t_{\text{age}}}$.
3.  **Common pitfall**: Systems engineers frequently confuse a frame’s arrival timestamp in userspace memory (${t_{\text{recv}}}$) with its actual physical observation time (${{t_{\text{capture}}}}_0$). On the navigation camera, the arrival timestamp overlooks 26.6 ms of front-end exposure, readout, and transport. At the maximum drive limit, this unmeasured interval conceals 39.9 mm of physical travel, saddling downstream controllers with an unmodeled phase lag.

When evaluated along the machine’s trajectory, observation age integrates directly into spatial displacement ([[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/03 Running the Machine/08 Sensor Perception/V4-08.05 Bounding Observation Age#^eq-percep-spatial-displacement|equation 5]]):

$$
{\Delta \mathbf{p}} = \int_{{{t_{\text{capture}}}}_0}^{{{t_{\text{capture}}}}_0+{t_{\text{age}}}} {\mathbf{v}}({t})\,d{t} \tag{5}
$$

 Travel is one of three error sources that perception must bound. Adding this motion-induced displacement to first-order stereo triangulation noise and small-angle extrinsic error gives a single error scale for the pipeline ([[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/03 Running the Machine/08 Sensor Perception/V4-08.05 Bounding Observation Age#^eq-percep-unified-uncertainty-envelope|equation 6]]):

$$
{\epsilon_{\text{scale}}} = \underbrace{\int_{{{t_{\text{capture}}}}_0}^{{{t_{\text{capture}}}}_0+{t_{\text{age}}}} \|{\mathbf{v}}({t})\|\,d{t}}_{\text{path-length travel}} + \underbrace{\frac{{z}^2}{{f} {b}}{\sigma_d}}_{\text{stereo depth standard deviation}} + \underbrace{{z} |{\theta_{\text{ext}}}|}_{\text{small-angle mounting error scale}} \tag{6}
$$

 The first term integrates instantaneous speed to compute path length, providing an upper bound on net translation during the perception delay. The latency waterfall below sizes it for the navigation camera. The second term characterizes depth uncertainty for a calibrated stereo camera with baseline ${b}$, focal length ${f}$, operating range ${z}$, and disparity standard deviation ${\sigma_d}$. [[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/03 Running the Machine/08 Sensor Perception/V4-08.07 Spatial Error and Clearance Bounds#^sec-perception-error-clearance|Section 8.6]] derives it and evaluates it on an illustrative rig. It is a standard-deviation scale rather than an absolute bound. The third term models the lever-arm displacement produced by a small extrinsic angular misalignment ${\theta_{\text{ext}}}$ over target distance ${z}$, the error [[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/03 Running the Machine/08 Sensor Perception/V4-08.04 Dating and Placing the Measurement#^sec-perception-handoff|section 8.3]] already sized at the rack end. The sum is therefore an order-of-magnitude scale, not a safety envelope. It adds magnitudes that point in different directions and leaves out calibration bias and the heavy tail of real residuals, which is why the clearance a consumer holds rests instead on a measured residual quantile ([[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/03 Running the Machine/08 Sensor Perception/V4-08.07 Spatial Error and Clearance Bounds#^eq-percep-clearance-inset|equation 9]]).

### The Latency Waterfall

^sec-perception-latency-waterfall

While the navigation camera integrates photons, clocks out pixel rows, transfers buffers across the bus, and runs neural inference kernels, the mobile base keeps moving at velocity ${v}$, turning every millisecond of pipeline delay into blind travel:

$$
{\Delta x_{\text{blind}}} = {v} \cdot {t_{\text{age}}}
$$

 On the navigation camera, the observation age ${t_{\text{age}}}$ accumulates stage by stage from the exposure midpoint to the moment perception publishes its processed measurement. When hardware stages execute sequentially, the total age expands as a **latency waterfall**; if adjacent stages overlap in hardware or stream through pipelined ring buffers, their combined duration replaces the naive sum of individual stage times:

$$
{t_{\text{age}}} = \frac{{t_{\text{exp}}}}{2} + {t_{\text{readout}}} + {t_{\text{transport}}} + {t_{\text{DMA}}} + {t_{\text{ISP}}} + {t_{\text{backbone}}} + {t_{\text{IPC}}} \tag{7}
$$

^eq-percep-latency-waterfall

A complete stopping margin must also count the downstream response: expiry of the chunk lease sized in **[[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/01 Machine Anatomy/04 The Nervous System/V4-04.05 Chunk Lease Sizing from the Stopping Budget#^sec-nervous-lease-sizing|Chunk Lease Sizing from the Stopping Budget]]**, the control tick, fieldbus transmission, and brake onset. Summing these terms yields the partial pre-brake delay ${\tau_{\text{delay}}} = {t_{\text{age}}} + {T_{\text{lease}}} + {T_{\text{tick}}} + {T_{\text{bus}}} + {T_{\text{act}}}$, which governs the corresponding subtotal of blind travel:

$$
{d_{\text{blind}}} = {v} \cdot {\tau_{\text{delay}}}
$$

 At constant operating velocity, every millisecond of unmodeled latency drains physical clearance linearly. Because the permission path runs concurrently on its own MCU, the safety calculation must reflect that dedicated architecture rather than assuming that every stalled neural forward pass postpones emergency braking. Notebook [8.2](https://mlsysbook.ai/vol4/08_perception/08_perception.html#nbk-perception-sensor-transduction-latency-spatial-information-age) steps through each hardware and software stage of this waterfall for the warehouse mobile manipulator, converting elapsed time into traveled distance.

**Napkin Math 8.2: Travel before the frame is read**

**Problem**: The mobile manipulator drives toward a rack end at its drive limit when a person steps out. How old is the navigation camera’s observation of that person when perception dispatches it, and how far has the base traveled by then?

**Variables**:

- Exposure ${t_{\text{exp}}} =$ 16 ms (Chosen low-light setting), so the midpoint lag is ${t_{\text{exp}}}/2 =$ 8 ms; rolling readout ${t_{\text{readout}}} =$ 16.6 ms (Datasheet).
- Residual MIPI CSI-2 transport ${t_{\text{transport}}} =$ 0.8 ms (Illustrative). The [Sony IMX477 flyer](https://www.sony-semicon.com/files/62/pdf/p-13_IMX477-AACK_Flyer.pdf) specifies a four-lane option up to $2.1\text{ Gbps/lane}$; the budget assigns only the transport left after streaming readout, not frame size divided by peak lane rate.
- DMA and driver queue ${t_{\text{DMA}}} =$ 1.2 ms, ISP debayering and rectification ${t_{\text{ISP}}} =$ 2.5 ms, INT8 vision backbone ${t_{\text{backbone}}} =$ 22 ms, and covariance packaging with zero-copy dispatch ${t_{\text{IPC}}} =$ 0.5 ms (all Illustrative).
- Drive limit ${v} =$ 1.5 m/s (Illustrative).

**Math**:

*Age at dispatch.* The serial stages of [[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/03 Running the Machine/08 Sensor Perception/V4-08.05 Bounding Observation Age#^eq-percep-latency-waterfall|equation 7]] sum to

$$
t_{\text{age}} = 8 ms + 16.6 ms + 0.8 ms + 1.2 ms + 2.5 ms + 22 ms + 0.5 ms = 51.6 ms
$$

*Travel.* Holding speed through the age window:

$$
\Delta x = (1.5 m/s) \times (0.0516 s) = 0.0774 m = 77.4 mm
$$

*Sensitivity.* Each further millisecond in any stage adds 1.5 mm of travel at the drive limit.

**Result**: The observation is 51.6 ms old at dispatch, and the base has covered 77.4 mm. The backbone is the largest single stage, but exposure and readout alone contribute 24.6 ms before any computation starts.

**Systems insight**: The base covers this distance before the perception record exists. Timestamped pose propagation can place the old observation correctly in the current frame, but no later stage returns the distance already traveled, so ${v}\,{t_{\text{age}}}$ enters the stopping budget as a term of its own.

The seven stages of [[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/03 Running the Machine/08 Sensor Perception/V4-08.05 Bounding Observation Age#^tbl-08-latency-budget|table 3]] sum to the observation age at dispatch, ${t_{\text{age}}} =$ 51.6 ms. Each stage value is a budgeted allocation, the most that stage may take under load, rather than a typical measurement. The sum is therefore the age the rest of the machine plans against, and the last column of the table names the measurement that must confirm it. The stopping budget then adds the active lease and response path of **[[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/01 Machine Anatomy/04 The Nervous System/V4-04.05 Chunk Lease Sizing from the Stopping Budget#^sec-nervous-lease-sizing|Chunk Lease Sizing from the Stopping Budget]]**:

$$
\tau_{\text{delay}} = \underbrace{t_{\text{age}}}_{51.6 ms} + \underbrace{T_{\text{lease}} + T_{\text{tick}} + T_{\text{bus}} + T_{\text{act}}}_{82 ms} = 133.6 ms
$$

 This is the pre-brake delay that **[[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/00 Introduction/01 The Causal Boundary/V4-01.05 Defining the Causal Boundary#^sec-boundary-kinematic-roofline|Delay consumes stopping clearance]]** and the event timing table of **[[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/01 Machine Anatomy/04 The Nervous System/V4-04 The Nervous System#^sec-nervous|The Nervous System]]** carried with ${t_{\text{age}}}$ entered as a given; its first term is now derived rather than assumed.

This subtotal is a **partial delay budget** rather than an end-to-end capture-to-response bound. In the timing breakdown of **[[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/01 Machine Anatomy/04 The Nervous System/V4-04.05 Chunk Lease Sizing from the Stopping Budget#^tbl-04-event-timing|The capture-to-brake-onset timing budget of the running machine]]**, perception governs the first interval from capture to dispatch, while actuator mechanics govern the third interval of physical braking. The observation continues to age across the intermediate second interval while the proposer completes inference, transmits the candidate trajectory, and awaits admission from the permission path. Let ${\Delta_{\text{da}}}$ denote this dispatch-to-admission interval, encompassing model planning and inter-process communication overheads. Because an active lease takes effect only upon candidate admission, the total delay under a stalled-proposer fault equals the displayed subtotal plus ${\Delta_{\text{da}}}$. The transport-budget calculation in **[[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/03 Running the Machine/13 Workload Placement/V4-13.07 Silicon Isolation Decisions#^sec-placement-dispatch-admission|The board-link budget and the dispatch-to-admission interval]]** allocates a budget for ${\Delta_{\text{da}}}$ that awaits empirical validation on hardware. That allocation includes a full inference after dispatch, and part of that inference overlaps the vision backbone already counted in ${t_{\text{age}}}$. Adding the two therefore counts the overlap twice and overstates the delay, which errs on the safe side; timestamping both intervals on the loaded machine removes the overlap.

Laid on one clock, the partial budget shows where the unbounded interval sits ([[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/03 Running the Machine/08 Sensor Perception/V4-08.05 Bounding Observation Age#^fig-08-capture-to-brake-strip|figure 5]]). Everything before dispatch at 51.6 ms belongs to perception, while everything after it belongs to the response path that reaches brake onset at 133.6 ms. The dispatch-to-admission interval ${\Delta_{\text{da}}}$ enters at the dispatch line, so whatever value it takes on the loaded machine shifts every later event to the right.

Because the permission path validates geometric safety without reprocessing raw images, it cannot retroactively compress this perception pipeline delay; the age reaches it strictly through the proposal’s stamped evidence epoch. At the 1.5 m/s drive limit, the observation-age term ${v}\,{t_{\text{age}}}$ contributes 77.4 mm of blind displacement. That travel is the fifth row of the stopping budget. The first three rows are the Body’s terms from **[[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/01 Machine Anatomy/02 The Physical Body/V4-02.04 Kinetic Momentum#^eq-body-stopping-distance|equation 2.2]]**, braking distance ${v}^2/2{a_{\text{brake}}}$, the overhead ${\delta_{\text{loc}}} + {\delta_{\text{margin}}}$, and brake-onset travel ${v}\,{T_{\text{act}}}$, written together below as ${d_{\text{base}}}$. The fourth row is the lease-path travel ${d_{\text{lease}}} = {v}\,({T_{\text{lease}}} + {T_{\text{tick}}} + {T_{\text{bus}}})$ of **[[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/01 Machine Anatomy/04 The Nervous System/V4-04.05 Chunk Lease Sizing from the Stopping Budget#^sec-nervous-lease-sizing|Chunk Lease Sizing from the Stopping Budget]]**. Adding the fifth row gives

$$
d_{\text{stop}} = \underbrace{d_{\text{base}} + d_{\text{lease}}}_{835.5 mm} + \underbrace{v \cdot t_{\text{age}}}_{77.4 mm} = 912.9 mm
$$

 This additional term raises the partial stopping distance from 835.5 mm to 912.9 mm. Although 187.1 mm remains within the nominal 1.10 m rack-end clearance, that remainder cannot be claimed as safety buffer. It must still absorb the travel during the dispatch-to-admission interval ${\Delta_{\text{da}}}$, the extra distance of a jerk-limited rather than instantaneous brake application (**[[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/03 Running the Machine/11 Trajectory Planning/V4-11.06 Behavior at the Seam#^sec-planning-base-stopping-budget|Base stopping budget under jerk-limited deceleration]]**), and the tracking inset of **[[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/03 Running the Machine/12 Safety Enforcement/V4-12.04 Stopping Envelopes#^sec-enforcement-stopping-envelopes|Stopping Envelopes]]**. This analysis sizes the perception subsystem’s contribution to the stopping budget and clarifies the empirical measurements required before an integrated motion claim can be certified; the final column of [[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/03 Running the Machine/08 Sensor Perception/V4-08.05 Bounding Observation Age#^tbl-08-latency-budget|table 3]] names that measurement stage by stage.

| Rolling readout    |   16.6 ms |                24.6 ms | Selected row and overlap with streaming |
| Residual transport |    0.8 ms |                25.4 ms | Selected sensor mode and host receiver  |
| DMA and queue      |    1.2 ms |                26.6 ms | Arbitration and queue tail              |
| ISP                |    2.5 ms |                29.1 ms | Mode and competing jobs                 |

Later consumers must add their elapsed post-dispatch interval.

**Checkpoint 8.1: Observation age and the stopping budget**

Observation age is paid in travel before any consumer can react, and no later stage returns it.

**Observation age**: Can you explain how the interval from the exposure midpoint to dispatch converts into travel that erodes the stopping clearance before a policy can react?

**Exposure midpoint vs. arrival timestamp**: Can you explain why dating a frame at DMA completion rather than at its exposure midpoint pairs the observation with the wrong pose?

**Pose propagation from capture time**: Can you explain how propagating the machine’s pose forward from an observation’s capture time places an old observation correctly in the current frame, and why that propagation cannot return the distance already traveled?

**Observation age in the stopping budget**: Can you explain why the observation-age term ${v}\,{t_{\text{age}}}$ adds to the stopping distance, and why the permission path, which never reads the image, cannot shorten it?

**Dropped-frame estimate**: If a dropped frame leaves a proposal resting on the previous frame, one frame period (16.7 ms) older, how much travel does that add at the 1.5 m/s drive limit, and what share of the 187.1 mm remainder does it consume?

### Dating the Machine’s LiDAR

^sec-perception-lidar-age

The permission path’s clearance check inspects range returns from the mobile base’s Ouster OS1-64 LiDAR, whose timing structure differs from that of the optical camera. A rolling-shutter camera exposes and reads out a discrete frame grid. In contrast, a spinning mechanical LiDAR continuously fires laser diodes and timestamps each azimuth column at the firing instant, disciplined by the IEEE 1588 Precision Time Protocol (PTP). Because every point carries an individual capture epoch, the scan lacks a singular frame time. The age of the resulting rack-end spatial estimate is therefore governed by how long the software pipeline buffers returns before releasing the angular sector facing the hazard.

At the configured scan rate, one mechanical revolution requires 50 ms. At a clear distance of 1.10 m, the 1.30 m aisle subtends a forward azimuth sector of 61.2°, which the sensor sweeps in 8.5 ms. [[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/03 Running the Machine/08 Sensor Perception/V4-08.05 Bounding Observation Age#^tbl-08-lidar-age|Table 4]] compares two ingestion architectures for handling this point stream: streaming sector packets to consumers immediately upon arrival vs. buffering the entire sweep before dispatching a complete 360° point cloud.

| Stage                                    | Stream the forward sector | Wait for the full sweep | Source                             |
|:-----------------------------------------|--------------------------:|------------------------:|:-----------------------------------|
| Acquisition of the oldest relevant point |                    8.5 ms |                   50 ms | Scan rate and aisle geometry       |
| Sensor data latency, upper bound         |                     10 ms |                   10 ms | Datasheet                          |
| Clock-conversion bound (PTP)             |                      1 ms |                    1 ms | Datasheet; measure on the machine  |
| Upper age before processing              |               **19.5 ms** |               **61 ms** | Computed                           |
| Travel at the drive limit                |                   29.2 mm |                 91.5 mm | Computed                           |
| De-skew and occupancy extraction         |      Needs measured value |    Needs measured value | Host measurement under camera load |

Table 4: **LiDAR age at the rack end**: The OS1-64’s upper age before host processing under two consumption policies, at the configured scan rate. Data latency and clock accuracy are the datasheet’s upper bounds; processing time must be measured on the machine under the synchronized camera and inference load.

^tbl-08-lidar-age

Waiting for a full revolution consumes 61 ms before host computation begins, exceeding the camera’s 51.6 ms acquisition/processing allocation. In contrast, streaming the forward azimuth sector immediately leaves 32.1 ms of processing headroom within that allocation for motion de-skew and occupancy extraction. This arithmetic compares pipeline allocations; different scan and frame phases, buffered reuse, and downstream waiting still determine which selected observation is older at consumption. The real-time latency budget therefore dictates the architecture. The robot streams and processes sector packets incrementally, propagating individual point timestamps rather than a single aggregated sweep time.

Two strands of evidence therefore meet at the rack end, and the oldest required capture sets the shared age charge. The proposal is anchored to the camera’s frame epoch, while the admission check rests on LiDAR range. The permission path must date both on its own clock at the same admission or tick, including bounded clock uncertainty. Streaming fits the illustrated camera-sized acquisition/processing allocation only when de-skew, occupancy extraction, and any phase, reuse, or waiting allowance required by the selected consumption policy fit the 32.1 ms headroom. Sharing the camera’s row-five charge further requires that the actual LiDAR capture be no older than the camera capture supporting the proposal at that common check. Otherwise the budget must charge the older required evidence, with each elapsed interval counted once. The dispatch-to-admission interval remains a separate downstream charge. Neither strand includes the internal latency of the certified safety scanner, because its worst-case response time is a factory-certified parameter accounted for separately in the scanner’s own timing budget described in **[[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/03 Running the Machine/12 Safety Enforcement/V4-12.04 Stopping Envelopes#^sec-enforcement-composite-budget|Sizing the composite stopping budget]]**.

Per-column timestamps also enable continuous motion de-skew. Over a single LiDAR revolution at full speed, the mobile base translates 75 mm, and the state estimator eliminates this distortion by interpolating robot odometry at each return’s precise firing timestamp ([[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/03 Running the Machine/08 Sensor Perception/V4-08.03 Capture Physics and Calibration#^sec-perception-transduction-calibration|section 8.2]]). While the optical camera avoids rotational sweeping delays, its serial latency budget has no spare stage to absorb arbitrary queueing, because row five charges exactly the budgeted age. In particular, collecting consecutive frames into multi-frame batches introduces substantial latency penalties before host memory contention is even considered.

### Temporal Batching

^temporal-batching

At the navigation camera’s 60 Hz sampling rate (${T_{\text{sample}}} \approx$ 16.7 ms), forming a batch of four consecutive frames to amortize kernel launch overhead or raise systolic-array utilization requires holding the first frame for 50 ms, the second for 33.3 ms, and the third for 16.7 ms, while only the fourth enters immediately. Accumulating frames across successive capture times ages the earliest samples: appending that 50 ms queueing delay to the baseline 51.6 ms observation age roughly doubles total age to 101.6 ms. Because row five of the stopping budget charges only the single-frame age, the observation contract at the end of this chapter sets its dispatch threshold at that age plus a sub-millisecond clock bound ([[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/03 Running the Machine/08 Sensor Perception/V4-08.09 The Observation Contract#^sec-perception-observation-contract|section 8.8]]). The batched frame is therefore refused before candidate admission begins. When multi-camera systems synchronize exposure pulses in hardware, grouping frames from distinct sensors across spatial dimensions can improve throughput safely; however, temporal batching across successive timestamps directly erodes closed-loop timeliness.

### Observation Age and Feedback Stability

^observation-age-and-feedback-stability

Beyond draining physical stopping distance, observation age also spends the phase margin of any feedback loop closed through perception, the extra lag that loop can absorb before its corrections reinforce the motion they were meant to damp. **[[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/01 Machine Anatomy/04 The Nervous System/V4-04.04 Multi-Rate Cadences#^sec-nervous-time-constants-phase-lag|Physical time constants and sampling phase lag]]** establishes that a pure delay removes phase in proportion to frequency without changing loop gain. A loop with nominal phase margin $\text{PM}_{\text{nom}}$ at gain crossover frequency ${\omega_c}$ therefore has no margin left once ${t_{\text{age}}} \ge \text{PM}_{\text{nom}}/{\omega_c}$. Perception draws on that same account. A visual-servoing tracking loop crosses over at 10 rad/s (approximately 1.6 Hz) with a nominal phase margin of 45$^\circ$ (0.785 rad). With no other delay charged, its nominal zero-margin ceiling for this perception contribution is ${t_{\text{age},\max}} = \text{PM}_{\text{nom}}/{\omega_c} =$ 78.5 ms. If sensor exposure, transport, and neural inference consume 60 ms, the perception pipeline spends 76.4 percent of the loop’s stability allowance before motor commands are even computed. Whether the integrated loop remains adequately damped requires qualification of its complete dynamics and delays.

*Line labeled PM 45 degrees falling linearly with age, with a dot marked 60 milliseconds, crossing a dashed zero line at a red dot marked 78.5 milliseconds; beyond it the line turns red in a light red region labeled unstable, where the nominal phase reserve is exhausted.*

*Delay spends phase margin linearly; at 78.5 ms none remains.*

The perception contribution to both checks spends the same elapsed duration in different currencies. Phase lag erodes closed-loop stability, while displacement consumes forward clearance (${\Delta x_{\text{blind}}} = {v} \cdot {t_{\text{age}}}$), so each consequence is checked on its own terms and the latency is never counted twice in the stopping budget. Rigorous capture timestamps, qualified reference frames, and explicit error bounds make these physical consequences observable throughout the software stack. Even when zero-copy DMA architectures eliminate intermediate buffer copies during host ingestion, memory controller arbitration and bus contention continue to introduce subtle waiting times.
