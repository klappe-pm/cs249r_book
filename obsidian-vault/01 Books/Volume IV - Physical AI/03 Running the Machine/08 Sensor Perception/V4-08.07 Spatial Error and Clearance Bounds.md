# V4-08.07 Spatial Error and Clearance Bounds

[[Home|Home]] · [[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/Volume 4 Overview|Volume Overview]] · [[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/03 Running the Machine/08 Sensor Perception/V4-08 Sensor Perception|V4-08 Sensor Perception]]

^sec-perception-error-clearance

If perception places a warehouse rack upright just a few centimeters farther from the base’s trajectory than it actually stands, the motion planner consumes those missing centimeters directly from its 0.15 m side clearance, although no stage of the pipeline decided to spend them. Once downstream control logic uses an estimated coordinate to authorize motion, every spatial error becomes a physical displacement drawn from the clearance that protects the robot from collision. Transforming a raw sensor observation into a trustworthy clearance claim requires **metric grounding**, which maps detector measurements into Euclidean space. This grounding relies on unprojecting two-dimensional sensor measurements into three dimensions, an inverse problem that assumes calibrated optics, rigid structural mountings, and predictable surface properties.

Classical projective geometry and triangulation ([[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/03 Running the Machine/08 Sensor Perception/V4-08.13 Source References#^ref-hartley2003multiple|Hartley and Zisserman 2003]]; [[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/03 Running the Machine/08 Sensor Perception/V4-08.13 Source References#^ref-szeliski2022computer|Szeliski 2022]]) govern how measurement noise converts into three-dimensional spatial displacement. In two-view streaming perception, two calibrated cameras with optical centers ${\mathbf{o}}_1, {\mathbf{o}}_2$ observe a physical scene point ${\mathbf{p}} \in \mathbb{R}^3$. The baseline vector ${\mathbf{t}} \in \mathbb{R}^3$ connecting ${\mathbf{o}}_1$ and ${\mathbf{o}}_2$, together with the target point ${\mathbf{p}}$, defines the **epipolar plane**. The intersection of this baseline with each image plane defines the two **epipoles** ${\mathbf{e}}_1, {\mathbf{e}}_2$.

For corresponding image coordinates ${\tilde{\mathbf{m}}}_1, {\tilde{\mathbf{m}}}_2$ expressed in homogeneous pixel coordinates, the rigid spatial transformation $({\mathbf{R}}, {\mathbf{t}}) \in \text{SE}(3)$ relating the camera frames establishes the fundamental **epipolar constraint**:

$$
{\tilde{\mathbf{m}}}_2^\top {\mathbf{F}} \, {\tilde{\mathbf{m}}}_1 = 0
$$

 where ${\mathbf{F}} = {\mathbf{K}^{-\top}}_2 {\mathbf{E}} \, {\mathbf{K}}_1^{-1} \in \mathbb{R}^{3\times3}$ is the **Fundamental matrix**, and ${\mathbf{E}} = [{\mathbf{t}}]_\times {\mathbf{R}} \in \mathbb{R}^{3\times3}$ is the **Essential matrix** (with $[{\mathbf{t}}]_\times$ denoting the skew-symmetric matrix of baseline translation). Geometrically, because the two unprojected bearing rays ${\mathbf{K}}_1^{-1}{\tilde{\mathbf{m}}}_1$ and ${\mathbf{K}}_2^{-1}{\tilde{\mathbf{m}}}_2$ meet at the same physical point, both rays and the baseline vector ${\mathbf{t}}$ lie in the epipolar plane, so expressing the first ray in the second camera’s frame via ${\mathbf{R}}$ and taking its cross product with ${\mathbf{t}}$ (written as multiplication by $[{\mathbf{t}}]_\times$) produces the plane normal that must be orthogonal to the second ray. The vector ${\mathbf{l}}_2 = {\mathbf{F}} {\tilde{\mathbf{m}}}_1$ defines the **epipolar line** in the second image; any valid physical correspondence ${\tilde{\mathbf{m}}}_2$ must lie strictly along ${\mathbf{l}}_2$.

In a calibrated **rectified stereo rig**, both images are digitally warped onto a shared virtual image plane parallel to the baseline (${\mathbf{R}} = {\mathbf{I}}$, ${\mathbf{t}} = [{b}, 0, 0]^\top$). Rectification forces epipolar lines to be exactly parallel and collinear with horizontal pixel scanlines (${v}_1 = {v}_2$). This geometric transformation collapses an expensive two-dimensional correspondence search, the kind dense optical-flow networks perform, into a one-dimensional search along the same horizontal scanline. Neural stereo networks such as RAFT-Stereo evaluate this correspondence as a 1D correlation cost volume ([[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/03 Running the Machine/08 Sensor Perception/V4-08.13 Source References#^ref-lipson2021raftstereo|Lipson et al. 2021]]). Disparity is then the horizontal coordinate difference ${d} = {u}_1 - {u}_2$, recovering metric depth through ${Z_c} = \frac{{f} {b}}{{d}}$.

When mechanical vibration, chassis twist, or thermal expansion induces uncalibrated relative pitch or roll between cameras, or when rolling-shutter readout shears scanlines during sharp platform turns, the true physical correspondence shifts vertically off the horizontal epipolar line (${\Delta} {v} \neq 0$). The 1D correlation cost volume fails or latches onto spurious texture matches across adjacent rows, injecting severe triangulation outliers into the reconstruction. Differentiating the unprojection ${Z_c} = \frac{{f} {b}}{{d}}$ with respect to disparity yields a negative signed derivative whose magnitude defines the small-error relation ([[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/03 Running the Machine/08 Sensor Perception/V4-08.07 Spatial Error and Clearance Bounds#^eq-percep-stereo-uncertainty|equation 8]]):

$$
|{\delta} {Z_c}| \approx \frac{{Z_c}^2}{{f} {b}} |{\delta} {d}| \tag{8}
$$

 where ${\delta} {d}$ represents disparity error. If ${\delta} {d}$ denotes a disparity standard deviation ${\sigma_d}$, first-order error propagation gives ${\sigma_z} \approx \frac{{Z_c}^2}{{f} {b}}{\sigma_d}$. Doubling range quadruples this modeled standard deviation at fixed focal length ${f}$, baseline ${b}$, and disparity precision ${\sigma_d}$. For distant landmarks, an estimator instead parameterizes depth as inverse depth[[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/03 Running the Machine/08 Sensor Perception/V4-08.14 Footnotes#^fn11|¹¹]] ${\rho} = 1/{Z_c} = {d} / ({f} {b})$. Differentiating inverse depth with respect to disparity yields:

$$
\frac{\partial {\rho}}{\partial {d}} = \frac{1}{{f} {b}} = \text{constant}
$$

 Because this derivative is independent of distance, measurement errors in disparity map linearly into inverse depth (${\delta} {\rho} = \frac{1}{{f} {b}} {\delta} {d}$). This linear mapping provides **inverse-depth metric grounding**, preserving numerical conditioning for distant landmarks when the disparity-error model is appropriate. Inverse depth does not eliminate mismatch outliers or guarantee estimator stability. In contrast, a pulsed time-of-flight sensor estimates range directly from transit time, ${R} = \frac{{c} {\Delta t}}{2}$; its range error depends on its timing and detection electronics, remaining approximately flat across its rated operating region.

^eq-percep-stereo-uncertainty

On the running machine, the rack-end range comes from the OS1-64 LiDAR, whose datasheet quotes one-standard-deviation precision of 5 mm to 30 mm beyond 1 m in the 1024×10 Hz mode. Applying that scale to the selected scan mode requires bias and residual-tail qualification under the intended surfaces and motion. A design that tried to range with cameras alone would have to triangulate, because the navigation camera by itself recovers only the bearing ray of [[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/03 Running the Machine/08 Sensor Perception/V4-08.03 Capture Physics and Calibration#^eq-percep-bearing-ray|equation 3]]. An illustrative stereo pair shows where such a design meets a range wall, by asking how far down the aisle its depth error stays inside the stopping budget’s allowances. Notebook [8.3](https://mlsysbook.ai/vol4/08_perception/08_perception.html#nbk-perception-stereo-range-wall) evaluates [[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/03 Running the Machine/08 Sensor Perception/V4-08.07 Spatial Error and Clearance Bounds#^eq-percep-stereo-uncertainty|equation 8]] on that rig, and [[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/03 Running the Machine/08 Sensor Perception/V4-08.07 Spatial Error and Clearance Bounds#^fig-08-depth-uncertainty-clearance|figure 7]] plots the curve.

**Napkin Math 8.3: The stereo range wall**

**Problem**: An illustrative stereo pair must place a rack upright for the base’s clearance checks. How large is its one-standard-deviation depth scale at the rack end, and at what range does that scale alone consume the localization allowance and then the whole side clearance?

**Variables**:

- Baseline ${b} =$ 0.20 m, focal length ${f} =$ 800 px, and disparity standard deviation ${\sigma_d} =$ 0.5 px over the tested range (Illustrative rig; the running machine carries no depth camera).
- Rack-end range ${Z_c} =$ 1.10 m (Illustrative).
- Allowances ${C}$: localization ${\delta_{\text{loc}}} =$ 50 mm and side clearance 0.15 m (Illustrative; **[[05 Reference/Volume IV - Physical AI/V4 Systems and Hardware#^sec-appendix-systems-machine-data-sheet|The machine data sheet]]**).

**Math**:

*Depth scale.* At the rack end, and between the figure’s two quoted ranges,

$$
\sigma_z = \frac{z^2 \sigma_d}{f b} = \frac{(1.10\ \text{m})^2 \times 0.5\ \text{px}}{800\ \text{px} \times 0.20\ \text{m}} = 3.8\ \text{mm}, \quad \frac{\sigma_z(10\ \text{m})}{\sigma_z(2\ \text{m})} = \frac{312.5}{12.5} = 25
$$

*Crossover ranges.* Setting ${\sigma_z}$ equal to an allowance ${C}$ and solving for range,

$$
z^{*} = \sqrt{\frac{C\,f b}{\sigma_d}}: \quad \sqrt{\frac{0.05\ \text{m} \times 160\ \text{px}\cdot\text{m}}{0.5\ \text{px}}} = 4\ \text{m}, \quad \sqrt{\frac{0.15\ \text{m} \times 160\ \text{px}\cdot\text{m}}{0.5\ \text{px}}} = 6.9\ \text{m}
$$

*Sensitivity.* Five times the range costs twenty-five times the depth scale, so the crossovers move only with the square root of ${f} {b} / {\sigma_d}$.

**Result**: At the rack end, one standard deviation is 3.8 mm. It equals ${\delta_{\text{loc}}}$ at 4 m and the whole side clearance at 6.9 m.

**Systems insight**: Stereo depth error is a range budget. This rig’s nominal depth scale fits the rack-end allowance, but supporting that decision still requires the tail, bias, and timing qualification developed below. A claim about an obstacle several meters down the aisle spends the stopping budget’s ${\delta_{\text{loc}}}$ on one standard deviation alone. Beyond a few meters, the clearance claim must therefore come from a range sensor whose error does not grow with ${Z_c}^2$, or the speed must fall.

The values in Notebook [8.3](https://mlsysbook.ai/vol4/08_perception/08_perception.html#nbk-perception-stereo-range-wall) are nominal **one-standard-deviation scales**, not clearance insets or certified 99th-percentile bounds. A usable safety inset requires an empirical tail quantile of the *full spatial residual*, calibrated on the intended sensor across realistic range, surface texture, platform motion, and ambient lighting conditions, with systematic bias and missed detections handled separately. A pervasive trap in visual estimation is assuming that measurement errors follow a nominal Gaussian distribution where a $3{\sigma}$ gate bounds 99.7 percent of all errors. In physical vision, disparity estimation relies on matching pixel appearance across views. When scenes contain repetitive textures (such as corrugated warehouse siding), specular reflections from polished concrete floors, or partial occlusions along object silhouettes, correspondence matching produces catastrophic multimodal outliers rather than Gaussian scatter. A fraction of stereo returns are then gross mismatch outliers lying many standard deviations from the true surface. Because that outlier fraction depends on scene texture, lighting, and the matching architecture, it must be measured directly on residuals from operational deployments rather than assumed. Relying on Gaussian $3{\sigma}$ bounds without robust loss formulations (such as Huber or Tukey loss) or heavy-tailed empirical quantile validation allows these gross outliers to distort the state estimate, directly compromising physical clearance. When validated range-error bounds exceed stopping margins, the vehicle must reduce speed or refuse the motion proposal entirely. Even when optical depth error is bounded in the camera’s own frame, however, the obstacle’s position must still be mapped across the robot’s physical joints and mounts into the frame of the moving body.

### Clearance Insets and Transform Chain Error

^clearance-insets-and-transform-chain-error

Before an estimated point can govern motion, it must propagate through kinematic coordinate transformations into the robot base frame and end effector. The mechanics of rigid body transformations and SE(3) kinematic frame composition follow classical treatments by Craig ([[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/03 Running the Machine/08 Sensor Perception/V4-08.13 Source References#^ref-craig2005introduction|Craig 2005]]) and Lynch and Park ([[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/03 Running the Machine/08 Sensor Perception/V4-08.13 Source References#^ref-lynch2017modern|2017]]). Each link in this transform chain contributes scale errors, mounting calibration drift, joint encoder quantization, and mechanical compliance under load. As demonstrated by the reference-frame principles of [[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/03 Running the Machine/08 Sensor Perception/V4-08.04 Dating and Placing the Measurement#^sec-perception-handoff|section 8.3]], angular misalignment at the sensor mount amplifies linearly with range. If the navigation camera’s mount shifts by ${\theta_{\text{ext}}} =$ 0.005 rad against the chassis, the lateral error ${\delta} {x} \approx {Z_c}\,{\theta_{\text{ext}}}$ at ${Z_c} =$ 10 m reaches 50 mm. The shift is lateral, so on the approach to the rack end it is charged against the side clearance, as the readout notebook charged lateral skew. Its size is the point, because it is as large as the entire 50 mm allowance ${\delta_{\text{loc}}}$ that the stopping budget holds for localization along the direction of travel.

The comparison therefore concerns error scale rather than which allowance pays. The localization allowance carries small, validated residuals, such as the sub-millimeter travel during the clock-conversion bound that the observation contract absorbs ([[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/03 Running the Machine/08 Sensor Perception/V4-08.09 The Observation Contract#^sec-perception-observation-contract|section 8.8]]). An extrinsic shift that consumes that entire budget represents a mechanical calibration failure; downstream consumers must detect and refuse such observations via calibration validity checks rather than absorb them. When combined with quadratic stereo depth dispersion, the total spatial error vector ${\boldsymbol{\epsilon}_{\text{spatial}}}$ at standoff range is anisotropic, dominated by longitudinal depth along the optical axis and angular lever-arm effects orthogonal to it.

↪ How the localization allowance enters the base’s kinetic barrier certificate is developed in **[[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/03 Running the Machine/12 Safety Enforcement/V4-12.06 Safe Sets as Conditional Permission#^sec-enforcement-kinetic-barrier-certificates|Kinetic barrier certificates and relative degree]]**.

Every physical safety barrier operates by defending a geometric boundary around the machine envelope. For a spatial position error vector ${\boldsymbol{\epsilon}_{\text{spatial}}}$, the triangle inequality gives a conservative relation between nominal and true clearance:

$$
{C_{\text{true}}} \ge {C_{\text{nominal}}} - \|{\boldsymbol{\epsilon}_{\text{spatial}}}\|
$$

 To ensure the true obstacle remains beyond the required safe physical clearance ${C_{\text{safe}}}$ (such as the base’s side clearance), the permission path enforces a clearance inset ${C_{\text{inset}}}$ ([[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/03 Running the Machine/08 Sensor Perception/V4-08.07 Spatial Error and Clearance Bounds#^eq-percep-clearance-inset|equation 9]]):

$$
{C_{\text{inset}}} = {C_{\text{safe}}} + \|{\boldsymbol{\epsilon}_{\text{spatial}}}\|_{\text{p99}} \tag{9}
$$

 Here $\|{\boldsymbol{\epsilon}_{\text{spatial}}}\|_{\text{p99}}$ is an empirically certified 99th-percentile quantile of the full spatial residual norm, incorporating optical noise, kinematic propagation, and uncalibrated mounting bias. The tempting shortcut replaces that quantile with one standard deviation from the stereo notebook. For a required clearance of ${C_{\text{safe}}} =$ 150 mm, it would hold an inset of 162.5 mm at 2 m or 462.5 mm at 10 m. Neither number covers the Gaussian tail beyond one standard deviation or the mismatch outliers described earlier. The measured quantile targets 99 percent coverage under the declared calibration assumptions; it does not provide a hard bound on the remaining residual tail. Closing that residual tail requires a justified hard bound on the sensing error, a feasible stopping trajectory, and a safety filter that responds within the remaining latency margin.

^eq-percep-clearance-inset

**Definition 8.2: Clearance inset**

***Clearance inset*** (${C_{\text{inset}}}$) is the separation a consumer holds between an estimated obstacle position and the machine’s envelope so that the true obstacle stays at least the required physical clearance away, except with a stated tail probability measured under the conditions of use.

1.  **Significance**: The inset converts measured spatial error into clearance that the motion gives up ([[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/03 Running the Machine/08 Sensor Perception/V4-08.07 Spatial Error and Clearance Bounds#^eq-percep-clearance-inset|equation 9]]). Every millimeter of qualified error widens the region the permission path treats as occupied, so the planner must slow or route farther away. On the illustrative stereo rig, one standard deviation of depth error alone equals the base’s 0.15 m side clearance at 6.9 m, so there the inset would double the clearance even if the residual tail were no wider than one standard deviation.
2.  **Distinction**: A covariance describes the second moments of the error an estimator believes it has; the inset rests on residuals measured against ground truth, so it includes the bias and heavy tail that the covariance omits. A 99th-percentile inset is also not a hard bound. It leaves 1 percent of residuals outside, whose containment requires a justified sensing-error bound, a feasible response under that bound, and a timely independent permission path. A stop calculated from the same unsupported observation does not independently cover its residual tail.
3.  **Common pitfall**: A frequent misconception is that a reported covariance, or a depth network’s predicted spread, sets the inset. Neither is measured against ground truth, so neither contains the calibration bias and mismatch outliers that the residual campaign of [[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/03 Running the Machine/08 Sensor Perception/V4-08.07 Spatial Error and Clearance Bounds#^sec-perception-qualifying-error|section 8.6.2]] captures.

The two halves of the argument sit side by side in [[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/03 Running the Machine/08 Sensor Perception/V4-08.07 Spatial Error and Clearance Bounds#^fig-08-depth-uncertainty-clearance|figure 7]]. In the upper panel, the stereo depth scale climbs quadratically past the LiDAR’s flat datasheet precision for the specified 1024×10 Hz mode. In the lower panel, the inset holds the estimate far enough out that only the measured residual tail, which unmodeled bias can enlarge, falls inside ${C_{\text{safe}}}$.

Because clearance depends on propagated spatial error, downstream motion planners and safety arbitration layers cannot consume raw coordinate tuples. A covariance matrix ${\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{\text{body}}} \in \mathbb{R}^{3\times3}$ alone cannot determine whether a $10\ \text{mm}$ or $500\ \text{mm}$ clearance inset is justified; establishing that buffer requires an independently validated tail quantile or hard bound. When spatial error is qualified, the permission path can expand the clearance inset, trading speed or workspace for separation. Downstream deterministic safety filters, the per-tick checks that keep the machine inside a region from which it can still stop, hold it outside this expanded boundary ${C_{\text{inset}}}$ and so preserve the clearance buffer against sensing and calibration error. Environmental hazards such as a textureless wall, solar glare, or lost calibration can invalidate that qualification without making a numerical covariance matrix literally infinite. The permission path must then refuse motion proposals that depend on the affected claim and select a feasible fallback.

↪ Safety filters, and the control barrier functions that formalize them, are developed in **[[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/03 Running the Machine/12 Safety Enforcement/V4-12.06 Safe Sets as Conditional Permission#^sec-enforcement-safe-sets-conditional-permission|Safe Sets as Conditional Permission]]**.

On every frame, the producer builds a spatial claim in a fixed order, where any failed check halts the pipeline. It first converts the capture timestamp to the consumer’s clock with a bounded synchronization error and computes the observation age ${t_{\text{age}}}$ at dispatch. If age plus clock uncertainty exceeds the dispatch threshold, it marks the observation stale, and proposals that need it are refused. At admission and across subsequent ticks, the permission path computes the current evidence age relative to capture time and compares it with a separate, later runtime staleness threshold ([[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/03 Running the Machine/08 Sensor Perception/V4-08.09 The Observation Contract#^sec-perception-observation-contract|section 8.8]]). For a fresh observation, the producer unprojects each pixel measurement into optical coordinates with a sensor covariance.[[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/03 Running the Machine/08 Sensor Perception/V4-08.14 Footnotes#^fn12|¹²]] It then carries point and covariance into the body frame by first-order SE(3) error propagation through the calibrated extrinsics at capture. A consumer that predicts forward must attach its motion-model uncertainty without altering the evidence epoch. Validated residuals, including bias, then set the inset radius ${C_{\text{inset}}}$, and when no valid bound remains, the affected motion is refused. What leaves perception is an error-qualified claim $({\mathbf{p}_{\text{body}}}, {\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{\text{body}}}, {C_{\text{inset}}}, {t_{\text{age}}})$, never a bare coordinate.

That claim describes one measurement at one instant, and perception’s responsibility ends with it. Keeping the rack-end range current between sweeps, fusing the LiDAR with a second sensor, joining detections into tracks, and applying a late measurement at its capture epoch are estimation over time. **[[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/03 Running the Machine/09 Spatial Memory/V4-09 Spatial Memory#^sec-memory|Spatial Memory]]** takes them up, beginning with state estimation in **[[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/03 Running the Machine/09 Spatial Memory/V4-09.03 Estimating State from Dated Measurements#^sec-perception-state-estimation|Estimating State from Dated Measurements]]**, and every one of them consumes the dated, framed, error-qualified claim built here. What perception must still supply is evidence that the claimed error was measured.

### Qualifying the Claimed Error

^sec-perception-qualifying-error

Plugging a datasheet precision value or an unmeasured network clock offset into a clearance check leaves systematic mounting bias, heavy-tailed mismatch outliers, and asymmetric path delays uncovered. A physical obstacle can then sit inside the safety boundary while the permission check passes. Two numbers in the claim just built therefore cannot come from a model or a datasheet: the tail quantile that sets the clearance inset, and the clock-conversion bound that dates the claim on the permission path’s clock. Each is fixed by a measurement campaign, and the observation contract of [[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/03 Running the Machine/08 Sensor Perception/V4-08.09 The Observation Contract#^sec-perception-observation-contract|section 8.8]] carries both. Whether a reported covariance matches empirical error is evaluated with consistency statistics that require an estimator’s prediction; that test is developed alongside the estimator (**[[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/03 Running the Machine/09 Spatial Memory/V4-09.03 Estimating State from Dated Measurements#^sec-memory-estimator-consistency|Testing whether the covariance is honest]]**).

The clearance inset of [[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/03 Running the Machine/08 Sensor Perception/V4-08.07 Spatial Error and Clearance Bounds#^eq-percep-clearance-inset|equation 9]] needs a 99th-percentile bound on the residual norm under intended operational conditions, not a theoretical covariance. The most direct procedure is empirical. Collect ${n_{\text{cal}}}$ residual norms against ground truth, sort them, and select the ${k_{\epsilon}}$-th smallest with ${k_{\epsilon}} = \lceil ({n_{\text{cal}}}+1)(1-{\epsilon}) \rceil$. This split-conformal rule guarantees that a future residual falls at or below the bound with probability at least $1 - {\epsilon}$, provided calibration and deployment residuals are exchangeable ([[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/03 Running the Machine/08 Sensor Perception/V4-08.13 Source References#^ref-angelopoulos2023gentle|Angelopoulos and Bates 2023]]). It is the inductive conformal construction that **[[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/01 Machine Anatomy/03 The Cognitive Brain/V4-03.07 Cognitive Vulnerabilities#^sec-brain-softmax-illusion|The softmax illusion and out-of-distribution overconfidence]]** used to turn a policy’s dispersion into a coverage guarantee, applied here to measured residual norms rather than to model scores. For ${\epsilon} =$ 1 percent, the rule requires at least 99 residuals, returning the largest observed value when evaluated on that minimum set. With 1,000 residuals, it returns the 991st smallest value (the 10th largest). This guarantee holds marginally over the calibration distribution. A new floor finish, a lighting change, or a mechanical recalibration breaks exchangeability, requiring new residual campaigns sized by the per-claim sample-size rule of **[[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/02 Teaching the Machine/07 Closed-Loop Evaluation/V4-07.08 Trial Budgets, Stopping Rules, and Comparisons#^sec-evaluation-trials-per-claim|Sample size requirements per empirical claim]]**. Because these residuals are measured directly against ground truth, they capture the sensor’s physical bias, which nominal covariance models omit.

The clock-conversion bound ${\epsilon_{\text{sync}}}$ is likewise measured rather than assumed. The IEEE 1588 Precision Time Protocol (PTP) exchange that **[[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/02 Teaching the Machine/05 Physical Data/V4-05.06 Action Label Selection Across the Control Boundary#^sec-data-hw-vs-sw-timestamps|Hardware timestamps vs. software arrival timestamps]]** used to pair logged frames with actions estimates a device’s clock offset by assuming that both directions of the path take equal time ([[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/03 Running the Machine/08 Sensor Perception/V4-08.13 Source References#^ref-ieee15882019|IEEE 2020a]]). **[[05 Reference/Volume IV - Physical AI/V4 Systems and Hardware#^sec-appendix-systems-ptp|Clock Synchronization and Network Timestamps]]** gives the message exchange and its servo. A path asymmetry ${\Delta t_{\text{asym}}}$ shifts the estimated offset by ${\Delta t_{\text{asym}}}/2$ and leaves no symptom in the exchange. The exchange must therefore be timestamped in hardware at the network interface, where host scheduling cannot add delay to one direction and not the other. Between synchronization exchanges, the local oscillator drifts. The OS1-64’s free-running clock is specified at 10 ppm ([[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/03 Running the Machine/08 Sensor Perception/V4-08.13 Source References#^ref-ouster2025os1|Ouster, Inc. 2025]]), which over a 1 s sync interval accumulates 10 μs of worst-case drift. Clock qualification records the measured tail of the servo’s reported offset, the path-asymmetry allowance, and the drift between corrections. A sampled tail is statistical evidence, not a maximum-error guarantee. A contract that treats synchronization error as a hard bound also needs a justified limit on unobserved offset excursions and a detector that invalidates the mapping when its assumptions fail. For a hardware-triggered camera, this bound is verified by timing the trigger edge against the IMU latch with a logic analyzer. Until those measurements exist, the clock-conversion bound in the navigation camera’s record remains illustrative, and the LiDAR’s datasheet guarantees only 1 ms. While empirical residual campaigns can qualify a stereo or LiDAR sensor whose physics directly measures depth, modern perception pipelines increasingly pair those metric sensors with neural networks that infer depth from a single image.

### Learned Depth as a Measurement

^learned-depth-as-a-measurement

A monocular depth network can predict plausible-looking meter values that err by meters on an unfamiliar floor texture or a life-sized photograph printed on a flat box, because a single camera physically measures only bearing and guesses scale from learned appearance priors. Learned models add two capabilities to metric perception: a semantic classification of spatial regions, and a depth estimate from a single camera. Lifting labeled detections into a persistent, object-centric scene graph carries state across frames, and **[[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/03 Running the Machine/09 Spatial Memory/V4-09.07 Certified Spatial Assertions in Scene Memory#^sec-memory-hierarchical-scene-graphs|3D dynamic scene graphs]]** develops the maintenance of that graph. Perception must settle a narrower systems question: whether a learned depth estimate may enter a safety clearance claim at all. The semantic layer identifies candidate objects and contacts; the metric layer must establish their physical geometry. Point clouds supply measured surface samples where pulses reflect, but missing returns never prove free space. Persistent representations that retain geometric evidence cannot alter an observation’s capture timestamp or convert a learned model score into an error bound.

↪ The volumetric maps that retain this geometric evidence across frames are developed in **[[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/03 Running the Machine/09 Spatial Memory/V4-09.07 Certified Spatial Assertions in Scene Memory#^sec-memory-volumetric-metric-maps|Volumetric metric maps and distance fields]]**.

Lifting 2D pixel features into 3D metric space requires combining 2D token embeddings with per-pixel depth distributions. In deep monocular depth networks (such as Depth Anything, MiDaS, or metric depth transformers), models predict dense depth maps from monocular visual cues. However, single-view depth estimation is inherently ill-posed due to projective scale ambiguity: an infinite family of physical 3D objects with different scales and distances project to identical pixel arrays. Relative-depth models output scale- or affine-ambiguous predictions, whose conversion to metric depth requires external calibration, represented by ${z_{\text{metric}}} = {s} \cdot {z_{\text{pred}}} + {t}$ when the model uses that parameterization. Metric-trained heads directly predict depth in meters, but their units do not certify their error. Both model classes require residual validation and can fail silently on unusual geometries, novel textures, or optical illusions (such as a 2D photograph printed on a flat surface).

In bird’s-eye-view (BEV) architectures such as Lift-Splat-Shoot ([[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/03 Running the Machine/08 Sensor Perception/V4-08.13 Source References#^ref-philion2020lift|Philion and Fidler 2020]]), the network predicts a categorical probability distribution over discrete depth bins along each viewing ray. Each 2D feature token is cast outward along its optical sightline ray and ‘splatted’ into discrete 3D spatial voxel cells weighted by these predicted depth probabilities, transforming disembodied image embeddings into an explicit 3D volumetric representation. The resulting spatial uncertainty ellipsoid deforms non-linearly under perspective unprojection through the unprojection Jacobian ${\mathbf{J}_{\text{unproj}}} = \frac{\partial {\mathbf{p}_c}}{\partial [{u}, {v}, {Z_c}]^\top}$ ([[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/03 Running the Machine/08 Sensor Perception/V4-08.07 Spatial Error and Clearance Bounds#^eq-percep-unproj-covariance|equation 10]]):

$$
{\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_c} = {\mathbf{J}_{\text{unproj}}} {\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{\text{meas}}} {\mathbf{J}_{\text{unproj}}}^\top \tag{10}
$$

 While transverse spatial standard deviation expands linearly with range (${\sigma_\perp} \approx \frac{{Z_c}}{{f}} {\sigma_u}$), stereo radial depth standard deviation expands quadratically (${\sigma_z} \approx \frac{{Z_c}^2}{{f} {b}} {\sigma_d}$). This perspective unprojection transforms near-field circular uncertainty disks into highly eccentric, needle-shaped ellipsoids oriented along the camera’s sightline, with extreme radial dispersion at long ranges that must be bounded before downstream safety filters can certify clearance.

Because the depth-bin distributions produced by learned lifting stages are uncalibrated model scores rather than certified metric error bounds, the clearance inset of [[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/03 Running the Machine/08 Sensor Perception/V4-08.07 Spatial Error and Clearance Bounds#^eq-percep-clearance-inset|equation 9]] cannot rest on learned depth alone. Downstream safety enforcers require continuous validation against calibrated active ranging sensors or stereo disparity. This is the division of labor on the running machine. A learned head on the navigation camera may classify the worker and even estimate depth, but the rack-end range that enters the clearance check comes from the LiDAR. The split also keeps the check independent of the proposal it judges, because a checker that consumed the camera’s own perception would share every blind spot of the proposer it exists to catch.

**Systems Perspective 8.3: The metric veto invariant**

**Safety is an invariant of metric clearance, not semantic understanding.** Suppose a slow 2 Hz vision-language foundation model classifies a pathway as “clear aisle” or dismisses an object as a harmless shadow. If the 20 Hz LiDAR, or the occupancy grid built from it, detects a physical obstacle return within the stopping envelope, the permission path must honor the qualified occupied-region evidence rather than let semantic confidence erase it. The permission path halts or slows the machine regardless of the semantic model’s high confidence. Semantic models propose high-level task goals; qualified metric sensing supplies clearance evidence to the permission path. Neither sensor pipeline itself owns actuator authority.

**Checkpoint 8.2: Spatial error and the clearance inset**

A spatial claim is spent from the same clearance as its age, and its error must be measured before it is charged.

**Quadratic depth error**: Can you explain why stereo depth error grows with the square of range while a pulsed LiDAR’s range precision stays roughly flat?

**Baseline estimate**: If the illustrative rig’s 0.20 m baseline were halved, at what range would one standard deviation of depth error reach the 0.15 m side clearance, compared with 6.9 m now?

**Covariance vs. clearance inset**: Can you explain why a residual quantile measured against ground truth, not a reported covariance, sets ${C_{\text{inset}}}$, and what that quantile still leaves uncovered?

**Learned depth vs. metric evidence**: Can you explain why a depth network’s predicted spread cannot enter a clearance claim until its residuals are validated, and why qualified metric evidence overrides a semantic label that calls a return a shadow?
