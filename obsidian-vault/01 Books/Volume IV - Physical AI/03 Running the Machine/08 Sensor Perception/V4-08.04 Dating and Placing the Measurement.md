# V4-08.04 Dating and Placing the Measurement

[[Home|Home]] · [[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/Volume 4 Overview|Volume Overview]] · [[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/03 Running the Machine/08 Sensor Perception/V4-08 Sensor Perception|V4-08 Sensor Perception]]

^sec-perception-handoff

A frame from the navigation camera lands in host memory as an array of pixel values. Nothing in that array says when the light was captured, where the camera was mounted, or how far a position read from it can be trusted, yet the planner that checks rack-end clearance needs all three. Every modality has the same gap. A trajectory optimizer cannot plan against unorganized time-of-flight returns, and a force controller cannot track a grid of raw intensities. Downstream estimation, planning, and spatial memory require structured measurements: metric positions, bounding volumes, and semantic classes, with directly measured velocity where the sensing modality supplies it. Translating raw streams into those measurements is the perception pipeline’s responsibility. Whatever form its output takes, whether an explicit message packet or an observation token sequence, it must carry three mandatory properties before any consumer can use it: a capture timestamp, a reference frame, and a qualified uncertainty envelope.

![[90 Assets/Volume IV/08 Perception/Spa Locator S 1df44af0c1.svg]]

*S·P·A locator triad highlighting the Sense node.* *The Sense stage: photons become dated, framed claims.*

The first mandatory property is the **capture timestamp** ${{t_{\text{capture}}}}_0$, which specifies the temporal midpoint of the energy integration window, the effective instant of a camera exposure or a laser firing. Physical sensors do not take instantaneous snapshots. An optical camera integrates photons across an exposure duration, and a mechanical lidar sweeps laser pulses over an azimuth arc across several milliseconds. Downstream state observers and vision-language-action policies require ${{t_{\text{capture}}}}_0$ to ground observation tokens into the machine’s dynamic equations of motion. By contrast, the arrival stamp ${t_{\text{recv}}}$ marks the moment the operating system kernel completes the direct memory access (DMA) transfer. It is the software arrival timestamp that **[[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/02 Teaching the Machine/05 Physical Data/V4-05.06 Action Label Selection Across the Control Boundary#^sec-data-hw-vs-sw-timestamps|Hardware timestamps vs. software arrival timestamps]]** showed corrupting logged velocities; at runtime it corrupts the pose an observation is paired with. Half of the exposure duration, sensor readout, physical transport, and host DMA transfer all elapse before that interrupt fires. Consequently, the arrival offset ${\Delta t_{\text{recv}}} = {t_{\text{recv}}} - {{t_{\text{capture}}}}_0$ is never zero. Substituting arrival time for capture time directly corrupts downstream state estimation and action generation.

At constant velocity ${v_{\text{base}}}$, the hidden travel is ${\Delta x} = {v_{\text{base}}}\,{\Delta t_{\text{recv}}}$. On the mobile manipulator, the navigation camera’s frame completes its DMA transfer 26.6 ms after the exposure midpoint. An estimator that timestamps the frame upon arrival dates the measurement late by that full duration. At the base’s 1.5 m/s drive limit, this temporal mismatch pairs the visual observation with an estimated pose 39.9 mm ahead of the physical position the camera occupied at capture.

Eliminating this arrival offset and bounding the camera-to-inertial offset ${\Delta t_{\text{imu}}}$ of [[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/03 Running the Machine/08 Sensor Perception/V4-08.03 Capture Physics and Calibration#^sec-perception-transduction-calibration|section 8.2]] require stamping the capture event directly in hardware rather than recording when a software thread first handles the frame buffer. Software timestamps inherit operating system scheduling jitter, driver queue delays, and thread preemption. While the base rotates at 0.5 rad/s, each millisecond of scheduling jitter misorients the camera by 0.5 mrad, producing a lateral displacement of 0.55 mm at rack-end distance. Two hardware architectures avoid dating capture by software-handler scheduling. In the first architecture, a hardware GPIO strobe (such as FSYNC) from the IMU microcontroller simultaneously latches an inertial sample and triggers the camera exposure. Because this trigger edge marks only the start of exposure for the first sensor row rather than the optical midpoint, the driver must add ${t_{\text{exp}}}/2$ to reach the exposure midpoint. It must also add ${y}\,{t_{\text{row}}}$ for the specific image row ${y}$ under consideration. Omitting this correction dates the navigation camera frame 8 ms early, translating to 12 mm of unmodeled travel at the drive limit. Furthermore, because automatic exposure control varies ${t_{\text{exp}}}$ dynamically across frames, the driver must apply the specific exposure time reported in each frame’s metadata. In the second architecture, used for networked sensors such as the lidar units and GigE Vision cameras, each peripheral timestamps samples against an onboard hardware clock. The Precision Time Protocol (PTP) ([[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/03 Running the Machine/08 Sensor Perception/V4-08.13 Source References#^ref-ieee15882019|IEEE 2020a]]), or its time-sensitive networking profile gPTP ([[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/03 Running the Machine/08 Sensor Perception/V4-08.13 Source References#^ref-ieee8021as2020|IEEE 2020b]]), disciplines these local clocks to a master reference across the Ethernet bus. Each free-running oscillator (the camera trigger counter, the LiDAR’s internal clock, the application processor, the safety MCU) defines its own **clock domain**. A capture time stamped in one domain cannot be compared with a time read in another until it is converted. The largest error that conversion may carry is the **clock-conversion bound** ${\epsilon_{\text{sync}}}$, which every dated observation must carry alongside its capture time; [[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/03 Running the Machine/08 Sensor Perception/V4-08.07 Spatial Error and Clearance Bounds#^sec-perception-qualifying-error|section 8.6.2]] details how to measure it.

The second mandatory property is the **reference frame** identifier ${\mathcal{F}_{\text{sensor}}}$. Every spatial measurement is natively resolved in the local coordinate system of the physical transducer. A camera measures bearing angles relative to its optical center, whereas a radar measures range and Doppler velocity relative to its antenna array. Downstream trajectory planners and occupancy maps, by contrast, operate in a chassis body frame ${\mathcal{F}_B}$ or an inertial navigation frame ${\mathcal{F}_W}$. Transforming an observation from ${\mathcal{F}_{\text{sensor}}}$ to ${\mathcal{F}_B}$ requires applying a rigid-body spatial transformation consisting of a rotation matrix and a translation vector that define the sensor mounting extrinsics, the calibrated location and orientation at which the sensor is bolted to the machine. Coordinate frames nest hierarchically from the physical operating environment down to the transducer: ${\mathcal{F}_{\text{earth}}} \to {\mathcal{F}_{\text{map}}} \to {\mathcal{F}_{\text{odom}}} \to {\mathcal{F}_B} \to {\mathcal{F}_{\text{sensor}}}$, where every link in this tree introduces its own transform and uncertainty ([[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/03 Running the Machine/08 Sensor Perception/V4-08.04 Dating and Placing the Measurement#^fig-08-coordinate-frame-tree|figure 4]]).

When perception outputs omit the frame identifier or rely on nominal CAD dimensions rather than calibrated physical extrinsics, small angular offsets create large spatial errors that scale with distance. The same uncalibrated yaw error of ${\theta_{\text{ext}}} =$ 1° that costs 19.2 mm at the 1.10 m rack end grows linearly as the planner looks farther down the aisle. For an object at an illustrative range ${z} =$ 15 m down a long aisle, it induces a transverse position error ${\Delta y} \approx {z} \sin{\theta_{\text{ext}}}$ of 0.26 m. This lateral offset is approximately 1.7× the 0.15 m side clearance that the base maintains from the warehouse racks, causing the planner to either steer into the racking or falsely reject an open aisle as obstructed.

The third mandatory property is an **uncertainty representation**, such as a measurement covariance ${\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_z}$ paired with a validated tail bound or hard error envelope when physical clearance depends on it. Environmental degradations such as low illumination, atmospheric scattering, specular reflections, and out-of-distribution neural network errors distort the residual distribution far beyond Gaussian assumptions. While a covariance matrix characterizes second moments, it does not guarantee a conservative bounding volume for the true physical state. Downstream state estimators and policy heads rely on ${\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_z}$ to weight incoming sensory updates against dynamic motion predictions. If perception reports an artificially optimistic covariance, the estimator over-weights spurious measurements and commands aggressive, erratic actuator responses. Robust physical safety decisions must therefore account explicitly for systematic bias, missed detections, and sensor operational limits alongside the reported covariance.

### Calibrating the Coordinate Chain

^sec-perception-coordinate-chain

Even when each sensor frame is named, an observation measured at the wrist camera still cannot guide the arm or base until the runtime chains every joint and mount transform back to the world at the exact capture instant ${{t_{\text{capture}}}}_0$. A coordinate frame transform maps a point expressed relative to one origin into the coordinate system of another: its rotational part reorients the coordinate axes, while its translational part accounts for the spatial offset between origins. Homogeneous matrix representations package both operations so that camera-to-wrist, wrist-to-base, and base-to-world transforms can be composed at ${{t_{\text{capture}}}}_0$. The notation below writes the destination as a lower index and the source as an upper index, expressing the same mapping direction as the two-subscript convention used for LiDAR de-skewing.

A robot’s spatial coordinate system forms a **directed acyclic graph (DAG)** of transforms ${\mathcal{G}} = ({\mathcal{V}}, {\mathcal{E}})$, where vertices ${\mathcal{V}} = \{{\mathcal{F}}_i\}$ represent physical coordinate frames and directed edges ${\mathcal{E}}$ represent time-varying rigid-body transforms ${\mathbf{T}}_{ij}({t}) \in \text{SE}(3)$. In practice, systems constrain this DAG to a directed tree where each non-root frame has exactly one parent, ensuring a unique path between any two frames. When unprojecting an optical observation ${\mathbf{p}_c}({{t_{\text{capture}}}}_0)$ into world coordinates, the runtime composes the transforms along that tree:

$$
{\mathbf{p}_{\text{world}}}({{t_{\text{capture}}}}_0) = {\mathbf{T}_{\text{world}}^{\text{map}}} \cdot {\mathbf{T}_{\text{map}}^{\text{odom}}}({{t_{\text{capture}}}}_0) \cdot {\mathbf{T}_{\text{odom}}^{\text{base}}}({{t_{\text{capture}}}}_0) \cdot {\mathbf{T}_{\text{base}}^{\text{flange}}}({{t_{\text{capture}}}}_0) \cdot {\mathbf{T}_{\text{flange}}^{\text{cam}}} \cdot {\mathbf{p}_c}({{t_{\text{capture}}}}_0)
$$

 The physical mounting location of the sensor on the kinematic chain dictates whether those transforms stay fixed on the body or update with the arm’s joints across this DAG. Two canonical mounting topologies govern how the extrinsic calibration is solved.

In an **eye-in-hand** configuration, the camera or depth sensor is bolted directly to the manipulator’s moving tool flange (${\mathcal{F}_{\text{flange}}}$). Moving any preceding joint alters the sensor’s optical center relative to the world, requiring continuous forward kinematics updates. Solving for the rigid extrinsic transform ${\mathbf{X}} = {\mathbf{T}_{\text{flange}}^{\text{cam}}} \in \text{SE}(3)$ requires executing robot motions across ${N} \ge 2$ non-parallel rotational axes while observing a stationary fiducial target, such as a ChArUco grid.[[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/03 Running the Machine/08 Sensor Perception/V4-08.14 Footnotes#^fn6|⁶]] The running machine’s wrist camera is mounted this way, which makes this the calibration its grasps depend on. Because the fiducial target stays fixed in the base frame across both poses, the composed base-to-target transform ${\mathbf{T}_{\text{base}, k}^{\text{flange}}} {\mathbf{X}} {\mathbf{T}_{\text{cam}, k}^{\text{target}}} = {\mathbf{T}_{\text{base}, k+1}^{\text{flange}}} {\mathbf{X}} {\mathbf{T}_{\text{cam}, k+1}^{\text{target}}}$ is identical at poses ${k}$ and ${k}+1$, and left-multiplying by $({\mathbf{T}_{\text{base}, k}^{\text{flange}}})^{-1}$ while right-multiplying by $({\mathbf{T}_{\text{cam}, k+1}^{\text{target}}})^{-1}$ isolates the relative motions on opposite sides of the unknown mount so that the closed kinematic loop establishes the classic **hand-eye matrix equation**:[[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/03 Running the Machine/08 Sensor Perception/V4-08.14 Footnotes#^fn7|⁷]]

$$
{\mathbf{A}} {\mathbf{X}} = {\mathbf{X}} {\mathbf{B}}
$$

 where ${\mathbf{A}} = ({\mathbf{T}_{\text{base}, k}^{\text{flange}}})^{-1} {\mathbf{T}_{\text{base}, k+1}^{\text{flange}}} \in \text{SE}(3)$ is the relative flange displacement reported by the forward kinematics chain, and ${\mathbf{B}} = {\mathbf{T}_{\text{cam}, k}^{\text{target}}} ({\mathbf{T}_{\text{cam}, k+1}^{\text{target}}})^{-1} \in \text{SE}(3)$ is the relative camera displacement extracted from optical target unprojection.

In an **eye-to-hand** calibration rig, the camera overlooks the workspace from a body-mounted mast or a stationary overhead beam. When calibrating against an overhead camera, the mobile base must remain parked so that the camera-to-base transform remains constant (${\mathbf{X}'} = {\mathbf{T}_{\text{base}}^{\text{cam}}} \in \text{SE}(3)$), while the target is affixed to the robot’s moving end-effector (${\mathbf{Z}} = {\mathbf{T}_{\text{flange}}^{\text{target}}} \in \text{SE}(3)$). At each joint configuration, let ${\mathbf{A}'} = ({\mathbf{T}_{\text{base}}^{\text{flange}}})^{-1}$ map base coordinates into the flange frame and ${\mathbf{B}'} = ({\mathbf{T}_{\text{cam}}^{\text{target}}})^{-1}$ map camera coordinates into the target frame. These are absolute pose transforms for this rig, rather than the relative motions used above. Both paths then map camera coordinates into the flange frame:

$$
{\mathbf{A}'} {\mathbf{X}'} = {\mathbf{Z}} {\mathbf{B}'}
$$

 If the mobile base subsequently moves relative to the overhead camera, the runtime must update the camera-to-base transform accordingly. For a mast-mounted camera that rides with the chassis, ${\mathbf{T}_{\text{base}}^{\text{cam}}}$ remains fixed on the body while ${\mathbf{T}_{\text{odom}}^{\text{base}}}({{t_{\text{capture}}}}_0)$ carries the base motion into the world frame.

Treating the nominal rigid transform chain from CAD as an exact physical calibration ignores load and temperature effects. Physical manipulators deflect under payload mass, accumulate lost motion and torsional wind-up in the joints (strain wave gears have near-zero backlash, but their flexsplines still twist under load), and expand with temperature (structural aluminum expands at ${\alpha} \approx$ 23$\times 10^{-6}/\text{K}$). For an illustrative 1 m aluminum link that warms uniformly by 10$^\circ\text{C}$, the corresponding expansion is 0.23 mm. A motor temperature rise alone does not establish that link-temperature change, as localized thermal gradients across structural members and encoder mountings require direct measurement before updating calibration parameters.

Fusing joint kinematics with optical frames also requires temporal alignment across the transform DAG. Any uncalibrated timestamp skew ${\Delta t_{\text{skew}}} = {t_{\text{cam}}} - {t_{\text{joint}}}$ between the motor communication bus (such as EtherCAT or CANopen) and the camera exposure window evaluates the forward kinematics chain at stale joint angles ${\mathbf{q}}({t_{\text{joint}}}) \neq {\mathbf{q}}({t_{\text{cam}}})$, inducing spatial tracking error:

$$
{\Delta \mathbf{p}_{\text{skew}}} = {\mathbf{v}_{\text{wrist}}} \, {\Delta t_{\text{skew}}} + ({\boldsymbol{\omega}_{\text{wrist}}} \times {\mathbf{r}_{P/O}}) \, {\Delta t_{\text{skew}}}
$$

 At an illustrative wrist linear velocity ${v} =$ 1 m/s and angular velocity ${\omega} =$ 2 rad/s with a camera lever arm ${r} =$ 0.15 m, an unmodeled 10 ms skew displaces the observation by 10 mm plus 3 mm, or 13 mm. This error causes the arm to miss a grasp whose position tolerance is 5 mm. Holding that displacement within the tolerance gives the timing requirement ${\Delta t_{\text{skew}}} \le {r_{\text{tol}}}/({v} + {\omega} {r})$, which evaluates to 3.8 ms. Millisecond-level alignment therefore suffices, whereas microsecond synchronization is unnecessary. Systems typically meet this requirement by timestamping both streams against a single shared clock (a fieldbus distributed clock or a PTP master) and interpolating the high-rate joint stream at the camera’s exposure midpoint.

The [REP 105 frame convention](https://reps.openrobotics.org/rep-0105/)[[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/03 Running the Machine/08 Sensor Perception/V4-08.14 Footnotes#^fn8|⁸]] names these frames and separates a locally continuous `odom` pose from a globally corrected `map` pose. On the mobile manipulator, ${\mathcal{F}_B}$ is the base’s `base_link`, tracked through the odometric frame `odom`, and the arm’s serial chain maps its mounting flange `tool0` through the joint angles back to `base_link`. [[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/03 Running the Machine/08 Sensor Perception/V4-08.04 Dating and Placing the Measurement#^tbl-08-rep105-tree|Table 2]] lists the frame roles and the behavioral rules each consumer follows. A local controller consumes a continuous estimate and rejects unexpected transform jumps, while runtime checks verify transform timestamps and estimator uncertainties before execution.

| Frame         | Parent in this example | Meaning                                     | Consumer rule                                                 |
|:--------------|:-----------------------|:--------------------------------------------|:--------------------------------------------------------------|
| `earth`       | root                   | Optional geodetic datum                     | Verify datum before global routing                            |
| `map`         | `earth` if present     | Global localization; corrections may jump   | Keep relocalization jumps out of local feedback               |
| `odom`        | `map`                  | Locally continuous pose estimate; may drift | Track motion locally; monitor uncertainty and discontinuities |
| `base_link`   | `odom`                 | Body-fixed origin                           | Body pose still inherits odometry uncertainty                 |
| `sensor_link` | `base_link`            | Calibrated sensor mounting frame            | Apply time-valid extrinsics and their uncertainty             |

Table 2: **REP 105 frame roles for a mobile robot**: A compact example of global, local, body, and sensor frames. REP 105 requires `odom`-relative pose to remain continuous but does not prescribe ${C}^1$ smoothness, a covariance growth rate, or fixed accuracy; these require estimator-specific evidence.

Figure 4: **Coordinate frame tree and its consumers**: Each edge states the property REP 105 or calibration gives it and the uncertainty it adds to the prose equation below. Only the odom to base_link edge is required to stay continuous; the map to odom correction may jump at relocalization, so local tracking loops consume the continuous branch while global planners reconcile the jump. A sensor observation reaches the consumers with its frame, capture epoch ${{t_{\text{capture}}}}_0$, and covariance ${\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_z}$.

^fig-08-coordinate-frame-tree

Splitting consumers across the tree keeps a relocalization jump out of the actuators ([[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/03 Running the Machine/08 Sensor Perception/V4-08.04 Dating and Placing the Measurement#^fig-08-coordinate-frame-tree|figure 4]]). Global planners absorb the discontinuous ${\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{\text{map}}}$ corrections at relocalization, while high-rate local tracking loops consume only the continuous `odom` $\to$ `base_link` branch, so a global correction never reaches the actuators as an abrupt tracking step. Mounting uncertainty still compounds with dynamic pose uncertainty across every intermediate link. Under a first-order propagation model with independent error sources:

$$
{\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{\text{world}}} = {\mathbf{J}_{\text{map}}}{\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{\text{map}}}{\mathbf{J}_{\text{map}}}^\top + {\mathbf{J}_{\text{odom}}}{\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{\text{odom}}}{\mathbf{J}_{\text{odom}}}^\top + {\mathbf{J}_{\text{ext}}}{\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{\text{ext}}}{\mathbf{J}_{\text{ext}}}^\top
$$

 where the Jacobians ${\mathbf{J}}$ project each frame’s spatial covariance into world coordinates. When frame errors correlate, the propagation must include their cross-covariance terms. A downstream consumer requires the frame identifier, capture epoch, and compounded uncertainty envelope, rather than a transform selected merely by its latest publication timestamp.
