# V4-10.02 Grounded Affordances

[[Home|Home]] · [[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/Volume 4 Overview|Volume Overview]] · [[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/03 Running the Machine/10 Grounded Intent/V4-10 Grounded Intent|V4-10 Grounded Intent]]

^sec-intent-grounded-affordances

An operator tells the warehouse mobile manipulator to “pick the red mug” from an active takeaway conveyor. The request names an outcome and leaves the grasp pose, approach velocity, contact force, and execution deadline unspecified. Spatial memory has already done its part by recording a belief about the mug’s handle with an evidence epoch, an initial error of 3 mm, and a growth law. Under that law, the moving belt carries the handle outside the 15 mm grasp tolerance 60 ms after capture. Because that record says only where the handle is and how long the claim holds, it neither specifies what the machine may do with the handle nor authorizes actuator torque. **Intent** bridges this gap by binding the spoken request to an admissible, time-bounded physical goal that the motion planner can pursue.

↩︎ The belief record a lease consumes (evidence epoch, initial error, growth law) is established in **[[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/03 Running the Machine/09 Spatial Memory/V4-09.11 The Belief Record Schema#^sec-memory-spatial-state-schema|The Belief Record Schema]]**.

The reach does not stay inside the arm. On the running machine a slow intent model sits above a fast chunk policy that proposes base velocity and arm targets together in one whole-body stream (**[[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/01 Machine Anatomy/03 The Cognitive Brain/V4-03.06 Chunk Supply and Evidence Validity#^sec-brain-two-proposers|Two proposers and the evidence horizon]]**). A wrong or stale mug target can steer the base as well as the wrist. What the permission path grants in answer to the request is therefore deliberately narrower than motion. It grants an intent lease ([[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/03 Running the Machine/10 Grounded Intent/V4-10.09 The Intent Lease Specification and Contract#^sec-intent-lease|section 10.8]]), a record that names a target and effort ceilings but never a path. For the mug, the lease binds the parent belief record’s digest and evidence epoch to a target pose in the handle’s affordance frame, a goal region of spatial and angular tolerances around that pose, and ceilings on tool speed, acceleration, and contact wrench. Alongside those geometric and dynamic bounds, it records the motion predicates already validated, an expiration deadline on the safety controller’s own clock, and the fallback that controller engages when the deadline passes.

Friction cones, force closure, and symbolic planners matter in this layer only because each supplies a field that the safety controller can check without re-running the upstream model: grasp mechanics fix the target pose, goal region, and wrench ceiling below; task operators ([[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/03 Running the Machine/10 Grounded Intent/V4-10.03 Task Operators and Geometric Samplers#^sec-intent-task-motion|section 10.2]]) and open-vocabulary grounding ([[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/03 Running the Machine/10 Grounded Intent/V4-10.04 Semantic Request Translation to Geometric Proposals#^sec-intent-request-to-proposal|section 10.3]]) determine which predicates and error envelopes accompany a candidate target; and belief drift ([[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/03 Running the Machine/10 Grounded Intent/V4-10.06 Expiring Goals and Temporal Validity Leases#^sec-intent-goals-expire|section 10.5]]) paired with kinematic reachability ([[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/03 Running the Machine/10 Grounded Intent/V4-10.07 Admissible Goal Sets#^sec-intent-admissible-goal-sets|section 10.6]]) bounds the expiration deadline and dynamic limits.

This grounding step is where a learned model’s output first turns into physical coordinates and contact limits that the robot can execute. Open-vocabulary object detectors, language models, and vision-language-action policies let operators specify manipulation tasks in plain words rather than through hard-coded skill libraries. What those neural networks emit, however, is only a statistical hypothesis, such as a bounding box, a semantic token, an uncalibrated point coordinate, or a chunk of normalized actions. An objective function that knows nothing of metric clearance, actuator torque limits, or physical time produced it. On the running machine, the division of responsibility is therefore strict: the learned Brain proposes candidate actions, and the deterministic permission path checks and admits them before any drive receives current.

↩︎ The proposal boundary and the permission path are established in **[[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/00 Introduction/01 The Causal Boundary/V4-01.08 The Machine in Five Levels#^sec-boundary-machine-model|The Machine in Five Levels]]**.

Before the permission path admits a proposed target for planning and execution, the target must satisfy three physical requirements:

1.  *Metric physicality*: The target cannot remain a symbolic label; it must resolve to an explicit geometric feature carrying a six-degree-of-freedom pose, a gripper aperture, and a bounded contact wrench profile.
2.  *Bounded error*: The proposal must include a certified spatial error envelope that fits strictly within the task tolerances and mechanical effort limits of both the manipulator and the workpiece.
3.  *Temporal expiration*: Every target must carry an explicit evidence epoch, bounding its valid lifespan as target objects shift and sensory observations grow stale during compute cycles.

Every request traverses an explicit pipeline to meet these three requirements. Sensory perception resolves symbolic task operators into measurable physical predicates, and calibration transforms image regions into metric poses. The permission path then evaluates effort limits, evidence age, kinematic reachability, and dynamic obstacle clearances before committing the target to the motion planner. A vision-language-action (VLA) model ([[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/03 Running the Machine/10 Grounded Intent/V4-10.13 Source References#^ref-brohan2023rt2|Brohan, Brown, Carbajal, Chebotar, Chen, et al. 2023]]) can propose the mug’s target directly, but its deliberative inference loop is slow. The running machine’s intent model executes at 2 Hz and requires 336 ms (derived in **[[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/01 Machine Anatomy/03 The Cognitive Brain/V4-03.05 The Cost of a Fresh Evaluation#^sec-brain-memory-wall|The memory wall]]**) to autoregressively decode 7 target values. These values are a 3D position, a three-value orientation (which the producer converts to the quaternion the wire format carries), and an open-close gripper command. The machine therefore splits proposal across two speeds (**[[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/01 Machine Anatomy/03 The Cognitive Brain/V4-03.06 Chunk Supply and Evidence Validity#^sec-brain-two-proposers|Two proposers and the evidence horizon]]**). The intent model names the target, and a smaller chunk policy streams motion toward it between intent inferences.

That split keeps the joints supplied with setpoints, but it does not keep the target true. If the conveyor moves while one intent inference runs, every chunk aims at where the mug was when the model’s frame was captured. One inference outlasts the handle’s 60 ms evidence window more than five times over. When target evidence expires between model inferences, the system must either switch to a high-rate local visual tracker or arrest motion entirely. [[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/03 Running the Machine/10 Grounded Intent/V4-10.06 Expiring Goals and Temporal Validity Leases#^sec-intent-goals-expire|Section 10.5]] quantifies these expiration dynamics. Safe physical agency demands dated geometric evidence, explicit capability limits, and an autonomous permission path capable of revoking execution authority whenever the neural model lags or stalls.

↩︎ The machine’s multi-rate cadences, from motor current loops through safety screening to deliberative planners, are established in **[[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/01 Machine Anatomy/04 The Nervous System/V4-04.04 Multi-Rate Cadences#^sec-nervous-time-constants-phase-lag|Physical time constants and sampling phase lag]]**.

Grounding the mug grasp starts with naming which physical feature the gripper will touch, expressing that feature in the robot’s base frame, and bounding the forces of contact. In ecological psychology, Gibson coined the term **affordance** for what an environment offers an agent capable of acting on it ([[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/03 Running the Machine/10 Grounded Intent/V4-10.13 Source References#^ref-gibson1979ecological|Gibson 1979]]).[[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/03 Running the Machine/10 Grounded Intent/V4-10.14 Footnotes#^fn1|¹]] Robotics turns that qualitative idea into four measurable quantities for the mug handle. The first is a target grasp pose ${\mathbf{T}_{\text{grasp}}} = ({\mathbf{R}_{\text{grasp}}}, {\mathbf{p}_{\text{grasp}}}) \in {SE(3)}$ in the robot base frame (where ${SE(3)}$ is the Special Euclidean group of 3D rigid-body positions and ${SO(3)}$ rotations).[[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/03 Running the Machine/10 Grounded Intent/V4-10.14 Footnotes#^fn2|²]] The others are a unit approach vector ${\hat{\mathbf{a}}_{\text{approach}}}$, a jaw aperture ${w_{\text{aperture}}}$ in meters, and an allowable contact wrench envelope ${\mathbf{w}_{\text{contact}}}$ bundling three-axis forces in newtons and three-axis torques in newton-meters. Because each usable site on an object imposes different geometry and force limits, each site defines its own **affordance frame** ${\mathcal{F}_{\text{affordance}}}$ attached to the object’s body coordinates. A single mug offers several distinct affordance frames across its surface. Its thin rim tolerates little normal force, its cylindrical wall requires a wide jaw span, and its handle requires a full six-degree-of-freedom (6-DoF) alignment that threads the fingers through the loop without collision.

Contact mechanics dictates the allowable contact wrench. Under the classical Coulomb friction model, tangential contact forces ${\mathbf{f}_t}$ remain stable without slipping only when constrained within a **friction cone**:

$$
\|{\mathbf{f}_t}\| \le {\mu} {f_n}
$$

 where ${f_n}$ represents the normal contact force and ${\mu}$ is the static coefficient of friction. For an idealized two-finger parallel gripper, Nguyen established that a grasp resists arbitrary in-plane disturbance forces if and only if the line connecting the two contact points lies strictly within the interior of both friction cones ([[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/03 Running the Machine/10 Grounded Intent/V4-10.13 Source References#^ref-nguyen1988constructing|Nguyen 1988]]).

The general formulation operates in wrench space. For each contact point ${i}$, a grasp map ${\mathbf{G}}_i$ transforms the local contact force ${\mathbf{f}}_i$ into an object wrench composed of the force and its moment ${\mathbf{d}}_i \times {\mathbf{f}}_i$ about the object reference frame. Here ${\mathbf{d}}_i$ denotes the displacement from the reference origin to the contact point. Mapping the extremal rays of every friction cone into wrench space yields a set of primitive contact wrenches. The grasp achieves **force closure** when the origin of wrench space lies strictly within the interior of the convex hull of these primitive wrenches, guaranteeing that admissible contact forces can equilibrate any external disturbance wrench. Because fingers can only push into the surface, the net wrench they apply is a nonnegative scaling of a point inside that convex hull, and enclosing the origin strictly inside the hull ensures that a ray pointing opposite to any external disturbance wrench intersects the hull interior so the fingers can scale up their normal forces to cancel disturbances from every direction. Ferrari and Canny formalized this condition into a scalar quality metric. The metric is the radius of the largest origin-centered ball inscribed within that convex hull, and it represents the minimum disturbance wrench the grasp resists per unit of applied normal force ([[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/03 Running the Machine/10 Grounded Intent/V4-10.13 Source References#^ref-ferrari1992planning|Ferrari and Canny 1992]]).

Force closure, however, remains an idealized geometric property that specifies stability without bounding magnitude. A physical grasp requires finite capacity. The normal squeeze force must counter the mug’s gravitational load and dynamic accelerations during transit while remaining strictly below structural limits that would fracture ceramic or spill contents. **[[05 Reference/Volume IV - Physical AI/V4 Control and Dynamics#^sec-appendix-control-grasp|Grasp mechanics: Friction cones and force closure]]** derives the lower bound on squeeze force from Newton’s second law and develops friction cones and force closure from first principles. These dynamic bounds constitute the effort envelope that the Brain proposes and the permission path enforces.

Turning a spoken request into a checkable physical target is the robotic form of the **symbol grounding problem** ([[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/03 Running the Machine/10 Grounded Intent/V4-10.13 Source References#^ref-harnad1990symbol|Harnad 1990]]), which asks how arbitrary symbols connect to measurable sensorimotor states.[[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/03 Running the Machine/10 Grounded Intent/V4-10.14 Footnotes#^fn3|³]] For the mug, that connection runs through the calibrated transform chain from the camera frame to the manipulator base, the handle’s affordance frame, and the contact wrench bounds. An uncalibrated detection box or an autoregressively sampled coordinate carries none of those bounds. The permission path must therefore catch and reject an unsupported model proposal (a hallucination, in machine learning terms[[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/03 Running the Machine/10 Grounded Intent/V4-10.14 Footnotes#^fn4|⁴]]) deterministically, regardless of the model’s reported confidence. When a spatial feature instead bundles a calibrated base-frame pose, certified wrench limits, and an evidence timestamp, it forms a grounded affordance.

**Definition 10.1: Grounded affordance**

***Grounded affordance*** is an affordance expressed in the robot’s own frame and units: a feature pose in the base frame, an approach direction, an aperture, and a contact wrench ceiling, each tied to dated sensor evidence through a calibrated measurement chain.

1.  **Significance**: It turns a label into quantities the permission path can check. The mug handle’s grounded affordance is a pose that must land inside a 15 mm task tolerance from evidence with a 3 mm initial error, and on the moving belt that evidence stays valid for only 60 ms ([[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/03 Running the Machine/10 Grounded Intent/V4-10.06 Expiring Goals and Temporal Validity Leases#^sec-intent-goals-expire|section 10.5]]).
2.  **Distinction**: Unlike a detector box or a language label, which designates where an object appears in image space or what it is called, a grounded affordance defines where and how an end effector may establish contact. Unlike Gibson’s perceptual affordance, it is bound to one gripper’s limits and to one capture time.
3.  **Common pitfall**: Treating a learned grasp score or a confident detection as grounding. Neither carries a metric error bound, a wrench limit, or an evidence epoch, so neither can be admitted or expired.

A high-level task action like `Grasp(Mug, Handle)` specifies an operational objective, but its preconditions require rigorous physical verification. Perceptual noise and latency prevent open-loop execution from guaranteeing that the handle remains unobstructed or that the jaws are properly aligned. **Motion predicates** formalize these physical prerequisites by mapping abstract conditions to measurable geometric tolerances and wrench readings:

1.  *Pre-contact approach predicate:* ${P_{\text{approach}}}({\mathbf{T}_{\text{TCP}}}) \iff \|{\mathbf{p}_{\text{TCP}}} - {\mathbf{p}_{\text{pre}}}\| \le {r_{\text{tol}}} \land \arccos({\hat{\mathbf{z}}_{\text{TCP}}} \cdot {\hat{\mathbf{a}}_{\text{approach}}}) \le {\theta_{\text{tol}}}$, requiring the tool center point (TCP) frame ${\mathcal{F}_{\text{TCP}}}$, whose origin sits between the gripper fingertips, to align with the affordance approach axis ${\hat{\mathbf{a}}_{\text{approach}}}$ within spatial tolerance ${r_{\text{tol}}}$ and angular orientation tolerance ${\theta_{\text{tol}}}$.
2.  *Guarded contact predicate:* ${P_{\text{contact}}}({\mathbf{w}}) \iff {f_{\min}} \le {f_n} \le {f_{\max}}$, checking a normal squeeze band under the illustrated parallel-jaw model. Here the contact-force magnitude denotes the relevant normal squeeze at each finger, measured or conservatively inferred under qualified contact geometry; a generic force-vector norm or net wrench does not establish that quantity. The task profile sets the band from friction, payload load and acceleration, and crush limits. Passing it supports retention under those premises but does not establish object identity or task completion.
3.  *Spatial clearance predicate:* ${P_{\text{clear}}}({\mathbf{p}}, {\mathcal{M}}) \iff \min_{{\mathbf{o}} \in {\mathcal{M}}} \|{\mathbf{p}} - {\mathbf{o}}\| \ge {d_{\text{margin}}}$, evaluating candidate point ${\mathbf{p}}$ against obstacle geometry stored in the spatial memory map ${\mathcal{M}}$. This point clearance test serves as a fast preliminary check. Certifying the physical gripper, manipulator links, and their swept transit volumes requires a continuous path collision query during trajectory synthesis.

Together, the spatial and angular tolerances delineate an admissible **geometric goal set** ${\mathcal{G}_{\text{goal}}} \subset {SE(3)}$:

$$
{\mathcal{G}_{\text{goal}}} = \left\{ {\mathbf{T}} = ({\mathbf{R}}, {\mathbf{p}}) \in {SE(3)} \;\middle|\; \|{\mathbf{p}} - {\mathbf{p}_{\text{target}}}\|_{{\mathbf{E}}} \le 1, \; \angle({\mathbf{R}}, {\mathbf{R}_{\text{target}}}) \le {\theta_{\text{tol}}} \right\} \tag{1}
$$

 Contact and obstacle clearance predicates operate as distinct, phase-specific execution constraints outside this static pose volume. Within [[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/03 Running the Machine/10 Grounded Intent/V4-10.02 Grounded Affordances#^eq-intent-goal-set-se3|equation 1]], the weighted ellipsoidal norm $\|{\mathbf{p}} - {\mathbf{p}_{\text{target}}}\|_{{\mathbf{E}}} = \sqrt{\sum_{i \in \{{x},{y},{z}\}} \left(\frac{{p}_i - {p_{\text{target},i}}}{{r}_i}\right)^2}$ bounds spatial deviation within an ellipsoid whose principal semi-axes are ${\mathbf{r}} = ({r_x}, {r_y}, {r_z})$. Here ${r_{\text{tol}}}$ denotes the corresponding semi-axis ${r}_i$ along any individual coordinate direction. The rotation error metric $\angle({\mathbf{R}}, {\mathbf{R}_{\text{target}}}) = \arccos\left(\frac{\operatorname{tr}({\mathbf{R}}^\top {\mathbf{R}_{\text{target}}}) - 1}{2}\right)$ bounds geodesic orientation divergence on ${SO(3)}$, equivalent to $2 \arccos(|{\mathbf{q}_{\text{quat}}} \cdot {\mathbf{q}_{\text{quat,target}}}|)$ when unit quaternions represent orientations. Terminal grasp poses falling outside ${\mathcal{G}_{\text{goal}}}$ lead directly to task aborts or damage. Translational errors exceeding ${r}_i$ cause gripper fingers to impact the ceramic rim or close on empty space, while angular misalignments exceeding ${\theta_{\text{tol}}}$ jam the fingers against the handle opening, tripping joint torque protection or shattering the object.

^eq-intent-goal-set-se3

For the warehouse mug, the nominal task profile assigns all three ellipsoidal semi-axes to the 15 mm grasp tolerance. A terminal TCP offset of 10 mm along the ${x}$-axis yields a normalized ellipsoidal distance of 0.67, remaining well within the admissible goal set. Conversely, an offset of 20 mm yields a normalized distance of 1.33, falling outside the set and triggering immediate rejection. Checking angular alignment requires the angular tolerance ${\theta_{\text{tol}}}$ specified in the verified task profile, which the mug model registers during commissioning.

This goal set is an operational task specification rather than an estimate of target pose. The **task tolerance region** defines the maximum terminal error that the physical grasp can absorb; state estimator covariance captures uncertainty in the target’s actual pose. For the mug pick, the approved task profile permits at most 15 mm of translational discrepancy. The proposed affordance must therefore supply a conservative target error bound that fits inside this envelope, leaving sufficient headroom for the motion controller’s dynamic tracking error. Artificially expanding the task tolerance to accept an uncertain perception estimate corrupts the safety invariants of the physical task.

Generating candidate affordances prior to intent proposal packaging typically relies on a **learned 6-DoF grasp generator** applied to an incoming point cloud. Architectures such as Contact-GraspNet predict grasp poses anchored directly to surface points in cluttered point clouds ([[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/03 Running the Machine/10 Grounded Intent/V4-10.13 Source References#^ref-sundermeyer2021contactgraspnet|Sundermeyer et al. 2021]]). Frameworks like AnyGrasp predict dense ${SE(3)}$ grasp distributions with learned quality scores and track them across temporal observation sequences ([[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/03 Running the Machine/10 Grounded Intent/V4-10.13 Source References#^ref-fang2023anygrasp|Fang et al. 2023]]). The generator outputs a set of candidate grasp configurations paired with scalar confidence scores. However, a learned score merely reflects the network’s predictive confidence relative to its training distribution. It provides neither an analytic stability metric like the Ferrari-Canny radius nor a measured empirical grasp success rate for this specific gripper and workpiece geometry.

Candidate poses surviving initial filtering must satisfy rigorous downstream physical constraints that learned generators omit:

1.  *Kinematic feasibility*: The manipulator must possess a valid inverse kinematics (IK) solution that avoids joint position and velocity limits. A well-conditioned kinematic Jacobian does not guarantee clearance from mechanical joint stops, making explicit joint travel verification mandatory.
2.  *Geometric compatibility*: Gripper jaw stroke must exceed workpiece thickness at the grasp site with sufficient clearance margin.
3.  *Corridor clearance*: The approach trajectory must provide an unobstructed approach vector clear of surrounding bin walls and adjacent cargo.

When no candidate grasp satisfies these constraints, or when target identification remains unresolved across ambiguous visual candidates, the control pipeline must not execute an argmax guess. Instead, the proposal’s producer on the application processor issues an explicit **intent grounding refusal** (`ERR_GROUNDING_AMBIGUITY`), signaling a typed rejection of the goal itself. A planning failure means that the planner has not produced a collision-free path to a valid, well-formed goal. An intent grounding refusal halts execution earlier. It rejects an ungrounded or ambiguous goal before invoking the motion planner, which prevents unverified guesses from generating physical trajectories. When a symbolic task planner sits above the grasp generator, its discrete task operators must discharge these same geometric and contact predicates before any step of the plan is admitted.
