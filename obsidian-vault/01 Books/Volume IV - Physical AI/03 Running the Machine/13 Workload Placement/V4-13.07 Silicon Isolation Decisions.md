# V4-13.07 Silicon Isolation Decisions

[[Home|Home]] · [[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/Volume 4 Overview|Volume Overview]] · [[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/03 Running the Machine/13 Workload Placement/V4-13 Workload Placement|V4-13 Workload Placement]]

^sec-placement-isolation-decisions

Suppose a loaded test catches one of these couplings, a tick that exceeds the declared bound under proposal load. The team then has four remedies, each paid for in a different currency: static reservation, spatial partitioning, workload reduction, and physical relocation to separate silicon ([[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/03 Running the Machine/13 Workload Placement/V4-13.07 Silicon Isolation Decisions#^tbl-13-replacement-decisions|table 3]]). Each remedy targets a specific coupling layer and leaves a residual vulnerability; when that residual interference still exceeds the timing or fault-containment bound, the design must escalate to the next row, culminating in the mobile manipulator’s choice of physical relocation and the board-link transport budget it requires.

| Remediation Strategy     | Target Coupling Layer                                            | Implementation Mechanism                                                                         | Systems Engineering Trade-Off                                                                    | Residual Vulnerability                                                                      | When to Escalate                                                                                     |
|:-------------------------|:-----------------------------------------------------------------|:-------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------|:-------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------|:--------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------|:-----------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------|
| **Static Reservation**   | CPU execution time, OS scheduling preemption                     | Fixed-priority scheduling, ARINC 653 time slots, per-core memory-bandwidth regulation (MemGuard) | Capacity withheld from other work, including during quiescent states                             | Micro-architectural contention beneath OS visibility (shared L3, DRAM bank conflicts)       | Memory bus or cache stalls violate real-time deadlines despite $100\%$ CPU core allocation           |
| **Spatial Partitioning** | Cache capacity thrashing, DRAM bus contention                    | Cache coloring or way partitioning, DRAM bank partitioning (PALLOC), Arm MPAM, MPU/IOMMU domains | Structural fragmentation; smaller perception working set may increase off-chip traffic           | Unpartitionable DRAM controller queues, row-buffer locality reordering, PDN voltage droop   | Measured rail disturbances violate characterized limits, or DRAM queue stalls exceed the bound       |
| **Workload Reduction**   | Memory throughput saturation, peak thermal power                 | Model quantization (INT8/FP8), context downsampling, halved policy rate                          | Degraded policy tracking fidelity; increased control phase lag near dynamic stability boundaries | Common clock distribution trees, shared Power Management Units (PMU), unified reset domains | Model accuracy drops below operational limits, or shared clock/reset faults propagate across domains |
| **Physical Relocation**  | Shared power, clock, reset, or unbounded controller interference | Separate enforcer MCU with independently assessed power, clock, memory, and sensor/actuator path | More board area and an external communication budget                                             | Harness, transceiver, and common-supply faults remain                                       | Choose when the shared die cannot provide evidence for required timing and fault containment         |

Table 3: **Workload re-placement and isolation decision matrix**: Remediation pathways for when a test falsifies independence on shared silicon. Each remedy trades distinct engineering currencies (throughput, capacity, latency, board footprint) to eliminate contention channels before escalating to physically segregated silicon.

### Four Remedies for Shared-Silicon Contention

^four-remedies-for-shared-silicon-contention

A remedy that does not match the active coupling channel leaves the deadline overrun intact and strands compute capacity. Examples include reserving CPU cores when the stall lives in the DRAM controller queue, and coloring cache sets while miss status holding registers remain shared. A queueing bottleneck requires an enforced bound on service time, a cache capacity conflict requires physical separation of working sets, and a power-supply or clock disturbance requires an isolated fault domain. Trimming proposal demand can recover operating headroom, but only a bound on worst-case residual interference can support a safety claim.

Under **static reservation**,[[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/03 Running the Machine/13 Workload Placement/V4-13.14 Footnotes#^fn12|¹²]] physical workload placement stays the same while the critical path gets dedicated CPU time slices or interconnect slots. Reserving CPU time alone does not bound memory access latency; the interconnect needs its own service guarantee, such as periodic time-division multiple access (TDMA) slots with fixed transaction beat quotas. When hardware crossbars lack slot arbitration, system software must step in. **Memory-bandwidth regulation** gives each core an access budget per regulation epoch, counts memory transactions with hardware performance counters, and stalls any aggressor core with an interrupt once it spends its quota until the next epoch begins ([[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/03 Running the Machine/13 Workload Placement/V4-13.13 Source References#^ref-yun2013memguard|Yun et al. 2013]]). By capping how much traffic an aggressor can queue ahead of a critical transaction, bandwidth regulation provides a demand bound from which the controller model and service guarantee can bound the queueing multiplier ${r_{\text{slow}}}$ of [[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/03 Running the Machine/13 Workload Placement/V4-13.04 Contention for Shared Resources#^sec-placement-interference-mechanisms|section 13.3.2]]. Throttling reads alone is not enough; defending against writeback-buffer saturation requires charging both reads and dirty-line writebacks against the aggressor’s budget ([[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/03 Running the Machine/13 Workload Placement/V4-13.13 Source References#^ref-bechtel2019denial|Bechtel and Yun 2019]]). The predictable execution model takes this separation further on the real-time core. It splits each task into an initial memory phase that pulls its working set into private scratchpad memory, followed by an isolated compute phase that touches no shared interconnect resources ([[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/03 Running the Machine/13 Workload Placement/V4-13.13 Source References#^ref-pellizzoni2011predictable|Pellizzoni et al. 2011]]). That pattern matches the boundary contract of [[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/03 Running the Machine/13 Workload Placement/V4-13.06 The Boundary Contract#^sec-placement-boundary-contract|section 13.5]], which performs one bounded read followed by local computation. The cost is stranded capacity, because memory throughput stays reserved for the safety path even when the enforcer is idle. Had the warehouse mobile manipulator kept its permission path on the SoC, reserving memory-bus slots for sporadic 4 KB sensor reads would throttle policy weight streaming across the accelerator even with the robot base parked.

When contention comes from competing working sets thrashing shared caches or banks, **spatial partitioning** isolates cache ways or physical memory channels. For example, splitting an 8 MB last-level cache into 6 MB for the proposal policy and 2 MB for enforcer permission tables prevents cross-domain cache-line evictions, though shrinking the perception working set can increase off-chip memory traffic. Operating systems can enforce this split without special hardware through **cache coloring**: the page allocator assigns physical pages whose index bits map to disjoint cache sets ([[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/03 Running the Machine/13 Workload Placement/V4-13.13 Source References#^ref-mancuso2013realtime|Mancuso et al. 2013]]). Extending address-bit coloring to the bits that select DRAM banks gives each domain private banks, removing the inter-core row-buffer conflicts of [[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/03 Running the Machine/13 Workload Placement/V4-13.04 Contention for Shared Resources#^eq-placement-dram-queuing|equation 1]]. Arm’s Memory System Resource Partitioning and Monitoring (MPAM) extension puts these controls in hardware, combining cache-portion bitmaps across shared caches with memory-bandwidth throttling registers downstream of the last-level cache ([[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/03 Running the Machine/13 Workload Placement/V4-13.13 Source References#^ref-linux2026mpam|The Linux Kernel Documentation 2026]]). Even so, spatial cache partitioning does not separate the shared MSHRs or writeback queues ([[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/03 Running the Machine/13 Workload Placement/V4-13.04 Contention for Shared Resources#^sec-placement-interference-mechanisms|section 13.3.2]]). Spatial partitioning must therefore be paired with bandwidth regulation, and neither removes a shared DRAM controller queue. Giving the safety path its own DRAM channel and controller removes controller contention, at the cost of permanently taking that channel’s bandwidth away from the proposal accelerator.

A **static-partitioning hypervisor** combines these mechanisms across one SoC. At boot, it statically binds CPU cores, physical memory ranges, and memory-mapped peripherals to isolated guest partitions, runs without a dynamic runtime scheduler, and colors cache sets across partitions, as in microkernels such as Bao ([[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/03 Running the Machine/13 Workload Placement/V4-13.13 Source References#^ref-martins2020bao|Martins et al. 2020]]). This is the standard way to host a hard real-time safety enclave beside a Linux stack on one die. The scheduling model behind the asymmetry between proposal overruns and enforcer guarantees is **mixed-criticality scheduling** ([[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/03 Running the Machine/13 Workload Placement/V4-13.13 Source References#^ref-vestal2007preemptive|Vestal 2007]]; [[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/03 Running the Machine/13 Workload Placement/V4-13.13 Source References#^ref-burns2017survey|Burns and Davis 2017]]). In the standard mixed-criticality model, execution budgets depend on the assurance level: if a high-criticality task overruns its lower-assurance budget, the scheduler drops low-criticality work so the high-criticality task keeps its larger budget. On the running machine, the safety enforcer is the high-criticality task and the generative proposer is the low-criticality workload. The lease protocol enforces the same priority: a proposer that misses its inference window forfeits lease renewal, while qualified hardware isolation protects the enforcer’s periodic permission tick.

When an application can accept coarser tracking, **workload reduction** lowers memory traffic and heat through model quantization, smaller intermediate tensor transfers, pruned telemetry logging, or a lower policy rate. The trade-off is tracking fidelity. On the mobile manipulator, stretching the action-chunk period past the 60 ms lease lets leases expire between renewals; keeping continuous control authority requires chunk admissions above 16.7 Hz, even before renewal jitter is charged. Average producer throughput cannot verify this condition. Post-training quantization also requires fresh testing to confirm that manipulation accuracy stays within tolerance. Finally, lowering average power does not remove fast current steps (${\Delta} {I}$) or inductive voltage droop (${L_{\text{pkg}}} \cdot d{I}/d{t}$), which remain separate electrical qualification items.

When on-chip software and hardware controls cannot bound shared-die interference, **physical relocation** moves the permission path to a dedicated microcontroller with private SRAM, its own crystal oscillator, a separate reset domain, and an isolated power supply. Private TCM removes DRAM bus contention, while an isolated rail with hold-up capacitance keeps the safety monitor alive through host-side voltage collapse. Relocation does not remove verification work; it trades on-die contention analysis for board-level signal integrity, transceiver qualification, and common-cause electrical fault containment.

Moving the permission enforcer to separate silicon shifts the cost from on-chip arbitration to board-level link latency and PCB routing. The drive command bus delay (${T_{\text{bus}}}$) does not change because joint drives sit on an EtherCAT fieldbus regardless of compute placement. What relocation adds is proposal transport across an inter-chip link (a DMA-driven SPI link, an isolated PCIe Non-Transparent Bridge, or a differential serial bus) from the application processor to the safety microcontroller. While this board hop does not delay an enforcer tick running on already admitted evidence, it ages the observation behind every newly admitted plan and must be charged to the dispatch-to-admission interval ([[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/03 Running the Machine/13 Workload Placement/V4-13.07 Silicon Isolation Decisions#^sec-placement-dispatch-admission|section 13.6.2]]). Jitter in transport and admission also spends the 5 ms renewal-jitter margin that the lease rule reserves between the 50 ms chunk period and the 60 ms lease. Host-side memory contention spends the same margin: an illustrative 12.5 percent stretch of the inference uses up the entire jitter allowance by itself, leaving nothing for link delays or validation variance. Lease renewal must therefore be checked against measured end-to-end admission timestamps rather than upstream inference rates.

↩︎ The renewal-jitter margin and lease renewal rule are established in **[[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/01 Machine Anatomy/04 The Nervous System/V4-04.05 Chunk Lease Sizing from the Stopping Budget#^sec-nervous-lease-sizing|Chunk Lease Sizing from the Stopping Budget]]**.

Physical relocation also changes where sensors attach. On the running machine, joint encoders and the IMU wire directly to peripheral controllers on the safety microcontroller (hardware quadrature counters and synchronous SPI channels), keeping host-processor arbitration out of the real-time feedback loop. Driver latency and register-read jitter on those peripherals still need measurement. By contrast, high-bandwidth perception sensors feed the application processor directly: RGB cameras over MIPI CSI-2 lanes, and lidar packets over Ethernet. Those heavy streams fill perception buffers without crossing the microcontroller’s internal buses. Every placement choice thus reshapes the board, trading on-die memory arbitration for pin counts, differential trace routing, and serial link overhead.

### The Board-Link Budget and the Dispatch-To-Admission Interval

^sec-placement-dispatch-admission

That board-level serial hop and split validation pipeline age sensor observations while the base keeps rolling down the aisle, closing the interval left unallocated in **[[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/03 Running the Machine/08 Sensor Perception/V4-08.05 Bounding Observation Age#^sec-perception-latency-waterfall|The latency waterfall]]**. This span, from perception dispatching an observation frame to the safety enforcer admitting the resulting action chunk, is called the **dispatch-to-admission interval** ${\Delta_{\text{da}}}$ (the second row of **[[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/01 Machine Anatomy/04 The Nervous System/V4-04.05 Chunk Lease Sizing from the Stopping Budget#^tbl-04-event-timing|The capture-to-brake-onset timing budget of the running machine]]**). It covers the remaining proposer inference after dispatch, serialization across the board link, frame receipt and deserialization on the microcontroller, host geometry checks and bounded local admission checking, trajectory buffer activation, and the wait for the next permission tick. Because the stopping budget starts at sensor capture, every millisecond of ${\Delta_{\text{da}}}$ is base travel on aging evidence, paid for out of the stopping budget’s spare clearance.

↩︎ The composite stopping budget, which leaves the dispatch-to-admission interval out of the pre-brake delay and pays for it from the remaining clearance, is established in **[[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/03 Running the Machine/12 Safety Enforcement/V4-12.04 Stopping Envelopes#^sec-enforcement-composite-budget|Sizing the composite stopping budget]]**.

**Napkin Math 13.3: The board-link toll**

**Problem**: The machine’s permission path runs on a discrete microcontroller that receives each chunk over a board link. How long may the interval from perception dispatch to admission last before the finished stopping budget has nothing left at the aisle speed?

**Variables**:

- Chunk policy p99 inference 40 ms (Illustrative), charged in full after dispatch, which is conservative because its vision stage already lies in the dispatch age of **[[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/03 Running the Machine/08 Sensor Perception/V4-08.05 Bounding Observation Age#^sec-perception-latency-waterfall|The latency waterfall]]**.
- Serialized chunk ${D_{\text{vol,chunk}}} =$ 4,096 bytes on a ${f_{\text{link}}} =$ 25 MHz SPI link (Illustrative).
- Deserialization 2 ms, aggregate trajectory validation 18 ms, and activation 1 ms (Illustrative allowances), and up to one 1 ms permission tick of waiting (Chosen).
- At ${v} =$ 1.3 m/s the finished budget leaves ${d_{\text{spare}}} =$ 131.0 mm (Derived).

The validation allowance covers both the host geometry check and the MCU’s bounded local admission check, including their scheduling delays. The example does not allocate a separate duration to each. Their combined elapsed contribution must fit this allowance; qualification cannot omit host validation or count the entire allowance again on the MCU.

**Math**: Serialization takes $8\,{D_{\text{vol,chunk}}}/{f_{\text{link}}} =$ 1.31 ms. The allocation totals ${\Delta_{\text{da}}} =$ 40 ms $+$ 1.31 ms $+$ 2 ms $+$ 18 ms $+$ 1 ms $+$ 1 ms $=$ 63.3 ms, which the base covers as 82.3 mm of travel. The spare pays for at most ${\Delta_{\text{da,max}}} = {d_{\text{spare}}}/{v} =$ 131.0 mm $/$ 1.3 m/s $=$ 100.8 ms, so the allocation leaves 37.5 ms, or 48.7 mm of positive reserve. Doubling the chunk or halving the link clock adds only another 1.31 ms; adding one more inference’s worth of delay, 103.3 ms in all, exhausts the spare.

**Systems insight**: The board link that buys silicon separation costs about one millisecond, a small fraction of ${\Delta_{\text{da}}}$; proposer inference and aggregate trajectory validation dominate the interval. The stipulated discrete-placement allocation therefore fits the stopping budget, whereas a slower policy or a heavier validation kernel could exhaust it. The allocation is a target that qualification must confirm, not a relaxed runtime margin. The deterministic portion of ${\Delta_{\text{da}}}$ ages observation evidence and is charged here, while runtime variance between renewals drains the 5 ms renewal-jitter budget, which host-side memory contention alone can saturate. Because host inference and local validation overlap as a pipeline across consecutive 50 ms chunk periods, a 63.3 ms dispatch-to-admission interval does not expire the 60 ms lease as long as the time between consecutive admissions stays within the chunk period plus that renewal-jitter allowance.

With ${\Delta_{\text{da}}}$ formally allocated, the event timing table (**[[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/01 Machine Anatomy/04 The Nervous System/V4-04.05 Chunk Lease Sizing from the Stopping Budget#^tbl-04-event-timing|The capture-to-brake-onset timing budget of the running machine]]**) contains no undefined intervals. Total plan replacement latency from sensor capture to chunk admission (**[[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/03 Running the Machine/11 Trajectory Planning/V4-11.04 Replacement Timing and the Planning Horizon#^eq-plan-replacement-latency|equation 11.3]]**) is 114.9 ms, while total elapsed time from capture to emergency brake onset in the event of an unresponsive proposer is 196.9 ms. Both figures represent budget allocations that the loaded stress-testing campaign in [[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/03 Running the Machine/13 Workload Placement/V4-13.08 Empirical Verification of Silicon Isolation#^sec-placement-testing-separation|section 13.7]] must validate using physical hardware trace timestamps.

Allocating the interval also exposes interference that the discrete placement keeps inside the microcontroller. The link’s receive DMA engine and its completion interrupt, the fieldbus master dispatching motor drive frames, background local admission checking, and the periodic ingestion of evidence snapshots all contend for the microcontroller’s internal crossbar, interrupt controller, and memory banks. Every potential interference path demands an enforceable bound: assigning SPI and fieldbus interrupt priorities strictly below the deterministic permission tick, allocating dedicated DMA channels, and mapping safety state to independent tightly coupled memory banks. The loaded validation campaign in [[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/03 Running the Machine/13 Workload Placement/V4-13.08 Empirical Verification of Silicon Isolation#^sec-placement-testing-separation|section 13.7]] must saturate the communication link and execute maximum-length plan validation while simultaneously triggering the refusal branch. Classical response-time analysis for the periodic tick, with its higher-priority preemption and lower-priority blocking terms, provides the formal static counterpart to those empirical stress tests.

↪ Response-time analysis for a periodic tick, with its preemption and blocking terms, is developed in **[[05 Reference/Volume IV - Physical AI/V4 Systems and Hardware#^sec-appendix-systems-timers|Real-Time Timers, Watchdogs, and Schedulability]]**.

These remediation pathways determine which computing resources engineers must statically reserve or physically isolate. Two subsequent architectural decisions govern the remaining placement budget: whether any portion of proposal generation may be offloaded across an external network, and which candidate accelerator silicon completes autonomous missions within the chassis energy reserve. Off-board compute is first a timing question, and it comes first because energy efficiency matters only among architectures that already meet their deadlines.

### Off-Board Compute and Mission Energy

^off-board-compute-and-mission-energy

Querying a remote data center over factory Wi-Fi or private 5G subjects an offloaded policy to radio multipath fading, base-station handoffs, and packet retransmissions, yet the returned trajectory must still arrive inside the local 55 ms lease-renewal window. Off-board computation operates under the exact same timing windows as a local board interconnect, so an offloaded result must fit the target admission window before it can be considered for admission:

$$
{t_{\text{net}}^{\text{tail}}} + {t_{\text{proc}}^{\text{tail}}} \le {W}, \tag{6}
$$

 where ${W}$ is the allocation for the network-and-remote-processing interval of a particular transaction. The complete dispatch-to-admission interval must also charge host validation, local deserialization and admission, activation, and tick waiting against the 100.8 ms ceiling (notebook [13.3](https://mlsysbook.ai/vol4/13_placement/13_placement.html#nbk-placement-transport-budget)). A continuing pipeline must satisfy that evidence-age ceiling and the accepted-result gap required for lease renewal. The lease less renewal jitter (55 ms on the running machine) directly limits transaction latency only under a serial, non-overlapping convention; with overlapping requests, stage capacity and actual inter-admission gaps require separate checks. Qualification must measure the complete end-to-end tail at the required reliability level and retain the same evidence, state, and lease checks as local admission.

^eq-placement-offload-window

Empirical robotics deployments demonstrate how severely this constraint restricts offloading. In RT-2, large vision-language-action (VLA) models ran in a multi-TPU data center queried over wireless networks; under real network conditions, the 55-billion-parameter model executed at only 1 to 3 Hz, while the 5-billion-parameter model reached approximately 5 Hz ([[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/03 Running the Machine/13 Workload Placement/V4-13.13 Source References#^ref-brohan2023rt2|Brohan et al. 2023]]). Wireless links in industrial deployments (whether Wi-Fi or private 5G) introduce severe latency tails from radio-frequency multipath fading, base-station handoffs, and packet retransmissions. At 5 Hz, a fresh plan arrives only once every 200 ms, 3.6 times the renewal window, while the 55-billion-parameter model delivers outputs at best every 333.3 ms. Neither remote pipeline could sustain lease renewal on this machine. Because the published study documents overall throughput frequencies rather than an explicit breakdown between network latency and remote execution phases, one cannot determine whether a lower-latency wireless link could close the gap. Qualifying any remote lease-renewal pipeline requires profiling the full tail distributions of both components. Instead, large remote foundation models are best positioned to synthesize coarse semantic goals or task decompositions, leaving high-frequency action chunk generation to an onboard policy. Coarser planning workloads operating on second-scale horizons are better candidates for edge offloading. FogROS2, for example, offloads ROS 2 compute graphs to cloud and fog instances, reducing grasp-planning latency from 14 s on local edge silicon to 1.2 s ([[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/03 Running the Machine/13 Workload Placement/V4-13.13 Source References#^ref-ichnowski2023fogros2|Ichnowski et al. 2023]]). That acceleration speeds macro-task completion while leaving high-frequency microcontroller execution untouched. Packet loss or complete link disconnection must leave the onboard enforcer and its certified safe-stop routine fully operational. Neither remote offloading nor offline compilation eliminates the obligation to guarantee timely execution on local silicon.

Placement also decides which accelerator hosts the proposal path, and that choice reaches the permission path through energy as well as time. The chip’s power is the load term that set the junction temperature in [[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/03 Running the Machine/13 Workload Placement/V4-13.05 Thermal and Timing Coupling#^sec-placement-thermal-timing-coupling|section 13.4]], and it draws on the same battery whose sag the isolated rail must ride through. Among architectures that satisfy these timing bounds, candidate silicon must also complete the physical mission within the chassis energy reserve. Energy optimization often presents a subtle engineering trade-off. A lower-power edge accelerator draws fewer watts during active inference, yet if it extends execution latency, peripheral loads (lidar, depth cameras, cooling fans, and motor holding currents) remain energized longer. Conversely, a higher-throughput neural accelerator reduces mission energy only as long as faster compute actively shortens overall task duration. Once physical manipulator kinematics or vehicle velocity limits dominate the execution timeline, accelerating compute further merely burns excess power without saving task time, driving up total energy consumed per completed cycle.

For an assigned physical workload with steady-state compute power and baseline chassis power draw, total mission energy follows the relation:

$$
{E_{\text{mission}}} = ({P_{\text{chip}}} + {P_{\text{chassis\_static}}}) \times {T_{\text{mission}}}
$$

 Evaluating this expression requires an empirical or rigorously modeled mission duration ${T_{\text{mission}}}$ for each candidate silicon configuration. Transient motor acceleration spikes, DC-DC converter losses, task retries, and quiescent dwell intervals demand explicit energy accounting whenever they diverge across platforms. Systems engineers cannot rank candidate SoCs simply by comparing datasheet figures such as peak tera-operations per second (TOPS) or nominal thermal design power (TDP). The workload to measure is the machine’s own proposal pipeline, from camera ingestion through the chunk policy, and final silicon placement requires evaluating the compiled neural graph, its memory footprint, and the thermal assembly as one system. This chapter does not rank accelerators for the running machine, whose record carries no measured mission duration for any candidate; the relation states what such a ranking must measure. With reserved memory bandwidth, board-level transport latency, and battery energy draw accounted for in the allocation model, the next step is experimental verification. It tests on the physical hardware whether these isolation choices keep the permission tick within its bound and preserve braking response under worst-case concurrent load.
