# V4-13.06 The Boundary Contract

[[Home|Home]] · [[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/Volume 4 Overview|Volume Overview]] · [[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/03 Running the Machine/13 Workload Placement/V4-13 Workload Placement|V4-13 Workload Placement]]

^sec-placement-boundary-contract

Relocating the permission path isolates the execution domains on separate processors, but the two still have to talk. Consider an upstream proposal writer, such as the chunk policy on the application processor, that stalls or crashes halfway through publishing an action chunk. The downstream safety enforcer running on the real-time microcontroller still owes the physical plant a deterministic actuation decision on its very next tick. Partitioning the compute engines isolates them only if the protocol lets the real-time reader inspect and reject an incomplete or in-flight record without ever waiting for the producer. The boundary contract therefore ties memory consistency directly to the deadline of the sensing and constraint-checking pipeline, and it is the one piece of the design that every placement, discrete or shared, must get right.

### Immutable Record Mailboxes and Non-Blocking Synchronization

^immutable-record-mailboxes-and-non-blocking-synchronization

Passing raw pointers into an application processor’s working buffer or coordinating across a blocking lock lets a slow or crashed planner expose the real-time reader to torn data structures, stale cache lines, or unbounded waits. To prevent those stalls, the boundary contract demands an immutable, self-contained record whose publication integrity the safety enforcer can verify in constant time. The proposal header and seqlock mechanism fix the message structure and publication rules, and workload placement adds the requirement to verify on the hardware that this channel meets its execution budget even when the interconnect is saturated.

Across heterogeneous silicon or separate physical chips, communication splits into two stages: link transport across the board and local publication inside the microcontroller’s memory domain. An ingress peripheral or DMA engine receives the payload over a physical link (SPI, PCIe, or Ethernet) and stages it into local memory. Where the target allows peripheral access to tightly coupled memory, this may be the mailbox directly; otherwise a bounded local copy publishes the received record into the mailbox. Link transport and local publication are therefore two separate timing obligations, and the boundary protocol governs only the second.

Inside that mailbox, the receive handler publishes fixed-size records with the single-attempt seqlock from the Nervous System, which discards a torn or in-flight record for this tick without a retry. Placement adds two obligations that the protocol alone does not discharge. Memory attributes, barriers, and cache maintenance on the target must keep the local copy coherent, and the copy itself, at its largest payload, must fit inside the tick’s declared WCET.

↩︎ The single-attempt seqlock protocol, whose reader never spins or retries within a tick, is established in **[[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/01 Machine Anatomy/04 The Nervous System/V4-04.04 Multi-Rate Cadences#^sec-nervous-mailbox-exchanges|Lock-free and wait-free mailbox exchanges]]**.

Memory consistency shows only that a proposal arrived without tearing, not that the command is safe to execute. Once the record passes sequence validation, the admission pipeline checks its algorithmic certificates, dynamic feasibility, transport integrity, and lease validity. Rejecting a corrupted or late proposal neither renews an expiring lease nor revokes an active, previously vetted command early. The actuator keeps executing the current plan while that plan’s lease, kinematic bounds, and state checks hold. If the current plan expires or the machine leaves its safe operating bounds, the microcontroller invokes the fallback ladder immediately. Host-side operating-system stalls, neural accelerator throttling, and asynchronous logging queues can never backpressure or delay this local decision.

Every cross-boundary exchange therefore carries two separately measured costs: board-level physical transport before publication, followed by a single bounded local read during the enforcement tick. Qualification test suites must record payload sizes, cache and memory settings, and worst-case rejection paths. Even a real-time enforcer that reads without blocking remains vulnerable if its own execution stalls, so software decoupling must be paired with independent hardware supervision of processor liveness.

### Windowed Watchdog Timers and Execution Liveness

^windowed-watchdog-timers-and-execution-liveness

A standard countdown watchdog refreshed from a timer interrupt keeps getting fed after the control task has hung ([[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/03 Running the Machine/13 Workload Placement/V4-13.13 Source References#^ref-barr2013toyota|Barr 2013]]). A single late-only threshold cannot catch a corrupted program counter or a runaway phase-locked loop (PLL) clock that jumps into the watchdog service routine ahead of schedule. The single-attempt seqlock catches torn publication, and memory protection blocks cross-domain writes, but neither verifies that the safety enforcer software is alive and running its checks at the intended rate. The Nervous System feeds the microcontroller’s self-watchdog only when a tick finishes all of its checks, which catches a late or missing feed. On shared or heterogeneous silicon, the enforcer can fail in either direction: bus contention and memory starvation can stall its progress, while PLL clock glitches or control-flow corruption can make instructions run too early. Protecting the plant therefore requires a two-sided bound on watchdog servicing.

↩︎ The supervisory timers, from the chunk lease to the self-watchdog timeout, are established in **[[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/01 Machine Anatomy/04 The Nervous System/V4-04.05 Chunk Lease Sizing from the Stopping Budget#^sec-nervous-supervisory-timers|Supervisory timers and proposal wire contracts]]**.

High-integrity physical AI systems enforce this discipline with **windowed watchdog timers**. Unlike standard countdown timers that catch only late feeds, a windowed watchdog enforces both a lower threshold (the closed window, ${T_{\text{lower}}}$) and an upper threshold (the open window, ${T_{\text{upper}}}$), rejecting any refresh that arrives too early or too late:

1.  **Early-kick violation (${t_{\text{service}}} < {T_{\text{lower}}}$)**: If software refreshes the watchdog before the lower threshold has elapsed, the hardware watchdog controller flags an immediate fault and trips the emergency safe state. An early refresh signals disrupted control flow: a corrupted program counter may have skipped safety barrier checks to branch straight into the housekeeping routine, a stack overflow may have aborted intermediate kinematic checks, or a PLL multiplier glitch may have sped up the clock. Because a compromised execution path could still happen to feed the timer inside the valid window, primary hardware monitors (independent clock monitors and rail supervisors with isolated voltage references) must catch PLL runaways and supply dips first, leaving the windowed watchdog as a backstop.
2.  **Late-kick violation (${t_{\text{service}}} > {T_{\text{upper}}}$)**: If the timer counts past ${T_{\text{upper}}}$ without a valid refresh from the enforcer, the counter underflows and asserts an unmaskable hardware reset line. A late kick shows that the safety enforcer stalled, whether starved of bus cycles by neural accelerator traffic on a shared crossbar, deadlocked in an unhandled exception handler, or delayed by cache evictions.
3.  **Valid service window (${T_{\text{lower}}} \le {t_{\text{service}}} \le {T_{\text{upper}}}$)**: A refresh inside this interval confirms only that the service call arrived on time. It does not prove that the software ran every required check in the dispatch loop. Full diagnostic coverage combines windowed timing with challenge-response checks or independent hardware supervision.

If the controller feeds once after each successful tick, the watchdog measures the interval *between feeds*. Let completion offset ${c}_n$ from tick ${n}$ start lie in $[{c_{\min}},{c_{\max}}]$, and let ${J_{\text{phase}}}$ bound the deviation of the interval between consecutive tick starts from ${T_{\text{period}}}$. A conservative range for the inter-feed interval is given by [[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/03 Running the Machine/13 Workload Placement/V4-13.06 The Boundary Contract#^eq-placement-watchdog-window|equation 5]]:

$$
{T_{\text{feed}}}\in[{T_{\text{period}}}-({c_{\max}}-{c_{\min}})-{J_{\text{phase}}},\;{T_{\text{period}}}+({c_{\max}}-{c_{\min}})+{J_{\text{phase}}}] \tag{5}
$$

 This formulation captures the two extreme consecutive tick completions:

^eq-placement-watchdog-window

- *Shortest interval (late-to-early compression):* Tick ${n}$ finishes as late as possible (${c_{\max}}$), and Tick ${n}+1$ finishes as early as possible (${c_{\min}}$); the elapsed time between consecutive feeds is compressed to ${T_{\text{period}}} - ({c_{\max}} - {c_{\min}}) - {J_{\text{phase}}}$.
- *Longest interval (early-to-late expansion):* Tick ${n}$ finishes as early as possible (${c_{\min}}$), and Tick ${n}+1$ finishes as late as possible (${c_{\max}}$); the elapsed time between consecutive feeds expands to ${T_{\text{period}}} + ({c_{\max}} - {c_{\min}}) + {J_{\text{phase}}}$.

Engineers set ${T_{\text{lower}}}$ and ${T_{\text{upper}}}$ around this range once timing analysis and test evidence justify its bounds, while keeping the late-trip threshold inside the plant’s fault budget. A single tick’s nominal execution time is not enough to set a watchdog window. On the running machine, ${T_{\text{period}}}$ is the 1 ms tick, and the declared worst-case execution time of 250 μs gives the execution contribution to ${c_{\max}}$. The completion offset must also include the internal blocking and interference charged to the tick, so both offset bounds must use the same tick-start reference. Conversely, the nominal unloaded measurement of 135 μs cannot serve as ${c_{\min}}$; early-exit paths, short branches, or trivial constraint checks can finish much faster than nominal paths. The shortest tick-start-to-completion interval observed during a stress and fault-injection campaign gives evidence for ${c_{\min}}$, and a justified lower bound must also cover healthy paths the campaign did not exercise. Similarly, measured cycle-to-cycle period jitter under peak bus contention supports the bound ${J_{\text{phase}}}$, with allowances for unobserved conditions, rather than an idealized absolute phase offset. If a team has only absolute phase bounds, it must take the maximum difference between consecutive phase offsets before using this expression.

The two ends of the window are set by different arguments. The lower end of [[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/03 Running the Machine/13 Workload Placement/V4-13.06 The Boundary Contract#^eq-placement-watchdog-window|equation 5]] sets ${T_{\text{lower}}}$. Once ${c_{\min}}$ and ${J_{\text{phase}}}$ have justified bounds, supported by a campaign and allowances for unobserved healthy paths, any feed earlier than the resulting lower bound lies outside the qualified healthy range. This gives the machine’s self-watchdog, which has only a late trip, the early trip it lacks. The upper end is not used as ${T_{\text{upper}}}$. It marks the latest a healthy tick can feed, so ${T_{\text{upper}}}$ must sit at or beyond it. How far beyond is a separate choice of fault-detection latency, judged against what the plant can tolerate while a dead enforcer goes undetected. The machine keeps the selected 5 ms timeout, several ticks beyond the window, so that transient delays do not trigger spurious resets; problem 5 converts that timeout into base travel. The late trip also answers a different question from the falsification threshold. A tick that runs past the declared WCET but still feeds within the window refutes the placement’s timing premise, and the loaded campaign, not the watchdog, must catch it. The watchdog’s upper end catches only an enforcer that has stopped feeding altogether.

Setting a multi-tick timeout is an engineered fault-detection latency, not proof that missing several control cycles is harmless. If the microcontroller crashes or resets, it cannot run MCU-controlled powered deceleration. A drive-autonomous SS1 remains available only if its independent trigger, drive controller, supply, and drive-energy and regenerative-sink paths survive the fault and are qualified for that response. Otherwise, the platform falls back to its separately qualified passive braking, friction deceleration, or hardwired mechanical interlocks. Total fault-handling latency (watchdog trip delay, supervisory reset propagation, and brake engagement) must therefore fit inside the plant’s certified safety-stop budget. Engineers cannot justify a slow watchdog timeout by citing powered braking distances when the fault being detected disables the processor that commands those brakes.

To catch control-flow corruption where a broken loop still passes through the watchdog service call inside the valid time window, high-integrity designs add **challenge-response checkpointing**. An external hardware supervisor or isolated safety coprocessor sends pseudo-random seeds to the enforcer at runtime. Instead of writing a fixed value to a register, the enforcer must return a signature computed from fresh sensor frame hashes, constraint-barrier checks, and intermediate trajectory stages. Diagnostic coverage depends on whether a corrupted execution path can replay or compute a valid signature by chance. If the enforcer fails the challenge or misses the window, the external supervisor asserts a hardware torque-inhibit line (such as Safe Torque Off on an electric drive). Direct torque removal stops actuators from driving into hazards, but it is not a controlled powered stop; the resulting passive coast-down or mechanical braking profile needs its own qualification and safety-case evidence.

Both the boundary contract and its hardware supervision apply to every workload placement. Whether the learned policy and the real-time safety enforcer sit on separate boards, separate chiplets, or partitioned cores on one SoC, the design must pass records without blocking and verify liveness in hardware. What changes from one placement to another is how much cross-domain interference the tick must absorb, and every mechanism that bounds that interference carries its own price in silicon area, bus bandwidth, and board complexity.
