# V4-06.02 Dual Failure Modes of Policy Training

[[Home|Home]] · [[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/Volume 4 Overview|Volume Overview]] · [[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/02 Teaching the Machine/06 Policy Training/V4-06 Policy Training|V4-06 Policy Training]]

^sec-training-policy-synthesis

A robot arm executing a learned trajectory can track every waypoint cleanly across hundreds of simulation episodes and still arrive at its first physical deployment with no evidence about whether it will fracture a latch. In classical robotics, control design begins with an explicit analytical model of the plant: engineers write down kinematic equations, inertia matrices, stability margins, and torque limits where they can be systematically inspected and verified. A learned neural policy, in contrast, derives its behavior entirely from its training distribution, setting network weights from empirical experience to map physical observations to proposed control actions. That experience may arrive as teleoperated demonstrations, recorded operational logs, rollouts inside a parallel physics simulator, trial-and-error interactions on physical hardware, or pretrained representations imported from foundation models. Although empirical data makes complex manipulation tractable, it also embeds unstated dynamic assumptions directly into the model weights, leaving no explicit record of which operating regimes the policy can safely navigate.

The central engineering question in embodied policy training is therefore not which regime achieves the highest optimization score, but which physical behaviors the training experience supports, and at what operational cost. Four quantities decide the answer. The first is coverage, because a policy is supported only in the states its experience actually visited, which is why the provenance record of **[[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/02 Teaching the Machine/05 Physical Data/V4-05.12 The Dataset Schema#^sec-data-schema|The Dataset Schema]]** lists its known absences beside its demonstrated cells. The second is inference latency, because a policy that evaluates too slowly misses its control deadline, however good its proposed trajectories are. The third is hardware cost, since experience gathered on the machine consumes machine hours, heats motor windings, and wears drivetrains. The fourth is fidelity, because synthetic experience from simulation scales across parallel accelerators but is only as faithful as the integrator and contact solver that produced it. A checkpoint’s policy manifest ([[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/02 Teaching the Machine/06 Policy Training/V4-06.09 The Policy Manifest#^sec-training-policy-manifest|section 6.8]]) records all four, and its declared operational design domain (ODD) is the part of the task’s scenario table that the checkpoint’s evidence supports. Two candidate latch policies show why a clean training curve does not establish physical viability.

Consider the cage-door latch task introduced in **[[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/02 Teaching the Machine/05 Physical Data/V4-05.02 Endogenous Experience#^sec-data-endogenous-experience|Endogenous Experience]]**, where two learned controllers both succeed under nominal warehouse conditions yet each carries a hidden dependency that physical hardware can expose, for different mechanical reasons. Both controllers share the same real-time execution model: Action Chunking with Transformers (ACT) ([[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/02 Teaching the Machine/06 Policy Training/V4-06.13 Source References#^ref-zhao2023learning|Zhao et al. 2023]]), a chunk-policy architecture that generates open-loop action sequences in a single decoder pass. The first candidate, `latch-bc-01`, was trained via supervised behavioral cloning on teleoperated door-opening demonstrations. The second candidate, `latch-sim-02`, is a distilled student policy. A reinforcement learning teacher was first trained inside a parallel physics simulator with full access to privileged state variables. Then `latch-sim-02`, initialized from `latch-bc-01`, learned through distillation to replicate the teacher’s actions using only onboard sensor streams ([[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/02 Teaching the Machine/06 Policy Training/V4-06.06 Physical Channels of the Sim-To-Real Gap#^sec-training-privileged-distillation|section 6.5.1]]).

Both candidates inherit the absence list of the baseline archive. Its demonstrations characterize only the nominal strike plate at a guarded approach velocity, so a displaced plate and a faster approach are both recorded as known absences, and each candidate meets one of them. While behavioral cloning must interpolate between these discrete demonstrations to form coherent trajectory proposals, interactive learning in simulation can explore novel state-action pairs. However, the validity of that synthetic exploration depends entirely on how accurately the simulated mechanics mirror the physical plant. Selecting a training regime is therefore an architectural decision that dictates which unverified assumptions must be empirically audited before a checkpoint is permitted to command actuators.

In this illustrative comparison, at the nominal strike plate and the guarded approach speed of 0.03 m/s, both policies open the door. Their failure modes diverge once physical deployment departs from that single nominal operating point:

1.  **Missing recovery experience**: When a sagging door places the strike plate off nominal, `latch-bc-01` encounters observations outside its training distribution. Because behavioral cloning minimizes supervised prediction error across the recorded demonstrations, the training loss supplies no direct supervision at unvisited recovery states, although updates to shared weights can still change the actions predicted there. When confronted with unfamiliar observations, the network still emits action setpoints, but those ungrounded commands drive the manipulator further off course. Trajectory errors compound at each 25 ms setpoint tick until the gripper binds against the strike plate’s chamfered edge. The root bottleneck is the absence of corrective recovery trajectories in the training distribution, rather than model capacity.
2.  **Misleading simulated contact**: In contrast, `latch-sim-02` is at risk on the nominal strike plate itself. Its simulator models contact dynamics using a compliant penalty spring[[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/02 Teaching the Machine/06 Policy Training/V4-06.14 Footnotes#^fn1|¹]] (${f_{k, \perp}} = {k_{\text{pen}}} {\delta} + {c} {\dot{\delta}}$), where surface penetration ${\delta}$ and penetration rate ${\dot{\delta}}$ govern the contact force. Because this numerical spring yields far more compliance than a physical steel plate, the simulated impact at 0.10 m/s peaks well under the latch’s 100 N limit. The teacher’s training reward paid for opening the door quickly and carried no term for contact force, so nothing in training charged for an aggressive approach, and the optimizer exploited the contact model to shave cycle time off each episode. The reinforcement learning teacher converges on an aggressive plunge, and policy distillation faithfully transfers that shortcut into the student. Whether the plunge breaks the real latch depends on how stiff the arm side of the contact is, a quantity nothing in training measured.

A contact-force budget demonstrates why the simulated benchmark score cannot be trusted on hardware. In the rigid-arm, constant-drive estimate, the physical strike plate’s stiffness sets the force rise. At the unbraked approach velocity of 0.10 m/s, force reaches the 100 N limit in 2.5 ms after touch. The tripwire fires at 0.375 ms; the subsequent 2 ms response places brake onset at 2.375 ms, leaving only 0.125 ms before the constant-drive ramp reaches the limit. That interval is not a stopping certificate. [[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/02 Teaching the Machine/06 Policy Training/V4-06.05 The Simulator as a System Component#^sec-training-contact-exploit|Section 6.4.2]] derives the timeline alongside the passive impact forces; sanctioning the fast plunge would require physical deceleration evidence that this simulation-only training process cannot establish.

Both failures expose dependencies hidden from validation loss and episodic reward: behavioral cloning depends on demonstrated recovery distributions, while simulation-to-real transfer depends on faithful contact and stiffness dynamics. Selecting a policy training regime therefore requires matching the physical properties of the training source to the mechanical demands of the operational task. Whichever regime produced it, a learned checkpoint sits above the proposal boundary as an unprivileged proposal generator. Safe and reliable manipulation requires action proposals that respect the machine’s actuator dynamics, contact deadlines, and physical safety envelopes as rigorously as they pursue the nominal task objective. Comparing the five regimes that can generate those proposals clarifies what each source of experience buys and what it leaves unverified.
