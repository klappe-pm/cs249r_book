# V4-06.04 Reinforcement Learning and Environment Interaction

[[Home|Home]] · [[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/Volume 4 Overview|Volume Overview]] · [[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/02 Teaching the Machine/06 Policy Training/V4-06 Policy Training|V4-06 Policy Training]]

^sec-training-learning-by-trial

When an archive lacks recovery demonstrations for a displaced strike plate, letting the robot discover a recovery on its own by trial and error means paying for every failed attempt in motor winding heat (${E_{\text{thermal}}} = \int {i_q}({t})^2 {R}\,d{t}$), gearbox fatigue reversals, jammed latch cams, and human reset labor. Yet when expert corrections are unavailable or a task demands strategies no demonstrator can supply, closed-loop trial is the only way to generate experience in the states the learner’s own actions reach. By executing actions on the physical system and observing the resulting state transitions, the policy experiences the consequences of its own exploratory deviations. In online policy search,[[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/02 Teaching the Machine/06 Policy Training/V4-06.14 Footnotes#^fn7|⁷]] the agent updates its parameter weights from the scalar advantage estimates accumulated across its own rollouts:

$$
\nabla_{\theta} {J}({\theta}) = \mathbb{E}_{{s_{0:T}}, {\mathbf{a}_{0:T}} \sim {p_\theta}} \left[ \sum_{t=0}^{T} \nabla_{\theta} \ln {\pi_\theta}({\mathbf{a}}_{t} \mid {s}_{t}) {\hat{A}}_t \right],
$$

 where ${\hat{A}}_t$, a scalar and not the bold action chunk ${\mathbf{A}}_t$ of [[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/02 Teaching the Machine/06 Policy Training/V4-06.03 Policy Training Regimes#^sec-training-action-chunking|section 6.2.5]], estimates the advantage ${Q^{\pi}}({s}_{t}, {\mathbf{a}}_{t}) - {V^{\pi}}({s}_{t})$ introduced in [[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/02 Teaching the Machine/06 Policy Training/V4-06.03 Policy Training Regimes#^sec-training-rl-primer|section 6.2.1]]. It quantifies whether an exploratory action yielded higher or lower return than the baseline policy expected. Because transitions are drawn from the state occupancy distribution ${d_\pi}({s})$ induced by the currently deployed policy, closed-loop execution directly exposes the agent to compounding drift and provides immediate evaluative feedback on attempted recoveries. A positive advantage (${\hat{A}}_t > 0$) reinforces the exploratory action relative to the baseline, whereas a negative advantage (${\hat{A}}_t < 0$) penalizes it. For this feedback to drive convergence, the reward formulation and sensory observations must make the underlying physical failure modes visible. While learning on policy-generated states resolves the covariate shift between expert demonstrators and the learner, it does not immunize the system against distribution shift caused by novel disturbances, unmodeled environment changes, or drifting hardware dynamics.

Because each physical rollout dissipates heat in motor windings, cycles actuator gearboxes, and consumes unrecoverable calendar time during resets, an engineering budget for physical training must treat thermal ceilings, mechanical wear, and operational logistics as first-order constraints alongside GPU compute. An algorithm that requires fewer physical interactions is almost always the better choice on hardware, even if each parameter update demands far more computation.

Closed-loop exploration on hardware test benches is billed primarily in calendar time. For an experimental campaign of ${N}$ trials, where each rollout runs for duration ${t_{\text{rollout}}}$ and requires a reset duration ${t_{\text{reset}}}$, distributed across ${N_{\text{rig}}}$ parallel rigs, the total wall-clock duration is

$$
{T_{\text{wall}}} = \frac{{N}\,({t_{\text{rollout}}} + {t_{\text{reset}}})}{{N_{\text{rig}}}}. \tag{5}
$$

 Consider the door-opening benchmark, where the arm regulates contact forces over an active horizon of ${t_{\text{rollout}}}$ = 8 s (the autonomous contact phase of an opening rather than a full teleoperated episode). Training vision-based policies from scratch with reinforcement learning typically demands on the order of $10^6$ trials; for instance, QT-Opt learned closed-loop grasping from over 580,000 real grasp attempts ([[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/02 Teaching the Machine/06 Policy Training/V4-06.13 Source References#^ref-kalashnikov2018qtopt|Kalashnikov et al. 2018]]). A baseline scenario of ${N} = 10^6$ exposes the logistical bottleneck. Between successive trials, an automated fixture, faster than the re-latch fixture used during demonstration collection, must re-latch the mechanism and return the arm to its initial configuration. However, when exploratory trajectories jam the latch cam or trigger joint overcurrent trips, automated reset sequences stall. A human technician must intervene to dislodge the latch, re-align the door, and re-home the joint encoders. In addition, each opening cycles every joint drivetrain through several bidirectional load reversals.

↩︎ The fixture re-latch that paces demonstration collection, with the yield and component wear it costs, is established in **[[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/02 Teaching the Machine/05 Physical Data/V4-05.07 Teleoperation Demonstration Costs#^sec-data-yield-fatigue-budgeting|Operational yield and mechanical fatigue budgeting]]**.

**Napkin Math 6.2: The reset wall**

**Problem**: A from-scratch reinforcement learning campaign of ${N} = 10^6$ latch openings runs on the machine. How long does it take with automated and with manual resets, what does a farm of rigs buy, and what does the drivetrain absorb regardless of the rig count?

**Variables**:

- Trials ${N} =$ 1.0 × 10⁶ (illustrative order of magnitude for learning from scratch)
- Rollout ${t_{\text{rollout}}} =$ 8 s (illustrative contact phase of one opening)
- Resets ${t_{\text{reset,auto}}} =$ 4 s and ${t_{\text{reset,manual}}} =$ 30 s (illustrative)
- Rigs ${N_{\text{rig}}} = 1$, or 14, the largest simultaneous arm count of [[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/02 Teaching the Machine/06 Policy Training/V4-06.04 Reinforcement Learning and Environment Interaction#^fig-06-robot-arm-farm|figure 4]] (measured)
- Load reversals per opening ${n_{\text{rev}}} =$ 4 (illustrative)

**Math**:

1.  **Execution only** ([[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/02 Teaching the Machine/06 Policy Training/V4-06.04 Reinforcement Learning and Environment Interaction#^eq-training-trial-wall-clock|equation 5]] with no reset): 1.0 × 10⁶ $\times$ 8 s $=$ 8.0 × 10⁶ s, or 92.6 days of round-the-clock operation.
2.  **Automated resets**: 1.0 × 10⁶ $\times$ (8 s $+$ 4 s) $=$ 1.2 × 10⁷ s, or 138.9 days.
3.  **Manual resets**: 1.0 × 10⁶ $\times$ (8 s $+$ 30 s) $=$ 3.8 × 10⁷ s, or 439.8 days; the reset is 78.9 percent of every trial.
4.  **Farm**: 439.8 days $/$ 14 $=$ 31.4 days, about a month.
5.  **Wear**: 1.0 × 10⁶ $\times$ 4 $=$ 4.0 × 10⁶ drivetrain load reversals, for any ${N_{\text{rig}}}$.

**Result**: Manual resets extend the campaign to more than a year on a single machine, and even a 14-rig farm requires an entire month of continuous operation.

**Systems insight**: Reset overhead dominates active execution time, and scaling out hardware rigs partitions the calendar without mitigating cumulative mechanical wear. Only reducing the interaction count ${N}$ relieves both calendar duration and physical fatigue. Consequently, algorithmic sample efficiency, rather than hardware parallelism, determines whether on-machine reinforcement learning is economically viable.

Adding parallel rigs scales floor space, capital cost, electrical power infrastructure, and supervisory labor in direct proportion to ${N_{\text{rig}}}$. A grasp-learning farm shows what that scale looks like ([[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/02 Teaching the Machine/06 Policy Training/V4-06.04 Reinforcement Learning and Environment Interaction#^fig-06-robot-arm-farm|figure 4]]): up to 14 arms, each with its own gripper, bin, and camera, ran day and night, and the cluster collected over 800,000 grasp attempts over two months. The farm was not running reinforcement learning. It used self-supervised grasp outcomes to train an offline success predictor, which an external servo loop queried to guide real-time grasping motions ([[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/02 Teaching the Machine/06 Policy Training/V4-06.13 Source References#^ref-levine2018learning|Levine et al. 2018]]). That is why it serves here as a picture of scale rather than of an algorithm. Whatever the learner, collecting close to $10^6$ physical trials takes a room of arms running for months and pays the wear that comes with it. QT-Opt, which did learn its grasping by reinforcement learning, paid the same price for its 580,000 attempts.

Mechanical component wear often becomes the binding constraint long before calendar limits are reached. The cumulative 4.0 × 10⁶ load reversals demanded by a from-scratch campaign must be absorbed by actuator drivetrains regardless of how many rigs divide the trials.[[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/02 Teaching the Machine/06 Policy Training/V4-06.14 Footnotes#^fn8|⁸]] Those same million contact events progressively gall the latch cam and strike plate, wear out compliant elastomer gripper pads, and cycle internal wiring harnesses toward fatigue failure.

In purely numerical simulation, an exploratory branch that destabilizes costs nothing more than a discarded buffer and an environment reset. On physical hardware, exploration answers to the first law, irreversibility (principle [[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/01 Machine Anatomy/V4 The Machine Anatomy#^pri-vol4-irreversibility|1]]), because a trial that goes wrong delivers its energy before any gradient step can take it back. Unconstrained exploratory torque commands can slam mechanisms into rigid fixtures, strip the precision teeth of harmonic drives, and heat motor stators beyond their 155 °C Class F insulation limit.

↩︎ The Class F winding limit and the insulation breakdown it guards against are established in **[[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/01 Machine Anatomy/02 The Physical Body/V4-02.06 Thermal Duty Cycles#^sec-body-thermal-capacity|Thermal capacity and insulation breakdown thresholds]]**.

Physical degradation systematically alters the plant throughout the training campaign. Standard Markov Decision Process formulations assume that the transition probability kernel ${p}({s}_{t+1} \mid {s}_{t}, {\mathbf{a}}_{t})$ remains strictly stationary. On physical hardware, this assumption fails. Repetitive impacts burnish contact surfaces, backlash in precision gearboxes expands, and structural fasteners loosen under continuous cyclic vibration. Consequently, the transition dynamics late in a campaign, ${p_{n \gg 1}}({s}_{t+1} \mid {s}_{t}, {\mathbf{a}}_{t})$, differ measurably from the pristine dynamics measured at trial ${n} = 1$. The physical plant mutates under the mechanical work of collecting its own training data, forcing the optimizer to track a drifting target.

Reward formulations can also inadvertently incentivize destructive hardware behaviors. In trial-based policy search, the scalar reward ${r}({s}, {\mathbf{a}})$ defines the optimization objective, while the action parameterization, lower-level controllers, and independent hardware interlocks define the admissible operating space. If neither the reward nor those controllers account for contact forces, torque slew rates, or thermal buildup, the policy optimizer will exploit those unpenalized degrees of freedom to maximize task return. Engineers mitigate these tendencies by penalizing excessive effort, jerk, and temperature:

$$
{r_{\text{total}}}({s}, {\mathbf{a}}) = {r_{\text{task}}}({s}, {\mathbf{a}}) - {\lambda_{\tau}} \|{\boldsymbol{\tau}}_t\|_2^2 - {\lambda_{\dot{\tau}}} \left\| \frac{{\boldsymbol{\tau}}_t - {\boldsymbol{\tau}}_{t-1}}{{\Delta} {t}} \right\|_2^2 - {\lambda_T} \max\left(0, {T_{\text{winding}}} - {T_{\text{limit}}}\right).
$$

 For example, an unregularized tracking reward often encourages the policy to bang current limits back and forth at high frequency, exciting mechanical resonances and generating severe thermal dissipation. Similarly, an unpenalized contact policy may brace rigidly against a mechanical stop to artificially constrain kinematic degrees of freedom. The penalty weights above (${\lambda_{\tau}}, {\lambda_{\dot{\tau}}}, {\lambda_T}$) trade task progress against effort, torque slew, and thermal rise. Soft reward penalties cannot guarantee safety, however. Hard physical stops and active current limits in the motor drive firmware must enforce strict operating envelopes. Practical on-machine training must also cut the million-trial budget by three orders of magnitude before wear and reset labor exhaust the workcell.

### Demonstration-Seeded Reinforcement Learning on the Machine

^sec-training-real-world-rl

Instead of spending 439.8 days of single-machine hardware wear on random exploration, human-in-the-loop sample-efficient reinforcement learning (HIL-SERL) achieves high success rates across precision assembly, dynamic manipulation, and dual-arm tasks after only 1 h to 2.5 h of physical training per task ([[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/02 Teaching the Machine/06 Policy Training/V4-06.13 Source References#^ref-luo2025hilserl|Luo et al. 2025]]). Seeding exploration with prior demonstrations circumvents the million-trial reset wall by trading raw trial count for dependencies on initial demonstration support, human intervention gating, and reward-classifier accuracy. Four mechanisms compress the required trial count ${N}$. First, demonstration seeding initializes the replay buffer with 20 to 30 teleoperated trajectories, ensuring that early gradient updates begin within competent task regimes. Second, aggressive off-policy data reuse, implemented via Reinforcement Learning with Prior Data (RLPD) ([[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/02 Teaching the Machine/06 Policy Training/V4-06.13 Source References#^ref-ball2023rlpd|Ball et al. 2023]]), replays each recorded transition across multiple gradient updates rather than limiting reuse to a recently collected rollout batch. Third, human-gated interventions truncate failing episodes; an operator takes over when the policy destabilizes, routing the corrective maneuver directly into the replay buffer as in human-gated DAgger ([[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/02 Teaching the Machine/06 Policy Training/V4-06.13 Source References#^ref-kelly2019hgdagger|Kelly et al. 2019]]). Finally, an autonomous binary success classifier trained on labeled visual examples replaces brittle, hand-engineered reward instruments.

At the latch task’s per-trial duration with automated fixture resets, the 2.5 h upper ceiling permits at most 750 physical rollouts. Under these latch timing assumptions, that budget contains a factor of 1,300 fewer trials than the illustrative from-scratch campaign, with 3,000 load reversals rather than 4.0 × 10⁶, shifting the operational burden of [[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/02 Teaching the Machine/06 Policy Training/V4-06.04 Reinforcement Learning and Environment Interaction#^eq-training-trial-wall-clock|equation 5]] from months to hours. However, each sample-efficiency mechanism introduces assumptions that must be explicitly tracked in the policy manifest ([[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/02 Teaching the Machine/06 Policy Training/V4-06.09 The Policy Manifest#^sec-training-policy-manifest|section 6.8]]). Demonstrations constrain the policy only within the support of the expert’s initial distribution. Human interventions generate authority-record transitions whose pre-takeover states serve as negative failure indicators rather than positive imitation targets. The success classifier acts as a learned perceptual sensor, and the policy optimizer will exploit its systematic false positives, such as mistaking a partially jammed cam for an open door.

↪ The authority record, in which the permission path logs every takeover and barrier trip, and from which intervention episodes are curated, is developed in **[[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/04 Governing the Machine/14 Supervisory Intervention/V4-14.09 The Authority Record#^sec-intervention-authority-log|The Authority Record]]**.

Even with demonstration seeding, direct on-hardware reinforcement learning fits only tasks whose interaction budget stays within the physical plant’s thermal endurance, fatigue life, and available calendar time. When broader exploration is needed and narrowing the task or adding stronger priors is insufficient, engineers offload rollouts to simulation. Moving exploration into a virtual workcell removes hardware wear and reset latency during training, but makes the learned policy vulnerable to every unmodeled dynamic, softened contact constraint, and missing transport delay in the simulator.
