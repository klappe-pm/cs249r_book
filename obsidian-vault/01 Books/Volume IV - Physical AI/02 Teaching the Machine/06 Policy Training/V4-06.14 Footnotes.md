# V4-06.14 Footnotes

[[Home|Home]] · [[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/Volume 4 Overview|Volume Overview]] · [[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/02 Teaching the Machine/06 Policy Training/V4-06 Policy Training|V4-06 Policy Training]]

^footnotes

<sup>1</sup> **Kelvin-Voigt Contact**: A linear spring and damper in parallel produce a force jump at first touch, because the damping term acts on the full approach velocity at zero penetration, and a pulling, “sticky” force as the bodies separate, because the damping term then outweighs the vanishing spring term. Hunt and Crossley ([[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/02 Teaching the Machine/06 Policy Training/V4-06.13 Source References#^ref-hunt1975coefficient|1975]]) scaled the damping with penetration to remove both artifacts. A policy trained against the linear model therefore meets its least faithful forces at contact onset and at release, the two events a latch opening depends on.

^fn1

<sup>2</sup> **Markov Decision Process**: Bellman ([[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/02 Teaching the Machine/06 Policy Training/V4-06.13 Source References#^ref-bellman1957dynamic|1957]]) formulated sequential decisions under uncertainty with the dynamic-programming recursion that [[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/02 Teaching the Machine/06 Policy Training/V4-06.03 Policy Training Regimes#^eq-training-bellman|equation 2]] restates, and Howard ([[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/02 Teaching the Machine/06 Policy Training/V4-06.13 Source References#^ref-howard1960dynamic|1960]]) supplied policy iteration to solve them. The formalism came from operations research and optimal control decades before reinforcement learning adopted it, which is why the cost-to-go translation below is an identity rather than an analogy, and why a control engineer’s tools for that recursion apply to a learned policy’s value function.

^fn2

<sup>3</sup> **Jensen’s Inequality**: The averaged action fails even where it happens to be admissible, because a nonlinear plant does not map the mean action to the mean outcome. In general $\mathbb{E}[{g}({\mathbf{a}})] \ne {g}(\mathbb{E}[{\mathbf{a}}])$, and for a convex ${g}$ Jensen’s inequality fixes the sign of the gap. Contact and friction are the most nonlinear parts of a manipulator’s plant, so a regression mean that looks like a compromise between two good demonstrations can produce a contact state that neither demonstration visited.

^fn3

<sup>4</sup> **Mixture Density Network**: Bishop ([[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/02 Teaching the Machine/06 Policy Training/V4-06.13 Source References#^ref-bishop1994mixture|1994]]) proposed it for inverse problems, whose inputs admit several valid outputs, and demonstrated it on the inverse kinematics of a robot arm, where one hand position has two joint solutions and least-squares regression returns their invalid average. The mobile manipulator’s two routes around the column are the same problem, met three decades later in action space, which is why the remedy is still a change of output distribution rather than a larger network.

^fn4

<sup>5</sup> **Huber Loss**: Huber ([[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/02 Teaching the Machine/06 Policy Training/V4-06.13 Source References#^ref-huber1964robust|1964]]) introduced it as an estimator of location that tolerates a small fraction of grossly wrong observations, quadratic near the center and linear in the tails, and showed that it minimizes the worst-case variance over such contaminated distributions. Teleoperation tremor and dropouts are exactly that contamination, and ${\delta}$ sets, in the declared normalized action coordinates, where an error stops counting as noise and starts counting as a gross error.

^fn5

<sup>6</sup> **Scope of the CQL Bound**: Under its data-coverage, optimization, and function-approximation assumptions, CQL constructs a conservative estimate whose *expected value for a policy* lower-bounds that policy’s true value ([[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/02 Teaching the Machine/06 Policy Training/V4-06.13 Source References#^ref-kumar2020conservative|Kumar et al. 2020]]). This is not a pointwise bound for every state-action pair or every dataset, and expected return does not certify physical safety.

^fn6

<sup>7</sup> **Policy Gradient**: Williams ([[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/02 Teaching the Machine/06 Policy Training/V4-06.13 Source References#^ref-williams1992simple|1992]]) derived the REINFORCE estimator, which recovers the gradient of expected return from sampled actions through the likelihood-ratio identity without requiring an explicit physics model. However, estimator variance scales steeply with the trajectory horizon. Practical implementations subtract a baseline, typically a learned value function that converts sampled returns into advantages, yet still require the extensive interaction budgets evaluated below.

^fn7

<sup>8</sup> **Bearing Life vs. Command Reversals**: ISO 281 models rolling-bearing fatigue life under continuous rated loads and rotational velocities. Precision transmission manufacturers specify wave-generator bearing fatigue life (${L_{10}}$) in operating hours under nominal operating conditions. However, converting high-frequency commanded torque reversals into consumed gearhead life requires an empirical, unit-specific duty-cycle model.

^fn8

<sup>9</sup> **Identification Experiments**: System identification treats the input excitation signal as part of the experiment design. Signals such as step functions, swept-frequency sines (chirps), and pseudo-random binary sequences (PRBS) must persistently excite the relevant mechanical and electrical modes, since nominal closed-loop operation rarely activates them ([[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/02 Teaching the Machine/06 Policy Training/V4-06.13 Source References#^ref-ljung1999system|Ljung 1999]]). Paired physical probing follows this discipline: it drives both the simulator and physical hardware with identical open-loop excitation trajectories rather than relying on passive operational logs, where feedback regulation actively keeps the machine away from the boundary contact dynamics listed in the omission log.

^fn9

<sup>10</sup> **Domain Randomization**: The technique originated in computer vision, where Tobin et al. ([[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/02 Teaching the Machine/06 Policy Training/V4-06.13 Source References#^ref-tobin2017domain|2017]]) randomized textures, lighting, and camera poses so that physical scenes appeared as ordinary variations to a detector trained purely on synthetic renders. Peng et al. ([[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/02 Teaching the Machine/06 Policy Training/V4-06.13 Source References#^ref-peng2018sim|2018]]) extended the concept to dynamics, randomizing link masses, friction coefficients, joint damping, and transport latencies for robotic manipulation. Appearance and dynamics differ in how a policy can exploit history. Appearance variation changes instantaneous visual observations, whereas dynamics variation changes how the physical plant responds to control inputs, a discrepancy that a policy with temporal memory can infer online.

^fn10

<sup>11</sup> **Partially Observable MDP**: Åström ([[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/02 Teaching the Machine/06 Policy Training/V4-06.13 Source References#^ref-astrom1965optimal|1965]]) formulated optimal control under incomplete state information, proving that an optimal controller must act on the conditional probability distribution of the state given the observation history (the *belief state*) rather than solely on the instantaneous observation. Reinforcement learning formalized this framework three decades later ([[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/02 Teaching the Machine/06 Policy Training/V4-06.13 Source References#^ref-kaelbling1998|Kaelbling et al. 1998]]). An onboard student network’s temporal observation history serves as a learned approximation of this belief state; its temporal window must span sufficient duration for physical contact and load dynamics to become observable in the sensor stream.

^fn11

<sup>12</sup> **Penalty-Contact Timestep**: The contact timescale scales as $\sqrt{{m_{\text{eff}}}/{k_{\text{eff}}}}$. For the latch’s 9 kg moving mass it is about 24 ms against the soft penalty spring and 4.7 ms against the real plate, so a timestep sized to resolve the simulated contact is about 5× too coarse once the spring is stiffened to match the plate. The integrator, damping, and solver tolerances set the final step, which is refined until contact onset, peak force, and impulse converge.

^fn12
