*Why can a policy improve its training score while becoming less reliable on the machine?*

A learned policy acquires its behavior from the experience its training supplies, and its weights keep no record of which experience that was. Demonstrations can omit the recovery from a displaced fixture, a simulator can reward a fast approach that a real contact surface cannot tolerate, and a model pretrained on other robots can behave plausibly in conditions no one has tested on this machine. In each case the training score rises while the dependency stays hidden, so ranking candidates by score can favor the one that exploited its data or its solver. Training also fixes how long the policy takes to propose an action, and every improvement gathered on the machine itself is paid for in heat and component life. An engineer who does not understand these mechanisms cannot choose a source of experience that supports the behavior the task needs, cannot state the conditions in which a checkpoint may be trusted, and discovers each hidden dependency only when the machine meets it. Because an admitted action crosses the causal boundary and its consequences cannot be undone by another gradient update, the limits of a policy’s evidence must be established before it is allowed to propose motion.

**Learning Objectives**

- Compare training regimes, from behavioral cloning to post-training a pretrained generalist, by the experience they supply and the physical dependencies they leave in the policy
- Diagnose the separate risks of averaging valid action choices and drifting beyond demonstrated states
- Calculate whether an action decoder fits its proposal deadline and identify the required execution safeguards
- Evaluate simulator mismatch and distinguish parameter variation from missing mechanisms or unavailable observations
- Bound real-machine adaptation by physical exposure, residual authority, and regression evidence
- Construct a policy manifest that confines a candidate’s declared operating region to its documented support
