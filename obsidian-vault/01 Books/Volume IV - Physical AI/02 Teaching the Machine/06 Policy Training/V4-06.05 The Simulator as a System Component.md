# V4-06.05 The Simulator as a System Component

[[Home|Home]] · [[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/Volume 4 Overview|Volume Overview]] · [[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/02 Teaching the Machine/06 Policy Training/V4-06 Policy Training|V4-06 Policy Training]]

^sec-training-simulator-component

A reinforcement learning policy trained on door latching learned an aggressive, unbraked plunge because its simulated strike plate yielded far more than the physical mechanism. Stripping away the simulator during physical deployment does nothing to unlearn that behavior. The physics simulator is not an external sandbox; it is an active component of the training pipeline whose equations, contact approximations, and timing assumptions dictate the exact experience supplied to the optimizer. Accepting simulated task success as evidence of hardware readiness requires measurable, verifiable error bounds on every one of those assumptions.

Like any physical subsystem, a simulator requires a formal interface contract stated in quantities that engineers can measure and budget. This interface contract specifies observation dimensions, action bounds, simulation time step ${\Delta} {t}$, transport latency between command issuance and physical actuation, actuator torque and current limits, sensor noise distributions, reset distributions, and termination conditions. For instance, if a simulator executes an observation loop at 100 Hz with an assumed zero-delay sensor channel and Gaussian noise of variance ${\sigma}^2 = 1.0 \times 10^{-4}\text{ (rad/s)}^2$, the policy tunes its feedback gains to those exact signal statistics. An unmodeled 15 ms bus transport delay on the physical machine then removes ${\omega_c} {T_d}$ of phase at the loop’s crossover frequency ${\omega_c}$ (**[[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/01 Machine Anatomy/04 The Nervous System/V4-04.04 Multi-Rate Cadences#^eq-nervous-delay-phase|equation 4.1]]**), and the loop goes unstable whenever that loss exceeds the phase margin the learned gains left. OpenAI’s Dactyl project faced this contract at full scale, with a 24-DoF Shadow Hand in a motion-capture cage and its MuJoCo twin ([[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/02 Teaching the Machine/06 Policy Training/V4-06.05 The Simulator as a System Component#^fig-06-sim2real-dactyl|figure 5]]) ([[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/02 Teaching the Machine/06 Policy Training/V4-06.13 Source References#^ref-andrychowicz2020learning|Andrychowicz et al. 2020]]). The team calibrated the simulator against the physical hand and then randomized physical parameters around those calibrated values, so that the policy met the remaining discrepancies as variation it had already seen in training.

**War Story 6.1: Sim-to-real hardware breakage (2018)**

**Context**: OpenAI trained a reinforcement learning policy entirely in a randomized MuJoCo simulation to reorient a block in a 24-DoF Shadow Dexterous Hand, whose joints are driven through tendons by motors in its base ([[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/02 Teaching the Machine/06 Policy Training/V4-06.13 Source References#^ref-andrychowicz2020learning|Andrychowicz et al. 2020]]).

**Mechanism**: The simulated hand applied torque directly at its joints, while the physical hand is tendon-actuated and has substantial backlash. The authors smoothed every action with an exponential moving average, in simulation and on the robot, to avoid abrupt changes in the action signal that could harm the physical hand.

**Impact**: The authors reported frequent hardware breakages during physical evaluation, concentrated at the wrist vertical joint under heavy load. Each repair altered tendon tension, joint friction, and sensor calibration, shifting the physical plant across trials and lengthening the evaluation campaign.

**Response**: Physical ablations revealed the mechanisms enabling successful transfer. Policies trained without domain randomization, or without modeling unmodeled hardware traits like drivetrain backlash and action delay, achieved far fewer consecutive rotations. Furthermore, mechanically locking the fragile wrist pitch joint produced substantially higher physical reliability.

**Systems lesson**: A simulator that neither wears nor breaks cannot price hardware exposure into the reward. The limits that protected the hand, an action filter and a locked joint, were imposed outside the learned policy, and every repair changed the plant on which the policy was being evaluated.

When a simulator uses an inaccurate rotor inertia, assigning ${J_{\text{rotor}}} = 2.0 \times 10^{-3}\text{ kg}\cdot\text{m}^2$ instead of the true $2.5 \times 10^{-3}\text{ kg}\cdot\text{m}^2$, the underlying equations of motion still contain the acceleration-dependent inertial-torque term, with the wrong magnitude. The parameter error alters the size of the response, but the causal relationship holds. In contrast, omitting an entire physical mechanism, such as drivetrain backlash, structural compliance, or bus communication jitter, removes that governing physical constraint entirely. An omitted delay or compliance represents an unmodeled physical degree of freedom. For instance, a simulator that treats robot joints as perfectly rigid permits instantaneous torque reversals. When commanded on a physical machine, those instantaneous reversals shock the drivetrain, load gear teeth beyond their fatigue limits, and excite destructive structural resonances.

Whether a simulator’s modeling compromises are acceptable depends on two properties: throughput (the rate of experience generated per wall-clock hour) and contact fidelity (how faithfully the contact model reflects physical strike-plate dynamics). Pure throughput motivates teams to adopt simulation in the first place.

### Massively Parallel Simulation and On-Policy Learning

^sec-training-parallel-simulation

Stepping thousands of virtual workcells in parallel on a single GPU compresses months of trial-and-error into minutes, making on-policy methods such as **proximal policy optimization** (PPO) ([[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/02 Teaching the Machine/06 Policy Training/V4-06.13 Source References#^ref-schulman2017ppo|Schulman et al. 2017]]) practical without continuous machine wear. Because PPO collects rollouts with the current policy, takes several gradient steps on that batch, and then discards the data, replacing the batch before policy drift makes its policy-ratio correction less reliable, it requires a simulator that produces fresh rollouts in bulk. To maintain training stability across those gradient steps, PPO constrains policy drift using the clipped surrogate objective of [[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/02 Teaching the Machine/06 Policy Training/V4-06.05 The Simulator as a System Component#^eq-training-ppo-clip|equation 6]]:

$$
{\mathcal{L}^{\text{CLIP}}}({\theta}) = {\hat{\mathbb{E}}}_t\left[\min\left({\rho}_t({\theta})\,{\hat{A}}_t,\ \operatorname{clip}\left({\rho}_t({\theta}), 1 - {\epsilon_{\text{clip}}}, 1 + {\epsilon_{\text{clip}}}\right){\hat{A}}_t\right)\right], \qquad {\rho}_t({\theta}) = \frac{{\pi_\theta}({\mathbf{a}}_{t} \mid {s}_{t})}{\pi_{{\theta_{\text{old}}}}({\mathbf{a}}_{t} \mid {s}_{t})}, \tag{6}
$$

 where ${\rho}_t({\theta})$ measures the probability ratio between updated and rollout policies, and ${\epsilon_{\text{clip}}}$ clips the surrogate incentive for favorable probability-ratio changes; it does not impose a hard trust-region bound on the updated policy. Once the first gradient step updates the network, the current policy no longer matches the rollout policy that collected the batch, so each logged transition is reweighted by the importance-sampling ratio ${\rho}_t({\theta})$ between the two distributions. Taking the minimum of the unclipped and clipped terms removes the gradient incentive to push that ratio above $1 + {\epsilon_{\text{clip}}}$ when the advantage ${\hat{A}}_t$ is positive or below $1 - {\epsilon_{\text{clip}}}$ when the advantage is negative.

^eq-training-ppo-clip

GPU-resident simulation achieves this scale by maintaining the state of thousands of parallel environments directly in accelerator memory. Physics solvers advance all environments concurrently using batched GPU compute kernels, streaming observation tensors directly into the policy network without crossing the PCIe bus to host CPU memory ([[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/02 Teaching the Machine/06 Policy Training/V4-06.13 Source References#^ref-makoviychuk2021isaac|Makoviychuk et al. 2021]]). Policy inference evaluates concurrently across the entire population, turning an environment step into a deterministic sequence of GPU kernel launches rather than thousands of separate CPU process calls.

Rudin and colleagues trained an agile quadruped locomotion policy using this architecture with 4,096 parallel simulated robots, each gathering 24 environment steps per update cycle to form a batch of 98,304 transitions. Completing 1,500 policy updates required less than 20 min on a single workstation GPU ([[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/02 Teaching the Machine/06 Policy Training/V4-06.13 Source References#^ref-rudin2022learning|Rudin et al. 2022]]). This run processed 1.47 × 10⁸ total policy steps, sustaining an aggregate throughput of at least 1.23 × 10⁵ steps per second and accumulating 34.1 days of simulated locomotion at the policy’s 50 Hz control frequency.

Applying that throughput to the latch scenario illustrates the appeal. The from-scratch training budget from [[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/02 Teaching the Machine/06 Policy Training/V4-06.04 Reinforcement Learning and Environment Interaction#^sec-training-learning-by-trial|section 6.3]] requires 3.2 × 10⁸ policy steps. At the GPU simulator’s reported rate, this training run completes in 43.4 min, compared to 138.9 days on an automated physical test fixture.

While this thousandfold speedup explains why modern robotic reinforcement learning relies heavily on simulation, raw step throughput can easily mislead. The quadruped benchmark measured locomotion across smooth heightfield terrain. The authors noted that simulation execution time varied by a factor of two over the course of training. As the robots dispersed and ceased colliding with one another, computational cost dropped precipitously because narrow-phase contact detection and linear complementarity solving dominate simulator runtime. Manipulating a latch involves complex multi-body interactions (an articulated door, a sliding cam, and a high-stiffness strike plate) where contact resolution operates at its slowest, and rendering camera observations introduces additional memory and compute bottlenecks. Furthermore, published throughput benchmarks often sacrifice fidelity for speed. For example, the 2024 launch benchmark for one GPU physics simulator sparked controversy because it evaluated throughput by issuing a single action followed by open-loop stepping, disabling robot self-collisions, and using aggressive, low-accuracy solver settings ([[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/02 Teaching the Machine/06 Policy Training/V4-06.13 Source References#^ref-genesis2024benchmarkissue|Tao 2024]]). High step rates provide no guarantee of physical validity. Training an optimizer against an unvalidated contact model drives the policy to exploit the simulator’s numerical artifacts, as demonstrated by the latch simulator’s contact response.

### The Sim-To-Real Contact Exploit

^sec-training-contact-exploit

When a physics simulator softens a steel strike plate into a compliant penalty spring to keep its numerical integrator stable at large timesteps, a reinforcement learning policy will exploit that artificial cushion by plunging the gripper into the latch at 0.10 m/s without braking. Contact mechanics govern every phase of door manipulation: the physics solver must map surface separation, material penetration, friction, and relative velocity into normal and tangential forces observable by the policy. **[[05 Reference/Volume IV - Physical AI/V4 Control and Dynamics#^sec-appendix-control-contact|Rigid Body Contact Mechanics]]** details rigid-contact complementarity formulations ([[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/02 Teaching the Machine/06 Policy Training/V4-06.13 Source References#^ref-stewart1996implicit|Stewart and Trinkle 1996]]) and the time-stepping and compliant approximations that simulators substitute for them. The immediate engineering criterion for training the latch policy is whether these approximations preserve the contact forces and timing boundaries that govern safe approach speeds.

Rigid-body physics engines typically model contact using one of two paradigms: constraint-based complementarity formulations, which enforce non-penetration by solving for constraint impulses, or penalty formulations, which permit geometric interpenetration and generate restoring forces proportional to penetration depth. In practice, real-time solvers approximate both approaches, and numerical tuning parameters alter the resulting dynamics. In the latch simulator, the penalty contact computes an instantaneous normal restoring force from penetration depth ${\delta}$ and penetration velocity ${\dot{\delta}}$ via ${f_{k, \perp}} = {k_{\text{pen}}} {\delta} + {c} {\dot{\delta}}$. Artificially softening this contact interface alters the relationship between approach velocity, contact compliance, and peak impact force. The calculation below models this interaction using an undamped kinetic-to-strain energy balance, demonstrating how penalty spring compliance distorts the resulting force profile.

The manipulator drives the latch tool into the strike plate with an effective moving mass of 9 kg at the tool center point. This value is a stipulation, not a derivation. The registry takes the mass an arm presents at its tool as half its structural mass, the same order as the third of its mass that a uniform link pivoting at its base presents at its tip. This scenario drops the 3 kg payload capacity because the gripper holds no external object at impact. In a physical manipulator, reflected inertia varies with arm posture, and drivetrain elasticity partially decouples motor rotor inertia during rapid impact transients ([[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/02 Teaching the Machine/06 Policy Training/V4-06.13 Source References#^ref-haddadin2009requirements|Haddadin et al. 2009]]). Consequently, effective impact mass is an empirical parameter that physical paired probing must calibrate. Equating initial kinetic energy to elastic strain energy at maximum deflection, $\tfrac{1}{2} {m} {v}^2 = {F_{\text{unbraked}}}^2 / (2{k_{\text{contact}}})$, yields the peak impact force for an unpowered mass ${m}$ impacting a linear spring of stiffness ${k_{\text{contact}}}$ at velocity ${v}$:

$$
{F_{\text{unbraked}}} = {v}\sqrt{{k_{\text{contact}}}\,{m}}. \tag{7}
$$

 On physical hardware, the compliance does not reside solely in the strike plate. The robot’s rubber finger pads, mechanical linkages, and elastic joints sit directly between the effective link mass and the strike plate, acting as a series spring chain. Because the contact force transmits equally through each element in series, total mechanical deflection is additive: ${\Delta} {x} = {f_{k, \perp}}/{k_{\text{latch}}} + {f_{k, \perp}}/{k_{\text{robot}}}$. The coupled assembly responds with an effective series stiffness:

$$
{k_{\text{eff}}} = \left(\frac{1}{{k_{\text{latch}}}} + \frac{1}{{k_{\text{robot}}}}\right)^{-1}. \tag{8}
$$

 In the limit of an infinitely rigid manipulator (${k_{\text{robot}}} \to \infty$), the effective stiffness collapses to ${k_{\text{eff}}} = {k_{\text{latch}}}$, establishing an upper bound on impact force. In contrast, the penalty simulator implements a standalone compliant spring, whose artificial stiffness depends entirely on the maximum allowable penetration depth.

**Napkin Math 6.3: The soft-spring exploit**

**Problem**: The simulator’s penalty contact lets the gripper sink 2.4 mm into the plate at the fast approach. What peak force does the modeled contact produce, what peak can the plate impose in the rigid-arm limit, and at what robot-side stiffness does the passive model cross the latch force limit?

**Variables**:

- Moving mass ${m} =$ 9 kg (stipulated from the arm’s datasheet mass)
- Fast approach ${v} =$ 0.10 m/s and guarded approach 0.03 m/s (illustrative)
- Penalty penetration ${\delta_{\text{pen}}} =$ 2.4 mm at the fast approach (illustrative)
- Plate stiffness ${k_{\text{latch}}} =$ 4.0 × 10⁵ N/m and latch limit ${F_{\text{lim}}} =$ 100 N (illustrative)

**Math**:

1.  **Simulated spring**: The penetration fixes ${k_{\text{pen}}} = {m} {v}^2/{\delta_{\text{pen}}}^2 =$ 1.6 × 10⁴ N/m, so [[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/02 Teaching the Machine/06 Policy Training/V4-06.05 The Simulator as a System Component#^eq-training-impact-peak|equation 7]] gives ${F_{\text{unbraked,sim}}} = {m} {v}^2/{\delta_{\text{pen}}} =$ 9 kg $\times$ (0.10 m/s)$^2 /$ 2.4 mm $=$ 37.5 N, under the limit.
2.  **Rigid robot**: With ${k_{\text{contact}}} = {k_{\text{latch}}}$, ${F_{\text{unbraked}}} = {v}\sqrt{{k_{\text{contact}}}\,{m}} =$ 0.10 m/s $\times$ (4.0 × 10⁵ N/m $\times$ 9 kg)$^{1/2}$ $=$ 189.7 N, 1.9× the limit.
3.  **Break-even chain**: The limit is reached at ${k_{\text{eff}}}^* = ({F_{\text{lim}}}/{v})^2/{m} =$ 1.11 × 10⁵ N/m, which [[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/02 Teaching the Machine/06 Policy Training/V4-06.05 The Simulator as a System Component#^eq-training-series-stiffness|equation 8]] turns into ${k_{\text{robot}}}^* = (1/{k_{\text{eff}}}^* - 1/{k_{\text{latch}}})^{-1} =$ 1.54 × 10⁵ N/m.
4.  **Guarded approach**: A rigid robot coasting in at 0.03 m/s peaks at 56.9 N.

**Result**: In this passive impact model, the fast approach exceeds the force limit if the robot side is stiffer than 1.54 × 10⁵ N/m and remains below it if softer; the guarded approach remains below the limit even with a rigid robot.

**Systems insight**: A spring 25.6× softer than the plate placed the training result on the safe side of a threshold that the machine’s own compliance decides. No training score reveals whether the physical robot sits above or below ${k_{\text{robot}}}^*$; empirical hardware measurement provides the only valid ground truth.

The teacher’s reward kept only the task and time terms of [[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/02 Teaching the Machine/06 Policy Training/V4-06.03 Policy Training Regimes#^eq-training-latch-reward|equation 1]], and the simulated contact placed the plunge on the compliant side of the damage boundary, so the plunge cost the optimizer nothing, shortened every episode, and earned maximum reward. (Had the force term been present, the simulated 37.5 N peak would have crossed the 15 N tripwire and drawn a penalty, but the real plate’s peak would still have gone unseen.) Consequently, the policy converged on an aggressive, unbraked plunge. In the absence of an empirical measurement of ${k_{\text{robot}}}$ for this specific arm, gripper, and fingertip assembly, the actual physical outcome remains undetermined. Determining true system behavior requires conducting a quasi-static force-deflection test at the tool center point. Until physical characterization confirms whether effective stiffness falls below ${k_{\text{robot}}}^*$, accepting the policy’s simulated success as proof of safety is unwarranted.

While the energy balance model analyzes an unpowered mass coasting into an obstacle, an actively powered drive pushing forward represents the failure mode that safety supervisory logic must interrupt. Contact force follows Hooke’s law, ${f_{k, \perp}} = {k_{\text{contact}}}\,{\Delta} {x}$. Assuming the actuator drives the mechanism into the plate at constant velocity ${v}$, penetration depth scales as ${\Delta} {x} = {v} {t}$, driving a linear force ramp ${f_{k, \perp}}({t}) = {k_{\text{contact}}} {v} {t}$ starting from the instant of physical contact. Setting ${f_{k, \perp}}({t}) = {F_{\text{lim}}}$ defines the **contact yield deadline**:

$$
{t_{\text{yield}}} = \frac{{F_{\text{lim}}}}{{k_{\text{contact}}} {v}}, \tag{9}
$$

 representing the available time before exceeding the mechanical structural threshold under constant-velocity, linear-elastic conditions. (A complete stopping analysis must additionally account for controller braking trajectories and actuator force slew rates.) Assuming an infinitely rigid manipulator yields a conservative lower bound on this deadline by using the strike plate stiffness ${k_{\text{latch}}}$ directly.

^eq-training-contact-yield-deadline

Under these conditions, a fast approach at 0.10 m/s reaches the latch limit of 100 N in 100 N $/$ (4.0 × 10⁵ N/m $\times$ 0.10 m/s) $=$ 2.5 ms after touch. The 15 N tripwire fires at 0.375 ms. Sampling, intervention computation, and drive response then consume 2 ms, placing brake onset at 2.375 ms. Only 0.125 ms remains before the counterfactual constant-drive ramp reaches the force limit. At the guarded approach of 0.03 m/s, the tripwire fires at 1.25 ms and braking begins at 3.25 ms, leaving 5.083 ms before the 8.333 ms touch-relative limit ([[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/02 Teaching the Machine/06 Policy Training/V4-06.05 The Simulator as a System Component#^fig-06-latch-contact-force|figure 6]]). Neither remaining interval establishes that the actual braking trajectory stays below the limit.

The plate-only deadline leaves too little demonstrated braking margin to credit the reactive force monitor at the fast approach, so protection must act before contact until the contact response is qualified. An independently monitored deceleration envelope, ${v_{\text{safe}}}({z}) \le \sqrt{2 {a_{\text{brake}}} {z}}$, is the stopping inequality of **[[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/00 Introduction/01 The Causal Boundary/V4-01.05 Defining the Causal Boundary#^sec-boundary-kinematic-roofline|Delay consumes stopping clearance]]** applied to the gripper. Here ${z}$ is the clearance to a stopping boundary placed a short standoff before the plate, after sensing and actuation latencies are charged, and ${a_{\text{brake}}}$ is the credible deceleration of the arm at the tool, not the base’s value on the machine card. Because ${v_{\text{safe}}}$ falls to zero at that boundary, the envelope forbids an unbraked arrival at the plate. Crossing the standoff is a separate act. A guarded contact mode, qualified on its own, takes over inside it and holds the gripper at or below the guarded approach speed of 0.03 m/s until touch. That mode needs its own evidence about approach speed, force rise rate, loop latency, and deceleration, and a force tripwire works only inside those validated bounds. This contact deadline, not the reward used in training, is what confines the policy’s declared ODD to the guarded approach speed ([[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/02 Teaching the Machine/06 Policy Training/V4-06.09 The Policy Manifest#^sec-training-policy-manifest|section 6.8]]).

Engineering teams must budget contact simulation fidelity in terms of observable physical quantities, such as contact onset timing, peak normal force, total transferred impulse, slip distance, and separation release delay, rather than abstract solver convergence metrics. Discrepancies between simulation and reality frequently manifest in both directions. For example, a penalty solver that resolves an impact within a single integration step concentrates momentum transfer into an impulse spike of extreme amplitude and sub-millisecond duration. On real hardware, viscoelastic fingertip deformation and joint compliance distribute that momentum transfer over dozens of milliseconds. In such cases, total integrated impulse may match closely between simulation and reality even as peak force differs by an order of magnitude. A policy trained to trigger reflexive retraction upon sensing an impulse spike will fail on the physical robot, because the attenuated real-world signal never crosses the detection threshold, causing the motor drive to stall against its maximum current limit.

⇄ **Contrast:** The 2.4 mm of penalty-spring penetration is compliance the solver invents, whereas the backlash and torsional compliance of the real drivetrain, analyzed in **[[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/01 Machine Anatomy/02 The Physical Body/V4-02.05 Actuator Transmission Limits#^sec-body-transmission-compliance|Transmission compliance, contact impedance, and tooth fracture]]**, are compliance a rigid-body solver omits.

**Systems Perspective 6.1: The contact fidelity invariant**

*Never trust a simulated contact policy whose success depends on instantaneous momentum absorption or zero-delay friction engagement; a policy that relies on penetrating the digital surface loads the physical workpiece with force the simulator never showed.*

The operational relevance of specific physical omissions depends directly on the task dynamics. A policy executing uninterrupted, high-velocity motions may tolerate crude Coulomb friction models because joint velocities never linger near zero. Conversely, a policy tasked with precise force regulation or static holding will destabilize into violent limit-cycle oscillations if the simulator ignores static friction thresholds, stick-slip transitions, and actuator deadbands.

Validating simulation fidelity requires **paired probing**. The systems engineer executes identical open-loop actuation trajectories on both the physical robot and its digital twin, quantifying trajectory divergence against explicit error budgets. For the latch assembly, this procedure commands identical velocity profiles into the simulated and physical strike plates, measuring discrepancies directly in milliseconds and newtons. Whenever empirical divergence violates the error margins demanded by the robot’s operating envelope, the simulator cannot be trusted to train that specific capability. That audit relies on knowing which differential equations the simulator solved; when the simulator is itself a neural network trained on video or trajectory logs, even the list of omitted mechanisms disappears.

### Learned World Models as Simulators

^sec-training-world-models

A policy trained or evaluated inside a learned **world model** can score near-perfect task completion by exploiting hallucinated physics where training data were sparse. An example is pushing through a contact transition that the neural predictor smooths over because it rarely saw high-force impacts, with no analytical equation available for an engineer to audit. Whereas physics-based simulators are built from explicit differential equations and mechanical models whose simplifications can be enumerated, a world model derives system dynamics from data by training a neural network to predict future observations (or compact latent states) conditioned on the current state and applied control actions. Contemporary physical AI architectures employ learned world models across three distinct operational roles:

1.  **Latent imagination**: Policies train via reinforcement learning within the simulated latent representations of a world model. For example, DayDreamer and DreamerV3 trained quadruped locomotion directly from physical robot interaction, mastering rolling, standing, and walking behaviors in several hours without hand-crafted physics simulators ([[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/02 Teaching the Machine/06 Policy Training/V4-06.13 Source References#^ref-wu2022daydreamer|Wu et al. 2023]]; [[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/02 Teaching the Machine/06 Policy Training/V4-06.13 Source References#^ref-hafner2025dreamerv3|Hafner et al. 2025]]).
2.  **Zero-shot planning**: Action-conditioned video models plan trajectory sequences directly. For instance, V-JEPA 2, post-trained on uncurated robotic manipulation video, guided pick-and-place manipulation across physical robot arms in novel laboratory environments without prior domain calibration ([[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/02 Teaching the Machine/06 Policy Training/V4-06.13 Source References#^ref-assran2025vjepa2|Assran et al. 2025]]).
3.  **Synthetic data generation and evaluation**: Scaled generative video models synthesize diverse visual trajectories for offline imitation learning and act as simulated environments to evaluate candidate policies ([[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/02 Teaching the Machine/06 Policy Training/V4-06.13 Source References#^ref-nvidia2025cosmos|NVIDIA, Agarwal, et al. 2025]]).

Unlike a physics engine, a neural world model provides no explicit list of the phenomena it leaves out. Prediction errors concentrate wherever training distribution coverage is sparse, and no analytical derivation exists to reveal which dynamic constraints are violated. Consequently, training data generated purely through world model rollouts must be cataloged as unqualified in the policy manifest, whose `simulation_provenance` field captures the model architecture, parameter checkpoint hash, and dataset lineage. Simulated success inside a learned world model cannot expand the policy’s declared ODD until empirical paired probing on physical hardware verifies the specific contact forces, transport latencies, and tracking tolerances required by the task. Treating a world model’s internal rollout predictions as verified evidence mistakes model hallucination for physical reality.

Because structural simulation assumptions, numerical integrator time steps, and contact approximations become encoded in the policy’s weights during training, the simulation configuration is an immutable part of the model’s lineage. Every deployable policy artifact must trace its complete provenance through the `simulation_provenance` metadata block ([[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/02 Teaching the Machine/06 Policy Training/V4-06.09 The Policy Manifest#^sec-training-policy-manifest|section 6.8]]). Deploying policy weights without explicit simulator provenance severs the trained model from the kinematic limits, noise distributions, and solver thresholds under which it was derived. A scalar task completion rate cannot audit that lineage. The reality gap has to be taken apart into its dynamics, sensing, appearance, and timing channels, each measured on its own.
