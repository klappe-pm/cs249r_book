# V4-06.03 Policy Training Regimes

[[Home|Home]] · [[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/Volume 4 Overview|Volume Overview]] · [[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/02 Teaching the Machine/06 Policy Training/V4-06 Policy Training|V4-06 Policy Training]]

^sec-training-synthesis-regimes

When `latch-bc-01` binds against a sagged strike plate and `latch-sim-02` plunges into the nominal plate at 0.10 m/s, neither failure can be repaired by training the same network for more epochs on the same dataset. Fixing an unvisited recovery state or an exploited contact solver requires changing the experience the learner sees. Training a control policy for the latch task can draw on five sources of experience: teleoperated door openings, logged archives of imperfect or scripted trajectories, a physics simulator of the door and cage, direct physical trial on the arm itself, and a generalist foundation checkpoint pretrained across multi-robot corpora. Each source buys distinct sensorimotor capabilities at a characteristic physical cost and leaves its own failure mode behind ([[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/02 Teaching the Machine/06 Policy Training/V4-06.03 Policy Training Regimes#^tbl-06-regimes|table 1]]). Behavioral cloning, batch offline reinforcement learning, and supervised post-training extract control policies from recorded datasets without runtime exploration, whereas online trial, conducted in simulation or on the physical mechanism, generates new interactions under closed-loop feedback. Comparing how those regimes evaluate and update actions begins with the reward quantities that score closed-loop behavior over an episode.

### The Latch Task as a Reward Problem

^sec-training-rl-primer

A reward function that pays a policy for opening the cage door and penalizes each control step for elapsed time without charging for contact force will train the arm to slam into the strike plate, because every millisecond shaved off the approach increases the episode’s score. Even behavioral cloning, which uses no reward during training, has its failures measured in the quantities that a reward problem defines: the states a policy visits, the return it earns over an episode, and the value of one action against another. Posing the latch mechanism as a discrete-time optimal control problem makes those quantities concrete and shows how an omitted force penalty or a discount factor mis-scaled against the control rate distorts learned behavior.

A **Markov decision process** (MDP)[[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/02 Teaching the Machine/06 Policy Training/V4-06.14 Footnotes#^fn2|²]] describes a task by its state ${s}_{t}$, its action ${\mathbf{a}}_{t}$, a transition ${p}({s}_{t+1} \mid {s}_{t}, {\mathbf{a}}_{t})$ that the physics supplies, and a scalar reward ${r}({s}_{t}, {\mathbf{a}}_{t})$ that the engineer writes. For the latch, the state holds the gripper pose, the plate position, and the measured contact force. The action is the setpoint the chunk policy emits every 25 ms. An episode is one opening, 8 s of autonomous contact-phase motion, or 320 decisions. A reward that states what the task values and what it must avoid is

$$
{r}_t = {r_{\text{open}}}\,\mathbb{1}[\text{door open at } {t}] - {\lambda_f} \max\left(0, {f}_t - {F_{\text{trip}}}\right) - {c_{\text{time}}}, \tag{1}
$$

 where ${F_{\text{trip}}}$ is the 15 N contact tripwire, the force threshold that triggers the machine’s qualified collection response (set below the latch’s damage limit), ${\lambda_f}$ is a weight in reward per newton of excess force, and ${c_{\text{time}}}$ charges each step for slowness. The weights make the terms commensurable, and choosing them is a design decision that the policy manifest records. Dropping the force term, as the simulator teacher of `latch-sim-02` did, leaves only the time term to shape the approach, and the time term rewards every millisecond an aggressive plunge saves. On hardware the success indicator must itself be measured, by a latch sensor or a learned classifier, and every error in that measurement is a surface the optimizer can exploit. When engineers introduce auxiliary rewards to guide exploration toward that success indicator, adding a potential difference ${\gamma}{\Phi}({s'}) - {\Phi}({s})$ provably leaves the optimal policy unchanged. Without further assumptions about the task, it is the only added term that does ([[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/02 Teaching the Machine/06 Policy Training/V4-06.13 Source References#^ref-ng1999policy|Ng et al. 1999]]). Any other shaping term can change what is optimal.

^eq-training-latch-reward

A **policy** ${\pi_\theta}({\mathbf{a}} \mid {s})$ is a distribution over actions, and its **return** from step ${t}$ is ${G}_t = \sum_{j \ge 0} {\gamma}^{j} {r}_{{t}+{j}}$ with discount factor ${\gamma} \in [0, 1)$. Because ${\gamma}$ applies once per decision, its horizon is measured in control steps, not seconds. At ${\gamma} =$ 0.99 and one decision every 25 ms, the door-open reward earned at the end of an opening reaches the first decision with weight 0.04, and the effective horizon $1/(1-{\gamma})$ of 100 steps spans only 2.5 s. For the terminal success to keep half its weight across the whole opening, ${\gamma}$ must be at least 0.9978. Changing the setpoint rate without re-deriving ${\gamma}$ therefore changes the task horizon in seconds. Training a policy means choosing ${\theta}$ to maximize the **objective** ${J}({\theta}) = \mathbb{E}_{{\pi_\theta}}[{G}_0]$, the return expected over whole episodes that start from the task’s initial states. It is a return to maximize, where the ${J}(\pi)$ of **[[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/02 Teaching the Machine/05 Physical Data/V4-05.04 Recovery and Compounding Error#^eq-data-quadratic-drift|equation 5.1]]** was an expected cost to minimize.

Two expectations of the return organize every reinforcement learning method. The **state value** ${V^{\pi}}({s}) = \mathbb{E}_\pi[{G}_t \mid {s}_{t} = {s}]$ is the return expected from ${s}$ when following $\pi$. The **action value** ${Q^{\pi}}({s}, {\mathbf{a}}) = \mathbb{E}_\pi[{G}_t \mid {s}_{t} = {s}, {\mathbf{a}}_t = {\mathbf{a}}]$ is the return expected after taking ${\mathbf{a}}$ first and following $\pi$ thereafter. Both obey the one-step **Bellman relation**

$$
{Q^{\pi}}({s}, {\mathbf{a}}) = {r}({s}, {\mathbf{a}}) + {\gamma}\, \mathbb{E}_{{s'} \sim {p},\ {\mathbf{a}'} \sim \pi}\left[ {Q^{\pi}}({s'}, {\mathbf{a}'}) \right], \qquad {V^{\pi}}({s}) = \mathbb{E}_{{\mathbf{a}} \sim \pi}\left[ {Q^{\pi}}({s}, {\mathbf{a}}) \right]. \tag{2}
$$

 The **advantage** ${A^{\pi}}({s}, {\mathbf{a}}) = {Q^{\pi}}({s}, {\mathbf{a}}) - {V^{\pi}}({s})$ measures whether an action does better or worse than the policy’s usual choice from that state. At the plate, an approach has positive advantage only if its expected return exceeds the policy’s usual choice. A force penalty can make a plunge less attractive, but the sign still depends on the reward weights, completion probability, and baseline policy.

^eq-training-bellman

Readers from control have met these objects under other names. With stage cost $\ell = -{r}$, the value is the negated **cost-to-go**, ${C}({s}) = -{V}({s})$, and [[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/02 Teaching the Machine/06 Policy Training/V4-06.03 Policy Training Regimes#^eq-training-bellman|equation 2]] is the dynamic-programming recursion. (Control texts write the cost-to-go as ${J}$; this chapter reserves ${J}({\theta})$ for the expected return a learner maximizes.) For linear dynamics in the plant state ${\mathbf{x}}$ and quadratic cost, that recursion has the closed-form solution ${C}({\mathbf{x}}) = {\mathbf{x}}^\top {\mathbf{P}} {\mathbf{x}}$ that the linear-quadratic regulator computes from a Riccati equation (**[[05 Reference/Volume IV - Physical AI/V4 Control and Dynamics#^sec-appendix-control-lqr|Linear quadratic regulator and algebraic Riccati equations]]**). Reinforcement learning keeps the recursion and changes the estimation problem: the dynamics are unknown, so ${V}$ and ${Q}$ are fitted from sampled transitions instead of solved from a model.

These quantities organize policy learning along two axes, what is estimated and where the data comes from. Fitting ${Q}$ over an immutable logged archive to select high-value actions is **offline reinforcement learning** ([[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/02 Teaching the Machine/06 Policy Training/V4-06.03 Policy Training Regimes#^sec-training-offline-rl|section 6.2.7]]). Generating fresh rollouts from ${\pi_\theta}$, using each batch for a few updates, and shifting probability toward positive-advantage actions is on-policy **policy-gradient** optimization ([[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/02 Teaching the Machine/06 Policy Training/V4-06.04 Reinforcement Learning and Environment Interaction#^sec-training-learning-by-trial|section 6.3]]). Between the two, value-based methods also run online and off-policy. The machine keeps collecting experience, but each transition enters a replay buffer and is reused across many value updates, which is how the demonstration-seeded methods of [[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/02 Teaching the Machine/06 Policy Training/V4-06.04 Reinforcement Learning and Environment Interaction#^sec-training-real-world-rl|section 6.3.1]] learn in hours. The value-fitting and policy-gradient derivations behind these approaches appear in **[[05 Reference/Volume IV - Physical AI/V4 Machine Learning Background#^sec-appendix-ml-affordances|Value Functions and Continuous Reinforcement Learning]]**. Before paying the reward-design or physical trial cost of those methods, however, the simplest training regime bypasses value estimation altogether and fits a policy directly to the expert setpoints the collection campaign already recorded.

### Behavioral Cloning and Its Action Targets

^sec-training-behavioral-cloning

Instead of designing a contact reward that an optimizer can game, `latch-bc-01` learns the nominal door opening directly from recorded human demonstrations, following the supervised imitation paradigm that ALVINN first demonstrated for road following ([[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/02 Teaching the Machine/06 Policy Training/V4-06.13 Source References#^ref-pomerleau1989alvinn|Pomerleau 1989]]). **Behavioral cloning** frames policy training as supervised regression or classification over a collected dataset of expert state-action pairs ${\mathcal{D}_{\text{demo}}} = \{({\mathbf{o}}_{t}, {\mathbf{a}}_t)\}$. On a physical machine, however, the four-stream action taps record both the operator’s mapped setpoints and the setpoints the permission path actually admitted:

$$
{\mathcal{D}_{\text{intent}}} = \left\{ \left({\mathbf{o}}_{t}, {\mathbf{u}_{\text{nom}, t:t+H-1}}\right) \right\} \quad \text{vs.} \quad {\mathcal{D}_{\text{verified}}} = \left\{ \left({\mathbf{o}}_{t}, {\mathbf{u}^*_{t:t+H-1}}\right) \right\}
$$

 The mapped setpoint ${\mathbf{u}_{\text{nom}}}$ is the record at the mapped-command tap ${a_{\text{cmd}}}$, and the admitted setpoint ${\mathbf{u}^*}$ is the record at the enforced-command tap ${a_{\text{enf}}}$. **[[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/02 Teaching the Machine/05 Physical Data/V4-05.06 Action Label Selection Across the Control Boundary#^sec-data-four-stream-action-tap|The four-stream action tap tuple]]** develops what each tap teaches a policy. In brief, cloning admitted setpoints couples the weights to one permission path’s intervention profile and tuning. An uncurated policy can then learn to steer straight into the safety clamps and rely on the hardware filter to bend its commands back onto the latch. Cloning mapped setpoints instead keeps intent separate from enforcement, at the price of more frequent runtime interventions. Neither tap yields an expert target without curation. The latch campaign took the mapped command as its target wherever the permission path admitted it unchanged, so `latch-bc-01` clones ${\mathbf{u}_{\text{nom}}}$ on those steps and treats every modified command as an attributed intervention.

Whichever tap supplies the target, the training loss compares the proposed setpoints with the recorded ones, over a single step or over the ${H}$-step sequence that [[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/02 Teaching the Machine/06 Policy Training/V4-06.03 Policy Training Regimes#^sec-training-action-chunking|section 6.2.5]] calls a chunk. Because this loss penalizes only the discrepancy between the commanded action and the recorded demonstration at observed states, the training objective does not evaluate task completion, terminal cost, or the stability of the closed-loop dynamical system. It fits actions to observations only, and that point-by-point fit breaks down at a single observation whenever demonstrators solve the same physical geometry in more than one valid way.

### Mode Averaging When Demonstrations Split

^sec-training-multimodality-breakdown

A mobile manipulator trained with least-squares regression on demonstrations that pass left of a central structural column half the time and right of it the other half will steer straight down the center line into the column. Each human teleoperator trajectory in the recorded archive ${\mathcal{D}_{\text{demo}}}$ is dynamically valid and collision-free, and not a single demonstration drives into the obstacle, yet a deterministic point-regression policy proposes the one path that every demonstrator avoided.

Under empirical mean squared error ($\ell_2$) loss, a deterministic behavioral cloning network cannot represent both modes for the same observation ${\mathbf{o}}_{t}$ and instead optimizes:

$$
{\mathcal{L}_{\text{MSE}}}({\theta}) = \mathbb{E}_{({\mathbf{o}}_{t}, {\mathbf{a}}) \sim {\mathcal{D}_{\text{demo}}}} \left[ \left\| {\pi_\theta}({\mathbf{o}}_{t}) - {\mathbf{a}} \right\|_2^2 \right].
$$

 The theoretical minimizer of this objective is the conditional expectation of the action distribution (derived formally in **[[05 Reference/Volume IV - Physical AI/V4 Machine Learning Background#^sec-appendix-ml-multimodal|Mode averaging and the decoders that avoid it]]**):

$$
\pi^*({\mathbf{o}}_{t}) = \arg\min_{{\hat{\mathbf{a}}}} \int \|{\hat{\mathbf{a}}} - {\mathbf{a}}\|_2^2\,{p_{\text{demo}}}({\mathbf{a}} \mid {\mathbf{o}}_{t})\,{d}{\mathbf{a}} = \mathbb{E}[{\mathbf{a}} \mid {\mathbf{o}}_{t}] = \int {\mathbf{a}}\,{p_{\text{demo}}}({\mathbf{a}} \mid {\mathbf{o}}_{t})\,{d}{\mathbf{a}}. \tag{3}
$$

 In conventional computer vision, predicting the conditional mean of ambiguous pixel targets generates blurry, averaged textures; in natural language processing, it produces diffused semantic embeddings. In Physical AI, however, the conditional expectation of two collision-free evasion commands (such as ${\mathbf{a}_{\text{left}}} = [{v}, +{\omega_z}]^\top$ and ${\mathbf{a}_{\text{right}}} = [{v}, -{\omega_z}]^\top$, where ${v}$ is forward linear speed and ${\omega_z}$ is angular yaw rate) evaluates to:

$$
\mathbb{E}[{\mathbf{a}} \mid {\mathbf{o}}_{t}] = \frac{1}{2}{\mathbf{a}_{\text{left}}} + \frac{1}{2}{\mathbf{a}_{\text{right}}} = \left[ {v}, 0.0 \right]^\top.
$$

 The regression mean proposes forward motion with zero lateral evasion. If admitted unchanged, that proposal heads straight into the central obstacle.[[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/02 Teaching the Machine/06 Policy Training/V4-06.14 Footnotes#^fn3|³]]

^eq-training-mse-minimizer

This invalid proposal is not caused by an optimization pathology, insufficient model capacity, or dataset scarcity. It is the exact minimizer of a point-regression loss over a multimodal distribution. Viewed as a function of the proposed action, the expected loss is a bowl with a single bottom at the mean, and that bottom sits in the zero-density gap between the two demonstrated modes (${p_{\text{demo}}}(\mathbb{E}[{\mathbf{a}}\mid{\mathbf{o}}_{t}] \mid {\mathbf{o}}_{t}) \approx 0$). The statement concerns the optimal output, not whether optimizing the network’s weights is convex.

**Definition 6.1: Mode averaging**

***Mode averaging*** is the failure in which a policy trained with a point-regression loss on demonstrations that contain several valid actions for one observation proposes their conditional mean, an action that lies between the demonstrated modes and that no demonstrator chose.

1.  **Significance**: More data, capacity, or training moves the output toward the mean, not away from it. Two equally weighted evasions at $\pm{\omega_z}$ average to zero yaw, a proposal aimed at the obstacle both demonstrations avoided, and it reaches the permission path as a smooth, confident command whose consequence cannot be undone once admitted.
2.  **Distinction**: Mode averaging concerns the action chosen at one observation; compounding error concerns the states that successive actions reach. A multimodal decoder removes the first and does nothing for the second, which needs recovery experience.
3.  **Common pitfall**: Reading a low validation loss as evidence against averaging. The mean minimizes squared error on held-out demonstrations drawn from the same modes, so a regression head can score best on its split and still propose the invalid middle action at every bifurcation.

Changing the regression penalty does not by itself represent alternative actions. Mean absolute error ($\ell_1$) admits conditional medians, while Huber loss limits the influence of large residuals, so neither guarantees a unique, coherent route through a multimodal demonstration distribution. Instead, the decoder must represent the alternatives, and execution must maintain a consistent choice across successive observations.

Several decoder families represent these alternatives at different computational costs. **Mixture Density Networks (MDNs)**[[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/02 Teaching the Machine/06 Policy Training/V4-06.14 Footnotes#^fn4|⁴]] parameterize a Gaussian Mixture Model (GMM): ${p_{\text{demo}}}({\mathbf{a}} \mid {\mathbf{o}}_{t}) = \sum_{{m}=1}^{{M}} \pi_{m}({\mathbf{o}}_{t}) {\mathcal{N}}\left({\mathbf{a}} \mid {\boldsymbol{\mu}_m}({\mathbf{o}}_{t}), {\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_m}({\mathbf{o}}_{t})\right)$. Here $\pi_{m}$ is the weight of component ${m}$ (not the policy), and ${\boldsymbol{\mu}_m}$ and ${\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_m}$ are its mean (not a friction coefficient) and covariance. They produce mixture parameters in one forward pass, but training must prevent covariance collapse (${\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_m} \to {\mathbf{0}}$) and loss of less frequent modes, which makes the component count and covariance representation consequential for high-dimensional chunks (${d_{\text{chunk}}} = {d_a} \times {H}$). Energy-based models instead score each candidate with a learned energy and must search for a low-energy action, so their iteration budget, rather than a head of fixed size, must fit the proposal deadline.

Generative architectures, including diffusion models ([[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/02 Teaching the Machine/06 Policy Training/V4-06.13 Source References#^ref-ho2020denoising|Ho et al. 2020]]) and flow matching ([[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/02 Teaching the Machine/06 Policy Training/V4-06.13 Source References#^ref-lipman2022flow|Lipman et al. 2022]]), offer another way to represent ${p_{\text{demo}}}({\mathbf{a}} \mid {\mathbf{o}}_{t})$ by training a model to refine a noisy action candidate toward the learned distribution rather than returning its conditional mean. Sampling can then select a coherent demonstration mode (${\mathbf{a}_{\text{left}}}$ or ${\mathbf{a}_{\text{right}}}$). Although representing multiple modes resolves the averaging problem, a generated candidate can still violate clearance or actuator constraints, so the permission path must check it before execution.

A mode boundary in a single-step Markov policy can also produce high-frequency temporal chattering. In a 50–100 Hz feedback loop, microscopic sensor noise drives consecutive observations back and forth across the mode boundary, causing a single-step controller mapping ${\mathbf{o}}_{t} \to {\mathbf{a}}_{t}$ to alternate between opposing actuator efforts (for example, +10 N·m and −10 N·m). This alternation excites drivetrain resonances, accelerates gear tooth fatigue, and drives motor winding temperatures toward their thermal limit. Even when a multimodal decoder commits to one route and avoids chattering across the boundary, however, a second imitation failure emerges as soon as a small tracking error pushes the robot outside the states the demonstrators visited.

### Compounding Errors and Recovery Experience

^sec-training-recovery-experience

A policy can choose a coherent route around the column and still drift into states its demonstrations never covered. Mode averaging concerns the action selected at one observation; compounding error concerns the observations created by successive actions. Keeping these failures separate prevents a richer action decoder from being mistaken for recovery experience. Cloning fits actions only at the states the demonstrators visited, whereas deployment visits the states the policy’s own actions create, and a fixed archive holds no example of the states the policy’s execution errors reach. In continuous physical state spaces, this decoupling produces **compounding covariate shift**, which is the second law (endogenous data, principle [[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/02 Teaching the Machine/V4 Teaching the Machine#^pri-vol4-endogenous-drift|2]]) acting on a fixed archive.

**[[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/02 Teaching the Machine/05 Physical Data/V4-05.04 Recovery and Compounding Error#^sec-data-curse-dimensionality-recoverability|Recovery and Compounding Error]]** bounds the price of this shift: fixed-archive cloning admits a worst-case imitation cost that grows as ${S}^2{\epsilon}$ over an ${S}$-step horizon (**[[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/02 Teaching the Machine/05 Physical Data/V4-05.04 Recovery and Compounding Error#^eq-data-quadratic-drift|equation 5.1]]**). That bound counts accumulated task cost, not displacement, and the physical drift behind it depends on what the action commands and on the feedback below the policy. A persistent error in a commanded velocity integrates once into position, so the drift grows linearly in time; a persistent error in a commanded torque integrates twice, so it grows quadratically. A velocity-level policy also sits above a joint velocity loop that rejects load disturbances between its decisions, whereas a torque-level policy has no such loop beneath it and must supply every correction itself. Offline validation on demonstration splits measures prediction accuracy on the demonstrated distribution; recovery after displacement requires additional closed-loop evidence.

The regime that supplies that recovery experience is interactive imitation learning, of which Dataset Aggregation (DAgger) ([[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/02 Teaching the Machine/06 Policy Training/V4-06.13 Source References#^ref-ross2011reduction|Ross et al. 2011]]) is the classic form. In DAgger, an expert labels the states the learner itself visits and the policy retrains on the aggregate. **[[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/02 Teaching the Machine/05 Physical Data/V4-05.09 Intervention and Recovery Data#^sec-data-intervention-recovery|Intervention and Recovery Data]]** develops DAgger, with its guarantee of a cost that grows linearly in the horizon (**[[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/02 Teaching the Machine/05 Physical Data/V4-05.09 Intervention and Recovery Data#^eq-data-dagger-bound|equation 5.8]]**) and the recoverability constant ${u}$ on which that guarantee depends. The constant bounds how much a single deviation can raise the expert’s remaining cost, so it is an assumption about the plant, not a property of the learner. On physical hardware, this theoretical recoverability depends directly on joint torque authority and contact friction limits.

On hardware, collecting those labels requires a recoverable rollout and an expert who has enough information to choose a correction. Because a camera log may support a path label while leaving contact force or joint torque ambiguous, live supervision supplies richer feedback, yet the machine continues moving during human reaction and control handoff. Consequently, intervention must begin within the available stopping margin, and the handoff must avoid discontinuous setpoints.

↪ The bumpless transfer that keeps a human takeover from stepping the command is developed in **[[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/04 Governing the Machine/14 Supervisory Intervention/V4-14.05 Transfer without Discontinuity#^sec-intervention-bumpless-transfer|Transfer Without Discontinuity]]**.

Expert availability also does not imply physical recoverability. The machine may lack enough actuator effort or frictional grip to return from a deviation. The simplified force balance ${\mathbf{M}}{\ddot{\mathbf{q}}} = {\boldsymbol{\tau}} + {\boldsymbol{\tau}_{\text{ext}}}$ is Newton’s second law written per joint. Here ${\mathbf{q}}$ denotes joint positions, ${\mathbf{M}}$ inertia, ${\boldsymbol{\tau}}$ actuator torque, and ${\boldsymbol{\tau}_{\text{ext}}}$ the torque that contact forces exert about the joints. Other forces are omitted for clarity, and **[[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/03 Running the Machine/11 Trajectory Planning/V4-11.05 Kinodynamic Feasibility#^sec-planning-rigidbody-dynamics|Rigid-body dynamics and continuous collision checks]]** develops the full equation. Actuator limits require $|{\tau}_i| \le {\tau_{\max}}$ on every joint axis, while available friction bounds tangential force by $\|{\mathbf{f}_{k, \parallel}}\| \le {\mu} {f_{k, \perp}}$, with normal force ${f_{k, \perp}}$ and friction coefficient ${\mu}$. These constraints can leave no action that restores the task, and then ${u}$ is unbounded and DAgger’s linear guarantee no longer applies. Interactive aggregation is therefore most useful where learner deviations can be contained, labeled, and repeated within the operator-time and hardware-exposure budget. The state space divides into three regions ([[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/02 Teaching the Machine/06 Policy Training/V4-06.03 Policy Training Regimes#^fig-06-compounding-error|figure 1]]). Cloning trains in the tube of demonstrated states. Just outside it lies a band where an expert label on a learner-visited state still restores the task. Beyond the band is an unrecoverable region, where actuator and friction limits leave no restoring action and every further label is vacuous. Interactive aggregation buys experience only in the middle band.

For the latch, these distinctions select a concrete next training step. The stipulated follow-up campaign in **[[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/02 Teaching the Machine/05 Physical Data/V4-05.09 Intervention and Recovery Data#^sec-data-intervention-recovery|Intervention and Recovery Data]]** supplies `latch-recovery-02`, a versioned archive of expert recovery from the positively displaced plate at the guarded approach. The recovery candidate `latch-bc-recovery-02` trains on the original nominal demonstrations and the stabilized, reviewed segment of each correction; the enforcer’s blend and the operator’s re-orientation after takeover stay masked. Learner commands that preceded takeover remain failure evidence unless an expert supplied replacement labels; they do not become imitation targets merely because the episode eventually succeeded. A multimodal chunk decoder can preserve a coherent back-off and re-seating sequence, while the additional demonstrations supply the recovery states that changing the decoder alone could not provide.

↪ Causal intervention masking, which admits only reviewed recovery segments as imitation targets, is enforced in **[[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/04 Governing the Machine/14 Supervisory Intervention/V4-14.09 The Authority Record#^sec-intervention-recovery-eligibility|Causal masking and recovery eligibility]]**.

The training decision is complete, but the improvement claim remains to be tested. Entire collection sessions are held out for nominal and recovery checks, so neighboring frames from one run do not appear on both sides of the split. Training diagnostics check whether the candidate represents the demonstrated correction; qualification must then test whether it completes the door task under the declared perturbation without regressing nominal behavior. The candidate carries that request to **[[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/02 Teaching the Machine/07 Closed-Loop Evaluation/V4-07.09 The Evaluation Record#^sec-evaluation-recovery-update|Qualifying a recovery update]]**, including task completion, cycle time, proposer intervention, and protective outcomes as separate measures. This is a stipulated engineering branch, not a report of collected data or measured improvement. The negative displacement and faster approach remain unsupported. Even on the supported path, emitting setpoints one tick at a time leaves the arm vulnerable to the mode-boundary chattering of [[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/02 Teaching the Machine/06 Policy Training/V4-06.03 Policy Training Regimes#^sec-training-multimodality-breakdown|section 6.2.3]] unless the decoder predicts a multi-step action sequence in one pass.

### Action Chunking and Multi-Step Trajectory Prediction

^sec-training-action-chunking

Action chunking (definition [3.2](https://mlsysbook.ai/vol4/03_brain/03_brain.html#dfn-brain-action-chunking)) suppresses both high-frequency actuator chattering and single-step mode oscillation by predicting an entire sequence of future actions simultaneously rather than re-querying the decoder at every 25 ms control tick. Instead of regressing a single action ${\mathbf{a}}_{t}$ from current observation ${\mathbf{o}}_{t}$, a chunking policy predicts a setpoint sequence ${\mathbf{A}}_t \in \mathbb{R}^{{H} \times {d_a}}$ across a prediction horizon of ${H}$ future timesteps and ${d_a}$ action dimensions. The execution path then interpolates this sequence into continuous motion and checks it for feasibility. In supervised behavioral cloning, the policy minimizes a multi-step trajectory loss over demonstrated trajectories:

$$
{\mathcal{L}_{\text{chunk}}}({\theta}) = \mathbb{E}_{({\mathbf{o}}_{t}, {\mathbf{A}}_t^*) \sim {\mathcal{D}_{\text{demo}}}} \left[ \sum_{i=0}^{{H}-1} \ell_{\delta}\left( {\pi_\theta}({\mathbf{o}}_{t})_i - {\mathbf{a}}^*_{t+i} \right) \right],
$$

 where ${\mathbf{A}}_t^* = [{\mathbf{a}}_t^*, \dots, {\mathbf{a}}_{t+{H}-1}^*]^\top$ represents the recorded demonstration chunk and ${\pi_\theta}({\mathbf{o}}_{t})_i$ is the ${i}$-th predicted future setpoint. Actions are expressed in declared normalized coordinates. For a vector residual ${\mathbf{r}}$, the chunk objective uses the componentwise sum $\ell_{\delta}({\mathbf{r}}) = \sum_{j=1}^{{d_a}}\ell_{\delta}({e}_j)$, where ${e}_j$ is the matching component of ${\mathbf{r}}$, with equal component weights. The scalar Huber penalty below acts on each normalized prediction error ${e}_j$, not on the reward of [[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/02 Teaching the Machine/06 Policy Training/V4-06.03 Policy Training Regimes#^sec-training-rl-primer|section 6.2.1]]:

$$
\ell_{\delta}({e}) = \begin{cases} \frac{1}{2} {e}^2 & \text{for } |{e}| \le {\delta}, \\ {\delta} \left(|{e}| - \frac{1}{2} {\delta}\right) & \text{otherwise.} \end{cases}
$$

 (ACT ([[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/02 Teaching the Machine/06 Policy Training/V4-06.13 Source References#^ref-zhao2023learning|Zhao et al. 2023]]), the architecture of both latch policies, uses an $\ell_1$ reconstruction penalty in place of $\ell_{\delta}$ and adds a conditional variational autoencoder (CVAE) term ${\beta} {\mathcal{D}_{\text{KL}}}({q}({\mathbf{z}} \mid {\mathbf{A}}_t^*, {\mathbf{o}}_{t}) \parallel {\mathcal{N}}({\mathbf{z}}))$ against a standard normal prior, which lets a latent variable absorb differences in style across demonstrations.)

Because teleoperated setpoints can contain outliers caused by tremor, tracking errors, or dropouts, squared loss gives a single large residual disproportionate influence over the fit. Huber loss[[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/02 Teaching the Machine/06 Policy Training/V4-06.14 Footnotes#^fn5|⁵]] is quadratic for small residuals ($|{e}| \le {\delta}$) and linear beyond them, so its gradient saturates at magnitude ${\delta}$ and no single outlier can dominate the fit. Even so, the threshold acts on prediction residuals rather than on the velocity, acceleration, or jerk of the resulting trajectory. Huber loss alone therefore establishes neither smooth motion nor actuator feasibility. Those properties require explicit constraints and checks on the proposed chunk.

Predicting ${H}$ future actions at once creates opportunities for more coherent execution, provided training and runtime checks preserve them:

1.  **Temporal and Dynamic Consistency**: Jointly predicting adjacent actions lets the model learn their relationships. The resulting sequence still requires checks for abrupt changes and actuator limits.
2.  **Preserving Modal Commitment**: A chunk can represent one coherent choice, such as passing left of the column, across adjacent actions. Predicting a sequence does not itself ensure that the decoder selects a single valid mode.
3.  **Receding-Horizon Replanning and Temporal Ensembling**: The deployed policy queries a fresh chunk every ${H_{\text{exec}}}$ steps (${H_{\text{exec}}} < {H}$), permitting feedback correction before the prediction horizon ends. Blending overlapping predictions is optional and must preserve a coherent mode and feasible seam.

The modal commitment that the chunk objective buys in training can be undone at execution. If stochastic sampling selects the left route at one query and the right route at the next, blending the overlapping chunks across the seam averages the two routes and resurrects the mode averaging that action chunking was chosen to eliminate ([[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/02 Teaching the Machine/06 Policy Training/V4-06.13 Source References#^ref-black2025realtime|Black, Galliker, et al. 2025]]).

↩︎ Temporal ensembling of overlapping chunks, and real-time chunking, which freezes the committed prefix and generates the rest of the next chunk to agree with it, are established in **[[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/01 Machine Anatomy/03 The Cognitive Brain/V4-03.06 Chunk Supply and Evidence Validity#^sec-brain-action-chunking|Action chunking]]**.

Chunking changes how often the policy replans, but does not supply recovery states absent from the demonstrations. Executing ${H_{\text{exec}}}$ actions between queries reduces the number of query boundaries to ${S_{\text{eff}}} = {S}/{H_{\text{exec}}}$ while leaving disturbances within each execution window to the lower-level feedback system. This count alone does not convert the imitation-learning cost bound $\mathcal{O}({S}^2 {\epsilon})$ into a task-survival curve, the probability that an episode reaches the goal without entering an unrecoverable state, plotted against the horizon. ACT ([[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/02 Teaching the Machine/06 Policy Training/V4-06.13 Source References#^ref-zhao2023learning|Zhao et al. 2023]]) and receding-horizon diffusion ([[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/02 Teaching the Machine/06 Policy Training/V4-06.13 Source References#^ref-chi2024diffusionpolicy|Chi et al. 2025]]) change temporal execution; DAgger ([[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/02 Teaching the Machine/06 Policy Training/V4-06.13 Source References#^ref-ross2011reduction|Ross et al. 2011]]) changes the training distribution by obtaining labels on learner-visited states. These are separate ways to improve behavior, and their closed-loop benefits must be measured on the task. Neither the drift sketch of [[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/02 Teaching the Machine/06 Policy Training/V4-06.03 Policy Training Regimes#^fig-06-compounding-error|figure 1]] nor the imitation-learning cost bound establishes a task-survival probability or ranks learning algorithms.

Increasing dataset size, model capacity, or tuning can reduce the one-step error rate on evaluated observations and therefore lower the chance of a first policy error during a fixed horizon. That improvement does not characterize what happens after an error. An external disturbance, uncalibrated payload, or voltage sag can also move the machine into weakly supported states without a policy disagreement. Recovery data, measured dynamics, and independent runtime limits determine whether either event remains within the physical envelope.

Drift off the demonstrated states raises no alarm of its own, so detecting it before physical contact cannot rely on the offline training loss. A feature-distance or uncertainty monitor can flag unfamiliar observations, but its threshold needs validation against missed detections and false alarms. For the latch, an independently measured plate position and approach condition provide a more direct check of the declared cells. A purely geometric monitor is weaker. One that accepts any state inside the convex hull of the demonstrated coordinates, a convex stand-in for the support hull of **[[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/02 Teaching the Machine/05 Physical Data/V4-05.08 Collection Policy Coverage#^sec-data-policy-coverage|Collection Policy Coverage]]**, still admits intermediate states that no demonstration visited. Nothing guarantees that those states are physically admissible or dynamically stable. Predicting an entire ${H}$-step chunk in a multimodal decoder solves the single-step averaging problem, but only if the hardware can finish evaluating that decoder before the next chunk is due.

### Decoder Latency and Proposal Deadlines

^sec-training-decoder-costs

Selecting a coherent route around an obstacle or into a latch does no good if the decoder finishes computing the trajectory after the actuator deadline has already passed. A decoder converts the model’s representation into an action or action sequence; iterative decoders repeatedly refine a candidate before returning it. Their equations describe how a candidate is generated, while the execution decision depends on the wall-clock time those dependent model evaluations consume. The central comparison is between measured proposal latency and the deadline of the buffer that feeds the permission path.

The decoders that fix mode averaging buy that fix with time. A generative head escapes the regression mean by sampling one demonstrated route, but it reaches its sample through a chain of dependent refinements, and each refinement is another model evaluation that must finish before the next proposal is due ([[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/02 Teaching the Machine/06 Policy Training/V4-06.03 Policy Training Regimes#^fig-06-diffusion-denoising|figure 2]]). The proposal then still has to cross from the application processor to the safety microcontroller’s own buffer before the permission path can check it, so the decoder’s time comes out of a budget it shares with transport and admission.

The decoder families themselves, from binned and compressed action tokens to parallel heads and iterative action experts, are introduced in **[[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/01 Machine Anatomy/03 The Cognitive Brain/V4-03.04 Learned Representations#^sec-brain-learned-representations|Learned Representations]]**. What matters for training is their dependency structure. A diffusion policy ([[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/02 Teaching the Machine/06 Policy Training/V4-06.13 Source References#^ref-chi2024diffusionpolicy|Chi et al. 2025]]) or a flow-matching expert ([[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/02 Teaching the Machine/06 Policy Training/V4-06.13 Source References#^ref-lipman2022flow|Lipman et al. 2022]]; [[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/02 Teaching the Machine/06 Policy Training/V4-06.13 Source References#^ref-black2024pi0|Black et al. 2024]]) refines a candidate chunk through evaluations that each consume the previous candidate. An autoregressive vision-language-action policy ([[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/02 Teaching the Machine/06 Policy Training/V4-06.13 Source References#^ref-kim2024openvla|Kim et al. 2024]]) generates its action tokens one after another. In each case the dependent evaluations run in sequence. None of these constructions guarantees collision-free or actuator-feasible output, and the diffusion and flow-matching objectives are developed in **[[05 Reference/Volume IV - Physical AI/V4 Machine Learning Background#^sec-appendix-ml-diffusion|Continuous trajectory diffusion and flow matching]]**. Some post-training recipes remove the token sequence from the run-time path altogether by training a separate action expert ([[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/02 Teaching the Machine/06 Policy Training/V4-06.03 Policy Training Regimes#^sec-training-pretrain-post-train|section 6.2.8]]). For the latch decision, the relevant consequence is how many dependent evaluations yield adequate action quality. The quantity that decides the execution arrangement is complete proposal latency, perception included, measured against the actual proposal deadline.

The number and dependency of decoder evaluations constrain proposal latency; the implementation and loaded hardware determine its measured value. The total wall-clock duration of a single policy inference cycle ${t_{\text{infer}}}$ is the sum of perceptual feature extraction and iterative generative trajectory sampling:

$$
{t_{\text{infer}}} = {t_{\text{enc}}}({\mathbf{o}}_{t}) + \sum_{k=1}^{{N_{\text{eval}}}} {t_{\text{pass}}}\left({\mathbf{A}^k}, {k}\right) \le {T_{\text{deadline}}}, \tag{4}
$$

 Here ${t_{\text{enc}}}$ is the visual backbone and proprioceptive encoding latency, and ${N_{\text{eval}}}$ is the number of dependent decoder evaluations (denoising or ODE solver steps). The term ${t_{\text{pass}}}$ is the latency of one forward pass of the action head (one denoising or solver step, not the whole-model evaluation time ${t_{\text{infer}}}$ of **[[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/01 Machine Anatomy/03 The Cognitive Brain/V4-03.05 The Cost of a Fresh Evaluation#^eq-brain-eval-latency|equation 3.4]]**). The chunk ${\mathbf{A}^k}$ is the candidate that the ${k}$-th evaluation refines, and ${T_{\text{deadline}}}$ is the real-time execution deadline.

^eq-training-decoder-latency

The encoder runs once per proposal, whereas each dependent decoder evaluation adds work. **[[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/01 Machine Anatomy/03 The Cognitive Brain/V4-03.06 Chunk Supply and Evidence Validity#^sec-brain-action-chunking|Action chunking]]** prices that work for each decoder family on the machine’s processor, where re-reading a large action head on every evaluation can dominate it. Training decides the two quantities those prices depend on, the head and its evaluation count ${N_{\text{eval}}}$, so the question here is whether a head chosen for its multimodal fit also meets the latch’s deadlines.

The latch policies avoid repeated evaluations: `latch-bc-01` decodes each chunk with the machine’s 80M-parameter ACT network in one pass, within the 40 ms p99 inference target. Consider instead replacing its decoder with an illustrative diffusion head, the kind that multimodal latch demonstrations might motivate. The head is sampled in ${N_{\text{eval}}} =$ 16 steps by the denoising diffusion implicit model (DDIM) sampler, a deterministic sampler whose step count sets the number of dependent evaluations. Stipulate a visual encoding latency of ${t_{\text{enc}}} =$ 8 ms and a total inference duration ${t_{\text{infer}}} \approx$ 48 ms once all evaluation work is included. These stipulated inputs define the comparison below. A measurement of the real head replaces them, and that measurement can only sit at or above the weight-streaming floor of **[[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/01 Machine Anatomy/03 The Cognitive Brain/V4-03.05 The Cost of a Fresh Evaluation#^sec-brain-memory-wall|The memory wall]]**.

Two deadlines apply, the setpoint step for a decoder that must supply every tick itself and the renewal period for one that runs beside an admitted prefix. The lease rule of **[[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/01 Machine Anatomy/04 The Nervous System/V4-04.10 The Handoffs Matrix#^sec-nervous-coupled-budget|Coupling the distance, lease, and supply budgets]]** adds an allowance for renewal jitter. That allowance belongs to the rule’s compute side, the renewal period plus jitter that the lease must exceed. Transport across the black-channel link, the permission tick, and the age of the evidence at admission are charged on its other side, against the stopping budget. The slack computed below is therefore the decoder’s alone. A slower decoder pays on that side too, because its inference time ages the evidence it is admitted on.

**Napkin Math 6.1: The renewal slack**

**Problem**: Can the stipulated diffusion head replace the one-pass ACT decoder of `latch-bc-01`, either synchronously or asynchronously, and how much of a stretched renewal can each decoder absorb?

**Variables**:

- Setpoint step ${\Delta t_{\text{step}}} =$ 25 ms (chosen) and renewal period ${T_{\text{chunk}}} =$ 50 ms (derived, two steps)
- ACT inference 40 ms at p99, including vision (illustrative target)
- Diffusion head: ${N_{\text{eval}}} =$ 16, ${t_{\text{enc}}} =$ 8 ms, ${t_{\text{infer}}} =$ 48 ms (illustrative, stipulated)
- Flow-matching candidate: ${N_{\text{eval}}} =$ 4 at the same per-evaluation cost (illustrative)
- Renewal-jitter allowance 5 ms (chosen, lease rule)

**Math**:

1.  **Per-evaluation cost** ([[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/02 Teaching the Machine/06 Policy Training/V4-06.03 Policy Training Regimes#^eq-training-decoder-latency|equation 4]]): ${t_{\text{pass}}} =$ (48 ms $-$ 8 ms)/16 $=$ 2.5 ms.
2.  **Synchronous step**: The diffusion head’s 48 ms exceeds the 25 ms step. The flow candidate needs 8 ms $+$ 4 $\times$ 2.5 ms $=$ 18 ms and fits it.
3.  **Renewal slack**: ACT leaves 50 ms $-$ 40 ms $=$ 10 ms; the diffusion head leaves 50 ms $-$ 48 ms $=$ 2 ms.
4.  **Stretched renewal**: 48 ms $+$ 5 ms $-$ 50 ms $=$ 3 ms past the renewal for the diffusion head, while ACT ends at 40 ms $+$ 5 ms, inside the period.

**Result**: The diffusion head can run only asynchronously, and a single jitter-sized stretch carries it past its renewal.

**Systems insight**: Renewal slack, rather than mean inference latency, dictates real-time schedulability. When a decoder’s slack drops below the jitter allowance, a single tail-latency stretch carries it past its renewal, and the active prefix keeps playing on evidence that grows older with every tick. The lease contract, not the neural decoder, then sets the hard safety bound on how long an admitted prefix may continue actuating the arm against aging sensor evidence.

The notebook separates sampler choice from execution authority. The flow candidate becomes a viable synchronous choice only if action quality, scheduling, transfer, and admission also fit, and a faster decoder does not remove the permission check. Its evaluation count is the quantity to compare, not its step count. A higher-order solver can use fewer integration steps while performing more network evaluations; a standard fourth-order Runge-Kutta (RK4) solver evaluates four stages per step, so compare that total work with the single-stage alternative before choosing it. Step distillation, which trains a sampler to reach the same chunk in fewer evaluations, or a different solver is useful when it reduces measured latency without losing the recovery behavior the latch needs.

The slower candidate can still generate chunks asynchronously against the 50 ms renewal cadence while the execution path plays an admitted prefix, provided loaded timing evidence confirms the schedule. Because generating an action chunk supplies candidate trajectory setpoints without conferring authorization to execute that entire window unchecked, the lease selected in **[[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/01 Machine Anatomy/04 The Nervous System/V4-04.05 Chunk Lease Sizing from the Stopping Budget#^sec-nervous-lease-sizing|Chunk Lease Sizing from the Stopping Budget]]** bounds active permission separately from renewal cadence and evidence age. Execution renews only when fresh sensor evidence arrives paired with a valid replacement proposal. Stamping a new timestamp header over stale sensor buffers therefore cannot extend execution authority. An invalid new proposal leaves an active prefix in place only while that prefix’s own state, evidence, and lease remain valid. Once the lease expires, the execution path invokes a state-dependent, physically qualified fallback rather than an unspecified emergency stop.

On the renewal schedule the two decoders differ in slack, not in mean latency ([[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/02 Teaching the Machine/06 Policy Training/V4-06.03 Policy Training Regimes#^fig-06-decoder-timing|figure 3]]). When a stretched diffusion run misses its renewal boundary, nothing in the decoder notices; the chunk lease is the only mechanism that bounds how long the previous prefix keeps playing.

**Diagnosing the failure**

**Mode averaging vs. compounding error**: A cloned navigation policy sometimes steers straight at the column. A cloned latch policy, on other runs, drifts until the gripper binds on a sagged door. ### Offline Reinforcement Learning and Out-Of-Distribution Actions

^sec-training-offline-rl

The decoder budget settles how fast a policy trained on recorded data can propose actions. The next question is what happens when the recorded archive mixes clean door openings with hesitant teleoperation, scripted routines, and aborted trials. Behavioral cloning copies every trajectory in such a log with equal supervised weight, good and bad alike. Running standard Q-learning ([[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/02 Teaching the Machine/06 Policy Training/V4-06.13 Source References#^ref-watkins1992q|Watkins and Dayan 1992]]) on the same static archive to cherry-pick the high-reward segments triggers a worse failure. The value network invents high returns for actions never tried in the log, and the deployed policy greedily selects ungrounded maneuvers that stall a joint or strike a fixture boundary. **Offline reinforcement learning (Batch RL)** applies the return and value quantities of [[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/02 Teaching the Machine/06 Policy Training/V4-06.03 Policy Training Regimes#^sec-training-rl-primer|section 6.2.1]] to a fixed transition log ${\mathcal{D}_{\text{demo}}} = \{({s}_{t}, {\mathbf{a}}_t, {r}_t, {s}_{t+1})\}$ without active hardware exploration. It uses rewards to stitch together high-performing composite trajectories from disjoint, imperfect demonstrations while suppressing **out-of-distribution (OOD) action overestimation** ([[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/02 Teaching the Machine/06 Policy Training/V4-06.13 Source References#^ref-kumar2020conservative|Kumar et al. 2020]]).

In standard Q-learning, the algorithm estimates the value of future states by asking its value network to predict the highest possible return it could achieve from the next step, which is [[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/02 Teaching the Machine/06 Policy Training/V4-06.03 Policy Training Regimes#^eq-training-bellman|equation 2]] with a maximum over next actions in place of the policy’s expectation. That maximum is taken over the entire continuous action space. For actions that were never executed in the logging dataset, the network has received no direct value-fitting target and often outputs erroneously large value estimates due to generalization variance, predicting a high return for an untried physical maneuver. The algorithm greedily selects these spurious peaks, propagating over-optimistic value errors backwards until the deployed policy commands unverified motions. Because the distinction from cloning is the selection rule $\pi({s}) = \arg\max_{{\mathbf{a}}} {Q}({s}, {\mathbf{a}})$, offline learning needs a way to limit that optimism without obtaining new transitions from the machine.

To reduce value overestimation without querying the physical environment, **Conservative Q-learning (CQL)** adds a conservative penalty to value fitting ([[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/02 Teaching the Machine/06 Policy Training/V4-06.13 Source References#^ref-kumar2020conservative|Kumar et al. 2020]]). The objective below has two parts. The bracketed term discourages optimism about unsupported actions, and the squared term fits the reward observed in logged transitions together with estimated future reward. Here ${\mathcal{A}}$ is the action space, ${Q}$ the value function being fitted, ${\hat{Q}}$ a fixed target estimate during this update, and ${\alpha_{\text{CQL}}}$ the penalty weight:

$$
\min_{Q} {\alpha_{\text{CQL}}} \cdot \mathbb{E}_{{s} \sim {\mathcal{D}_{\text{demo}}}} \left[ \ln \int_{{\mathcal{A}}} \exp({Q}({s}, {\mathbf{a}}))\,{d}{\mathbf{a}} - \mathbb{E}_{{\mathbf{a}} \sim {\mathcal{D}_{\text{demo}}}}\left[ {Q}({s}, {\mathbf{a}}) \right] \right] + \frac{1}{2} \mathbb{E}_{({s}, {\mathbf{a}}, {r}, {s'}) \sim {\mathcal{D}_{\text{demo}}}} \left[ \left( {Q}({s}, {\mathbf{a}}) - {\mathcal{B}}^* {\hat{Q}}({s}, {\mathbf{a}}) \right)^2 \right],
$$

 where ${\mathcal{B}}^* {\hat{Q}}({s}, {\mathbf{a}}) = {r}({s}, {\mathbf{a}}) + {\gamma} \max_{{\mathbf{a}'}} {\hat{Q}}({s'}, {\mathbf{a}'})$ is the empirical Bellman target operator and ${\alpha_{\text{CQL}}} > 0$ balances value conservatism against Bellman error minimization.[[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/02 Teaching the Machine/06 Policy Training/V4-06.14 Footnotes#^fn6|⁶]]

This conservative penalty reshapes value estimates across the continuous action space rather than establishing an absolute physical execution boundary:

1.  The logarithmic integral term $\ln \int_{{\mathcal{A}}} \exp({Q}({s}, {\mathbf{a}}))\,{d}{\mathbf{a}}$ acts as a soft-maximum that actively pushes *down* on Q-values across the entire action space, aggressively depressing values in unvisited action regions where function approximation variance would otherwise create spurious positive spikes.
2.  The dataset expectation $\mathbb{E}_{{\mathbf{a}} \sim {\mathcal{D}_{\text{demo}}}}[{Q}({s}, {\mathbf{a}})]$ pushes *up* on Q-values exclusively for state-action pairs executed during the logging campaign.
3.  When ${\alpha_{\text{CQL}}} = 0$, the conservative penalty disappears and policy extraction uses $\arg\max_{{\mathbf{a}}} {Q}({s}, {\mathbf{a}})$ without that correction. With ${\alpha_{\text{CQL}}} > 0$, unsupported actions face a value penalty, but they are not categorically excluded. Physical action limits and checks of operating conditions remain obligations of the independent permission path.

Implicit Q-learning avoids the overestimation at its source by never evaluating an action outside the dataset; it estimates the value of the best logged actions with an expectile regression ([[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/02 Teaching the Machine/06 Policy Training/V4-06.13 Source References#^ref-kostrikov2022iql|Kostrikov et al. 2021]]). In robotics, offline learning now serves mostly as a starting point. A policy pretrained on the log is fine-tuned online, and calibrating its value estimates during pretraining keeps the first online updates from unlearning it ([[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/02 Teaching the Machine/06 Policy Training/V4-06.13 Source References#^ref-nakamoto2023calql|Nakamoto et al. 2023]]). Without this calibration, a conservative critic depresses the estimated values of logged expert actions below the true return scale of the physical environment, so when online rollouts begin, even mediocre exploratory actions receive real environment returns that exceed those depressed offline estimates and cause early policy updates to shift away from the demonstrated behavior until the critic catches up.

Applied to the latch, the retained archive holds successes and labeled tripwire failures, both rewardable, so a conservative objective could reweight nominal behavior toward the cleaner openings. It cannot supply displaced-plate competence. Those cells are known absences among the successes and the failures alike, and no offline objective extracts behavior from states the log never visited. When the task archive itself is sparse, teams often try to borrow that missing competence from a foundation model pretrained across other robots.

### Pretrained Generalist Policies and Post-Training

^sec-training-pretrain-post-train

A model pretrained on hundreds of thousands of episodes from other robots can move smoothly toward a displaced strike plate even when the target campaign never demonstrated that offset, making it easy to mistake broad visual and motor familiarity for verified support on this arm, door, and contact surface. Whereas `latch-bc-01` starts from randomly initialized weights and learns only from the latch campaign, such a **generalist policy** starts from pooled multi-robot data and is then **post-trained** on demonstrations of the target task. The reference manipulator already carries such a checkpoint as its intent model.

↩︎ The intent model, the slower of the machine’s two learned proposers, is established in **[[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/01 Machine Anatomy/03 The Cognitive Brain/V4-03.02 The Embodied Brain#^sec-brain-embodied|The Embodied Brain]]**.

That checkpoint is OpenVLA-7B, a 7B-parameter vision-language-action model trained on 970K robot episodes from the Open X-Embodiment collection ([[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/02 Teaching the Machine/06 Policy Training/V4-06.13 Source References#^ref-kim2024openvla|Kim et al. 2024]]; [[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/02 Teaching the Machine/06 Policy Training/V4-06.13 Source References#^ref-open-x-embodiment-collaboration2024open-c3f8bd04|Open X-Embodiment Collaboration et al. 2024]]). Chunk-level generalists are built the same way ([[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/02 Teaching the Machine/06 Policy Training/V4-06.07 Real-Robot Fine-Tuning and Residual Control#^tbl-06-state-of-practice|table 3]]). Post-training may update every weight, a low-rank adapter, or only a new action head, and the base may first be retrained on the target machine’s action space.

Pretraining transfers broad visual and motor competence across varied domains. A base network trained on thousands of grasps, door mechanisms, and cluttered tabletop scenes often requires substantially fewer task demonstrations and demonstrates greater resilience to varying illumination, visual distractors, and object geometries than a policy trained from scratch. Pretraining supplies no operational evidence for this plant, however, and the scenario-table join of **[[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/02 Teaching the Machine/05 Physical Data/V4-05.08 Collection Policy Coverage#^sec-data-curse-dimensionality-recoverability-geometry|Choosing a tractable coverage table]]** shows exactly where the gap lies. The scenario table for this door on this machine has 6 cells. The pretraining episodes were collected on other arms, fixtures, and grippers under embodiment contracts the table does not describe, so none of them joins to any latch cell. The post-trained candidate therefore inherits only the campaign’s own support, 1 cell out of 6 with 5 known absences. It may execute the nominal cell more reliably and may even propose plausible maneuvers at the displaced plate, but those proposals are borrowed competence, not evidence. Its declared ODD, the region the policy manifest of [[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/02 Teaching the Machine/06 Policy Training/V4-06.09 The Policy Manifest#^sec-training-policy-manifest|section 6.8]] computes from the join and the simulator omissions, gains no cell from pretraining.

Three dependencies follow, each requiring explicit tracking in the policy manifest. First, the base checkpoint is an external dependency whose underlying pretraining data rarely permits cryptographic digest verification, as its data mixture, filtering heuristics, and training run were permanently fixed by the upstream provider. The manifest therefore records the base checkpoint by the cryptographic digest of its weights and flags its pretraining provenance as a known unknown within the `base_checkpoint` field of [[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/02 Teaching the Machine/06 Policy Training/V4-06.09 The Policy Manifest#^tbl-06-training-schema|table 4]]. Second, the base model’s native action space rarely aligns with the target machine. A generalist checkpoint emitting normalized end-effector velocity deltas on an asynchronous clock must be re-headed or mapped into discrete joint setpoints every 25 ms, exposing the pipeline to the normalization fallacy analyzed in **[[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/02 Teaching the Machine/05 Physical Data/V4-05.10 Cross-Embodiment Transfer#^sec-data-embodiment-contracts|Embodiment contracts and the normalization fallacy]]**. Third, fine-tuning risks catastrophic forgetting. Gradient updates fitting the latch demonstrations can degrade broader capabilities that the base previously mastered but the target task never exercises. The same hazard returns, on a smaller scale, when a policy is later fine-tuned on the machine itself ([[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/02 Teaching the Machine/06 Policy Training/V4-06.07 Real-Robot Fine-Tuning and Residual Control#^sec-training-real-machine-tuning|section 6.6]]); here it is magnified across a large, poorly documented behavioral repertoire. Post-training need not stop at imitation. A pretrained policy can also be improved with reinforcement learning on its own experience, in simulation or on the machine, and [[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/02 Teaching the Machine/06 Policy Training/V4-06.07 Real-Robot Fine-Tuning and Residual Control#^sec-training-rl-post-training|section 6.6.2]] compares those methods by what each one freezes.

The decoder question also carries over, because post-training often changes the head that runs at deployment. A vision-language backbone can be adapted on discrete action tokens while a separate continuous action expert is trained beside it. The expert’s gradient is stopped before it reaches the backbone, so the newly initialized expert does not disturb the pretrained weights. This **knowledge insulation** trained faster and generalized better than letting the expert’s gradient flow into the backbone ([[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/02 Teaching the Machine/06 Policy Training/V4-06.13 Source References#^ref-driess2025knowledge|Driess et al. 2025]]). The run-time decoder is then the one-pass or few-step action expert, not the token sequence. A study of post-training recipes likewise found that action chunking with a plain $\ell_1$ regression head matched iterative decoders on its benchmark while decoding far faster ([[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/02 Teaching the Machine/06 Policy Training/V4-06.13 Source References#^ref-kim2025openvlaoft|Kim et al. 2025]]). Either way, the deadline arithmetic of [[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/02 Teaching the Machine/06 Policy Training/V4-06.03 Policy Training Regimes#^sec-training-decoder-costs|section 6.2.6]] governs the post-trained head just as it governs the latch’s own.

Supervised behavioral cloning, action chunking, batch offline RL, and generalist post-training all generate action proposals beyond the state-action support of their logging archives. A low training loss or an inflated Q-value does not constitute physical evidence of safety or success. When an archive lacks the recovery trajectories that a physical perturbation demands, the system must generate new closed-loop experience through direct environment interaction.
