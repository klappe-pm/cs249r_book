# V4-06.06 Physical Channels of the Sim-To-Real Gap

[[Home|Home]] · [[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/Volume 4 Overview|Volume Overview]] · [[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/02 Teaching the Machine/06 Policy Training/V4-06 Policy Training|V4-06 Policy Training]]

^sec-training-sim-to-real-gap

A robotic simulation rarely diverges from physical hardware in only one channel. In the cage-door latch mechanism, for instance, the simulator departs from reality in two different ways: a penalty spring far softer than the physical strike plate, and an idealized command path that omits transport lag. Collapsing these discrepancies into a single scalar trajectory error blends them into an uninformative aggregate that obscures both mechanisms. Errors in different physical channels carry different units, propagate along distinct paths within the closed loop, and trigger different failure modes. The sim-to-real mismatch must therefore be treated as a coupled vector of physical components spanning dynamics, sensing, appearance, and timing. Sizing this vector requires an explicit accounting of physical errors against task tolerances, evaluated from paired command-response traces recorded under identical excitations. Each channel demands its own mitigation and runtime interceptor. [[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/02 Teaching the Machine/06 Policy Training/V4-06.06 Physical Channels of the Sim-To-Real Gap#^tbl-06-reality-gap|Table 2]] lists these interceptors across actuator dynamics, contact mechanics, sensing, and timing; appearance mismatch is sized separately and charted in panel (d) of [[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/02 Teaching the Machine/06 Policy Training/V4-06.06 Physical Channels of the Sim-To-Real Gap#^fig-06-sim2real-gap|figure 7]].

**Definition 6.2: Sim-to-real reality gap vector**

***Sim-to-real reality gap vector*** ${\boldsymbol{\Delta}_{\text{sim2real}}} = [{\Delta_{\text{dyn}}}, {\Delta_{\text{sens}}}, {\Delta_{\text{vis}}}, {\Delta_{\text{lat}}}]$ is the coupled, multi-channel decomposition of physical discrepancies between simulated and hardware environments spanning contact mechanics (${\Delta_{\text{dyn}}}$), sensor transduction and noise (${\Delta_{\text{sens}}}$), visual appearance (${\Delta_{\text{vis}}}$), and asynchronous transport latency and jitter (${\Delta_{\text{lat}}}$).

1.  **Significance**: Treating the “reality gap” as a scalar difficulty metric or pure visual domain mismatch causes engineers to overlook dynamical and temporal divergences. Discrepancies in actuator back-EMF, gearbox compliance, or bus jitter deplete feedback stability margins before the policy completes its first physical control step.
2.  **Distinction**: An inaccurate parameter value (such as a 10 percent error in link mass) preserves the causal differential equations of motion, whereas an omitted mechanism (such as zero-delay communication or unmodeled Coulomb stick-slip) entirely severs physical feedback paths, leading policies to exploit non-physical simulator artifacts.
3.  **Common pitfall**: Relying on domain randomization alone to bridge a large gap. Randomization covers only the mechanisms the simulator models; for a memoryless policy, indistinguishable parameter instances can favor a compromise or leave low-probability cases poorly served.

Dynamics mismatch measures discrepancies between commanded actuator effort and the resulting physical motion or contact force across the operating envelope. Its contact case is the impulse-spike example of [[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/02 Teaching the Machine/06 Policy Training/V4-06.05 The Simulator as a System Component#^sec-training-contact-exploit|section 6.4.2]]. A simulator with rigid links, zero backlash, and stiff contact predicts an abrupt force onset. Real drivetrain compliance and drive delays soften and delay that rise. A policy that keyed its braking to the sharp onset keeps driving into the hard stop. The mismatch has the opposite sign of the soft-spring exploit, where the simulator was softer than the real plate rather than stiffer, and the two together are why the dynamics channel has to be measured in both directions.

Sensing mismatch measures discrepancies between the true physical state of the machine and the observation vector delivered to the policy network. Simulated range and depth sensors typically yield clean, uniformly quantized measurements corrupted only by independent zero-mean Gaussian noise. Physical sensors, by contrast, suffer from optical calibration bias, structural noise, temperature-dependent drift, and sustained dropout bursts caused by specular reflections on polished surfaces. An uncalibrated range bias shifts the physical standoff distance where the controller transitions from rapid approach to gentle force regulation. A multi-frame dropout burst leaves the policy momentarily blind during high-speed descent, creating an unobserved window where collisions occur before feedback can intervene.

Image-space distance metrics such as Mean Squared Error (MSE), Peak Signal-to-Noise Ratio (PSNR), or Fréchet Inception Distance (FID) cannot reliably qualify appearance transfer. These summary statistics fail to weight visual discrepancies by policy sensitivity, that is, by the policy Jacobian $\partial {\mathbf{a}} / \partial {\mathbf{o}}$ relating actions to observations. Consequently, a benign aggregate difference can mask a task-critical localization error. A photorealistic rendering with ray-traced lighting might register a high pixel MSE because of background shadows or wall textures that the policy’s visual encoder discards. Conversely, a sub-millimeter pixel shift along the silhouette of an assembly peg might alter the overall image MSE by less than 0.1 percent. Yet that subtle shift moves the policy’s predicted insertion coordinate outside the mechanical chamfer tolerance, driving the peg directly into a rigid fixture shoulder. Appearance mismatch must therefore be quantified by evaluating policy-relevant perceptual representations (detected keypoints, surface normal tokens, or latent feature centroids) under controlled variations in physical geometry, illumination, material reflectance, and camera exposure.

Timing mismatch governs the temporal alignment between observation capture, policy evaluation, and actuation. Identical state transitions occurring at different moments in physical time yield entirely different closed-loop dynamics. A simulation that executes observation, inference, and actuation instantaneously at the beginning of each discrete integration step omits camera sensor exposure times, PCIe bus serialization, neural network tail latency, and EtherCAT fieldbus transport, along with their cycle-to-cycle jitter. Every millisecond of omitted transport delay translates directly into physical distance the mechanism traverses open-loop before corrective actuation begins. Unmodeled delay also introduces frequency-dependent phase lag. Near gain crossover, the added lag consumes the available open-loop phase margin. Whether the loop remains stable depends on the sampled plant, compensation, jitter, and total response; **[[05 Reference/Volume IV - Physical AI/V4 Control and Dynamics#^sec-appendix-control-zoh|Discrete-time sampling and zero-order hold phase lag]]** quantifies the delay contribution.

A single illustrative scenario, kept separate from the latch so that its numbers are not mistaken for latch measurements, shows the four channels diverging at once ([[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/02 Teaching the Machine/06 Policy Training/V4-06.06 Physical Channels of the Sim-To-Real Gap#^fig-06-sim2real-gap|figure 7]]). A linear drive approaches a surface at 0.20 m/s and regulates contact force to 10 N. In the dynamics channel, an unmodeled drive delay of 8 ms and a softer physical contact pad cut the force rise from 500 N/s in simulation to 250 N/s on hardware (a rate the force regulator sets as it ramps toward its target, far below the free-impact rate ${k_{\text{contact}}} {v}$ of the latch analysis). The target force therefore arrives at 40 ms instead of 20 ms. The drive overtravels by 3 mm, and a hard current-limit shutdown trips. In the sensing channel, a calibration bias of +3.2 mm triggers premature deceleration, and a specular dropout burst of 4 frames blinds the controller during final descent. In appearance, a pixel difference of only 5.5 percent hides a 6 mm lateral shift in the contact-patch estimate, more than the 2 mm chamfer tolerance. In timing, an illustrative p99 actuation delay of 49.5 ms carries the mechanism 9.9 mm past the point the simulator predicts with its idealized 0 ms observation-to-actuation delay. The simulator’s 20 ms integration step is a numerical parameter, not pipeline latency, and cannot be subtracted from the physical transport delay. Each channel fails in its own units (milliseconds of force rise, millimeters of bias, millimeters of patch shift, millimeters of unobserved travel). A scalar loss can report that hardware departs from simulation but cannot say which channel failed, and separating the channels is what lets [[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/02 Teaching the Machine/06 Policy Training/V4-06.06 Physical Channels of the Sim-To-Real Gap#^tbl-06-reality-gap|table 2]] give each one its own paired-trace acceptance test and its own permission-path interceptor.

The first line of defense against dynamics mismatch is empirical measurement. **System identification**[[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/02 Teaching the Machine/06 Policy Training/V4-06.14 Footnotes#^fn9|⁹]] calibrates the simulator’s parameter values (link masses, friction coefficients, torque constants, and transport delays) against paired command-response traces recorded on the physical robot. When a physical subsystem resists accurate first-principles modeling, engineers can replace analytical equations with data-driven approximations. In quadrupedal locomotion work, Hwangbo et al. ([[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/02 Teaching the Machine/06 Policy Training/V4-06.13 Source References#^ref-hwangbo2019learning|2019]]) trained an *actuator network* that predicted physical joint torque directly from a rolling history of position errors and joint velocities, executing this neural network inside the simulation loop instead of an idealized torque actuator. Similarly, delta-action residual models learn state-dependent corrections from physical rollouts, adjusting simulated commands so that simulated trajectories track physical behavior ([[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/02 Teaching the Machine/06 Policy Training/V4-06.13 Source References#^ref-he2025asap|He et al. 2025]]). In the cage-door latch task, these techniques help reconcile actuator response and bus transport latency, yet they share one limit. A learned correction model is valid only across the empirical state distribution visited during data collection, and outside that distribution the model is uncharacterized. Consequently, any learned simulator correction must enter the policy manifest’s omission log with its own explicitly bounded domain of validity.

Repeatedly training against a single point estimate of physical parameters, such as an assumed strike-plate stiffness, encourages a policy to overfit to that exact numerical value. **Domain randomization**[[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/02 Teaching the Machine/06 Policy Training/V4-06.14 Footnotes#^fn10|¹⁰]] addresses parameter uncertainty by perturbing modeled simulation parameters across plausible distributions during training, forcing the policy to encounter varied physical responses ([[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/02 Teaching the Machine/06 Policy Training/V4-06.13 Source References#^ref-tobin2017domain|Tobin et al. 2017]]; [[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/02 Teaching the Machine/06 Policy Training/V4-06.13 Source References#^ref-peng2018sim|Peng et al. 2018]]). However, sampling from a parameter distribution merely defines training weights; it does not constitute a formal certificate of robustness across the parameter set. How effectively a policy handles this diversity depends heavily on whether its architecture incorporates memory.

In standard reinforcement learning, a policy maximizes expected return within a single nominal simulation environment parameterized by the static vector ${\boldsymbol{\xi}}_0$:

$$
{J}({\theta}; {\boldsymbol{\xi}}_0) = \mathbb{E}_{{s_{0:T}}, {\mathbf{a}_{0:T}} \sim {p_\theta}(\cdot \mid {\boldsymbol{\xi}}_0)} \left[ \sum_{k=0}^{T} {\gamma}^{k} {r}({s}_{k}, {\mathbf{a}}_{k}) \right].
$$

 Domain randomization reformulates the physical environment as a distribution over plausible Markov Decision Processes parameterized by physical vector ${\boldsymbol{\xi}} \sim {p}({\boldsymbol{\xi}})$, where ${\boldsymbol{\xi}}$ encompasses link masses, centers of mass, surface friction coefficients, joint damping, transport delays, and sensor noise variances. The domain randomization objective maximizes the expected return marginalized across this distribution:

$$
{J_{\text{DR}}}({\theta}) = \mathbb{E}_{{\boldsymbol{\xi}} \sim {p}({\boldsymbol{\xi}})} \left[ {J}({\theta}; {\boldsymbol{\xi}}) \right] = \int_{{\Xi}} \left( \mathbb{E}_{{s_{0:T}}, {\mathbf{a}_{0:T}} \sim {p_\theta}(\cdot \mid {\boldsymbol{\xi}})} \left[ \sum_{k=0}^{T} {\gamma}^{k} {r}({s}_{k}, {\mathbf{a}}_{k}) \right] \right) {p}({\boldsymbol{\xi}})\,{d}{\boldsymbol{\xi}}. \tag{10}
$$

 Marginalizing over ${p}({\boldsymbol{\xi}})$ incentivizes policies to maintain performance across sampled physical configurations rather than collapsing to a single operating point. Nevertheless, optimizing expected return can mask catastrophic control failures occurring in low-probability regions of the distribution; high average reward does not imply uniform stability across the support ${\Xi}$.

^eq-training-dr-objective

An alternative formulation adopts a minimax objective from robust control, optimizing performance against the worst-case modeled configuration:

$$
\max_{\theta} \min_{{\boldsymbol{\xi}} \in {\Xi}} {J}({\theta}; {\boldsymbol{\xi}}).
$$

 Here, the inner minimization identifies the worst-case return within the bounded uncertainty set ${\Xi}$, while the outer optimization searches for policy parameters ${\theta}$ that maximize this floor. Minimax optimization targets the worst return within the modeled set, but this remains an optimization statement over modeled rewards rather than a physical stability proof. Neither distribution-averaging nor minimax optimization can protect against physical mechanisms that the simulator omits.

Two constraints govern domain randomization. First, hardware operating outside the sampled support (${\boldsymbol{\xi}_{\text{real}}} \notin \text{supp}({p}({\boldsymbol{\xi}}))$) is entirely unrepresented during training. Second, parameter randomization cannot compensate for structural omissions in the physics engine: no amount of variation in link mass or friction will expose a policy to the unmodeled lost motion of a harmonic drive or the preemptive jitter of a real-time operating system. Within the modeled support, the operational effect of broad randomization depends on whether the policy maintains temporal state. A memoryless feedforward network receives only the current observation ${\mathbf{o}}_{t}$. When different parameter instances produce indistinguishable observations but require different actions, the policy cannot adapt its choice to the active instance. Optimizing average return may then favor a compromise, and neither conservatism nor stability across the envelope is guaranteed. Conversely, a policy that conditions on a temporal history of observations and actions can infer physical plant dynamics online from the system’s transient response, effectively performing implicit system identification. For this reason, recurrent policies trained across randomized dynamics transfer with substantially higher fidelity than memoryless architectures ([[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/02 Teaching the Machine/06 Policy Training/V4-06.13 Source References#^ref-peng2018sim|Peng et al. 2018]]), and memory is what lets a wider randomization range produce adaptation to the real plant rather than conservative detuning.

In engineering practice, parameter distributions are configured through two distinct strategies grounded in different forms of evidence. In *calibration-first* engineering, developers measure nominal masses, torque constants, bus latencies, and friction parameters directly on test benches. Randomization bounds are then derived from known physical mechanisms. For example, motor winding thermal limits determine the envelope of torque-constant variation, and surface degradation models govern friction bounds. These ranges represent verifiable physical claims about the robot and its environment. In contrast, *automatic domain randomization* (ADR) initializes the simulator at calibrated values and dynamically expands parameter ranges as the policy’s training performance at the distribution boundaries exceeds predefined success thresholds ([[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/02 Teaching the Machine/06 Policy Training/V4-06.13 Source References#^ref-akkaya2019solving|OpenAI et al. 2019]]). ADR parameter bounds reflect the limits of what the reinforcement learning optimization can master rather than physical properties of the hardware, often expanding to unphysical extremes. The dexterity achieved by ADR in robotic in-hand manipulation relied on a recurrent policy learning to adapt online to these artificially broad dynamics. Regardless of the scheduling strategy, paired hardware traces must ultimately audit each channel against explicit error budgets before deployment; discrepancies exceeding tolerance require simulator revision rather than blind parameter widening. A randomization range expanded by an automated training curriculum represents an optimization scaffold, not an empirical characterization of the hardware, and therefore cannot expand the declared ODD.

Where domain randomization and simulation fidelity reach their physical limits, deterministic safety mechanisms must enforce system invariants. Every action proposed by a learned policy must pass through an independent permission path before reaching hardware actuators. Within this execution layer, hard force-derivative clamps, torque slew-rate limiters, and velocity-dependent stopping governors restrict commands to the envelope supported by verified sensing and braking capabilities.

↪ The Control Barrier Function filter that minimally corrects unverified policy proposals on every permission tick is developed in **[[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/03 Running the Machine/12 Safety Enforcement/V4-12.08 The Minimal Intervention Principle and Optimization Filters#^sec-enforcement-minimal-intervention|The Minimal Intervention Principle and Optimization Filters]]**.

Decomposing the reality gap channel by channel divides the operating space in two. Inside the region where paired traces match simulation within verified tolerances, the simulated experience counts as support. Every unmodeled phenomenon, uncalibrated parameter, and unrepresented disturbance outside it becomes an entry in the omission log, the policy manifest’s record of what the simulator leaves out ([[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/02 Teaching the Machine/06 Policy Training/V4-06.09 The Policy Manifest#^sec-training-policy-manifest|section 6.8]]). The log therefore holds two kinds of entry, mechanisms the simulator omits and parameters no paired probe has yet calibrated, because an uncalibrated parameter leaves the simulated experience just as unsupported until a probe bounds it. For the latch, one of each heads the log: the strike-plate stiffness, a parameter the penalty spring sets far softer than the plate, and the transport latency of the actuator bus, a mechanism the simulator omits. No paired probe has yet closed either, so the high-speed contact states they govern fall outside what the simulation supports. Each entry also states a requirement for a runtime monitor or safety filter, which must detect when execution leaves the regime where the policy’s simulated training remains valid. Calibrating or randomizing simulator parameters still leaves a second gap: the simulator exposes exact contact forces, friction coefficients, and fixture offsets during training that the physical robot’s onboard sensors cannot directly measure at run time.

### Teacher-Student Privileged State Distillation and Asymmetric Actor-Critic

^sec-training-privileged-distillation

While policy training can exploit privileged simulator state, such as ground-truth contact wrenches, friction coefficients, and exact strike-plate offsets, the onboard policy proposer must execute exclusively from the noisy, bandwidth-limited sensors installed on the physical machine. In actor-critic reinforcement learning, the actor proposes control actions while the critic evaluates expected future returns to compute policy gradients. Systems engineers bridge this sensor gap through two main architectures. *Asymmetric actor-critic* grants privileged observations exclusively to the critic during training. *Teacher-student distillation* first trains a privileged teacher policy and subsequently trains a sensor-limited student policy to imitate the teacher’s actions. Both architectures require validating whether the runtime observation stream delivered to the student contains enough information to reconstruct or compensate for the missing state.

The core challenge stems from **partial observability**, which casts physical robot control as a partially observable Markov decision process (POMDP).[[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/02 Teaching the Machine/06 Policy Training/V4-06.14 Footnotes#^fn11|¹¹]] Identical sequences of measured joint positions and velocities can produce drastically different physical trajectories depending on whether a footpad grips dry concrete or slips on wet oil. A conventional critic ${V_{\phi}}({\mathbf{o}}_{t})$ conditioned solely on instantaneous sensory observations cannot distinguish between these physical regimes, producing high-variance or biased value estimates. In simulation, exposing hidden contact wrenches ${\mathbf{w}}$, friction coefficients ${\mu}$, terrain profiles, and payload inertias directly to the critic stabilizes temporal-difference learning without burdening the onboard runtime actor.

Asymmetric actor-critic and teacher-student distillation deploy privileged information through contrasting computational pipelines ([[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/02 Teaching the Machine/06 Policy Training/V4-06.13 Source References#^ref-pinto2018asymmetric|Pinto et al. 2018]]; [[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/02 Teaching the Machine/06 Policy Training/V4-06.13 Source References#^ref-lee2020learning|Lee et al. 2020]]; [[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/02 Teaching the Machine/06 Policy Training/V4-06.13 Source References#^ref-kumar2021rma|Kumar et al. 2021]]). Under asymmetric actor-critic, the actor receives only the onboard sensor history available at runtime, while the critic receives full privileged simulation state. Because the critic is discarded after training, the deployed actor architecture remains clean and lightweight. However, training an unprivileged actor directly against complex, contact-rich tasks can suffer from high sample complexity.

Distillation splits the work across the proposal boundary ([[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/02 Teaching the Machine/06 Policy Training/V4-06.06 Physical Channels of the Sim-To-Real Gap#^fig-06-privileged-distillation|figure 8]]). Everything privileged stays on the simulation side, and only the frozen student’s proposals, built from the machine’s own sensor history and renewed every 50 ms, cross to the permission path. In Phase 1, the privileged teacher policy $\pi_{\text{teacher}}({\mathbf{a}}_{t} \mid {s_{\text{priv}}}, {\mathbf{o}}_{t})$ learns an effective control strategy via standard reinforcement learning, exploiting full access to contact forces and surface friction. In Phase 2, the student policy $\pi_{\text{student}}({\mathbf{a}}_{t} \mid {\mathbf{o}_{t-H_{\text{hist}}:t}})$ is trained via supervised imitation learning (such as DAgger or behavioral cloning) to reproduce the teacher’s proposed actions using only a sliding history of onboard observations. While this two-phase approach simplifies reinforcement learning exploration, it introduces a transfer bottleneck. If the teacher’s control strategy relies on distinctions that cannot be reconstructed from the onboard observation history ${\mathbf{o}_{t-H_{\text{hist}}:t}}$, the student cannot replicate the teacher’s behavior. Privileged information accelerates training convergence, but it cannot make physically unobservable states identifiable on hardware.

Consider the cage-door latch mechanism. A student network equipped with a temporal history can compare commanded motor efforts against observed joint accelerations over recent timesteps, inferring changes in contact stiffness or friction through the resulting mechanical resistance. However, identical joint kinematics can arise from multiple distinct physical causes, and an unobserved geometric offset in the strike plate cannot be inferred simply because the simulation teacher possessed that knowledge. A temporal convolutional network or transformer encoder can output a learned latent embedding of the environment, but its accuracy must be audited against physical ground truth. Adding auxiliary loss functions to predict privileged parameters during distillation regularizes the representation, but an auxiliary loss cannot create physical information that is absent from the underlying sensor stream.

Selecting the observation history length ${H_{\text{hist}}}$ (distinct from the chunk horizon ${H}$) involves a trade-off in real-time estimator design. A window that is too short cannot filter high-frequency sensor noise or integrate enough kinematic evidence to distinguish sudden payload changes from transient disturbances. Conversely, an excessively long window increases onboard memory footprint, inflates neural inference latency, and risks diluting transient signals, such as the exact moment of initial mechanical contact, under an accumulation of pre-contact free-space observations. System qualification for the cage-door latch policy must verify contact-onset braking dynamics under the chosen history length while accounting for real hardware sensing delays. Buffering a longer temporal history in memory does not inherently add latency to incoming sensor frames, but the temporal encoder must be structured so that the newest observation immediately influences the output action.

**Checkpoint 6.2: Simulator fidelity and transfer**

A simulator supplies cheap experience only for the mechanisms it models, and the evidence that it models them comes from the machine.

**Sizing the gap**

**Parameter error vs. omitted mechanism**: Can you classify the rotor-inertia error, unmodeled backlash, and a missing transport lag of this chapter, say which of them domain randomization can cover, and explain why the latch’s penalty stiffness, a parameter error, still sits in the omission log until a paired probe calibrates it?

**Compliance estimate**: Suppose the push test returned exactly the break-even robot-side stiffness of notebook [6.3](https://mlsysbook.ai/vol4/06_training/06_training.html#nbk-training-soft-spring-exploit). How long after touch would a drive that keeps pushing at the fast approach reach the latch limit, and how does that compare with the plate-only contact yield deadline and the contact loop’s response?

**Crediting the result**

**Randomization range**: Can you explain why a range widened by a success threshold never enlarges the declared ODD, while a range set from a measured mechanism can be checked against the plant?

**Privileged information**: Can you say which of the latch teacher’s privileged quantities the student can recover from its observation history, and why it cannot infer an unobserved plate offset merely because the teacher knew it?

Even after system identification, domain randomization, and privileged state distillation, residual discrepancies persist in high-precision, contact-rich operational regimes. For the latch, both omission-log entries remain open. Engineers can resolve certain localized kinematic and friction mismatches through fine-tuning directly on the physical fixture. Physical training, however, consumes hardware operational life through mechanical wear and reopens qualification claims that depend on changed proposer behavior, timing, interfaces, or physical conditions. Every updated policy checkpoint produced by on-robot training demands fresh qualification evidence before it can be cleared for autonomous operation.
