# V4-05.06 Action Label Selection Across the Control Boundary

[[Home|Home]] · [[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/Volume 4 Overview|Volume Overview]] · [[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/02 Teaching the Machine/05 Physical Data/V4-05 Physical Data|V4-05 Physical Data]]

^sec-data-action-label

Suppose a teleoperator pushes the gripper into the cage-door strike plate aggressively enough to trip a velocity clamp, or stalls against the latch while the wrist motor holds torque. A logger that records only the operator’s raw command teaches the policy to repeat a move the machine refused to execute, whereas a logger that records only the measured encoder velocity teaches it that stalling against a latch is a deliberate command to stop. By the time the gripper contacts the strike plate, a single operator motion has become four different numerical signals across two decoupled processing domains:

- The unprivileged **Linux application processor** executes perception pipelines, handles video frame ingestion, runs teleoperation input mapping, and computes the neural policy forward pass (${\pi_\theta}({\mathbf{o}}_{t})$).
- On the permission side of the proposal boundary, a hard real-time **safety microcontroller (MCU)** runs joint and permission checks at the chosen 1 kHz cadence. Dedicated motor-drive logic closes the faster field-oriented current loop; its current samples remain distinct from the telemetry retained at each permission tick.[[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/02 Teaching the Machine/05 Physical Data/V4-05.17 Footnotes#^fn4|⁴]]

### The Four-Stream Action Tap Tuple

^sec-data-four-stream-action-tap

If a dataset keeps only one signal from those two processing domains, any discrepancy between what the operator asked for and how the arm actually moved becomes impossible to diagnose. A command must therefore be logged at each of the four causal stages it passes through. The **four-stream action tap** $({a_{\text{req}}}, {a_{\text{cmd}}}, {a_{\text{enf}}}, {a_{\text{meas}}})$ records all four in one hardware-synchronized telemetry log ([[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/02 Teaching the Machine/05 Physical Data/V4-05.06 Action Label Selection Across the Control Boundary#^fig-05-action-taps|figure 2]]). Two are logged on the unprivileged Linux host before the black-channel link, and two on the discrete safety MCU after the 1 kHz permission check. Logged together, the taps keep interventions and tracking errors auditable.

![[90 Assets/Volume IV/05 Data/Fig05 Four Stream Action Taps 04cada8f92.svg]]

*Block diagram. On the application processor, a policy or operator box feeds a kinematic mapping box; tap 1, a req, sits between them and tap 2, a cmd, sits after the mapping. A black-channel link with no shared memory crosses the dashed proposal boundary to the discrete safety MCU, which holds the permission path at a 1 kHz tick. Tap 3, a enf, sits after the permission path, which feeds the drives that close the current loops and apply the torque limit. The drives move the plant, joint encoders measure the motion over an interval, and tap 4, a meas, leaves the encoders. All four taps run down to a synchronized telemetry log stamped with hardware timestamps.*

Figure 2: **Four-stream action tap tuple**: Four places a command can be logged, from raw policy intent on the application processor through to measured plant response. Which tap a dataset records decides what the model learns to imitate. The four-tap decomposition is this book’s construct; the teleoperation rigs it instruments follow Zhao et al. ([[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/02 Teaching the Machine/05 Physical Data/V4-05.16 Source References#^ref-zhao2023learning|2023]]) and Wu et al. ([[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/02 Teaching the Machine/05 Physical Data/V4-05.16 Source References#^ref-wu2023gello|2024]]).

^fig-05-action-taps

Dividing telemetry logging across the unprivileged Linux host and the hard real-time MCU captures four distinct physical signals:

- **Tap 1: The requested action (${a_{\text{req}}}$, “Asked”):** Logged in user space on the application processor, this represents unconstrained human operator input (from a leader arm or joystick) or the raw forward-pass output from an unverified policy, tagged with its native coordinate frame and issue timestamp prior to software validation. This proposal reflects unadulterated human or policy intent, but it frequently demands infeasible joint accelerations or violates spatial bounding envelopes.
- **Tap 2: The mapped command (${a_{\text{cmd}}}$, “Mapped”):** The kinematically retargeted setpoint (such as Cartesian end-effector velocities converted to joint space setpoints via inverse kinematics), incorporating coordinate frame transformations, trajectory interpolation, and workspace limits before transmission across the black-channel board link to the safety MCU.
- **Tap 3: The enforced command (${a_{\text{enf}}}$, “Allowed”):** Logged on the bare-metal MCU on the permission side of the boundary, this represents the command after the permission path has checked or modified the proposal. The checks include deterministic Control Barrier Functions (CBFs, safety filters that keep states within a forward-invariant safe set), velocity and acceleration clamps, torque saturation envelopes, and collision guards. Whichever tap a dataset chooses as its label, the imitation loss has the same form; written for the admitted command, it is:

$$
{\mathcal{L}_{\text{policy}}}({\theta}) = \mathbb{E}_{({\mathbf{o}}_{t}, {\mathbf{a}_{\text{enf}, t}}) \sim {\mathcal{D}_{\text{demo}}}} \left[ {\mathcal{L}}({\pi_\theta}({\mathbf{o}}_{t}), {\mathbf{a}_{\text{enf}, t}}) \right],
$$

 Here the training dataset must contain curated, correctly attributed targets. Passing the permission check does not make a command an expert demonstration, because a clamp or emergency stop may be a safeguard’s response to a poor request. Override flags and companion taps determine which records can serve as imitation targets and which remain evidence of intervention.
- **Tap 4: The measured response (${a_{\text{meas}}}$, “Done”):** The physical plant reaction measured by optical joint encoders and motor current shunt resistors over the executed control tick, recording actual mechanical displacement and electrical effort. This measured response serves as proprioceptive state input (${\mathbf{o}_{\text{proprio}, t+1}}$) for subsequent policy steps rather than an action label.

↪ Control barrier functions and the forward-invariant safe sets they enforce are developed in **[[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/03 Running the Machine/12 Safety Enforcement/V4-12.06 Safe Sets as Conditional Permission#^sec-enforcement-safe-sets-conditional-permission|Safe Sets as Conditional Permission]]**.

These distinctions become critical when commanded intent and physical motion diverge. If a teleoperator requests an aggressive movement that the safety permission path clamps, the resulting state transition follows the enforced command (${a_{\text{enf}}}$), not the unconstrained request (${a_{\text{req}}}$). Pairing that transition only with the request conceals the safety intervention and teaches a policy to repeat demands the physical machine cannot execute.

At the latch, the opposite ambiguity arises. Contact resistance can bring measured joint velocities to zero (${\dot{\mathbf{q}}_{\text{meas}}} \approx {\mathbf{0}}$) while the motors continue exerting holding torque against the strike plate. Treating that response as a deliberate zero-velocity target loses the distinction between maintaining effort against the latch and choosing to stop. The appropriate imitation target depends on the policy’s action interface; the other taps explain how that target became physical motion.

Retaining all four streams is what makes any single choice of label interpretable. A policy trained on the request alone learns commands the permission path would have clamped, and one trained on the measured response alone learns the arm’s compliance, backlash, and external disturbances as if they were intended control. A log that keeps only the final encoder state or only the operator’s input discards exactly the stages at which software and safety hardware changed the command. The tuple is also only as reliable as its shared clock. The application processor and the MCU must stamp their taps in one hardware-synchronized timebase, such as the IEEE 1588 Precision Time Protocol (PTP), written ${t_{\text{PTP}}}$. A few milliseconds of skew between them misaligns each visual observation from the torque it prompted ([[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/02 Teaching the Machine/05 Physical Data/V4-05.06 Action Label Selection Across the Control Boundary#^sec-data-multimodal-time-sync|section 5.5.2]]).

For the latch campaign, the imitation target is the mapped command whenever it represents an expert request that the permission path accepted unchanged. In that case ${a_{\text{cmd}}}$ and ${a_{\text{enf}}}$ carry the same value, so the choice between the two taps matters only for commands the permission path modified. A modified command remains a separate, attributed intervention; a curated recovery can later supply an expert target at that state. Each training pair therefore carries two times. The observation is dated when its physics was true, at the exposure midpoint (the instant halfway through the interval over which the camera integrates light), and the command is dated when the operator issued it. The gap between them is part of the demonstration and is recorded rather than erased, because it contains the operator’s own reaction time ([[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/02 Teaching the Machine/05 Physical Data/V4-05.07 Teleoperation Demonstration Costs#^sec-data-bilateral-passivity|section 5.6.1]]). The alignment developed below supplies the first of these times.

↪ The capture timestamp, taken in hardware at the exposure midpoint, is enforced for every sensor in **[[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/03 Running the Machine/08 Sensor Perception/V4-08.04 Dating and Placing the Measurement#^sec-perception-handoff|Dating and Placing the Measurement]]**.

### Multi-Rate Time Synchronization and Exposure-Midpoint Alignment

^sec-data-multimodal-time-sync

Recording the right action tap still leaves open when each recorded observation was physically true, because a robot’s sensors run at different rates and transport delays. A naive data loader that latches the latest available sensor sample pairs each action with a camera frame whose optical exposure was centered $25\text{--}40\text{ ms}$ before the frame arrived in host memory, recording that delayed image as the present state. Trained on those pairs, the policy learns to map historical visual states to present actions, baking an artificial phase lag into its weights that shows up at deployment as overshoot and chattering against the latch. Across the machine, cameras integrate photons over exposure windows of $10\text{--}35\text{ ms}$ at $30\text{--}60\text{ Hz}$, 6-axis force-torque sensors stream over a Serial Peripheral Interface (SPI) or Controller Area Network (CAN) bus at $100\text{--}500\text{ Hz}$, joint and permission telemetry is retained at the MCU’s chosen $1\text{ kHz}$ tick while motor-drive current sampling serves the faster local current loop, and neural policy models dispatch setpoints at $10\text{--}50\text{ Hz}$. Unlike the operator’s reaction time, this pairing error belongs to the logging pipeline rather than the demonstration, so stamping each sample at its physical capture instant lets the alignment pipeline remove it.

To quantify the physical hazard of temporal misalignment, consider the spatial tracking error ${\Delta \mathbf{x}_{\text{sync}}}$ injected into the dataset when an observation and an action are paired across an uncompensated phase lag ${\Delta t_{\text{lag}}}$:

$$
{\Delta \mathbf{x}_{\text{sync}}}({t}) = \int_{t - {\Delta t_{\text{lag}}}}^{{t}} {\mathbf{v}}({t'})\,d{t'} \approx {\mathbf{v}}({t}) \cdot {\Delta t_{\text{lag}}}. \tag{3}
$$

 The latch campaign moves far more slowly than an arm in transit, and notebook [5.1](https://mlsysbook.ai/vol4/05_data/05_data.html#nbk-data-pairing-lag) applies [[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/02 Teaching the Machine/05 Physical Data/V4-05.06 Action Label Selection Across the Control Boundary#^eq-data-sync-displacement|equation 3]] at both speeds.

^eq-data-sync-displacement

**Napkin Math 5.1: The pairing lag**

**Problem**: A logger pairs each action with a camera frame whose exposure midpoint lies an uncompensated 35 ms earlier. Does the resulting pairing error fit the 2 mm latch clearance at transit speed and at the latch campaign’s two approach speeds, and how much lag can each approach tolerate?

**Variables**:

- Uncompensated lag ${\Delta t_{\text{lag}}} =$ 35 ms (illustrative; exposure-to-arrival delay of an untimed logger)
- Latch clearance ${c} =$ 2 mm (illustrative)
- Transit speed 0.50 m/s (illustrative); guarded approach 0.03 m/s and faster approach 0.10 m/s (illustrative scenario speeds of the running machine)

**Math**:

1.  **Transit**: 0.50 m/s $\times$ 35 ms $=$ 17.5 mm, 8.8× the clearance.
2.  **Guarded approach**: 0.03 m/s $\times$ 35 ms $=$ 1.05 mm, inside the clearance.
3.  **Faster approach**: 0.10 m/s $\times$ 35 ms $=$ 3.5 mm, outside it.
4.  **Lag ceilings**: ${\Delta t_{\text{lag,max}}} = {c}/{v_{\text{app}}}$, so 2 mm $/$ 0.03 m/s $=$ 66.7 ms and 2 mm $/$ 0.10 m/s $=$ 20 ms.

**Result**: The same lag fits the modeled pairing-error allocation at the guarded approach and exceeds it at the faster one.

**Systems insight**: A timing error becomes a position error at the speed of the motion, just as observation age becomes blind travel ${v}\,{\tau_{\text{delay}}}$ in the stopping budget derived in **[[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/01 Machine Anatomy/02 The Physical Body/V4-02.04 Kinetic Momentum#^sec-body-stopping-distance-prebrake|Kinematic stopping distance and pre-brake delay]]**. The faster approach cannot be collected without the exposure-midpoint alignment of the four-stage pipeline in [[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/02 Teaching the Machine/05 Physical Data/V4-05.06 Action Label Selection Across the Control Boundary#^sec-data-alignment-pipeline|section 5.5.2.3]].

When an imitation learning model trains on misaligned pairs, it fits a relationship in which the action appears to precede the visual evidence that prompted it. During closed-loop deployment, that artificial phase lag appears as overshoot, control hunting, and chattering against rigid surfaces. Because displacement grows linearly with lag, each approach speed has a lag ceiling equal to the clearance divided by the speed, and [[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/02 Teaching the Machine/05 Physical Data/V4-05.06 Action Label Selection Across the Control Boundary#^fig-05-sync-displacement|figure 3]] places those ceilings beside a second timing error the notebook did not count. A rolling-shutter sensor exposes its pixel rows one after another rather than all at once, so the bottom row of a frame is captured later than the top ([[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/02 Teaching the Machine/05 Physical Data/V4-05.06 Action Label Selection Across the Control Boundary#^sec-data-rolling-shutter-skew|section 5.5.2.2]] quantifies the delay). A full-frame rolling-shutter skew of 15 ms to 33 ms is comparable to the uncompensated lag, so a logger that stamps every frame correctly can still breach the faster approach’s 20 ms ceiling through readout alone.

![[90 Assets/Volume IV/05 Data/Plot 05 Sync Displacement Output 1 bdbfaada2c.png]]

#### Hardware Timestamps Vs. Software Arrival Timestamps

^sec-data-hw-vs-sw-timestamps

The 35 ms pairing lag in notebook [5.1](https://mlsysbook.ai/vol4/05_data/05_data.html#nbk-data-pairing-lag) arises when a logger stamps a sensor packet with the clock reading at the instant a Linux thread wakes up to read it rather than when the hardware captured the sample. Even on the $1.0\text{ ms}$ kinematic stream, half a millisecond of kernel scheduling jitter cuts a nominal tick in half or stretches it by half, doubling or shrinking the finite-difference velocity label for the exact same physical motion. Both errors come from confusing when an optical or electrical event happened in hardware with when the operating system noticed it in software:

- **Software arrival timestamps (${t_{\text{host}}}$):** In naive logging pipelines, an image frame or CAN packet is timestamped when the Linux kernel handles the device interrupt or when a user-space thread dequeues the buffer from Video4Linux2 (V4L2) or a socket. This host timestamp incorporates camera pixel readout time, USB 3 or MIPI CSI-2 bus serialization, Direct Memory Access (DMA) latency, and operating system scheduling jitter:

$$
{t_{\text{host}}} = {t_{\text{mid}}} + \frac{{t_{\text{exp}}}}{2} + {t_{\text{readout}}} + {t_{\text{bus}}} + {t_{\text{driver\_irq}}} + {\delta_{\text{jitter}}}.
$$

 Host timestamps lag physical reality by $20\text{--}45\text{ ms}$. In an untuned system, operating system scheduling jitter (${\delta_{\text{jitter}}} = \pm 5\text{--}15\text{ ms}$), driven by kernel preemption latency, CPU C-state power wake-up transitions, and USB host controller interrupt coalescing (where packets are held in hardware buffers until a threshold count or timer expires), corrupts numerical differentiation. When joint velocities are computed via backward difference:

$$
{\dot{\mathbf{q}}}_k = \frac{{\mathbf{q}}_k - {\mathbf{q}}_{k-1}}{{t}_k - {t}_{k-1}},
$$

 a nominal $1\text{ kHz}$ sampling interval (${\Delta} {t} = 1.0\text{ ms}$) perturbed by software timestamp jitter ${\delta_{\text{jitter}}} = \pm 0.5\text{ ms}$ causes the denominator to fluctuate between $0.5\text{ ms}$ and $1.5\text{ ms}$. The effect is doubling the inferred velocity at the shorter interval and reducing it by one third at the longer interval, for the same measured displacement. If thread preemption reverses packet dequeuing order (${t}_k < {t}_{k-1}$), the computed velocity reverses sign, injecting spurious velocity spikes into the training targets.
- **Hardware PTP timestamps (${t_{\text{PTP}}}$):** Hardware timestamps isolate the physical event from host scheduling delays. IEEE 1588 Precision Time Protocol (PTP) estimates clock offset across distributed nodes using a deterministic two-way message exchange.[[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/02 Teaching the Machine/05 Physical Data/V4-05.17 Footnotes#^fn5|⁵]] The reference clock sends at ${t}_1$ and receives the reply at ${t}_4$; the remote clock receives at ${t}_2$ and replies at ${t}_3$. Under symmetric path delays, the propagation delay and offset are:

$$
{T_{\text{prop}}} = \frac{({t}_4 - {t}_1) - ({t}_3 - {t}_2)}{2}, \qquad {\theta_{\text{offset}}} = \frac{({t}_2 - {t}_1) - ({t}_4 - {t}_3)}{2}.
$$

 Because the forward timestamp difference ${t}_2 - {t}_1 = {T_{\text{prop}}} + {\theta_{\text{offset}}}$ adds the remote clock offset to the one-way propagation delay while the return difference ${t}_4 - {t}_3 = {T_{\text{prop}}} - {\theta_{\text{offset}}}$ subtracts that same offset, adding the two differences cancels ${\theta_{\text{offset}}}$ to isolate the propagation delay and subtracting them cancels ${T_{\text{prop}}}$ to isolate the clock offset. Hardware timestamping bounds uncertainty, but a network packet timestamp does not automatically equal an optical exposure midpoint. Production logs must preserve the capture event, clock domain identity, and bounded conversion error. The key collection decision is whether that uncertainty fits within the task’s spatial alignment tolerance.

↪ **Protocol:** The IEEE 1588 message exchange, its clock servo, and its path-asymmetry uncertainty are developed in **[[05 Reference/Volume IV - Physical AI/V4 Systems and Hardware#^sec-appendix-systems-ptp|Clock Synchronization and Network Timestamps]]**.

#### Rolling Shutter Skew Across Camera Streams

^sec-data-rolling-shutter-skew

When a camera with a rolling shutter pans past the cage door or rides on a moving link, straight vertical bars bend into diagonal arcs because the bottom pixel row is exposed up to $33\text{ ms}$ later than the top row. This distortion is **rolling shutter skew**: low-cost consumer sensors and webcams expose pixel rows one after another rather than all at once. For a sensor with ${H}$ vertical scanlines and line readout duration ${t_{\text{row}}}$, row ${y} \in [0, {H}-1]$ begins its exposure at ${t_{\text{scan}}}({y})$, and its exposure midpoint, half an exposure later, depends linearly on its vertical coordinate:

$$
{t_{\text{scan}}}({y}) + \frac{{t_{\text{exp}}}}{2} = {t_{\text{start}}} + {y} \, {t_{\text{row}}} + \frac{{t_{\text{exp}}}}{2}.
$$

 Across a full frame, the temporal skew between the top row (${y} = 0$) and bottom row (${y} = {H} - 1$) accumulates to:

$$
{\Delta t_{\text{skew}}} = ({H} - 1) \, {t_{\text{row}}}, \tag{4}
$$

 which for common sensors lies between about 15 ms and 33 ms, the upper end being one full frame period at 30 Hz. When the robot arm or camera moves at 0.50 m/s, the bottom of the manipulator in the image is captured while the physical link has moved up to 16.5 mm relative to the top of the link in the same frame. Even at the faster latch approach of 0.10 m/s, the worst-case skew moves the gripper 3.3 mm within one frame, exceeding the 2 mm clearance. This geometric warping (the “jello effect”) deforms straight edges into curved arcs and breaks rigid-body geometry.

^eq-data-rolling-shutter-skew

Independent rolling shutters can depict the same moving arm at different poses within nominally simultaneous frames. Hardware-triggered global shutters simplify alignment; rolling shutters require a row-time model and a motion bound or correction. Sensor choice must follow the task tolerance rather than an unyielding demand for a single shutter architecture.

↪ The same row timing as a spatial error on a moving base, charged against clearance and removed by per-row motion compensation, is developed in **[[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/03 Running the Machine/08 Sensor Perception/V4-08.03 Capture Physics and Calibration#^sec-perception-transduction-calibration|Capture Physics and Calibration]]**.

#### A Four-Stage Alignment Pipeline

^sec-data-alignment-pipeline

Hardware timestamps fix when each sample was taken, and the row-time model fixes when each part of an image was exposed. A collection pipeline still has to turn those facts into training pairs whose pairing error fits inside the clearance budget of notebook [5.1](https://mlsysbook.ai/vol4/05_data/05_data.html#nbk-data-pairing-lag). It can implement that alignment contract in four stages:

1.  **Hardware-triggered optical exposure midpoint:** The real-time MCU dispatches hardware GPIO sync pulses or PTP-synchronized trigger packets to every triggerable camera. The rig mixes shutter types. The wrist camera’s global shutter exposes all rows together, while the navigation camera and the external cameras’ color sensors read out row by row, so their frames also need the row-time model above to assign each row its own midpoint. The camera latches the trigger at ${t_{\text{start}}}$, and the driver calculates the true optical midpoint ${t_{\text{mid}}} = {t_{\text{start}}} + {t_{\text{exp}}} / 2$ in hardware PTP clock units (${t_{\text{PTP}}}$), avoiding host arrival time as the exposure timestamp.[[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/02 Teaching the Machine/05 Physical Data/V4-05.17 Footnotes#^fn6|⁶]]
2.  **Lock-free time-sorted jitter compensation:** Because network and bus transfers experience variable transport delay ${\delta_{\text{jitter}}}$, incoming sensor packets are stamped with their hardware PTP capture timestamp and inserted into a lock-free, time-sorted jitter ring buffer in shared memory. An asynchronous consumer thread advances a playhead lagging real-time by a fixed, bounded window (${T_{\text{buf}}} = 15\text{--}25\text{ ms}$), absorbing transport bursts and re-ordering packets into monotonic hardware timestamp sequence.
3.  **Cubic Hermite kinematic interpolation:** Because joint encoders sample at $1\text{ kHz}$ ($1\text{ ms}$ period) while cameras capture at $30\text{ Hz}$ or $60\text{ Hz}$, the camera midpoint ${t_{\text{mid}}}$ falls between two discrete MCU telemetry ticks ${t}_k$ and ${t}_{k+1}$. The synchronization thread interpolates joint angles ${\mathbf{q}}({t_{\text{mid}}})$ using cubic Hermite splines across the bounding kinematic records $({t}_k, {\mathbf{q}}_k, {\dot{\mathbf{q}}}_k)$ and $({t}_{k+1}, {\mathbf{q}}_{k+1}, {\dot{\mathbf{q}}}_{k+1})$:

$$
{\mathbf{q}}({t_{\text{mid}}}) = {h}_{00}({\lambda}) {\mathbf{q}}_k + {h}_{10}({\lambda}) {\Delta t_s} {\dot{\mathbf{q}}}_k + {h}_{01}({\lambda}) {\mathbf{q}}_{k+1} + {h}_{11}({\lambda}) {\Delta t_s} {\dot{\mathbf{q}}}_{k+1},
$$

 where ${\Delta t_s} = {t}_{k+1} - {t}_k = 1\text{ ms}$, ${\lambda} = ({t_{\text{mid}}} - {t}_k)/{\Delta t_s} \in [0, 1]$ is the fraction of the tick elapsed at the exposure midpoint, and ${h}_{ij}({\lambda})$ are the cubic Hermite basis functions (**[[05 Reference/Volume IV - Physical AI/V4 Control and Dynamics#^sec-appendix-control-splines|Smooth Trajectories and Bumpless Transfer]]**). This gives a smooth state estimate between valid samples. The interpolation error still needs a bound, especially across contact or missing telemetry.
4.  **Pre-serialization alignment:** Container formats store multi-rate topics with the timestamps provided by the logger. Alignment may occur during capture or in a versioned preprocessing pass. Preserve the raw timestamps and interpolation provenance in either case, so the training pair carries an estimated state and its uncertainty rather than an assertion of exact simultaneity.

The same pairing contract governs deployment. A policy trained on aligned pairs must also be fed aligned observations and have its actions executed when the training data implies. UMI measures each observation stream’s latency and aligns all streams to the slowest, usually the camera. It then sends commands ahead of time to cover the arm’s and gripper’s execution latency, and it discards actions whose execution time has already passed. With latency matching disabled, the same policy’s motion became visibly jittery ([[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/02 Teaching the Machine/05 Physical Data/V4-05.16 Source References#^ref-chi2024umi|Chi et al. 2024]]). UMI also expresses actions relative to the gripper’s current pose rather than in a fixed robot frame, which removes the dependence on a base calibration and lets one policy run on more than one arm.

**Checkpoint 5.1: Action labels and pairing time**

A training pair is only as trustworthy as the tap it imitates and the time it assigns to each observation.

**Clamped request**: A teleoperator requests a motion that the permission path clamps. Which tap does the resulting state transition follow, and where does the record keep the evidence of the clamp?

**Stalled latch**: Measured joint velocity is zero while the motors hold torque against the latch. Why is ${a_{\text{meas}}}$ the wrong imitation target at that step?

**Unchanged mapped command**: Under what condition can ${a_{\text{cmd}}}$ serve as the expert target, and what happens to the pair when that condition fails?

**Lag budget**: A tighter strike plate leaves only 1 mm of clearance. At the faster approach of 0.10 m/s, what is the largest uncompensated lag the pairing tolerates, and does a logger with the 35 ms lag of notebook [5.1](https://mlsysbook.ai/vol4/05_data/05_data.html#nbk-data-pairing-lag) meet it?

A tap and a timestamp are only as good as the loop that produced them. In teleoperation that loop runs through a human, and the interface between human and machine shapes the recorded dynamics, storage bandwidth, and hardware wear long before the data reaches a model ([[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/02 Teaching the Machine/05 Physical Data/V4-05.07 Teleoperation Demonstration Costs#^sec-data-teleop-costs|section 5.6]]).
