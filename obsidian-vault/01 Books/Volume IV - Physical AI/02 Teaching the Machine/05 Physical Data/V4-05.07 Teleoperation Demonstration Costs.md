# V4-05.07 Teleoperation Demonstration Costs

[[Home|Home]] · [[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/Volume 4 Overview|Volume Overview]] · [[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/02 Teaching the Machine/05 Physical Data/V4-05 Physical Data|V4-05 Physical Data]]

^sec-data-teleop-costs

When an operator guides a leader arm to unlatch the warehouse cage door, every corrective nudge must traverse a multi-stage physical loop before the robot can respond. Teleoperation is a data-generation pipeline with a human embedded in the real-time feedback loop. It charges for each demonstration in three currencies: the phase lag and stability margin of the loop itself, the write bandwidth and storage the session demands, and the cell time and component life a campaign consumes. The loop comes first ([[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/02 Teaching the Machine/05 Physical Data/V4-05.07 Teleoperation Demonstration Costs#^fig-05-teleop-rig|figure 4]]). The operator sees the scene on a display after a delay ${t_{\text{disp}}}$ and perceives, decides, and moves the leader in ${t_{\text{hum}}}$. The command crosses to the follower in ${t_{\text{cmd}}}$. The follower’s actuators then accelerate the links against inertia, drivetrain friction, and contact in ${t_{\text{mech}}}$, before the camera shows the result on the next cycle.

↪ Where a deployment moves operators’ labor, and which uses of their recorded sessions it must bar, is developed in **[[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/04 Governing the Machine/17 Responsible Physical AI/V4-17.09 The Labor the Machine Moves#^sec-responsibility-worker-burden|The Labor the Machine Moves]]**.

![[90 Assets/Volume IV/05 Data/Fig05 Leader Follower Loop 93e61383e4.svg]]

*Block diagram of a teleoperation loop. An operator box labeled see, decide, move sends an arrow labeled t hum to a small leader arm with joints q1, q2, and q3. The leader feeds a joint copy box stating q follower equals q leader, noted no inverse kinematics, which sends an arrow labeled t cmd to a larger follower arm with the same three joints, reaching toward a latch across a gap labeled t mech. A dashed arrow runs from the follower down to a workspace camera, whose arrow labeled t disp runs left to a display, which feeds back up to the operator.*

Figure 4: **Kinematically isomorphic teleoperation loop**: The operator moves a leader arm whose joints mirror the follower’s, and the host copies joint angles across, as in low-cost leader-follower rigs such as GELLO ([[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/02 Teaching the Machine/05 Physical Data/V4-05.16 Source References#^ref-wu2023gello|Wu et al. 2024]]). Because the leader shares the follower’s joint structure, the interface needs no inverse kinematics and inherits none of its singularities (configurations where joints align and the arm loses a direction of motion), preserving the operator’s direct joint-level coordination. The loop still closes through the operator: human reaction, command transfer, mechanical response, and display delay all enter the recording as phase lag, so the demonstration carries the operator’s delay as if it were policy.

^fig-05-teleop-rig

By mapping each leader joint directly to the corresponding follower joint (${\mathbf{q}_{\text{leader}}} \equiv {\mathbf{q}_{\text{follower}}}$), this isomorphic leader-follower topology sidesteps numerical inverse kinematics and algorithmic kinematic singularities. Even without software kinematic conversion, however, every recorded trajectory remains bound to the physical transport and neuromuscular delays of the complete loop, placing hard upper bounds on corrective bandwidth.

### Human-In-The-Loop Latency and Bilateral Passivity Breakdown

^sec-data-bilateral-passivity

![[90 Assets/Volume IV/05 Data/Margin Teleop Loop Latency Strip da7a27f651.svg]]

*One horizontal bar divided into the four stages of a teleoperated correction, drawn to scale under a bracket labeled 340 milliseconds loop: display 50 ms, human 200 ms, command 10 ms, and mechanics 80 ms. The human segment is the longest by far.*

*The operator’s own reaction is 58.8 percent of the 340 ms correction loop.*

Suppose the follower gripper grazes the side of the latch strike plate at time ${t}$. If a logger naively pairs the observation ${\mathbf{o}}_{t}$ captured at that instant with the command the operator issued at ${t}$, it pairs a contact state with an action chosen for a trajectory state 250 ms earlier, before the contact ever happened. A behavioral cloning policy trained on those pairs learns that delay as intentional strategy and, when run autonomously at high control frequencies, over-corrects into destructive oscillations. That stale pairing arises because every stage of the teleoperation loop adds phase delay. In an illustrative reference setup with a high-speed camera and a wired local network, display pipeline processing takes ${t_{\text{disp}}} =$ 50 ms across sensor exposure, H.264 decoding, and display raster refresh. Human visual perception, motor planning, and neuromuscular transmission add an operator response latency of ${t_{\text{hum}}} =$ 200 ms. Command quantization, packetization, and fieldbus transport add ${t_{\text{cmd}}} =$ 10 ms. Actuator torque ramp-up against link inertia then adds a mechanical response latency of ${t_{\text{mech}}} =$ 80 ms before the arm reaches the commanded acceleration. Their sum is the **teleoperation round-trip latency** ${t_{\text{loop}}}$, the elapsed time from a physical contact disturbance to the corrective actuator response:

$$
{t_{\text{loop}}} = {t_{\text{disp}}} + {t_{\text{hum}}} + {t_{\text{cmd}}} + {t_{\text{mech}}}. \tag{5}
$$

^eq-data-teleop-loop-latency

Following the contact at time ${t}$ through those four stages shows why the recorded command lags the state. The operator sees the error on the display 50 ms later and commands a physical correction at ${t} +{}$ 250 ms. The follower arm finally pushes back at ${t} +{}$ 340 ms, after drifting under momentum and contact compliance throughout the interval. Round-trip latency is therefore a property of the resulting dataset rather than just an artifact of the rig, because it injects a systematic phase lag into every recorded demonstration, dominated by the operator’s own reaction time (58.8 percent of the loop in this setup).

This delay differs in kind from the sensor-pipeline delay of [[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/02 Teaching the Machine/05 Physical Data/V4-05.06 Action Label Selection Across the Control Boundary#^sec-data-multimodal-time-sync|section 5.5.2]]. That one was a pairing artifact of the logger, and exposure-midpoint stamping lets the alignment pipeline account for it. The operator’s delay is a real property of the demonstrator, so no timestamp correction can remove it, and silently erasing it would misstate what the demonstration contains. The logger therefore keeps both times of every pair (the observation’s exposure midpoint and the command’s issue time) and records the session’s measured loop latency. Training can then treat the observation-to-action interval as a declared part of the demonstration, either by conditioning the policy on enough observation history to span it or by pairing each command with the earlier frame the operator was answering. Deployment can then match the interval it was trained with, as UMI’s latency matching does ([[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/02 Teaching the Machine/05 Physical Data/V4-05.06 Action Label Selection Across the Control Boundary#^sec-data-alignment-pipeline|section 5.5.2.3]]). Because the operator’s reaction varies from command to command, no single fixed shift recovers the exact frame each command answered, which is why the latch campaign records the interval instead of baking one correction into the archive.

Force reflection introduces a deeper stability challenge into the collection interface. In **bilateral teleoperation**, the operator manipulates the leader device while measured contact forces from the follower arm reflect back across a delayed communication channel. Over a closed motion cycle of period ${T_{\text{cycle}}}$, the net mechanical energy delivered at that port is:

$$
{\Delta E} = \int_0^{{T_{\text{cycle}}}} {F_{\text{contact}}}({t} - {T_d}) {v}({t})\,d{t}.
$$

 The sign of ${\Delta E}$ depends on the force–velocity phase relationship. Under the declared port orientation, energy delivered in excess of received and initially stored energy violates passivity;[[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/02 Teaching the Machine/05 Physical Data/V4-05.17 Footnotes#^fn7|⁷]] a positive value over one cycle alone does not establish that excess. In an undelayed elastic contact, the reflected force opposes the leader handle during compression and drops as the handle rebounds, whereas transmission delay ${T_d}$ causes peak contact force to continue pushing the leader handle after the operator has already reversed direction, aligning force with rebound velocity ${v}({t})$ to pump net mechanical energy into each oscillation cycle and excite contact instability ([[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/02 Teaching the Machine/05 Physical Data/V4-05.16 Source References#^ref-anderson1989bilateral|Anderson and Spong 1989]]). The wave-variable transformation derived in **[[05 Reference/Volume IV - Physical AI/V4 Control and Dynamics#^sec-appendix-control-passivity|Passivity and Energy-Bounded Interaction]]** keeps the ideal delayed channel passive and lossless under its constant-delay model, while passivity observers (monitors that track the energy a port has generated and dissipate any excess) restore passivity by injecting variable damping. Preserving channel passivity imposes a force-fidelity cost at contact. Injected damping, and the explicit low-pass filtering often added to delayed force channels, suppress the fastest components of the reflected force first, so a stabilized channel behaves as a low-pass filter on contact. In data collection, this imposes an unavoidable label-fidelity trade-off. The filtering that stabilizes delayed haptic feedback also attenuates the sharp contact transients the operator needs to demonstrate precise tactile insertion. System builders must log the active feedback architecture and filter cutoffs alongside raw trajectories to document this phase distortion.

The mechanical interface therefore filters which behaviors can reach the archive at all. A force-reflecting leader allows an operator to feel mechanical binding and ease off insertion forces. A visual-only Cartesian rate controller instead forces the step-and-wait hunting of [[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/02 Teaching the Machine/05 Physical Data/V4-05.05 Physical Telemetry Sources and Collection Paths#^sec-data-human-idiosyncrasies|section 5.4.1]], because the operator must wait for display frames to confirm each small motion. A backdriven joint-space leader such as ALOHA, recorded at $50\text{ Hz}$ ([[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/02 Teaching the Machine/05 Physical Data/V4-05.16 Source References#^ref-zhao2023learning|Zhao et al. 2023]]), occupies an intermediate design point, providing inertial and friction feedback from the leader mechanism itself without reflecting follower contact forces. Across all these interfaces, human operators instinctively decelerate prior to contact, because closing a high-gain corrective loop around round-trip latency ${t_{\text{loop}}}$ would erode phase margin to zero. ### Ingestion Rate and the Preservation Requirement

^sec-data-telemetry-ingestion-storage

The loop’s latency is the first cost a demonstration carries; the second is what it takes to write everything the loop produced to disk without silent data loss. If an onboard logger tries to stream raw multi-camera video and $1\text{ kHz}$ kinematics onto a SATA solid-state drive, write buffers overflow within seconds, dropping either the camera frames or the four action taps of [[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/02 Teaching the Machine/05 Physical Data/V4-05.06 Action Label Selection Across the Control Boundary#^sec-data-four-stream-action-tap|section 5.5.1]] that explain what the permission path did. Whether an edge collection computer can sustain a teleoperated session without dropping samples is storage-bus arithmetic: the aggregate write rate set against the sequential write bandwidth of the drive.

**Napkin Math 5.2: The cheapest stream**

**Problem**: One teleoperated door-opening session on the warehouse mobile manipulator logs every camera and every axis raw. What sustained write rate and storage does it demand, and what share of the bytes carries the four action taps?

**Variables** (camera geometry is datasheet; logging formats and shift length are chosen):

- **Navigation camera:** 4056 by 3040 px at 60 Hz, logged as 24-bit RGB (3 bytes/px).
- **Wrist camera:** 1440 by 1080 px at 60 Hz, logged the same way.
- **External cameras:** 2 RGB-D cameras viewing the door, color at 1920 by 1080 px and 30 Hz, with depth aligned to the color frame as raw `uint16` millimeters (2 bytes/px).
- **Kinematics:** 9 axes (two base wheels, seven arm joints) logged at 1 kHz. Each step stores, for every axis, 6 values of 4 bytes (position and velocity, which form the measured response ${a_{\text{meas}}}$; measured torque; and the requested, mapped, and enforced commands), plus one 8-byte timestamp.
- **Shift:** 8 hours of collection on one machine.

**Math**:

1.  **Kinematic stream**: 9 $\times$ 6 $\times$ 4 B $+$ 8 B $=$ 224 B per step, and at 1 kHz that is 0.22 MB/s.
2.  **Navigation camera**: 4056 $\times$ 3040 px $\times$ 3 B $\times$ 60 Hz $=$ 2219.44 MB/s. The wrist camera’s product is 279.94 MB/s.
3.  **External pair**: 2 $\times$ 1920 $\times$ 1080 px $\times$ (3 B $+$ 2 B) $\times$ 30 Hz $=$ 622.08 MB/s, of which 373.25 MB/s is color and 248.83 MB/s depth.
4.  **Total and storage**: The streams sum to 3121.68 MB/s, about 3.12 GB/s; over one hour that is 11.24 TB, and over the shift 89.9 TB.

**Result**: Raw logging needs a sustained 3.12 GB/s write path, and the kinematic stream is 0.007 percent of the bytes.

**Systems insight**: The navigation camera alone writes 4.0× what a SATA SSD sustains (550 MB/s). The full session fits within a Gen4 NVMe drive’s 7 GB/s, but only by consuming storage at 11.24 TB per hour. Intra-frame video compression at an illustrative 8× brings the rate to about 390.2 MB/s (1.40 TB per hour) at the cost of encoder latency and artifacts in the frames the policy will train on. The kinematic stream carries all four action taps, so the record that preserves the causal chain is the cheapest stream to log, and a logger that drops data under buffer pressure must never drop it first.

![[90 Assets/Volume IV/05 Data/Margin Sensory Ingestion Bandwidth Ladder b8c923136f.svg]]

*Horizontal bar ladder of write bandwidth in megabytes per second: three bars for all camera and kinematic streams together at 3,122, the navigation camera alone at 2,219, and the session compressed eightfold at 390, between a red dashed SATA solid-state drive write ceiling at 550 and a gray dashed NVMe Gen4 ceiling at 7,000. Both raw bars cross the SATA ceiling and only the compressed bar stays below it.*

*The navigation camera alone writes 4.0× what a SATA SSD sustains; only the compressed session fits under it.*

Keeping that write rate under the drive ceiling with video compression trades storage bandwidth against visual fidelity, because lossy encoders discard fine spatial detail at the latch edge and inject blocking artifacts into the frames the policy trains on. A session can be stored in custom HDF5 files, ROS 2 MCAP bags (a container format for multimodal robotics data), or standardized schemas such as LeRobot ([[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/02 Teaching the Machine/05 Physical Data/V4-05.16 Source References#^ref-cadene2024lerobot|Cadene et al. 2024]]). Whichever format is used, the logger must flush complete chunks to non-volatile storage before a power drop or process halt can truncate the write buffer. It must also carry all four control taps, hardware timestamps, clock-domain identifiers, missing-sample flags, and spatial calibration parameters through every format conversion. Otherwise, a file that deserializes without error proves only that its byte layout is intact, not that its sensory and motor streams remain physically synchronized.

### Operational Yield and Mechanical Fatigue Budgeting

^sec-data-yield-fatigue-budgeting

![[90 Assets/Volume IV/05 Data/Margin Facility Yield Burndown Envelope c9f00e3386.svg]]

*Three bars on a shared minute scale: scheduled session 60, active motion window 35, retained demonstration time 14.73.*

*In this illustrative session, setup, rest, resets, and rejected attempts leave under a quarter of the scheduled hour as retained demonstrations.*

Sizing the NVMe drive settles what one hour in the workcell writes to disk, but less than a quarter of that scheduled hour survives as usable demonstration motion. Before the arm records a single latch opening, a scheduled hour in the workcell pays for bus synchronization, camera and torque-sensor calibration, and mandatory operator rest. The active motion window that remains is further divided among door-reset strokes, mid-trial contact trips, and offline quality filtering. Because every attempt, whether retained or discarded, flexes the wrist cable harness and reverses load on the harmonic drive, a collection campaign is bounded as much by component fatigue life as by facility reservations.

The ultimate metric of collection efficiency is **demonstration yield**: the number of validated, policy-ready trajectory records produced in a scheduled session after accounting for cell startup, sensor calibration, operator rest, mechanical resets, and offline quality filtering. Given an active motion window ${T_{\text{motion}}}$, an attempt-and-reset cycle duration ${t_{\text{cyc}}} = {t_{\text{att}}} + {t_{\text{rst}}}$, and an end-to-end retention rate ${R}$, the session yields:

$$
{Y_{\text{usable}}} = \left\lfloor \frac{{T_{\text{motion}}}}{{t_{\text{cyc}}}} \right\rfloor {R}. \tag{6}
$$

 Mechanical fatigue accumulates per physical motion cycle rather than per facility hour. This illustrative accounting assigns the same stress-cycle counts to failed and successful attempts and assumes component ratings expressed under the ${L_{10}}$ convention. Under it, the rating is the cumulative cycle count at which 10 percent of an identical component population is statistically expected to fail.[[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/02 Teaching the Machine/05 Physical Data/V4-05.17 Footnotes#^fn8|⁸]] Notebook [5.3](https://mlsysbook.ai/vol4/05_data/05_data.html#nbk-data-flex-cable-ceiling) evaluates an illustrative door-opening collection session and its full campaign in both currencies: facility hours and component fatigue cycles.

^eq-data-demonstration-yield

**Napkin Math 5.3: The flex-cable ceiling**

**Problem**: The latch campaign must retain 5,000 demonstrations. How much scheduled cell time does that cost, and which wrist component’s rated life does it spend fastest?

**Variables** (illustrative except the chosen session length and target):

- Session ${T_{\text{sched}}} =$ 60 min (chosen), leaving an active window ${T_{\text{motion}}} =$ 35 min after startup, calibration, and operator rest
- Attempt ${t_{\text{att}}} =$ 25 s, one teleoperated opening; reset ${t_{\text{rst}}} =$ 15 s, the door re-latched by its fixture
- In-trial failure rate 20 percent; post-collection quality filtering 15 percent
- Wear per attempt: ${n_{\text{gear}}} =$ 14 torque reversals of the wrist’s strain-wave gear; ${n_{\text{cable}}} =$ 8 flex cycles of its internal cable bundle (6 while opening, 2 on the return stroke)
- Rated lives ${L_{\text{gear}}} =$ 5,000,000 stress cycles and ${L_{\text{cable}}} =$ 200,000 bending cycles

**Math**:

1.  **Session**: ${t_{\text{cyc}}} =$ 25 s $+$ 15 s $=$ 40 s, and $\lfloor$ 35 min $/$ 40 s $\rfloor =$ 52 attempts, with 20 s left idle.
2.  **Yield** ([[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/02 Teaching the Machine/05 Physical Data/V4-05.07 Teleoperation Demonstration Costs#^eq-data-demonstration-yield|equation 6]]): ${R} = (1 -$ 0.20$)(1 -$ 0.15$) =$ 0.68, so ${Y_{\text{usable}}} =$ 52 $\times$ 0.68 $=$ 35.36 episodes, 14.73 min of validated motion, 24.6 percent of the scheduled hour.
3.  **Campaign**: $\lceil$ 5,000 $/$ 0.68 $\rceil =$ 7,353 attempts at 52 per session, or 141.4 h of scheduled cell time, 4.07× the 5,000 $\times$ 25 s of motion the campaign keeps.
4.  **Wear**: 7,353 $\times$ 14 $=$ 102,942 gear cycles, 2.1 percent of ${L_{\text{gear}}}$; 7,353 $\times$ 8 $=$ 58,824 cable bends, 29.4 percent of ${L_{\text{cable}}}$.

**Result**: Less than a quarter of each scheduled hour survives as retained demonstration time, and one campaign spends far more of the cable’s rated life than of the gear’s.

**Systems insight**: The internal flex cable consumes the larger fraction of its assumed rating, informing maintenance planning rather than setting an individual replacement schedule. Three campaigns on one machine would consume most of its rated life, and an intermittent conductor break corrupts the very sensor streams being recorded. Because every attempt costs wear whether or not it is retained, the retention rate ${R}$ divides both bills, while a faster reset shortens only the cell time.

Operator rest requirements and fatigue rates depend heavily on interface ergonomics and cognitive task difficulty; teams must empirically calibrate sustained operator throughput and retention on their specific hardware. A collection campaign must therefore be budgeted in both validated trajectory time and consumed fatigue life, not in facility clock hours. The Hub held 1,085 such datasets at the end of 2024, 26,548 a year later, and 80,132 by the retrieval date. Episode counts tell a different story. Of the 59,031 datasets whose card records one, 58 percent hold fewer than 16 episodes, and only 2 percent hold 512 or more. The median dataset falls in the 8 to 15 episode bin, less than the roughly 35 validated demonstrations of the illustrative hour in [[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/02 Teaching the Machine/05 Physical Data/V4-05.07 Teleoperation Demonstration Costs#^tbl-05-collection-budget|table 3]]. Community data therefore grows wide rather than deep. The archive gains embodiments, scenes, and operators far faster than it gains repeated coverage of any one machine’s states, and pooling it inherits the transfer problem of [[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/02 Teaching the Machine/05 Physical Data/V4-05.10 Cross-Embodiment Transfer#^sec-data-embodiment-contracts|section 5.9.1]].

The three costs compound into a fourth that no bill shows. Teleoperated demonstrations are bounded by what a human operator can perceive through video displays, coordinate through mechanical input devices, and stabilize against closed-loop delays. A campaign that pays for every attempt in cell time and cable life spends its budget where the operator is comfortable. The resulting dataset reflects the behavioral envelope of the coupled human-machine composite rather than the robot’s full physical dynamics. That selection bias decides which situations the archive covers, and measuring it requires a standard outside the archive ([[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/02 Teaching the Machine/05 Physical Data/V4-05.08 Collection Policy Coverage#^sec-data-policy-coverage|section 5.7]]).
