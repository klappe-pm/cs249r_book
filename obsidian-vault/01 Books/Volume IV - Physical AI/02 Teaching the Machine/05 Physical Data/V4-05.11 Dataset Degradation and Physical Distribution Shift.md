# V4-05.11 Dataset Degradation and Physical Distribution Shift

[[Home|Home]] · [[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/Volume 4 Overview|Volume Overview]] · [[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/02 Teaching the Machine/05 Physical Data/V4-05 Physical Data|V4-05 Physical Data]]

^sec-data-failure-modes

A demonstration archive can pass every schema check and fill its scenario table on paper while silently rotting from within. A technician may bump a camera mount by $3\text{ mm}$ between shifts, a hot motor winding may lose peak torque during an afternoon session, or a curation script may mistake dropped force-sensor packets for a clean trajectory. Because physical datasets capture closed-loop interactions between controllers and hardware rather than static observations, verifying an archive requires auditing the mechanical, optical, and temporal meaning of recorded trajectories alongside array shapes and scenario-table counts.

### Silent Plant Drift and Kinematic Reprojection Verification

^sec-data-plant-drift

Suppose afternoon latch demonstrations begin missing the strike plate by two millimeters even though the same operator is running the same cell. Before an engineer can decide whether to relabel the afternoon runs, recalibrate the wrist camera, or service the arm, the pipeline must separate three sources of drift that occur inside a single supported scenario cell. These are changes in the physical plant, changes in the human operator’s control habits, and silent shifts in sensor calibration.

#### Passive Covariate Shift Vs. Physical Dynamics Shift

^sec-data-passive-vs-physical-shift

In those afternoon runs, the first question is whether the world around the arm changed or the arm itself changed, which is the distinction between passive observation shift and active plant drift introduced in [[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/02 Teaching the Machine/05 Physical Data/V4-05.03 Mechanics of Endogenous Covariate Shift#^sec-data-covariate-shift|section 5.2]]. Shifting afternoon sunlight across the cage door alters camera pixel intensities without changing how the joints respond to a torque command, whereas hot motor windings, worn gearbox teeth, abraded gripper pads, and varying door friction alter the plant dynamics directly. Before an ingestion pipeline pools or partitions demonstration episodes, it must determine which underlying physical conditions have shifted. Motor heating shows why the answer depends on where in its envelope the machine operated. A hot winding has higher resistance, which leaves the drive less voltage headroom. Inside the current-regulated region of the torque-speed envelope the drive still delivers the commanded torque, so the modeled voltage-headroom effect alone does not distinguish slow, hot and slow, cold demonstrations; other thermal and plant changes still require checking. Near the voltage limit at high joint speed, the same resistance rise caps the current and lowers the available torque, as [[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/02 Teaching the Machine/05 Physical Data/V4-05.03 Mechanics of Endogenous Covariate Shift#^sec-data-covariate-shift|section 5.2]] described, so a fast demonstration recorded hot reflects a weaker plant than one recorded cold.

↩︎ The torque-speed envelope and the back-EMF roll-off at high speed are established in **[[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/01 Machine Anatomy/02 The Physical Body/V4-02.06 Thermal Duty Cycles#^sec-body-torque-speed-envelopes|Torque-speed operating envelopes and back-EMF roll-off]]**.

That dependence is why pipelines stratify episodes before pooling them. Stratifying by measured actuator temperatures, maintenance logs, and workpiece wear enables engineers to verify whether identical actuation commands yield consistent plant dynamics. The standard diagnostic tool for this verification is a repeatable excitation trajectory. At the start of each operating shift, the manipulator replays a predetermined joint-space trajectory while stationary. If the tracking error residual exceeds an established threshold, the pipeline flags the plant as mechanically altered. Logging timestamps alone cannot establish that a machine degraded during an operational shift; dataset curation pipelines must justify pooling across sessions based on verified physical invariants and intended training objectives rather than superficial uniformity of array dimensions.

Demonstration collection policies drift just as readily as mechanical hardware. Across 141.4 h of scheduled cell time, teleoperators naturally adapt their control strategies. An operator who initially moved cautiously, executing small corrective sub-movements and pausing deliberately before striking a latch plate, gradually develops muscle memory. Over time, that operator rounds corners, anticipates contact forces, and exploits system momentum. These mature demonstrations rely on feedforward compensation and tight timing rather than reflecting changes in joint friction. Partitioning datasets into temporal windows and monitoring episode durations, corrective jerk counts, command amplitudes, and teleoperator intervention rates exposes these subtle shifts in human behavior. This behavioral tracking reveals whether later trajectories contribute advanced manipulation skills or introduce an entirely distinct behavioral regime that requires segregated dataset splits or specialized behavioral labels.

Sensor calibration also deteriorates silently across operating sessions. Standard storage containers such as HDF5, Zarr, and Apache Parquet preserve array topologies, data types, and chunk structures, but they cannot verify whether stored coordinates correspond to physical reality. If a robot is serviced, bumped by personnel, or re-zeroed between collection sessions, joint encoder offsets and external camera poses shift. A camera mount displaced by merely $3\text{ mm}$ moves workpiece pixels across image frames while the stored arrays remain syntactically valid. Clock synchronization drift across distributed fieldbus controllers introduces comparable corruption, skewing the temporal alignment between visual frames and tactile force measurements by tens of milliseconds.

↩︎ Distributed-clock synchronization across fieldbus drive nodes is established in **[[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/01 Machine Anatomy/04 The Nervous System/V4-04.06 Real-Time Command Transport and Bus Determinism#^sec-nervous-fieldbus-architectures|Event-triggered and time-triggered bus architectures]]**.

To detect this silent drift, automated ingestion pipelines rely on **kinematic reprojection verification**, formulated below and evaluated across a photographic mosaic in [[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/02 Teaching the Machine/05 Physical Data/V4-05.11 Dataset Degradation and Physical Distribution Shift#^fig-05-camera-calibration|figure 10]]. Let ${\mathbf{q}} \in \mathbb{R}^{n}$ denote the joint angles latched from optical encoders at the optical exposure midpoint. Forward kinematics ${\mathbf{FK}}({\mathbf{q}})$ maps these angles to the 3D surface vertices of the robot’s links in the base coordinate frame, ${\mathbf{p}_{\text{base}}} \in \mathbb{R}^3$. The camera’s extrinsic spatial transformation ${\mathbf{T}_{\text{cam} \leftarrow \text{base}}} = \begin{bmatrix} {\mathbf{R}} & {\mathbf{t}} \\ {\mathbf{0}}^\top & 1 \end{bmatrix} \in {SE(3)}$ maps base coordinates into the camera’s optical frame (${\mathbf{p}_c} = {\mathbf{R}} {\mathbf{p}_{\text{base}}} + {\mathbf{t}}$). Under the pinhole camera model parameterized by intrinsic matrix ${\mathbf{K}} \in \mathbb{R}^{3 \times 3}$,[[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/02 Teaching the Machine/05 Physical Data/V4-05.17 Footnotes#^fn12|¹²]] link points project to homogeneous image pixel coordinates ${\tilde{\mathbf{m}}} = [{u}, {v}, 1]^\top$:

$$
{Z_c} \begin{bmatrix} {u} \\ {v} \\ 1 \end{bmatrix} = {\mathbf{K}} \left( {\mathbf{R}} \, {\mathbf{FK}}({\mathbf{q}}) + {\mathbf{t}} \right) = \begin{bmatrix} {f_x} & 0 & {c_x} \\ 0 & {f_y} & {c_y} \\ 0 & 0 & 1 \end{bmatrix} \left( {\mathbf{R}} \, {\mathbf{FK}}({\mathbf{q}}) + {\mathbf{t}} \right),
$$

 where ${Z_c}$ is the point’s depth along the camera’s optical axis, the scale that division removes when the projection $\operatorname{proj}_{{\mathbf{K}}}$ maps a camera-frame point to its pixel. An automated check run on every recording session evaluates the reprojection residual between the projected link wireframe and the observed physical arm in the image:

$$
{\epsilon_{\text{reproj}}} = \left\| {\tilde{\mathbf{m}}_{\text{observed}}} - \operatorname{proj}_{{\mathbf{K}}}\left( {\mathbf{T}_{\text{cam} \leftarrow \text{base}}} \, {\mathbf{FK}}({\mathbf{q}}) \right) \right\|_2. \tag{9}
$$

 If mechanical vibration, fastener relaxation, or an accidental impact displaces the camera bracket by even ${\Delta} {\mathbf{t}} = 3\text{ mm}$ or tilts it by extrinsic misalignment ${\Delta} {\theta_{\text{ext}}} = 0.5^\circ$, the reprojection residual ${\epsilon_{\text{reproj}}}$ rises from its calibrated baseline of roughly one pixel to a large geometric offset. The focal length and working distance determine the size of that offset. While the underlying HDF5 or Zarr arrays remain syntactically flawless, an end-to-end visuomotor policy trained on shifted frames learns an erroneous spatial mapping between pixel coordinates and the end-effector tool center point (TCP). During closed-loop autonomous deployment, the policy issues reaching commands that miss physical targets by the uncompensated calibration offset, converting valid demonstrations into persistent latching jams or collisions with fixtures.

^eq-data-reprojection-residual

[[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/02 Teaching the Machine/05 Physical Data/V4-05.11 Dataset Degradation and Physical Distribution Shift#^fig-05-camera-calibration|Figure 10]] displays this verification pipeline across a $7 \times 7$ grid of workspace scenes from the DROID dataset. In each, a colored silhouette of the robot arm, rendered from its Unified Robot Description Format (URDF) kinematic mesh at the recorded joint angles, is projected through each camera’s extrinsic calibration matrix and overlaid directly onto the raw photographic frame. Where the mask and the physical arm coincide, camera extrinsics and joint encoders are geometrically aligned; where the colored silhouette departs from the links, the check exposes camera mount slippage before corrupted trajectory pairs enter training. The DROID corpus showed how easily mount drift escapes standard checks. Although its cameras were initially calibrated with checkerboard targets during data collection, independent re-analysis revealed that rendered kinematic masks barely overlapped visible robot links across numerous recorded episodes ([[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/02 Teaching the Machine/05 Physical Data/V4-05.16 Source References#^ref-lu2025ctrnetx|Lu et al. 2025]]). The DROID research team subsequently evaluated stored extrinsics by measuring mask intersection-over-union, discarded failed sessions, re-estimated camera poses using learning-based hand-eye estimators, and published updated calibrations for approximately 36,000 episodes ([[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/02 Teaching the Machine/05 Physical Data/V4-05.16 Source References#^ref-khazatsky2024droid|Khazatsky et al. 2024]]). This calibration error shipped within a major open-source corpus because it was transparent to standard file format parsers. To prevent these silent failures, ingestion architectures must bind every recorded episode to an immutable calibration identifier and real-time clock synchronization metrics. Trajectories collected under conflicting geometric or temporal calibrations force neural policies to average incompatible sensorimotor distributions within identical state spaces.

### Sim-To-Real Transfer Artifacts in Rigid-Body Simulators

^sec-data-sim-to-real-artifacts

In a rigid-body simulator with a relaxed soft-contact solver, a scripted gripper can interpenetrate a jammed latch plate by a millimeter and slide it open without binding. A behavioral cloning policy trained on those synthetic trajectories learns to push straight through resistance that stalls the physical arm. Because synthetic data generation replaces hardware wear with numerical approximations, degradation in a simulated dataset comes from the solver’s contact model rather than from slipped camera brackets or fatigued operators. Discrete-time physics engines resolve contact constraints once per integration step, and the chosen formulation (whether strict complementarity constraints ([[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/02 Teaching the Machine/05 Physical Data/V4-05.16 Source References#^ref-stewart1996implicit|Stewart and Trinkle 1996]]), convex soft-contact relaxations ([[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/02 Teaching the Machine/05 Physical Data/V4-05.16 Source References#^ref-todorov2012mujoco|Todorov et al. 2012]]), or penalty-based spring-damper models) sets penetration depths, slip thresholds, and drivetrain compliance. Whereas reinforcement learning agents actively exploit numerical quirks to maximize synthetic reward, behavioral cloning policies passively inherit whatever unphysical contact impulses, penetrations, or frictionless transitions the simulator produces. Co-training on simulated and physical demonstrations can nonetheless improve real-world policy success despite discrepancies between the two regimes ([[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/02 Teaching the Machine/05 Physical Data/V4-05.10 Cross-Embodiment Transfer#^tbl-05-state-of-practice|table 5]]) ([[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/02 Teaching the Machine/05 Physical Data/V4-05.16 Source References#^ref-maddukuri2025cotraining|Maddukuri et al. 2025]]). A physical ingestion pipeline cannot enforce exact contact fidelity across arbitrary geometries; its job is to preserve provenance. Every synthetic trajectory must record its simulation engine, software version, contact solver parameters, and integration time step. The scenario table also flags cells populated solely by synthetic rollouts ([[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/02 Teaching the Machine/05 Physical Data/V4-05.12 The Dataset Schema#^tbl-05-provenance-schema|table 6]]), so downstream training and diagnostics can separate solver artifacts from hardware behavior (**[[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/02 Teaching the Machine/06 Policy Training/V4-06.05 The Simulator as a System Component#^sec-training-contact-exploit|The sim-to-real contact exploit]]**).

↪ The decomposition of transfer discrepancies into dynamics, sensing, appearance, and timing channels, sized from paired command-response traces, is developed in **[[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/02 Teaching the Machine/06 Policy Training/V4-06.06 Physical Channels of the Sim-To-Real Gap#^sec-training-sim-to-real-gap|Physical Channels of the Sim-to-Real Gap]]**.

### Rig-To-Rig Divergence Across Nominally Identical Fleets

^sec-data-fleet-divergence

When a worn secondary manipulator with $0.35^\circ$ of gearbox backlash and a $22\text{ ms}$ torque-response lag records latch demonstrations for a fleet, the human operator unconsciously adds aggressive lead compensation to overcome the deadband. Replaying or imitating those demonstrations on a newly commissioned arm with $0.02^\circ$ of backlash drives the new arm into limit-cycle oscillations. Even among factory-identical robots of the same model number, individual units differ in gearbox wear, thermal dissipation, and motor-controller filter tunings. To keep fleet-wide pooling from mixing incompatible plants, data ingestion pipelines must bind each unit’s embodiment contract (its kinematic parameters, actuator serial numbers, controller firmware revisions, and identified dynamic models) to every recorded file. They then filter out trajectories from units that violate the target platform’s compatibility bounds.

Aggregating fleet data also exposes a second failure mode: confusing physical task failures with telemetry logging faults. A failed attempt is a complete, physically valid sequence in which the robot tried a task and missed the goal. Discarding those episodes introduces survivorship bias[[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/02 Teaching the Machine/05 Physical Data/V4-05.17 Footnotes#^fn13|¹³]] and strips the dataset of the boundary states, recovery paths, and negative outcomes needed to train stabilizing policies. A failed recording, by contrast, is an infrastructure fault, such as a dropped network packet, a torn image frame, or a ring-buffer overrun. Ingesting a corrupted recording without tracking gaps presents missing or uneven sample intervals as a continuous fixed-rate stream, corrupting finite-difference state derivatives. Data pipelines must therefore sort every session into one of three categories: a retained success, a retained physical failure, or an excluded logging fault tagged with diagnostic error codes. In the door-latching campaign, an episode halted by the 15 N contact tripwire is a valid physical failure and stays in the provenance archive, whereas a run in which the 6-axis force-torque sensor dropped packets is quarantined with its bus error code. Retaining a physical failure does not make its commands expert imitation targets; only stabilized, reviewed recovery segments or expert-relabeled states qualify under the intervention curation rules.

Even when every packet arrives intact, a fleet interface can still corrupt an archive by recording numbers in the wrong physical units or coordinate frame. Because standard schema validators check only data types and value ranges, such mismatches pass ingestion silently until a downstream consumer interprets the numbers under a different convention (war story [5.1](https://mlsysbook.ai/vol4/05_data/05_data.html#ws-data-mars-climate-orbiter-unit-mismatch)).

**War Story 5.1: Mars Climate Orbiter unit mismatch (1999)**

**Context**: During its nine-month cruise to Mars in 1998 and 1999, the Mars Climate Orbiter fired small thrusters to desaturate its reaction wheels, and the navigation team on the ground folded the resulting velocity changes into the trajectory models used to plan each correction maneuver ([[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/02 Teaching the Machine/05 Physical Data/V4-05.16 Source References#^ref-nasa1999mco|Stephenson et al. 1999]]).

**Mechanism**: A ground software file (called “Small Forces” in the investigation) reported thruster impulse in pound-force-seconds ($\text{lbf}\cdot\text{s}$), while the trajectory models that consumed it assumed newton-seconds ($\text{N}\cdot\text{s}$) as the interface specification required. Because $1\text{ lbf}\cdot\text{s} = 4.45\text{ N}\cdot\text{s}$, every desaturation event entered the models too small by a factor of $4.45$. The values were well formed, in range, and of the expected type, so no schema check could flag them.

**Impact**: The accumulated error was never resolved during cruise. At orbit insertion the spacecraft passed about $57\text{ km}$ above the surface instead of the planned $226\text{ km}$ and was lost.

**Response**: The Mishap Investigation Board traced the loss to the missing unit conversion in the ground file. It recommended that the Mars Polar Lander project verify consistent units throughout design and operations and audit all data transferred between JPL and Lockheed Martin Astronautics for compliance with the interface specification, along with stronger navigation review and anomaly escalation.

**Systems lesson**: A number carries its meaning only together with its units and its frame. A robot dataset runs the same risk on every action tap and every calibration. A record that stores a value without the units, frame, and calibration identifier it was produced under lets a mismatched rig pass every tensor check, which is why the provenance record of [[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/02 Teaching the Machine/05 Physical Data/V4-05.12 The Dataset Schema#^sec-data-schema|section 5.11]] binds each tap to them.

### Operational Design Domain Blind Spots and Unsafe Interpolation

^sec-data-odd-blind-spots

If the warehouse manipulator contacts the cage door during a fast 0.10 m/s approach, an unpopulated cell in its scenario table, a policy trained on the `latch-recovery-02` archive can trigger the same withdraw-and-reseat motion that worked during a slow 0.03 m/s approach. The motion yanks the gripper back before impact strain has dissipated and bends the latch pin. Encountering an unobserved operational state does not make a neural controller pause; the network continues emitting commands, often replaying familiar recovery primitives in regimes where their physical preconditions fail. Each recovery primitive therefore needs its own scenario-table coverage for the physical states in which it executes, rather than a log of the failure conditions that trigger it. Collection supplies experience, and closed-loop evaluation must establish competence in the conditions where the policy is to operate. A qualified permission restriction or physical constraint must exclude regimes that remain unsupported. The 2023 Cruise pullover recounted in **[[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/00 Introduction/01 The Causal Boundary/V4-01.09 When the Responsibilities Fail Together#^sec-boundary-when-fails|When the Responsibilities Fail Together]]** is the same failure at road scale. A familiar maneuver, the pullover toward a minimal-risk condition, ran in a state no one had covered, with a pedestrian beneath the vehicle (**[[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/03 Running the Machine/12 Safety Enforcement/V4-12.10 The Fallback Ladder#^sec-enforcement-four-rungs|The four rungs of the fallback ladder]]** gives the full account).

Sensor calibrations, human operator habits, actuator wear states, and operational design domain boundaries define the physical semantics of every recorded trajectory. If data ingestion pipelines strip these physical metadata to generate flat, uniform tensors, downstream evaluations cannot determine whether an autonomous failure stems from model capacity limitations or from unmodeled distribution shifts embedded within the training corpus. The structured provenance schema of [[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/02 Teaching the Machine/05 Physical Data/V4-05.12 The Dataset Schema#^sec-data-schema|section 5.11]] defines the mechanisms that preserve this physical context throughout the dataset lifecycle.
