# V4-05.10 Cross-Embodiment Transfer

[[Home|Home]] · [[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/Volume 4 Overview|Volume Overview]] · [[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/02 Teaching the Machine/05 Physical Data/V4-05 Physical Data|V4-05 Physical Data]]

^sec-data-cross-embodiment

Borrowing manipulation demonstrations from another robot arm looks effortless in tensor space, because both datasets store image frames and seven-element action vectors. That ease disappears when a trajectory recorded on a wide-stroke, $50\text{ Hz}$ gripper drives a narrower, $20\text{ Hz}$ gripper into its mechanical endstops or misses a moving conveyor part by centimeters. Physical demonstration trajectories never represent a task in the abstract. Every recorded stream of sensor observations and actuator commands embeds the link lengths, joint velocity limits, camera optical paths, bus latencies, and contact-surface compliances of the logging hardware directly into its numbers. Reusing demonstration datasets across robots therefore requires separating environmental task invariants from machine-specific realizations, checking kinematic and dynamic compatibility, and resolving topological mismatches. This applies whenever the warehouse manipulator supplements its local latch demonstrations with data from foreign arms and grippers.

### Embodiment Contracts and the Normalization Fallacy

^sec-data-embodiment-contracts

Suppose a training pipeline rescales gripper commands from two different robots to the unit interval $[0, 1]$ so a single network head can predict both. A normalized command that opens a wide-stroke donor gripper far enough to clear a workpiece will open a narrower target gripper to only a fraction of that span, driving its fingers straight into the part. That failure occurs because an observation-action trajectory $({\mathbf{o}}_{t}, {\mathbf{a}}_t)$ permanently encodes the **embodiment contract** between a specific machine and its physical surroundings. This contract covers the sensor geometry and optical calibration and the sampling period and transport latency of the perception bus. It also covers the coordinate frames and kinematic limits of the actuators, the torque-speed saturation curves of the motors (including reflected rotor inertia ${N_{\text{gear}}}^2 {J_{\text{rotor}}}$ in geared transmissions), and the friction and compliance of the contact interface. For the running machine’s recorded teleoperation, the contract specifies a rolling-shutter navigation camera at 4056 by 3040 px and 60 Hz, a global-shutter wrist camera at 1440 by 1080 px on its dedicated bracket. The contract also specifies a 9-axis kinematic log sampled at the 1 kHz permission tick (notebook [5.2](https://mlsysbook.ai/vol4/05_data/05_data.html#nbk-data-ingestion-bandwidth-storage-budgets-multi-camera)). Every latch demonstration in that archive reflects that camera suite, that sensor bracket, and that exact arm. Altering any parameter in this contract alters the mapping between commanded inputs and physical state transitions. Even when two robots follow an identical Cartesian tool trajectory ${\mathbf{x}}({t})$, their required actuator torques diverge. Written per joint, Newton’s second law for a serial manipulator yields the manipulator equation, met earlier as the model classical control inverts:

$$
{\boldsymbol{\tau}} = {\mathbf{M}}({\mathbf{q}}){\ddot{\mathbf{q}}} + {\mathbf{C}}({\mathbf{q}}, {\dot{\mathbf{q}}}){\dot{\mathbf{q}}} + {\mathbf{g}}({\mathbf{q}}) + {\boldsymbol{\tau}_{\text{ext}}},
$$

 where ${\mathbf{q}}$ denotes joint positions, ${\mathbf{M}}({\mathbf{q}})$ is the configuration-dependent mass matrix, ${\mathbf{C}}({\mathbf{q}}, {\dot{\mathbf{q}}})$ collects Coriolis and centrifugal forces, ${\mathbf{g}}({\mathbf{q}})$ is the gravitational torque vector, and ${\boldsymbol{\tau}_{\text{ext}}}$ represents torques exerted by external contact forces. **[[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/03 Running the Machine/11 Trajectory Planning/V4-11.05 Kinodynamic Feasibility#^sec-planning-rigidbody-dynamics|Rigid-body dynamics and continuous collision checks]]** builds this equation up from the single-joint case. Because every term depends directly on link masses, rotor inertias, and gear ratios, the same tool trajectory demands different torque profiles across different robot embodiments.

↩︎ The manipulator equation, which classical control inverts to compute joint torques, is established in **[[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/01 Machine Anatomy/03 The Cognitive Brain/V4-03.03 The Classical Wall#^sec-brain-classical-wall|The Classical Wall]]**.

↩︎ Reflected rotor inertia, ${N_{\text{gear}}}^2 {J_{\text{rotor}}}$, and its effect on joint dynamics are established in **[[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/01 Machine Anatomy/02 The Physical Body/V4-02.05 Actuator Transmission Limits#^sec-body-reflected-rotor-inertia|Reflected rotor inertia and optimal inertia matching]]**.

While the embodiment contract defines the physics of a single machine, the **cross-embodiment compatibility contract** determines whether trajectories logged under one embodiment contract can safely serve as action targets on another. This compatibility contract defines the forward kinematics mapping (translating joint angles to tool pose), control-rate transformations (${\Delta} {t}$), and actuator effort bounds. These bounds prevent a high-force profile or rapid spatial velocity demonstrated on a rigid industrial arm from driving a lightweight cobot beyond its structural limits. Large pooled corpora rarely provide such guarantees. Open X-Embodiment maps action fields into a coarsely aligned seven-dimensional end-effector format and normalizes each dataset independently. However, it deliberately leaves coordinate frames unaligned and allows action vectors to represent either absolute or relative poses and velocities across different sub-corpora ([[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/02 Teaching the Machine/05 Physical Data/V4-05.16 Source References#^ref-open-x-embodiment-collaboration2024open-c3f8bd04|Open X-Embodiment Collaboration et al. 2024]]). A multi-task model trained on this pool learns to interpret identical action vectors differently based on embodiment tokens. In contrast, DROID sidesteps embodiment mismatch altogether by collecting all data on a single standardized robot model ([[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/02 Teaching the Machine/05 Physical Data/V4-05.16 Source References#^ref-khazatsky2024droid|Khazatsky et al. 2024]]). Demonstrations from diverse robots can enrich representation learning without an explicit compatibility contract, but such a contract becomes strictly mandatory the moment borrowed actions are imitated directly as supervisory labels or dispatched to a physical plant.

How much work the compatibility contract has to do is visible in the largest pooled robot corpus. The Open X-Embodiment collection merges independently recorded datasets into one training mixture and publishes, for each, its episode count, robot morphology, scene type, and command rate ([[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/02 Teaching the Machine/05 Physical Data/V4-05.16 Source References#^ref-open-x-embodiment-collaboration2024open-c3f8bd04|Open X-Embodiment Collaboration et al. 2024]]). [[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/02 Teaching the Machine/05 Physical Data/V4-05.10 Cross-Embodiment Transfer#^fig-05-oxe-composition|Figure 8]] weights each dataset by its episodes.

^fig-05-oxe-composition

Of the 2.4 million episodes across 72 datasets, 91 percent come from single arms and 89 percent from table-top scenes. Mobile manipulators, the class of the running machine, contribute 5 percent, and two datasets supply 95 percent of those. The command rates are as uneven: 57 percent of episodes were logged at 10 Hz, 28 percent declare no rate, and only 2 percent were recorded at or above the machine’s 20 Hz chunk rate. Corpus size therefore says little about coverage of one machine’s contract. Almost every episode in the mixture needs the rate transformation and morphology mapping of the compatibility contract before it can serve as an action target here.

A data-reuse pipeline must separate environmental task invariants from embodiment-specific realizations. A task invariant is a geometric or dynamical condition imposed by the physical process, for example bringing a compliant probe into contact with a terminal pad within $5^\circ$ of the surface normal while sustaining a normal contact force between $2.0\text{ N}$ and $5.0\text{ N}$. An embodiment realization is the concrete sequence of joint encoder counts, pulse-width modulation duty cycles, perception transport latencies, and sensor pixel rasters used to achieve that condition on a particular test rig. While the contact goal exists in world coordinates, the dataset stores only the realization. When a model trains directly on raw hardware realizations, it internalizes the idiosyncratic mechanics of the source testbed, including structural flexure, drive backlash, and sensor mounting oscillations.

Consider transferring a demonstration dataset collected on Machine A to Machine B for an identical pick-and-place task:

- **Machine A** operates a parallel-jaw gripper with a stroke of $0\text{ to }100\text{ mm}$, a $50\text{ Hz}$ command rate ($20\text{ ms}$ period), a continuous grasp force limit of $40\text{ N}$, and a wrist camera mounted $45\text{ mm}$ behind the tool center point with a $15\text{ ms}$ transport delay.
- **Machine B** operates a compact gripper with a stroke of $0\text{ to }60\text{ mm}$, a $20\text{ Hz}$ command rate ($50\text{ ms}$ period), a continuous force limit of $15\text{ N}$, and a wrist camera mounted $20\text{ mm}$ behind the tool center point with a $35\text{ ms}$ transport delay.

Projecting Machine A’s demonstrations directly through the physical constraints of Machine B reveals immediate points of hardware failure. For a workpiece of width $75\text{ mm}$, Machine A commands a gripper aperture of $80\text{ mm}$. That command exceeds Machine B’s mechanical stroke by $20\text{ mm}$, driving its leadscrew into the mechanical endstop. During the hold phase, Machine A applies $30\text{ N}$ of clamping force; executing that command on Machine B doubles its continuous rating of $15\text{ N}$ and forces its motor amplifier into current saturation. In the time domain, subsampling the $50\text{ Hz}$ trajectory onto Machine B’s $20\text{ Hz}$ bus aliases rapid contact dynamics and increases the half-period average hold age ${\Delta} {t} / 2$ from $10\text{ ms}$ to $25\text{ ms}$, introducing $15\text{ ms}$ of additional zero-order hold phase lag (**[[05 Reference/Volume IV - Physical AI/V4 Control and Dynamics#^sec-appendix-control-zoh|Discrete-time sampling and zero-order hold phase lag]]**). Compounded by the $20\text{ ms}$ difference in camera transport delay ($35\text{ ms} - 15\text{ ms}$), the added low-frequency phase-equivalent delay is $35\text{ ms}$, of which the camera difference contributes $20\text{ ms}$. At the assumed constant approach velocity of $0.10\text{ m/s}$, that phase approximation corresponds to an equivalent displacement of $3.5\text{ mm}$. Actual contact overrun requires measured or bounded sampling phase, capture and transport, computation, actuation, and plant response; the phase-equivalent value does not establish when the first correction dispatches. Meanwhile, the $25\text{ mm}$ disparity in wrist camera mounting introduces optical parallax that shifts the apparent position of the workpiece boundary across the camera raster.

Standard machine learning pipelines often try to reconcile these discrepancies by normalizing observation and action channels to $[0, 1]$ or $[-1, 1]$. That rescaling aligns tensor ranges while leaving the physical mismatch intact. Normalizing gripper travel to $[0, 1]$ turns Machine A’s safe $80\text{ mm}$ opening (${\tilde{a}} = 0.80$) into a $48\text{ mm}$ opening on Machine B ($0.80 \times 60\text{ mm}$) that drives its fingers into the $75\text{ mm}$ workpiece during approach. Normalizing feature ranges rescales numbers, but it cannot lengthen a leadscrew, lower motor coil resistance, or recover bus transport delay. Demonstration trajectories plotted in normalized unit spaces may look identical, yet only the subset inside the physical intersection (apertures below $60\text{ mm}$, forces under $15\text{ N}$, and command frequencies below $20\text{ Hz}$) can run on the target hardware without saturation or contact damage. Even within that intersection, two arms with different joint layouts can collide with the workspace when they share a single Cartesian command ([[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/02 Teaching the Machine/05 Physical Data/V4-05.10 Cross-Embodiment Transfer#^sec-data-kinematic-topology|section 5.9.2]]).

### Kinematic Topology Mismatches and Null-Space Projection

^sec-data-kinematic-topology

Even when end-effectors, force ratings, and control rates align, differences in arm geometry can break a transferred trajectory. When a 7-DoF arm’s demonstration is compressed into a 6-DoF Cartesian hand displacement so it can be pooled with other robots, the log discards the arm’s elbow posture; replaying that 6-DoF stream through a target robot’s inverse kinematics solver can swing the elbow into a shelf upright or drive a lighter arm through a singularity, even though the hand follows the exact demonstrated path. Combining robots with different kinematic structures introduces two topological conflicts:

1.  **Action space incompatibility**: A 7-DoF arm records joint positions ${\mathbf{q}} \in \mathbb{R}^7$, a bimanual workstation records ${\mathbf{q}} \in \mathbb{R}^{14}$, and a mobile base logs planar velocities $({\dot{x}}, {\dot{y}}, {\dot{\theta}_{\text{head}}})$. Because joint-space commands cannot transfer directly across distinct kinematic arrangements, multi-embodiment models project action sequences into a common Cartesian end-effector displacement:

$$
{\Delta} {\mathbf{x}}_t = ({\Delta} {x}, {\Delta} {y}, {\Delta} {z}, {\Delta} {\phi_{\text{roll}}}, {\Delta} {\phi_{\text{pitch}}}, {\Delta} {\phi_{\text{yaw}}}) \in \mathbb{R}^{6}, \quad {a_{\text{gripper}}} \in [0, 1],
$$

 where the final three elements represent local roll, pitch, and yaw increments under a declared rotation convention. This displacement vector is not itself a member of the Special Euclidean group ${SE(3)}$; any numerical integration must handle angle wrapping and representation singularities. Projecting into Cartesian space standardizes spatial geometry, but it strips away actuator dynamics. A Cartesian step that an industrial arm executes effortlessly near its nominal pose can force a lighter arm through a kinematic singularity, a configuration in which aligned joints leave the tool unable to move in some direction. The manipulator Jacobian ${\mathbf{J}}({\mathbf{q}})$, a configuration-dependent matrix that **[[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/03 Running the Machine/11 Trajectory Planning/V4-11.03 The Planning Handoff and the Trajectory Record#^sec-planning-handoff-kinematics|Mapping tool motion to joint limits]]** develops on the machine’s own arm, maps joint velocities to tool spatial velocities through ${\dot{\mathbf{x}}} = {\mathbf{J}}({\mathbf{q}}){\dot{\mathbf{q}}}$. When ${\mathbf{J}}({\mathbf{q}})$ loses row rank at a singularity, no finite joint velocity can generate motion along the lost Cartesian direction. Commanded displacements near such poses demand joint velocities that immediately exceed the target machine’s actuator velocity bounds.
2.  **Null-space projection and elbow swivel ambiguity**: Away from singularities, mapping a 7-DoF arm into a 6-DoF Cartesian delta projects out the one-dimensional null space ($7 - 6 = 1$ redundant degree of freedom). For a redundant manipulator with Jacobian ${\mathbf{J}}({\mathbf{q}}) \in \mathbb{R}^{6 \times 7}$, the general kinematic relationship between Cartesian velocity ${\dot{\mathbf{x}}}$ and joint velocity ${\dot{\mathbf{q}}}$ is:

$$
{\dot{\mathbf{q}}} = {\mathbf{J}^\dagger}({\mathbf{q}}) {\dot{\mathbf{x}}} + \left( {\mathbf{I}} - {\mathbf{J}^\dagger}({\mathbf{q}}) {\mathbf{J}}({\mathbf{q}}) \right) {\dot{\mathbf{q}}_0},
$$

 where ${\mathbf{J}^\dagger} = {\mathbf{J}}^\top ({\mathbf{J}} {\mathbf{J}}^\top)^{-1}$ is the Moore-Penrose pseudoinverse (the minimum-norm joint velocity producing ${\dot{\mathbf{x}}}$ whenever ${\mathbf{J}}$ has full row rank), and the projection operator $({\mathbf{I}} - {\mathbf{J}^\dagger} {\mathbf{J}})$ filters an arbitrary joint-velocity bias ${\dot{\mathbf{q}}_0} \in \mathbb{R}^7$ into self-motion that leaves the end-effector still. For an anthropomorphic arm with shoulder and wrist geometry that admits this construction, a fixed end-effector pose in ${SE(3)}$ and a fixed shoulder anchor leave an elbow orbit around the shoulder-to-wrist axis, parameterized by the **elbow swivel angle** ${\psi}$. A general redundant arm instead requires its own configuration parameter or the full joint vector; one redundant degree of freedom does not imply this circular geometry. Discarding this internal degree of freedom to impose a uniform 6-DoF action representation erases the human operator’s postural intent. When the target robot receives this 6-DoF command stream, its local inverse kinematics (IK) solver must resolve the redundancy using an arbitrary mathematical heuristic, such as joint-limit centering or manipulability maximization. Without the original swivel angle, the solver may adopt an elbow posture that drives joints into hard stops or sweeps the elbow into nearby shelving, fixtures, or personnel during a motion that was entirely collision-free in the source environment. Preserving the applicable redundancy parameter, or the complete joint-state vector, within the trajectory’s provenance metadata retains the operator’s postural intent for target-specific retargeting and collision checks. The running mobile manipulator archive maintains this intent directly: its arm has redundant kinematics, and its logger captures all 9 axes of the arm and base at the 1 kHz permission tick. Any pooled 6-DoF delta generated from a latch-servicing trajectory can always be traced back to the demonstrator’s original elbow posture.

↪ Forward kinematics, the manipulator Jacobian, its singularities, and the pseudoinverse are developed in **[[05 Reference/Volume IV - Physical AI/V4 Control and Dynamics#^sec-appendix-control-kinematics|Forward kinematics and singularities]]**.

Reusing physical data across different robots therefore requires two validation gates at the ingestion boundary, corresponding to two distinct roles in training:

- The **pretraining admission gate** evaluates whether a trajectory can safely contribute to general representation learning. This gate validates data provenance, observation integrity, coordinate frame conventions, action semantics (reference frame, absolute vs. relative representations, position vs. velocity modes), and sampling rates. In particular, source actions admitted at this gate need not be physically executable on the target robot. Multi-embodiment foundation models rely on this gate to ingest broad visual and task structures across dozens of robot morphologies before fine-tuning on target hardware ([[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/02 Teaching the Machine/05 Physical Data/V4-05.16 Source References#^ref-open-x-embodiment-collaboration2024open-c3f8bd04|Open X-Embodiment Collaboration et al. 2024]]; [[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/02 Teaching the Machine/05 Physical Data/V4-05.16 Source References#^ref-black2024pi0|Black et al. 2024]]).
- The **target-label gate** applies whenever recorded actions serve as direct imitation labels or execution setpoints on the physical plant. This gate maps the source trajectory through target forward kinematics to verify coordinate consistency ([[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/02 Teaching the Machine/05 Physical Data/V4-05.16 Source References#^ref-lynch2017modern|Lynch and Park 2017]]). It confirms that commanded joint velocities and accelerations remain within the target plant’s dynamic envelope ([[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/02 Teaching the Machine/05 Physical Data/V4-05.16 Source References#^ref-tedrake2023manipulation|Tedrake 2023]]). It also verifies that task-critical features remain within the field of view and dynamic range of the target sensors. When commanded trajectories violate kinematic reach or motor torque limits, an ingestion pipeline cannot simply clamp the values to target boundaries. Clamping distorts velocity profiles, alters phase relationships, and destroys the causal link between observed spatial errors and corrective control outputs. Trajectories that satisfy the target dynamics pass untouched; trajectories that admit valid geometric retargeting through inverse kinematics are relabeled; and trajectories that demand unobtainable actuator authority or rely on unobservable states are rejected.

Evaluating the two-gripper scenario of [[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/02 Teaching the Machine/05 Physical Data/V4-05.10 Cross-Embodiment Transfer#^sec-data-embodiment-contracts|section 5.9.1]] through this target-label gate resolves each trajectory segment systematically. The initial opening phase must be rejected rather than relabeled, because satisfying the task invariant (clearing the $75\text{ mm}$ workpiece) demands an aperture that Machine B cannot physically achieve. The $30\text{ N}$ grasp hold is rejected on identical grounds, as it exceeds Machine B’s continuous motor rating. Conversely, the $50\text{ Hz}$ trajectory can be resampled and relabeled onto Machine B’s $20\text{ Hz}$ control bus only if the slower command stream preserves the task-critical contact dynamics and remains within the target’s verified tracking and force limits. The data record must also log the added zero-order hold lag and camera transport delay so that policy training accounts for the altered timing. An ingestion pipeline lacking this gate injects dynamically invalid trajectories into the training distribution. A policy trained on such unvetted data fails in deployment not because of neural network underfitting, but because its training labels were physically valid only on the donor hardware. For the latch-servicing campaign, the target plant is the mobile manipulator’s own arm and gripper. Every borrowed trajectory proposed as a latch training label must pass this gate against the mobile manipulator’s verified limit record, regardless of the donor platform’s reported capabilities.

↩︎ The limit record, which states each measured limit with its operating conditions, uncertainty, and margin, is established in **[[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/01 Machine Anatomy/02 The Physical Body/V4-02.08 Measuring a Machine’s Own Limits#^sec-body-limit-record-schema|The six elements of a limit record]]**.

The warehouse conveyor pick demonstrates a transfer mismatch rooted in task dynamics rather than robot kinematics. At the sorting station, the arm grasps a workpiece moving along a belt at 0.20 m/s. The workpiece translates 5 mm during each 25 ms control step and advances 80 mm over a single 16-step action chunk of 400 ms, a displacement of 5.3× the allowable 15 mm grasp tolerance. A borrowed tabletop demonstration recorded on a stationary bench defines its grasp trajectory entirely in the static table frame. If a policy imitates that trajectory unchanged at the conveyor, an open-loop action chunk aims at the spatial location the part occupied when the chunk was generated. The target-label gate can adapt such a static trajectory only by transforming the end-effector path into the moving workpiece coordinate frame. This coordinate transformation requires both the instantaneous object pose in the donor metadata and the conveyor velocity in the target environment record. An external trajectory lacking this telemetry can enter the pipeline solely through the pretraining admission gate. Teleoperation data collected directly on the warehouse machine requires no such mathematical rescue, as the human operator generated the demonstration commands with the conveyor in motion.

### Choosing Pooled Experience for the Target Task

^sec-data-scaling-frontier

Pretraining on a million pooled trajectories from foreign robots does not help the warehouse manipulator open a displaced latch if the training mixture drowns out the machine’s own contact dynamics. The same holds if the mixture adds ten thousand repetitions of a single tabletop scene instead of covering new operating cells. Large-scale pooled corpora such as DROID ([[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/02 Teaching the Machine/05 Physical Data/V4-05.16 Source References#^ref-khazatsky2024droid|Khazatsky et al. 2024]]), BridgeData v2 ([[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/02 Teaching the Machine/05 Physical Data/V4-05.16 Source References#^ref-walke2023bridgedata|Walke et al. 2023]]), and Open X-Embodiment ([[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/02 Teaching the Machine/05 Physical Data/V4-05.16 Source References#^ref-open-x-embodiment-collaboration2024open-c3f8bd04|Open X-Embodiment Collaboration et al. 2024]]) offer broad scene diversity and multi-robot demonstrations, but their value to a specific deployment depends on what information survives the admission gates of [[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/02 Teaching the Machine/05 Physical Data/V4-05.10 Cross-Embodiment Transfer#^sec-data-kinematic-topology|section 5.9.2]]. Even when kinematic mismatches prevent direct imitation of donor joint commands, foreign demonstrations can supply visual representations, object affordances, and high-level task structures, provided the campaign measures how those additions fill its scenario table.

Empirical scaling studies since 2024 support the premise behind the scenario table, that policies generalize with the number of distinct environmental conditions in their data rather than with repetitions of the same ones. In an extensive empirical evaluation spanning more than 40,000 demonstrations collected with handheld grippers, policy success across novel environments and unseen objects scaled as a power law with the count of distinct training environments and objects. Adding further demonstrations within existing environments yielded sharply diminishing returns once a modest baseline was achieved. A dataset collecting 50 demonstrations across each of 32 environments achieved 90 percent on novel environment-object pairs ([[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/02 Teaching the Machine/05 Physical Data/V4-05.16 Source References#^ref-lin2025datascaling|Lin et al. 2025]]). Similarly, evaluating mobile manipulation across residential settings revealed steady performance gains as the training distribution expanded from 3 to 104 homes. At 104 locations, the generalized model matched the performance of an in-domain baseline trained exclusively within the evaluation homes ([[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/02 Teaching the Machine/05 Physical Data/V4-05.16 Source References#^ref-black2025pi05|Black et al. 2025]]). Viewed through the scenario table, the primary scaling axis is the count of distinct operational cells populated by the corpus. The declared minimum coverage threshold ${N_{\min}}$ identifies cells that still need collection; it does not establish that additional trajectories within a populated cell have no value. Populating operational cells is necessary but not sufficient on its own. As Belkhale et al. ([[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/02 Teaching the Machine/05 Physical Data/V4-05.16 Source References#^ref-belkhale2023dataquality|2023]]) demonstrate, dataset utility depends on two additional quality metrics: action divergence (the discrepancy between expert actions and learned policy outputs at reachable states) and transition diversity (the stochasticity of outcomes from given state-action pairs). Uncurated state diversity does not automatically yield performance improvements.

These demonstration corpora also diverge in how they acquire operational diversity. Open X-Embodiment aggregates 60 distinct datasets across 22 different robotic embodiments with different, unaligned action spaces; despite this lack of structural alignment, joint pretraining improved success rates across the smaller constituent datasets ([[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/02 Teaching the Machine/05 Physical Data/V4-05.16 Source References#^ref-open-x-embodiment-collaboration2024open-c3f8bd04|Open X-Embodiment Collaboration et al. 2024]]). DROID and AgiBot World instead hold the embodiment contract constant while maximizing environmental and task diversity. DROID deploys a single manipulator model across 564 in-the-wild environments ([[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/02 Teaching the Machine/05 Physical Data/V4-05.16 Source References#^ref-khazatsky2024droid|Khazatsky et al. 2024]]). AgiBot World scales this principle with a homogeneous fleet of more than 100 identical robots, producing pretraining data that demonstrated superior downstream transfer compared to Open X-Embodiment ([[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/02 Teaching the Machine/05 Physical Data/V4-05.16 Source References#^ref-bu2025agibot|AgiBot-World-Contributors et al. 2025]]). A homogeneous fleet removes nominal kinematic mismatches, but each unit must still satisfy the target-label gate through verified calibration and dynamic compatibility. The fleet also remains vulnerable to the shared kinematic blind spots of that specific platform. Conversely, a multi-robot corpus can broaden perceptual learning through the pretraining gate; its actions become target labels only after passing the target-label gate. Balancing these disparate data streams requires deliberate weighting within the training mixture; dynamically weighting datasets to safeguard the worst-performing target domain significantly outperforms uniform data sampling across Open X-Embodiment ([[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/02 Teaching the Machine/05 Physical Data/V4-05.16 Source References#^ref-hejna2025remix|Hejna et al. 2025]]).

Placing these landmark robot archives alongside community uploads and web-scale foundation-model corpora exposes both how physical collection has scaled and where its ceiling sits ([[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/02 Teaching the Machine/05 Physical Data/V4-05.10 Cross-Embodiment Transfer#^fig-05-robot-data-scaling|figure 9]]). Across 11 major physical collection campaigns since 2015 ([[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/02 Teaching the Machine/05 Physical Data/V4-05.10 Cross-Embodiment Transfer#^fig-05-robot-data-scaling|figure 9]], panel a), robot datasets progressed from single-lab grasping farms (50,000 to 800,000 trials in a fixed bin ([[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/02 Teaching the Machine/05 Physical Data/V4-05.16 Source References#^ref-levine2018learning|Levine et al. 2018]]; [[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/02 Teaching the Machine/05 Physical Data/V4-05.16 Source References#^ref-kalashnikov2018qtopt|Kalashnikov et al. 2018]])) through multi-scene teleoperation (60,096 trajectories in BridgeData V2 and 76,000 in DROID ([[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/02 Teaching the Machine/05 Physical Data/V4-05.16 Source References#^ref-walke2023bridgedata|Walke et al. 2023]]; [[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/02 Teaching the Machine/05 Physical Data/V4-05.16 Source References#^ref-khazatsky2024droid|Khazatsky et al. 2024]])) to million-episode pooled and fleet archives (2,419,193 episodes in Open X-Embodiment and 1,001,552 in AgiBot World ([[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/02 Teaching the Machine/05 Physical Data/V4-05.16 Source References#^ref-open-x-embodiment-collaboration2024open-c3f8bd04|Open X-Embodiment Collaboration et al. 2024]]; [[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/02 Teaching the Machine/05 Physical Data/V4-05.16 Source References#^ref-bu2025agibot|AgiBot-World-Contributors et al. 2025]])). Those pooled campaigns sit five orders of magnitude above the 8–15 episode median of single-session community uploads in [[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/02 Teaching the Machine/05 Physical Data/V4-05.07 Teleoperation Demonstration Costs#^fig-05-lerobot-hub|figure 5]]. Even million-episode robot campaigns, however, confront the cross-domain gap in panel b. Across 902 language, vision, and robotics models with disclosed training dataset sizes in the Epoch AI catalog ([[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/02 Teaching the Machine/05 Physical Data/V4-05.16 Source References#^ref-epochai2026models|Epoch AI 2026]]), frontier language and vision corpora reach $10^{13}$ to $10^{14}$ tokens or images, whereas $\pi_0$’s $10{,}000$-hour robot pretraining corpus (1,000,000,000 state-action steps ([[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/02 Teaching the Machine/05 Physical Data/V4-05.16 Source References#^ref-black2024pi0|Black et al. 2024]])) sits 14,800× below DeepSeek-V3’s 14.8 trillion tokens.

Because every physical trajectory is throttled by real-time motor execution and session yield ([[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/02 Teaching the Machine/05 Physical Data/V4-05.07 Teleoperation Demonstration Costs#^eq-data-demonstration-yield|equation 6]]), robot learning cannot close a four-order-of-magnitude data gap by brute-force teleoperation alone. The core engineering decision is whether incorporating compatible pooled experience improves target-task policy performance relative to an identical baseline trained strictly on in-domain data. Rigorous evaluation requires fixing the target task distribution and test protocol, maintaining full data provenance, and benchmarking both nominal task completion and recovery behavior. Merely increasing the number of embodiments in a dataset does not guarantee positive transfer. In the mobile manipulator’s displaced-latch campaign, the control baseline is a policy trained exclusively on 5,000 retained teleoperation demonstrations from the machine itself, populating a single cell within the six-cell scenario table. An expanded multi-robot corpus justifies its training overhead and ingestion complexity only if the resulting policy improves robustness on long-tail target conditions without regressing nominal latch-servicing performance.

While cross-embodiment compatibility contracts guard the ingestion boundary against hardware mismatches, demonstration data collected on the native machine can fail through internal mechanisms. Teleoperator drift, sensor calibration drift, fixture flexure, and misclassified execution failures corrupt physical datasets without altering tensor dimensions or triggering schema errors ([[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/02 Teaching the Machine/05 Physical Data/V4-05.11 Dataset Degradation and Physical Distribution Shift#^sec-data-failure-modes|section 5.10]]).
