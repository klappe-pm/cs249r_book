# V4-05.04 Recovery and Compounding Error

[[Home|Home]] · [[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/Volume 4 Overview|Volume Overview]] · [[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/02 Teaching the Machine/05 Physical Data/V4-05 Physical Data|V4-05 Physical Data]]

^sec-data-curse-dimensionality-recoverability

Opening a latch from a nominal centered approach and recovering from an off-axis sideways slip require different physical actions ([[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/02 Teaching the Machine/05 Physical Data/V4-05.04 Recovery and Compounding Error#^fig-05-latch-corridor|figure 1]]). Every retained demonstration guides the gripper forward along the narrow corridor centered on the strike plate, which populates the archive with thousands of forward-progress actions. The archive contains zero demonstrations that start from a gripper displaced a few millimeters toward the fixture and return it to the centerline. Because a skilled human operator rarely lets the gripper drift into those boundary regions, and because teleoperation runs that drifted noticeably were aborted or discarded during curation, nothing in the training data constrains the policy’s output at such a displaced state.[[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/02 Teaching the Machine/05 Physical Data/V4-05.17 Footnotes#^fn1|¹]] The **recoverability problem** is this absence of demonstrated corrective actions that steer the system back into the nominal corridor.

![[90 Assets/Volume IV/05 Data/Fig05 Latch Corridor 1b01dd8be2.svg]]

*Top-view sketch. A shaded horizontal corridor runs left to right toward a strike plate on the right, with five arrows pointing along its centerline. Above the corridor, a small square marks a displaced gripper. A dashed arrow from the gripper toward the corridor is labeled as the missing return action, and a dashed arrow from the gripper toward a shaded fixture band at the top is labeled as drift into contact.*

Figure 1: **The latch corridor seen from above**: Retained demonstrations supply dense forward-progress actions inside a narrow corridor centered on the strike plate. A gripper displaced toward the fixture sits where the archive holds no demonstration, so nothing in the data specifies the action that returns it to the corridor, and an unconstrained output can carry it farther toward contact.

^fig-05-latch-corridor

Whether this missing return action leads to task failure depends on the closed-loop dynamics of the complete physical plant. Because the robot’s physical state encompasses joint coordinates, joint velocities, motor drive currents, contact wrenches (combined three-axis contact forces and torques), and raw camera streams, a nominal demonstration traces an infinitesimally thin manifold through a high-dimensional state space. Almost any unmodeled disturbance displaces the system off this path. If the physical plant and its low-level joint controllers inherently restore small lateral displacements toward the centerline through compliant passive dynamics, an imperfect policy output near the corridor boundary will naturally attenuate. If the system dynamics lack this self-centering stability, the tracking error will persist or compound until a learned recovery policy, the safety permission path, or a human supervisor intervenes. While production demonstration archives often contain occasional human corrections, their coverage cannot be assumed; it must be audited against an explicit scenario table ([[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/02 Teaching the Machine/05 Physical Data/V4-05.08 Collection Policy Coverage#^sec-data-policy-coverage|section 5.7]]).

The quadratic worst-case scaling of behavioral cloning can be derived through a discrete summation over horizon steps. Suppose an autonomous agent follows the expert trajectory until encountering its first execution error at step ${k} \in \{1, \dots, {S}\}$. If this error propels the system into an unsupported state from which it never recovers, the agent incurs an adverse task cost at every remaining step (${S} - {k}$). Assigning each time step an upper bound ${\epsilon}$ on the probability of committing a first error and normalizing the per-step cost to unity (${C}_t = 1$) yields the following worst-case cost accumulation. This discrete summation serves as an upper-bound derivation rather than an exact expectation over independent failure events:

$$
\sum_{{k}=1}^{S} {\epsilon} ({S} - {k}) = {\epsilon} \left( {S}^2 - \sum_{{k}=1}^{S} {k} \right) = {\epsilon} \left( {S}^2 - \frac{{S}({S}+1)}{2} \right) = \frac{{S}({S}-1)}{2} {\epsilon} \approx \frac{1}{2} {S}^2 {\epsilon}.
$$

 In open-loop supervised learning, by contrast, if per-step inference errors were independent and exerted zero feedback on subsequent model inputs, an error rate of ${\epsilon}$ per step would scale linearly over ${S}$ steps as $\sum_{t=1}^{S} {\epsilon} = {S}{\epsilon}$. The extra factor of ${S}$ appearing in ${\mathcal{O}(S^2 \epsilon)}$ is the mathematical signature of closed-loop endogenous feedback.

This quadratic bound characterizes the accumulation of task cost over time, not the physical trajectory of the machine. The result does not imply that spatial tracking errors grow quadratically as ${t}^2$. Closed-loop contact forces, mechanical compliance, and actuator saturation govern how physical trajectories evolve; the derivation formalizes only the cost of operating in states where the policy lacks training support.

Writing ${J}(\pi)$ and ${J}(\pi^*)$ for the expected cumulative task costs of the learned policy $\pi$ and expert policy $\pi^*$ over those ${S}$ steps, let ${\epsilon}$ bound the single-step action disagreement across expert-visited states under a 0–1 action loss: $\mathbb{E}_{{s} \sim {p^{\pi^*}}} [{\mathbb{I}}(\pi({s}) \ne \pi^*({s}))] \le {\epsilon}$. Assuming per-step task costs lie in $[0,1]$, behavioral cloning admits the worst-case bound ([[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/02 Teaching the Machine/05 Physical Data/V4-05.04 Recovery and Compounding Error#^eq-data-quadratic-drift|equation 1]]) derived by Ross and Bagnell ([[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/02 Teaching the Machine/05 Physical Data/V4-05.16 Source References#^ref-ross2010efficient|2010]]) and restated by Ross et al. ([[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/02 Teaching the Machine/05 Physical Data/V4-05.16 Source References#^ref-ross2011reduction|2011]]):

$$
{J}(\pi)-{J}(\pi^*) \le {S}^2{\epsilon}. \tag{1}
$$

 The published theorem carries a constant of one rather than the sketch’s one half; both are worst-case bounds, and their quadratic dependence on the horizon is the same.

^eq-data-quadratic-drift

In practice, this theoretical bound can exceed the maximum possible cost achievable in a finite episode. Because per-step costs are bounded in $[0,1]$, the excess cost is trivially bounded by ${S}$; hence, $\min({S}, {S}^2{\epsilon})$ represents the operational upper bound. Once the quadratic expression surpasses ${S}$, it provides no meaningful prediction regarding physical failure rates or contact severity. The practical engineering value of this bound lies in exposing why behavioral cloning has no defense in states its demonstrations never reached, rather than in predicting exact physical collision rates.

↪ The full derivation of the quadratic bound, with its loss and cost assumptions, is developed in **[[05 Reference/Volume IV - Physical AI/V4 Machine Learning Background#^sec-appendix-ml-compounding|Imitation Learning and Compounding Covariate Shift]]**.

The actual scaling with horizon length depends heavily on the chosen loss function, the action space, and the closed-loop plant stability. Consequently, [[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/02 Teaching the Machine/05 Physical Data/V4-05.04 Recovery and Compounding Error#^eq-data-quadratic-drift|equation 1]] reflects a classical result for discrete 0–1 loss rather than an absolute law for all imitation architectures. When trained under logarithmic loss (minimizing the negative log-probability assigned to expert actions) over a well-specified policy class containing the expert, behavioral cloning can achieve a sample complexity that does not grow with the horizon length. This result holds provided total reward is bounded and the policy class capacity is controlled ([[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/02 Teaching the Machine/05 Physical Data/V4-05.16 Source References#^ref-foster2024behavior|Foster et al. 2024]]). Continuous action spaces, however, introduce the opposite challenge. Even when the physical closed loop is exponentially stable (small mechanical perturbations decay at a fixed rate) and the expert policy is smooth and deterministic, any smooth, deterministic imitator can accumulate closed-loop execution errors that scale exponentially with horizon length relative to its training loss ([[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/02 Teaching the Machine/05 Physical Data/V4-05.16 Source References#^ref-simchowitz2025pitfalls|Simchowitz et al. 2025]]). The same analysis identifies two ways around this compounding ([[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/02 Teaching the Machine/05 Physical Data/V4-05.16 Source References#^ref-simchowitz2025pitfalls|Simchowitz et al. 2025]]). The first changes the policy class. A policy that is non-smooth, that is non-Markovian (its action depends on recent history rather than solely the current observation), or whose action distribution is multimodal (it can place probability on several distinct actions at once, rather than averaging them) escapes the result. Action chunking and diffusion decoders have these properties. The second changes the data, using demonstrations deliberately spread across a wide tube surrounding the nominal trajectory. That second remedy is a data-collection decision, and [[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/02 Teaching the Machine/05 Physical Data/V4-05.09 Intervention and Recovery Data#^sec-data-intervention-recovery|section 5.8]] returns to it as noise injection during teleoperation. Because the mobile manipulator operates in a continuous control space, closed-loop success ultimately hinges on whether the combined policy and low-level controllers actively restore a displaced gripper to the nominal path.

↩︎ Action chunking, in which the policy emits a short sequence of setpoints per inference, is established in **[[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/01 Machine Anatomy/03 The Cognitive Brain/V4-03.06 Chunk Supply and Evidence Validity#^sec-brain-action-chunking|Action chunking]]**.

Collecting more nominal expert demonstrations does not resolve endogenous covariate shift. Adding another thousand hours of clean, centered door openings merely increases sample density along the nominal trajectory; the empirical support outside this narrow tube remains strictly zero (${p^{\pi^*}}({s}) = 0$). This pitfall is the *nominal demonstration trap*. Beyond this coverage limitation, logging physical robot experience carries direct hardware costs. While a web crawler scrapes static text with zero marginal mechanical wear, every recorded second of robot telemetry consumes actuator fatigue life, heats motor windings ($d{T_{\text{winding}}}/d{t}$), wears down gearbox teeth, and depletes battery reserves. In practical data-collection campaigns, required pauses for operator fatigue, sensor recalibration, scene resets, and discarded trials often leave less than one quarter of scheduled workcell time as usable demonstration data ([[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/02 Teaching the Machine/05 Physical Data/V4-05.07 Teleoperation Demonstration Costs#^sec-data-teleop-costs|section 5.6]]). Physical telemetry is scarce, wear-intensive, and costly; collecting it without modeling closed-loop recovery dynamics compromises policy training.

↩︎ Actuator thermal dissipation ($d{T_{\text{winding}}}/d{t}$) and winding damage limits are established in **[[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/01 Machine Anatomy/02 The Physical Body/V4-02.06 Thermal Duty Cycles#^sec-body-thermal-duty-cycles|Thermal Duty Cycles]]**.

A physical command also changes meaning as it crosses the control stack toward the actuators. In a system partitioned by the proposal boundary, an operator’s or policy’s requested action is mapped into a trajectory setpoint, checked and potentially clamped by the safety permission path, and executed by the physical plant to produce a measured response. Logging only the raw requested action trains the policy on commands the plant may be physically incapable of executing; logging only the measured plant response teaches the policy to copy link flexure, gear backlash, and contact stalls as if they were intentional human skill. A physical data-collection campaign must therefore settle two decisions before recording its first trajectory. The first is which collection path generates the actions ([[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/02 Teaching the Machine/05 Physical Data/V4-05.05 Physical Telemetry Sources and Collection Paths#^sec-data-collection-sources|section 5.4]]). The second is which of the four representations of one command (the request, the mapped setpoint, the admitted command, or the measured response) the dataset preserves as its training label ([[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/02 Teaching the Machine/05 Physical Data/V4-05.06 Action Label Selection Across the Control Boundary#^sec-data-action-label|section 5.5]]).

↩︎ The proposal boundary, which separates a learned proposer from the permission path that alone may admit an action, is established in **[[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/00 Introduction/01 The Causal Boundary/V4-01.08 The Machine in Five Levels#^sec-boundary-machine-model|The Machine in Five Levels]]**.
