# V4-05.05 Physical Telemetry Sources and Collection Paths

[[Home|Home]] · [[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/Volume 4 Overview|Volume Overview]] · [[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/02 Teaching the Machine/05 Physical Data/V4-05 Physical Data|V4-05 Physical Data]]

^sec-data-collection-sources

When an engineer records demonstrations of the warehouse cage-door latch, the choice of collection rig decides which contact forces, hesitations, and recovery maneuvers ever reach the archive. Guiding the robot arm by hand gives the demonstrator direct tactile feel of the binding latch, but the demonstrator’s torso blocks the onboard cameras. Puppeteering the arm over a network clears the camera view, but display delay makes the operator inch forward in a stop-and-go hunt. Running a scripted spline produces clean, repeatable approaches that jam the moment the strike plate shifts by a millimeter. Because every attempt on the physical robot spends facility hours, joint life, and human effort, choosing a collection path trades collection cost against the behaviors the resulting archive can support. Seven collection paths appear in practice, six on physical hardware and a seventh in simulation:

1.  In **kinesthetic teaching**, a colocated human demonstrator physically grasps and guides the unpowered or gravity-compensated links of the mechanism. The logging pipeline records joint encoder positions at the native controller rate without network transport delays. However, human biomechanics govern the motion’s bandwidth, moving far more slowly than the controller’s sampling loop, while labor costs scale $1:1$ with demonstrator time. The demonstrator’s body also frequently occludes onboard camera views, introducing systematic visual distribution shifts relative to autonomous operation.
2.  In **teleoperation**, a remote operator manipulates an input device to puppet the follower robot across a network fabric.[[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/02 Teaching the Machine/05 Physical Data/V4-05.17 Footnotes#^fn2|²]] Teleoperation interfaces differ in how operator inputs map to robot actuators and how much physical contact information reflects back to the human. Joint-space leader-follower arms such as ALOHA ([[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/02 Teaching the Machine/05 Physical Data/V4-05.16 Source References#^ref-zhao2023learning|Zhao et al. 2023]]) and GELLO ([[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/02 Teaching the Machine/05 Physical Data/V4-05.16 Source References#^ref-wu2023gello|Wu et al. 2024]]) mirror the follower’s joint topology, often at reduced scale, streaming joint angles directly to the follower without inverse kinematics (computing the joint angles that place the tool at a commanded pose). In contrast, tracked spatial controllers and rate-controlled space mice command Cartesian tool poses or velocities that require inverse kinematics solvers at runtime, while force-reflecting bilateral arms actively transmit measured contact forces back to the operator’s hands. [[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/02 Teaching the Machine/05 Physical Data/V4-05.07 Teleoperation Demonstration Costs#^tbl-05-teleop-taxonomy|Table 2]] compares these interfaces and names the artifact each leaves in the recording.
3.  In **scripted collection**, a deterministic trajectory generator executes automated motion primitives, such as cubic splines or impedance-governed state machines. Ingestion rates are high and predictable ($50\text{--}500\text{ Hz}$), but trajectories traverse rigid, repetitive manifolds. Because these paths fail to generate corrective recovery data during off-nominal contact, the manipulator risks jamming whenever workpiece dimensions deviate by even a millimeter.
4.  In **exploratory collection**, an active neural policy or reinforcement learning agent selects actions to maximize an information-theoretic objective, such as epistemic entropy or intrinsic curiosity. Although data throughput matches the robot’s peak policy cadence ($50\text{--}100\text{ Hz}$), unconstrained exploratory actions risk high-energy collisions, tripping hardware safety tripwires or degrading mechanical gearboxes through repeated high-torque shock loads.
5.  In **autonomous deployment logging**, production policies control actuators during routine factory or warehouse operation, streaming multimodal telemetry into local circular buffers in situ. This channel yields zero-marginal-labor data at the system’s full execution rate ($10\text{--}50\text{ Hz}$ for high-level policies, $1\text{ kHz}$ for joint servos). However, runtime safety barriers heavily censor the recorded state distribution, capturing almost exclusively nominal operating regimes while excluding boundary failures.
6.  In **robot-free demonstration**, the operator carries a hand-held gripper fitted with a wrist camera and performs tasks directly in the physical environment, as in the Universal Manipulation Interface (UMI) ([[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/02 Teaching the Machine/05 Physical Data/V4-05.16 Source References#^ref-chi2024umi|Chi et al. 2024]]). Collection incurs zero robot operating hours or actuator wear, allowing a single demonstrator to gather data across locations where no robot is deployed. The engineering cost shifts to label synthesis: because the hand-held rig records its own Cartesian trajectory and visual feed without robot joint states, actions must be expressed relative to the gripper’s pose frame and executed downstream through the target robot’s controller. The deployed robot’s observation and actuation latencies must also be calibrated to match the recording timing ([[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/02 Teaching the Machine/05 Physical Data/V4-05.06 Action Label Selection Across the Control Boundary#^sec-data-multimodal-time-sync|section 5.5.2]]).

A seventh source removes physical hardware entirely. Simulation pipelines eliminate mechanical wear by substituting a numerical contact model for the physical plant, generating synthetic demonstrations at scale. Trajectory generators can expand a few hundred human seed demonstrations across novel object poses and scene layouts, replaying them in physics engines to synthesize tens of thousands of varied trajectories ([[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/02 Teaching the Machine/05 Physical Data/V4-05.16 Source References#^ref-mandlekar2023mimicgen|Mandlekar et al. 2023]]). Downstream policies can then co-train on simulated and real telemetry at a specified mixing ratio. However, these synthetic streams require rigorous metadata tagging: records must log their source generator, physics engine build, integrator step size, and contact solver parameters. The dataset’s scenario table, the list of required operating conditions that [[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/02 Teaching the Machine/05 Physical Data/V4-05.08 Collection Policy Coverage#^sec-data-curse-dimensionality-recoverability-geometry|section 5.7.1]] builds, must also flag every condition covered solely by synthetic rollouts, ensuring that training diagnostics can isolate simulation artifacts from measured physical plant behavior. The provenance requirements for synthetic telemetry appear under dataset degradation ([[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/02 Teaching the Machine/05 Physical Data/V4-05.11 Dataset Degradation and Physical Distribution Shift#^sec-data-sim-to-real-artifacts|section 5.10.2]]).

Because the collection source determines how the machine interacts with its physical environment, these data streams occupy disjoint state-action manifolds even when executing identical manipulation tasks. Consider releasing the warehouse mobile manipulator’s cage-door latch. A colocated demonstrator guiding the arm feels the latch bind through direct tactile resistance and immediately adjusts wrist compliance, whereas a remote teleoperator watching a compressed video stream lacks haptic feedback and falls into step-and-wait hunting, pausing repeatedly to check strike-plate contact. By contrast, a scripted controller advances along a fixed geometric path at constant velocity and produces the same rigid contact-force spike on every repetition, while an exploratory policy approaches at oblique angles that bind the latch until safety tripwires abort the trial. Far from representing noisy variations of a single ground-truth trajectory, these recorded streams reflect distinct dynamical systems with different bandwidths, transport latencies, and mechanical compliance profiles, as summarized in [[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/02 Teaching the Machine/05 Physical Data/V4-05.05 Physical Telemetry Sources and Collection Paths#^tbl-05-sources-taxonomy|table 1]].

The cheapest sources in [[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/02 Teaching the Machine/05 Physical Data/V4-05.05 Physical Telemetry Sources and Collection Paths#^tbl-05-sources-taxonomy|table 1]] also produce the highest volume of records. This abundance frequently invites a misconception borrowed from disembodied machine learning, where empirical scaling laws correlate model capability directly with raw token count or pretraining dataset volume (${N}$). In physical telemetry, treating every logged control step as an independent observation is a category error. An edge logger sampling at 1 kHz records 3.6 × 10⁶ state-action pairs every hour, yet adjacent timesteps carry near-total temporal autocorrelation (${\rho} \to 1.0$) because successive kinematic readings sampled 1 ms apart differ only by sub-millimeter structural displacements and sensor quantization noise. For a stationary scalar telemetry statistic with geometrically decaying autocorrelation ${\rho}_k = {\rho}^{k}$, as characterized by a first-order autoregressive AR(1) process, the large-sample *effective sample size* (ESS)[[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/02 Teaching the Machine/05 Physical Data/V4-05.17 Footnotes#^fn3|³]] for estimating its mean is:

$$
{N_{\text{eff}}} = {N} \frac{1 - {\rho}}{1 + {\rho}}. \tag{2}
$$

 Under this autoregressive model, an autocorrelation of ${\rho} =$ 0.999 across 3.6 × 10⁶ raw records yields only 1,800 statistically independent observations. While lag-1 correlation alone does not govern ESS across every non-stationary telemetry stream, high-frequency sampling still cannot substitute for operational diversity. Whereas consecutive tokens in language modeling represent high-entropy transitions across a large vocabulary, high-rate robot telemetry mostly oversamples the same physical trajectory. It can resolve fast contact transients when they occur, but raising the logger’s clock frequency never populates an unvisited scenario cell. Physical dataset coverage expands instead through independent physical episodes, diverse workpiece placements, varying illumination, and shifting friction coefficients. A curated archive containing several hundred episodes that systematically sweep initial geometric and mechanical conditions provides far richer empirical support than millions of repetitive timesteps collected across a static test bench.

### Human Demonstration Idiosyncrasies and Mechanical Artifacts

^sec-data-human-idiosyncrasies

When a behavioral cloning policy is trained directly on raw teleoperated joint velocities, the deployed robot arm often chatters its harmonic drives at $8\text{--}12\text{ Hz}$ or stalls for $300\text{ ms}$ a few millimeters in front of the latch strike plate. Neither defect comes from the network architecture. Because a human operator executed the training motions, the recorded telemetry embeds the demonstrator’s neuromuscular bursts, perceptual delays, and visual blind spots alongside the intended trajectory:

- **Neuromuscular sub-movements and physiological tremor:** Human motor control operates through discrete motor unit recruitment bursts rather than continuous, smooth trajectories. These neuromuscular dynamics introduce corrective sub-movements at $2\text{--}3\text{ Hz}$ overlaid with involuntary physiological tremor at $8\text{--}12\text{ Hz}$. If a high-frequency behavioral cloning policy trains on raw joint velocities without low-pass filtering, the neural network imitates this biological tremor as high-frequency torque chatter, accelerating mechanical fatigue in the robot’s harmonic drives and gearboxes.
- **Visual latency and step-and-wait hunting:** When an operator guides a manipulator toward a contact fixture using 2D camera monitors, monocular depth ambiguity and video compression latency force an unnatural *step-and-wait* hunting cycle. The operator inches the gripper forward by a few millimeters, pauses for $200\text{--}400\text{ ms}$ to visually confirm clearance or contact across the video stream, and only then resumes motion. An imitation policy trained on uncurated velocity streams treats these latency-induced pauses as deliberate actions, yielding deployed behaviors that stutter, oscillate, or stall mid-trajectory.
- **Attentional narrowing and gaze-conditioned bias:** Human demonstrators naturally focus visual attention on the active tool center point (TCP). Because the operator rarely tracks the peripheral links of the arm during fine manipulation, the robot’s elbow or shoulder links frequently sweep perilously close to structural supports or table edges. An imitation policy trained on these demonstrations inherits trajectories that graze workspace obstacles, yet lacks the human’s peripheral situational awareness or rapid collision avoidance reflexes.

Because an ingestion logger records every physical tremor, visual hesitation, and marginal joint clearance as part of the demonstration, imitation policies reproduce these flaws unless the training pipeline filters or abstracts them. Choosing a telemetry source decides what raw experience reaches the archive; choosing which control signal serves as the action label decides whether the policy learns the operator’s intent, the safety filter’s clamp, or the plant’s compliance ([[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/02 Teaching the Machine/05 Physical Data/V4-05.06 Action Label Selection Across the Control Boundary#^sec-data-action-label|section 5.5]]).
