# V4-07.05 Evidence Regimes

[[Home|Home]] · [[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/Volume 4 Overview|Volume Overview]] · [[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/02 Teaching the Machine/07 Closed-Loop Evaluation/V4-07 Closed-Loop Evaluation|V4-07 Closed-Loop Evaluation]]

^sec-evaluation-evidence-regimes

Before undertaking twenty physical door runs, an engineering team evaluating a robotic latch policy could score candidate predictions against logged human demonstrations, execute them inside a simulated physics environment, run them in shadow mode while a validated controller retains physical authority over the arm, or deploy them directly on the real mechanism with contact tripwires armed. These four configurations constitute the primary **evidence regimes** of embodied evaluation. [[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/02 Teaching the Machine/07 Closed-Loop Evaluation/V4-07.05 Evidence Regimes#^fig-07-evidence-regimes|Figure 2]] and [[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/02 Teaching the Machine/07 Closed-Loop Evaluation/V4-07.05 Evidence Regimes#^tbl-07-evidence-regimes|table 2]] map them across two structural axes: physical realism on the vertical axis (from logged and synthetic feeds up to live hardware) and causal feedback on the horizontal axis (from open loop to closed loop). Each regime establishes a distinct coupling between the policy, its sensory observations, and the underlying physical machine states, so each regime supports only a bounded empirical claim. None of these four settings alone reveals how an unassisted policy behaves during open-ended physical deployment, because each environment either breaks the control loop’s causal feedback or alters the distribution of states the robot encounters.

### The Four Evaluation Evidence Regimes

^sec-evaluation-four-regimes

The first axis, causal feedback, governs whether a candidate’s emitted actions alter future states and subsequent sensor observations ($\partial {\mathbf{x}}_{t+1}/\partial {\mathbf{a}}_{t} \ne {\mathbf{0}}$), or whether the policy merely reads a prerecorded, invariant sensory stream. In an open-loop configuration, execution errors cannot compound because mistakes never propagate into future inputs. In a closed loop, small policy errors perturb subsequent machine states and accumulate into compounding drift at a rate dictated by system dynamics and closed-loop feedback gains. The second axis, physical realism, governs whether sensory observations originate from physical transducers interacting with unmodeled contact dynamics, gear backlash, tire slip, and variable illumination (${f_{\text{real}}}$), or instead emerge from a synthetic numerical simulator or static buffer (${f_{\text{sim}}}$). No single testing configuration can maximize both axes simultaneously without exposing expensive hardware, or nearby operators, to an unproven and potentially destructive policy. Each quadrant of [[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/02 Teaching the Machine/07 Closed-Loop Evaluation/V4-07.05 Evidence Regimes#^fig-07-evidence-regimes|figure 2]] therefore provides evidence for specific claims and carries its own structural blind spot.

Replay cannot observe compounding drift, simulation leaves the reality gap open, shadow operation locks the trajectory to the baseline controller, and constrained testing truncates failures at the constraint boundary $\partial{\mathcal{S}}$. In recorded replay, the candidate policy executes strictly as a feedforward computation over static sensor buffers. Because the physical environment cannot respond to emitted actions, the state sequence remains fixed to the exact trajectory recorded during prior data collection. This regime provides solid empirical evidence for computational efficiency, dynamic memory allocation, and loss metrics against a reference benchmark. It can verify, for example, whether neural network inference fits within its target execution budget on an edge accelerator, and whether output distributions align numerically with logged human demonstrations. However, recorded replay cannot substantiate any claim regarding closed-loop stability or control authority. Because predicted actions never feed back into subsequent sensor frames, errors never induce consequences. If the policy commands an aggressive steering angle, the subsequent logged frame still shows the mobile base tracking straight down the corridor.

Closed-loop simulation restores the causal feedback path by substituting an explicit mathematical or numerical model, ${f_{\text{sim}}}$, for the physical robot and its operating environment. When the policy emits an action and the modeled safety permission path selects the control command ${\mathbf{u}}_t$, the physics engine numerically integrates the equations of motion, advances the scene geometry, and renders synthetic sensor observations for timestep ${t}+1$. This regime supports extensive testing of closed-loop dynamics, kinematic reachability, and recovery maneuvers. It can demonstrate, for example, whether a mobile base recovers gracefully from an initial tracking offset, or whether an articulated arm avoids self-collision throughout its reachable workspace across thousands of parallelized instances. However, the resulting evidence applies exclusively to the synthetic operator ${f_{\text{sim}}}$. Unmodeled transmission backlash, tire deformation, dynamic contact friction transitions, and optical sensor artifacts are omitted by construction. Demonstrating stability within ${f_{\text{sim}}}$ therefore establishes that the policy stabilizes the model, not that it will stabilize physical hardware.

Shadow operation deploys the candidate policy directly onto an active physical machine while a validated baseline controller, or a human operator, retains exclusive authority over the physical actuators. The candidate policy ingests live streaming sensor data, executes onboard inference amid real mechanical vibrations and fieldbus contention, and computes candidate actions ${\mathbf{a}_t^{\text{cand}}}$.[[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/02 Teaching the Machine/07 Closed-Loop Evaluation/V4-07.16 Footnotes#^fn4|⁴]] These proposed actions are logged to high-speed storage but never dispatched to the motor drives. Shadow operation yields direct evidence about real-time system performance. It can confirm, for instance, whether the latch policy maintains its target 20 Hz chunk rate under sustained thermal throttling, and quantify how frequently ${\mathbf{a}_t^{\text{cand}}}$ deviates from the baseline control action ${\mathbf{a}_t^{\text{base}}}$ beyond an acceptable safety threshold.

Shadow operation is limited, however, by the **trajectory confinement problem**. Because the baseline controller exercises exclusive control over the actuators, the physical robot traces trajectories governed strictly by the baseline state distribution ${d^{\pi_{\text{base}}}}$. If the candidate policy generates an action that would cause the arm to jam the latch, veer off course, or strike a support strut, the physical plant never executes the command. The candidate’s sensory stream remains confined to the safe states visited by the baseline supervisor. As a result, shadow testing cannot reveal whether the candidate could recover from an unvisited off-nominal state, nor can it expose whether its divergent actions would have caused mechanical damage.

Constrained physical testing closes the causal feedback loop on actual hardware by routing candidate policy proposals through the permission path to the actuators, strictly within an enforced safety envelope. These operational boundaries are enforced via hardware tethers, software velocity clamps, geofenced test enclosures, or a dedicated real-time permission path that intercepts and overrides unsafe setpoints with deterministic latency. Constrained testing delivers authentic empirical evidence regarding real-world contact mechanics, friction transitions, structural compliance, and sensor noise under actual operating loads, which is why the experimental door-latch trials belong in this regime.

When the safety system triggers an intervention, it alters or truncates the raw policy trajectory (${{n_{\text{int}}}}_i \ge 1, {A}_i = 0$), leaving the candidate’s unmitigated behavior permanently unobserved. Such a run answers two different questions, and the record must keep them apart. For the autonomy outcome it is an unambiguous failure, ${A}_i = 0$, because the candidate policy could not finish without external override. Even if the supervisor guides the robot to successful task completion, the trial cannot be scored as an autonomous success. For the separate event of an unassisted crash, the question of whether the policy would have damaged something had nobody stepped in, the run is right-censored.[[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/02 Teaching the Machine/07 Closed-Loop Evaluation/V4-07.16 Footnotes#^fn5|⁵]] The intervention terminated the trial before that terminal state could manifest, so the observation confirms neither unassisted success nor an inevitable crash. Any rigorous evaluation must report completion rates, intervention frequencies, and post-intervention recoveries strictly inside the verified operating envelope. Conflating safety-supervised task completion with unassisted policy survival commits the **censored trial fallacy**, which attributes to the machine learning policy a level of robustness that the permission path or the safety operator provided.

Certain software properties transfer cleanly across regime boundaries when inputs and execution semantics match. Deterministic numerical calculations, floating-point arithmetic invariants, and tensor shape contracts can be checked under the same precision, rounding, and overflow rules. An activation overflow transfers only when the simulator and target implement the same arithmetic behavior. In contrast, runtime execution latency, bus contention, and memory bandwidth utilization cannot transfer purely across regimes. They require re-measurement under the actual scheduling, fieldbus, and thermal environments of the deployment hardware. Above all, the operating state distribution never transfers directly. The instant the environmental operator transitions from synthetic simulation ${f_{\text{sim}}}$ to live physical dynamics ${f_{\text{real}}}$, or when the arbitration parameters of the runtime permission path change, the state trajectory ${\mathbf{x}}_0, {\mathbf{x}}_1, \dots, {\mathbf{x}}_t$ rapidly diverges.

Treating these four regimes as a monotonic ladder of fidelity is a mistake. Transitioning across replay, simulation, shadow execution, and constrained testing does not simply increase fidelity; every step exchanges one systemic distortion for another. Simulation secures closed-loop causality at the expense of authentic contact physics and sensory noise. Shadow execution reclaims real physical sensing and bus timing but surrenders candidate closed-loop causality. Constrained physical testing restores both causality and physical grounding, yet it systematically censors the state space beyond its safety boundary. Observing zero supervisor interventions across one hundred constrained trials proves that the policy maintained states within the un-triggered safety envelope; it offers no statistical guarantee about how the policy behaves once it breaches that boundary.

System verification therefore demands assigning each engineering question to the specific regime equipped to answer it ([[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/02 Teaching the Machine/07 Closed-Loop Evaluation/V4-07.05 Evidence Regimes#^tbl-07-evidence-regimes|table 2]]). For the latch manipulation policy, verifying whether chunk inference executes reliably within its allocated cycle time belongs first in recorded replay and subsequently in hardware shadow mode. Testing whether the manipulator can recover from a wide range of positional offsets belongs in high-throughput simulation, where thousands of permutations inflict zero hardware wear. Evaluating whether passive gripper compliance and contact friction remain within structural force limits belongs strictly in constrained physical trials. Each regime remains blind to the failure modes of the others.

### Simulation as a Conditional Proxy

^sec-evaluation-proxy-transfer-law

A simulator can screen candidate latch policies before expensive hardware is committed to physical trials, but its screening validity depends on how accurately it models the physical mechanisms governing the decision. Paired probing, developed in **[[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/02 Teaching the Machine/06 Policy Training/V4-06.05 The Simulator as a System Component#^sec-training-contact-exploit|The sim-to-real contact exploit]]**, drives the simulated and physical strike plates with identical commands and measures their discrepancies in newtons and milliseconds. The evaluation protocol incorporates those measured discrepancies directly into its test plan. A favorable simulated recovery qualifies a checkpoint for physical verification, but that result cannot resolve unmodeled discrepancies in the contact physics or data transport pipeline. Visual rendering artifacts, numerical friction regularization, and absent force-torque feedback can each alter candidate policy rankings.

Whether a simulator ranks candidate policies correctly is an empirical, measurable property. Proxy fidelity is measured with a paired benchmark across both regimes. The benchmark evaluates the same set of candidate policies (such as the latch training checkpoints) on both the simulated door mechanism and the physical hardware under tightly matched initial conditions, then compares the resulting performance orderings. While a standard Pearson or Spearman correlation coefficient between simulated and physical success rates summarizes overall monotonic agreement, correlation alone can hide the failure that matters most. A high correlation coefficient can coexist with a rank inversion, in which the simulator promotes a checkpoint that fails on the physical door. To address this risk, Li et al. ([[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/02 Teaching the Machine/07 Closed-Loop Evaluation/V4-07.15 Source References#^ref-li2024simpler|2024]]) measure rank agreement with the mean maximum rank violation (MMRV), computed across approximately 1,500 paired simulated and physical episodes. For each policy, MMRV finds its worst inversion against any other policy, weighting each pair the simulator orders wrongly by the two policies’ real-world performance gap. MMRV then averages those worst cases across policies, so a simulator scores badly exactly when it swaps policies whose physical performance differs most.

This evaluation principle applies directly to the latch manipulation task. A simulator whose relative rankings across historical checkpoints correlate reliably with physical trials earns the operational authority to screen incoming models. Conversely, a simulation environment that has never been calibrated against paired physical benchmarks possesses no screening credibility, regardless of how photorealistic its graphics engine appears. The identical constraint governs learned neural world models deployed as simulated environments: predicted candidate success serves as a justification for scheduling physical tests, not as verifiable evidence of physical capability, until paired real-world trials validate model calibration. Furthermore, even a validated ranking proxy only authorizes candidate filtering. Physical trials remain the ground truth for safety and performance claims, because knowing that policy A outperforms policy B reveals nothing about whether policy A satisfies the machine’s absolute failure-rate limits.

↩︎ Learned world models used as simulators, whose simplifications cannot be enumerated, are established in **[[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/02 Teaching the Machine/06 Policy Training/V4-06.05 The Simulator as a System Component#^sec-training-world-models|Learned world models as simulators]]**.

Two additional systems factors dictate the generalization bounds of any simulation-based evaluation: parameter domain coverage and execution timing fidelity. The first factor is the physical parameter variation spanned during simulation trials. A simulation calibrated against telemetry logs from a single robotic arm provides evidence strictly for that physical unit under those specific ambient conditions. Similarly, domain randomization evaluates policy resilience only across the bounded support of the randomized parameter distributions; it cannot account for dynamic phenomena entirely absent from the simulator’s underlying differential equations. Formal sim-to-real gap analysis identifies unmodeled dynamics and compiles them into the omission log that the policy manifest carries. The closed-loop evaluation protocol must incorporate that log directly, cataloging the operating regimes and edge conditions about which simulation can make no empirical claims.

↩︎ Formal sim-to-real gap analysis, which yields a supported region and a residual mismatch list, is established in **[[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/02 Teaching the Machine/06 Policy Training/V4-06.06 Physical Channels of the Sim-To-Real Gap#^sec-training-sim-to-real-gap|Physical Channels of the Sim-to-Real Gap]]**.

The second factor is whether the evaluation regime reflects true hardware execution dynamics. Standard software-in-the-loop simulations pause virtual simulation time while the policy executes neural inference, masking real-world computational bottlenecks. A simulator that pauses virtual time during inference cannot expose the consequences of a late inference chunk, a rejected stale proposal, or an executing chunk that continues under its 60 ms chunk lease while awaiting updated sensor verification. Explicit delay models can exercise those transitions, but establishing their actual timing requires the neural policy and runtime permission arbiter on target embedded compute hardware interconnected via the physical buses.

↩︎ The chunk lease, sized between the renewal floor and the stopping budget, is established in **[[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/01 Machine Anatomy/04 The Nervous System/V4-04.05 Chunk Lease Sizing from the Stopping Budget#^sec-nervous-lease-sizing|Chunk Lease Sizing from the Stopping Budget]]**.

Closed-loop simulation, whether executed purely in software or integrated with hardware in the loop, serves as a filter for screening candidate models. Only physical trials, however, measure embodied competence, and engineers must formalize the safety envelopes and evaluation protocols long before the first trial begins.

↪ The qualification ladder, which stages software-, processor-, and hardware-in-the-loop benches before physical fault rigs, is developed in **[[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/04 Governing the Machine/15 Adversarial Verification/V4-15.08 The Qualification Ladder#^sec-verification-qualification-ladder|The Qualification Ladder]]**.

Before fixing a physical trial protocol, review evidence regimes and fidelity trade-offs:

**Regime causality trade-offs**: Contrast the causal feedback properties of recorded trace replay against closed-loop simulation.

**Shadow-mode trajectory confinement**: Explain why shadow-mode evaluation on the running machine cannot test the candidate policy’s recovery maneuvers.

**Transfer across regimes**: Describe which properties transfer reliably across regimes and which one stays behind.
