# V4-07.06 Fixing the Trial Protocol

[[Home|Home]] · [[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/Volume 4 Overview|Volume Overview]] · [[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/02 Teaching the Machine/07 Closed-Loop Evaluation/V4-07 Closed-Loop Evaluation|V4-07 Closed-Loop Evaluation]]

^sec-evaluation-trial-protocol

If an engineering team executes fifty ad hoc physical runs across arbitrary trajectories in a constrained test cell and reports a 90 percent success rate, that statistic lacks an interpretable denominator. Empirical success probabilities and intervention rates are meaningful only with respect to a specific joint distribution ${\mathcal{D}}$ over initial system states, environmental parameters, and task goals, so the intended operating region must be formalized before executing a single physical trial. The **target ODD** is the concrete subset of the declared ODD recorded by the policy manifest of **[[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/02 Teaching the Machine/06 Policy Training/V4-06.09 The Policy Manifest#^sec-training-policy-manifest|The Policy Manifest]]** that the evaluation campaign samples. Its sampling weights define ${\mathcal{D}}$, and no rigorous empirical claim reaches beyond its support. A measured success rate supports a target-distribution claim only when the support and sampling weights of ${\mathcal{D}}$ are specified in advance, setting explicit bounds on scene illuminance, payload mass and inertia, surface friction coefficients, allowable initial state offsets, and the specific hardware serial numbers under test.

Extracting valid confidence bounds from empirical trials requires three operational conditions:

1.  *Independent and identically distributed (iid) sampling:* Trials must be independent realizations drawn from ${\mathcal{D}}$. In embodied physical systems, standard offline dataset intuition often fails; randomly shuffling log records cannot eliminate physical coupling. On real robotic hardware, thermal accumulation in motor windings from resistive heating (${I}^2 {R}$), battery voltage sag under discharge, and progressive mechanical backlash introduce temporal autocorrelation across sequential trials. Correlated trials, like the serially correlated telemetry of **[[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/02 Teaching the Machine/05 Physical Data/V4-05.05 Physical Telemetry Sources and Collection Paths#^sec-data-collection-sources|Physical Telemetry Sources and Collection Paths]]**, carry fewer independent observations than their count suggests. Run 20 is not drawn from the same physical distribution as Run 1 unless operators strictly enforce thermal cooldown intervals and battery recharging cycles between runs. Furthermore, repeated trials executed on a single physical unit (such as Unit 01) cannot serve as iid draws from the broader fleet distribution ${\mathcal{M}}$, because unit-level manufacturing tolerances and sensor calibrations bias the sample.
2.  *Fixed outcome predicates:* The binary evaluation function ${\phi}$ and termination criteria ${\kappa}$ must remain frozen throughout the entire campaign. Modifying the execution deadline ${T_{\text{deadline}}}$, adjusting collision thresholds, or widening the spatial tolerance of goal region ${\mathcal{G}_{\text{goal}}}$ midway through testing alters the definition of success. Doing so commits post-hoc metric drift and invalidates statistical comparisons against baseline models.
3.  *Full-denominator accounting:* Every initiated run must remain in the trial log, including hardware communication faults, sensor timeouts, emergency protective stops, and operator interventions. Culling inconvenient or anomalous runs corrupts the statistical denominator. A hardware abort that halts execution prior to goal completion yields a task failure (${Y} = 0$). Under a strict autonomy metric, any human intervention forces ${A} = 0$. An operator nudge that guides the robot around an obstacle followed by on-time completion records ${Y} = 1$ and ${A} = 0$. Safety monitoring systems must record kinematic limit violations and physical damage separately from task completion. When a safety tripwire or human supervisor intercepts an impending collision, the counterfactual unmitigated trajectory remains unknown. Consequently, zero recorded damage cannot be credited as evidence of unassisted survival. Discarding aborted or intercepted runs changes the underlying question and inflates perceived reliability by measuring an idealized survivor population.

When these conditions hold, the sample mean provides an unbiased point estimate of the policy’s closed-loop performance under ${\mathcal{D}}$. The remaining engineering challenge is determining how much statistical uncertainty a finite sample leaves, particularly when high-reliability targets yield zero observed failures across the test budget.
