# V4-07.07 Confidence from a Finite Campaign

[[Home|Home]] · [[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/Volume 4 Overview|Volume Overview]] · [[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/02 Teaching the Machine/07 Closed-Loop Evaluation/V4-07 Closed-Loop Evaluation|V4-07 Closed-Loop Evaluation]]

^sec-evaluation-closed-loop-survival

With the operational outcome, target population, evaluation regime, and trial denominator fixed, the twenty clean nominal runs pose an immediate statistical problem: determining what degree of reliability the campaign has actually demonstrated. A confidence bound quantifies the uncertainty that remains under the declared sampling model. Systems engineers must treat this bound as a measure of statistical evidence rather than an assessment of physical failure severity or an automatic deployment permit.

The standard textbook interval for a proportion, the Wald normal approximation ${\hat{p}} \pm 1.96\sqrt{{\hat{p}}(1-{\hat{p}})/{N}}$, collapses to zero width when the observed success fraction ${\hat{p}}$ reaches 1.0 because its sample variance term vanishes. This collapse exposes the breakdown of the asymptotic normal assumption near the boundary rather than empirical certainty. Evaluating the statistical assurance of a zero-failure record requires only the product rule for independent events.

Suppose the true per-run success probability of the latch policy is ${p}$. If individual trials are independent, twenty consecutive runs all succeed with joint probability ${p}^{20}$. Consider a flawed policy that fails one run in eight (${p} =$ 0.875). That policy still produces an unbroken twenty-run success streak in 6.9 percent of evaluation campaigns. An empirical run of twenty successes cannot rule it out. As the candidate success rate ${p}$ decreases, a clean record becomes increasingly improbable. When ${p}$ drops to 0.861, the probability of observing twenty consecutive successes falls to exactly ${\alpha_{\text{sig}}} =$ 0.05, which corresponds to the significance level for a 95 percent confidence requirement ($1 - 0.95$). Any underlying success probability lower than 0.861 would make the observed twenty-run clean record rarer than a 1-in-20 chance (${p}^{20} < 0.05$). The one-sided lower confidence bound therefore identifies the threshold below which candidate policies become statistically inconsistent with the experimental data. In standard frequentist qualification, “95 percent confidence” describes the long-run coverage of the testing protocol. Across repeated qualification campaigns, the procedure traps the true parameter ${p}$ above the calculated bound at least 95 percent of the time. The bound represents a conservative floor under the declared sampling model rather than a point estimate of ${p}$ or a Bayesian posterior belief about this specific policy.

Reading the inversion as a curve makes the logic visible. Plotting ${p}^{20}$ against every candidate ${p}$ ([[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/02 Teaching the Machine/07 Closed-Loop Evaluation/V4-07.07 Confidence from a Finite Campaign#^fig-07-clean-run-inversion|figure 3]]) shows the clean record ruling out only the candidates whose curve lies below the ${\alpha_{\text{sig}}}$ line, the region left of ${p} =$ 0.861. The one-in-eight policy sits just above that line, which is why twenty flawless runs cannot reject it.

For a predeclared zero-failure campaign of ${n}$ trials, this inversion yields the exact one-sided Clopper-Pearson lower bound[[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/02 Teaching the Machine/07 Closed-Loop Evaluation/V4-07.16 Footnotes#^fn6|⁶]] ([[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/02 Teaching the Machine/07 Closed-Loop Evaluation/V4-07.15 Source References#^ref-clopper1934use|Clopper and Pearson 1934]]). Setting ${p}^{n} = {\alpha_{\text{sig}}}$ gives

$$
{p_{\text{lower}}} = {\alpha_{\text{sig}}}^{1/{n}} \tag{3}
$$

 with the corresponding upper confidence limit on the per-run failure probability given by:

$$
{p_{\text{fail, upper}}} = 1 - {\alpha_{\text{sig}}}^{1/{n}}
$$

 [[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/02 Teaching the Machine/07 Closed-Loop Evaluation/V4-07.07 Confidence from a Finite Campaign#^sec-evaluation-bounds-with-failures|Section 7.6.1]] extends this inversion principle to evaluation campaigns that record failures.

^eq-eval-zero-failure-bound

↪ The discrete and continuous-time derivations of the zero-failure bound, and why the normal approximation fails at zero failures, are developed in **[[05 Reference/Volume IV - Physical AI/V4 Machine Learning Background#^sec-appendix-ml-exposure|Zero-Failure Testing and the Exposure Wall]]**.

For rare failures (${p_{\text{fail}}} \ll 1$), a first-order Taylor series expansion linearizes this bound into the **rule of three** ([[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/02 Teaching the Machine/07 Closed-Loop Evaluation/V4-07.15 Source References#^ref-hanley1983nothing|Hanley and Lippman-Hand 1983]]). Expanding $\ln(1 - {p_{\text{fail}}}) = -{p_{\text{fail}}} - {p_{\text{fail}}}^2/2 - \dots \approx -{p_{\text{fail}}}$ and setting the clean-run probability $(1 - {p_{\text{fail}}})^{n}$ equal to ${\alpha_{\text{sig}}} = 0.05$ yields $-{n}\,{p_{\text{fail}}} \approx \ln(0.05) \approx -2.996 \approx -3.0$. When ${n}$ independent trials pass without a single failure, the upper 95 percent confidence limit on the per-trial failure probability simplifies to:

$$
{p_{\text{fail, upper}}^{95\%}} \approx \frac{3}{{n}}. \tag{4}
$$

 Inverted to size test campaigns, the rule establishes the empirical burden for any target reliability: ${n} \approx 3 / (1 - {p_{\text{target}}})$. Each additional “nine” of target reliability multiplies the required trial volume tenfold. For the twenty door-opening trials, [[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/02 Teaching the Machine/07 Closed-Loop Evaluation/V4-07.07 Confidence from a Finite Campaign#^eq-eval-rule-of-three|equation 4]] yields an approximate upper failure bound of $3/20 =$ 0.15, whereas the exact Clopper-Pearson computation in notebook [7.1](https://mlsysbook.ai/vol4/07_evaluation/07_evaluation.html#nbk-evaluation-derivation-20-run-lower-bound) establishes 13.9 percent. A policy with a true failure probability as high as 13.9 percent remains entirely compatible with twenty consecutive successes at 95 percent confidence. The campaign cannot substantiate an industrial claim of 99 percent reliability. Formally certifying a 99 percent lower confidence limit (${p_{\text{target}}} = 0.99$) at 95 percent confidence demands:

$$
{n} = \left\lceil \frac{\ln({\alpha_{\text{sig}}})}{\ln({p_{\text{target}}})} \right\rceil = \left\lceil \frac{\ln(0.05)}{\ln(0.99)} \right\rceil \tag{5}
$$

 which equals 299 consecutive clean trials, an order of magnitude beyond the twenty trials conducted in the initial campaign.[[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/02 Teaching the Machine/07 Closed-Loop Evaluation/V4-07.16 Footnotes#^fn7|⁷]]

^eq-eval-zero-failure-sample-size

^eq-eval-rule-of-three

The bound inherits the iid condition of [[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/02 Teaching the Machine/07 Closed-Loop Evaluation/V4-07.06 Fixing the Trial Protocol#^sec-evaluation-trial-protocol|section 7.5]]. Twenty runs on Unit 01 therefore bound Unit 01’s success probability under the sampled conditions and nothing more. The unit-to-unit variance of [[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/02 Teaching the Machine/07 Closed-Loop Evaluation/V4-07.03 What a Physical Success Rate Measures#^sec-evaluation-what-measured|section 7.2]] puts Unit 02 in a different population, whose bound needs its own trials or a sample drawn across the fleet.

**Napkin Math 7.1: The clean-record bound**

**Problem**: The mobile manipulator completes ${n} =$ 20 door-opening runs with no contact-tripwire crossing and no intervention. What one-sided 95 percent lower limit on its per-run success probability ${p}$ does that record support, and what does it cost to claim 99.9 percent instead?

**Variables**:

- ${n} =$ 20 clean runs (Illustrative; the chapter’s opening campaign, independent and identically distributed draws from the target operational design domain).
- ${\alpha_{\text{sig}}} =$ 0.05 (Chosen; one minus the 95 percent confidence level).

**Math**: Setting ${p}^{{n}} = {\alpha_{\text{sig}}}$ ([[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/02 Teaching the Machine/07 Closed-Loop Evaluation/V4-07.07 Confidence from a Finite Campaign#^eq-eval-zero-failure-bound|equation 3]]) and taking logarithms, $\ln(1/{\alpha_{\text{sig}}}) =$ 2.996, divided by ${n}$ gives the exponent 0.1498, and ${p_{\text{lower}}}$ is ${e}$ raised to minus that exponent, 0.861. The failure upper limit is $1 - {p_{\text{lower}}}$, or 13.9 percent, which the rule of three ([[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/02 Teaching the Machine/07 Closed-Loop Evaluation/V4-07.07 Confidence from a Finite Campaign#^eq-eval-rule-of-three|equation 4]]) approximates as $3/20 =$ 0.15.

**Result**: 86.1 percent success lower limit at 95 percent confidence; a 99.9 percent limit needs 2,995 clean runs (notebook [7.2](https://mlsysbook.ai/vol4/07_evaluation/07_evaluation.html#nbk-evaluation-statistical-scaling-high-reliability-assurance)).

**Systems insight**: Twenty clean runs leave a failure probability as high as one run in seven statistically consistent with the empirical data. The evaluation campaign establishes a formal lower confidence bound under its specific sampling assumptions. It should never be conflated with a steady-state failure rate, an MTBF, or an unconditional deployment authorization. Improving the bound requires scaling ${n}$, because the numerator $\ln(1/{\alpha_{\text{sig}}}) \approx 3$ remains anchored by the chosen confidence level. This structural property explains why the rule of three matches the exact Clopper-Pearson bound whenever failure probabilities are small.

### Bounds When Failures Occur

^sec-evaluation-bounds-with-failures

If the mobile manipulator jams on the twentieth door run after nineteen clean openings, the zero-failure rule of three no longer applies, yet discarding the campaign would waste nineteen valid observations. Encountering an operational failure during testing does not invalidate statistical evaluation. Instead, it shifts the calculation from the zero-failure boundary to the full binomial distribution. Given ${k}$ successful trials out of ${N}$ total runs, the one-sided lower confidence limit is the parameter value ${p}$ for which the probability of observing ${k}$ or more successes equals ${\alpha_{\text{sig}}}$:

$$
\Pr({X} \ge {k} \mid {N}, {p}) = \sum_{{j}={k}}^{{N}} \binom{{N}}{{j}} {p}^{{j}} (1-{p})^{{N}-{j}} = {\alpha_{\text{sig}}}, \tag{6}
$$

 where the random variable ${X}$ denotes the success count. A hypothesis test rules out a low candidate reliability ${p}$ by asking how likely a policy that poor would be to produce an outcome at least as favorable as the observed ${k}$ successes, so summing from ${j} = {k}$ up to ${N}$ captures the full upper tail of outcomes that meet or exceed the observed record. When ${k} = {N}$, the summation collapses to a single term, ${p}^{N} = {\alpha_{\text{sig}}}$, recovering the zero-failure Clopper-Pearson bound of [[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/02 Teaching the Machine/07 Closed-Loop Evaluation/V4-07.07 Confidence from a Finite Campaign#^eq-eval-zero-failure-bound|equation 3]]. With that single failure across twenty runs (${k} = 19$), the relationship expands to ${p}^{20} + 20\,{p}^{19}(1-{p}) = {\alpha_{\text{sig}}}$. Although this polynomial lacks a closed-form algebraic root, numerical root-finding yields a 95 percent lower confidence limit of 78.4 percent. Even though the empirical success fraction is 95 percent, a single observed failure drops the certified lower bound from 86.1 percent to 78.4 percent. In production evaluation pipelines, libraries compute this limit directly using quantiles of the incomplete beta function, which follows from the exact identity between binomial tails and Beta distributions.

^eq-eval-bound-with-failures

Two engineering cautions govern this method. First, any observed failure must remain permanently logged in the audit trail. Halting a test campaign upon observing an anomaly and restarting a clean run counter introduces optional-stopping bias. Engineers cannot subsequently claim a zero-failure bound on the post-restart run sequence ([[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/02 Teaching the Machine/07 Closed-Loop Evaluation/V4-07.08 Trial Budgets, Stopping Rules, and Comparisons#^sec-evaluation-trials-per-claim|section 7.7.1]]). Second, the Clopper-Pearson method is conservative by construction because its coverage probability equals or exceeds the nominal confidence level for every value of ${p}$. For standard exploratory data analysis, Brown et al. ([[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/02 Teaching the Machine/07 Closed-Loop Evaluation/V4-07.15 Source References#^ref-brown2001interval|2001]]) recommend the Wilson score interval[[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/02 Teaching the Machine/07 Closed-Loop Evaluation/V4-07.16 Footnotes#^fn8|⁸]] or Jeffreys prior intervals, which maintain average coverage closer to nominal while producing tighter intervals. For physical qualification and safety certification, however, overestimating autonomous reliability is the costly error. The conservative Clopper-Pearson bound therefore serves as the defensible systems default, and engineering test records must explicitly state the inference method applied.

The twenty nominal door-opening runs now have a concrete statistical interpretation under non-asymptotic sampling theory. Elevating the certified lower bound requires expanding the trial budget, which directly compounds the physical testing hours, mechanical wear, and facility costs needed to qualify embodied machine learning policies.
