# V4-07.04 Closed-Loop Dynamics

[[Home|Home]] · [[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/Volume 4 Overview|Volume Overview]] · [[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/02 Teaching the Machine/07 Closed-Loop Evaluation/V4-07 Closed-Loop Evaluation|V4-07 Closed-Loop Evaluation]]

^sec-evaluation-closed-loop-dynamics

A policy checkpoint that achieves the lowest validation loss on held-out teleoperation logs can drive the mobile base into a warehouse rack or jam the door latch on its first physical trial. Offline scoring resets the robot’s state to the demonstrator’s recording at every step, erasing the cumulative consequences of the policy’s own actions. In standard deep learning workflows, model evaluation centers on empirical risk minimization over a static, held-out validation dataset ${\mathcal{D}_{\text{val}}} = \{({x}_i, {y}_i)\}_{i=1}^{N}$:

$$
{\mathcal{L}_{\text{val}}}({\theta}) = \frac{1}{{N}} \sum_{i=1}^{N} {\mathcal{L}}({f_{\theta}}({x}_i), {y}_i)
$$

 Whether the target metric is Top-1 classification accuracy, next-token perplexity, an F1 score, or coordinate regression loss, the evaluation pipeline remains passive and decoupled. The underlying data-generating process is feedforward and memoryless with respect to the model’s outputs: mispredicting sample ${i}$ exerts zero causal influence on sample ${i}+1$ ($\partial {x}_{i+1} / \partial {\hat{y}}_i = 0$).

Transporting this static paradigm to physical control creates the **offline evaluation illusion**, the unfounded assumption that lower validation loss, higher action cosine similarity, or smaller Mean Squared Error (MSE) on logged data guarantees superior physical execution. Offline loss metrics of the form ${\mathcal{L}_{\text{val}}}({\theta}) = \frac{1}{{N}} \sum_{i=1}^{N} \|{\mathbf{a}^{*}}_i - \pi_{\theta}({\mathbf{x}}_i^*)\|^2$ evaluate candidate predictions exclusively along frozen demonstrator trajectories. Controlled benchmarks across simulated and physical manipulation tasks demonstrate this failure directly: the training checkpoint with the lowest validation loss performed substantially worse during closed-loop rollouts than alternative checkpoints, with task success frequently rising even as validation loss climbed ([[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/02 Teaching the Machine/07 Closed-Loop Evaluation/V4-07.15 Source References#^ref-mandlekar2021matters|Mandlekar et al. 2021]]).

The decoupling that justifies offline scoring vanishes in physical AI. Admitted policy actions cross the causal boundary into mass, momentum, and non-smooth contact mechanics. Instead of assigning categorical labels, the policy outputs forces, torques, and velocity setpoints that physically perturb the plant, endogenously generating future sensory observations through the system dynamics (${\mathbf{x}}_{t+1} = {f}({\mathbf{x}}_t, {\mathbf{u}}_t)$). These commands interact continuously with inertia, friction, and mechanical backlash. Consequently, a navigation policy can track recorded demonstrator trajectories with negligible regression error during offline playback, yet exhaust the vehicle’s entire lateral clearance against a warehouse rack before traversing a third of the aisle. The failure stems directly from what each evaluation regime feeds back to the policy.

In an **open-loop evaluation**, a candidate policy $\pi_{\theta}$ is scored across a static dataset of recorded demonstrations ${\mathcal{D}_{\text{demo}}} = \{({\mathbf{x}}_t^*, {\mathbf{a}^{*}}_t)\}_{t=1}^{{S}}$. At each discrete timestep ${t}$, the policy receives an expert state ${\mathbf{x}}_t^*$ and predicts an action ${\hat{\mathbf{a}}}_t = \pi_{\theta}({\mathbf{x}}_t^*)$, which a standard loss function such as mean squared error or cross-entropy compares against the recorded expert action ${\mathbf{a}^{*}}_t$. Because the harness then feeds the next recorded state ${\mathbf{x}}_{t+1}^*$ directly from storage and discards ${\hat{\mathbf{a}}}_t$ without ever executing it on hardware, the incoming state sequence remains strictly independent of prior predictions ($\partial {\mathbf{x}}_{t+1}^* / \partial {\hat{\mathbf{a}}}_t = {\mathbf{0}}$). In a **closed-loop evaluation**, by contrast, the policy proposes an action ${\mathbf{a}}_{t} = \pi_{\theta}({\mathbf{x}}_t)$ from live sensory feedback. The deterministic permission path then validates that proposal against current plant state and safety invariants before dispatching an approved command ${\mathbf{u}}_t$ (or an intervention fallback) to the actuators. The plant then advances according to physical dynamics (${\mathbf{x}}_{t+1} = {f}({\mathbf{x}}_t, {\mathbf{u}}_t)$), so every admitted command alters the physical state and shapes all subsequent sensory inputs across the causal boundary.

### Compounding Error and Kinematic Integration

^sec-evaluation-compounding-error

That feedback path turns small one-step tracking residuals into growing state deviations, following the worst-case structure derived in **[[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/02 Teaching the Machine/05 Physical Data/V4-05.04 Recovery and Compounding Error#^sec-data-curse-dimensionality-recoverability|Recovery and Compounding Error]]**. If a policy deviates from expert actions with probability at most ${\epsilon}$ per step on recorded expert states, open-loop evaluation across a horizon of ${S}$ steps accumulates an expected cost bounded by ${S}{\epsilon}$. In closed loop, however, an early deviation can steer the robot into unvisited regions of the state space for the remainder of the trajectory, driving the cumulative imitation cost to scale as ${S}^2{\epsilon}$ (**[[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/02 Teaching the Machine/05 Physical Data/V4-05.04 Recovery and Compounding Error#^eq-data-quadratic-drift|equation 5.1]]**). An open-loop benchmark can therefore understate closed-loop task divergence by a factor proportional to the operating horizon. This bound governs zero-one decision divergence rather than unbounded quantities such as contact force or structural yield. Classical kinematics supplies the physical mechanism. Uncorrected heading and steering biases integrate forward into lateral displacements that exhaust the robot’s clearance envelope.

A policy whose own actions displace it from its training distribution is the second law at work on a test bench, and evaluation must quantify what that displacement costs on physical hardware. Because the mobile manipulator’s base remains stationary during door manipulation trials, the platform’s warehouse navigation stack supplies the clearest illustration of kinematic accumulation. Consider the base traversing a 30 m aisle at its 1.5 m/s drive limit, where the nominal expert trajectory tracks the centerline with heading ${\theta_{\text{head}}}^* = 0\text{ rad}$ and lateral offset ${y_{\text{base}}}^* = 0\text{ m}$. Suppose the candidate navigation policy possesses a systematic heading bias of ${\Delta}{\theta_{\text{head}}} = 1.0^\circ$ (0.01745 rad) at every control step. The deterministic permission path admits each individual velocity command because, in isolation, a one-degree heading adjustment remains dynamically feasible while lateral clearance persists.

↩︎ How a policy’s own actions shift the states it visits away from the demonstrator’s occupancy is established in **[[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/02 Teaching the Machine/05 Physical Data/V4-05.03 Mechanics of Endogenous Covariate Shift#^sec-data-covariate-shift|Mechanics of Endogenous Covariate Shift]]**.

In an open-loop evaluation, the policy receives the demonstrator state ${\mathbf{x}}_t^* = ({{x_{\text{base}}}}_t^*, 0, 0)$ at each step and predicts forward velocity with a $1.0^\circ$ steering offset. The offline scoring pipeline records a mean absolute angular error of $1.0^\circ$ (0.01745 rad) and a mean squared error of $\text{MSE}_{{\theta_{\text{head}}}} = (0.01745)^2 \approx 3.05 \times 10^{-4}\text{ rad}^2$.[[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/02 Teaching the Machine/07 Closed-Loop Evaluation/V4-07.16 Footnotes#^fn2|²]] The number is small because it is measured in radians squared. More important, the replay score stays at that same per-step value however long the aisle is, because each next input returns to the centerline and the error never accumulates. During closed-loop execution, however, the robot’s physical movement reflects previously admitted commands, allowing uncorrected heading errors to integrate continuously. Under planar unicycle kinematics[[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/02 Teaching the Machine/07 Closed-Loop Evaluation/V4-07.16 Footnotes#^fn3|³]] (${\dot{y}} = {v} \sin {\theta_{\text{head}}} \approx {v} {\Delta}{\theta_{\text{head}}}$), a persistent angular offset integrates directly into a lateral displacement that grows linearly with the time ${t_{\text{drift}}}$ since the base left the centerline:

$$
{y_{\text{base}}}({t_{\text{drift}}}) = \int_0^{{t_{\text{drift}}}} {v} \sin {\Delta}{\theta_{\text{head}}} \, d{t'} \approx {v} {\Delta}{\theta_{\text{head}}} {t_{\text{drift}}}
$$

 If the policy carries a persistent yaw-rate bias ${\omega_{\text{bias}}}$ (steering drift) rather than a fixed angular offset, the heading angle itself grows linearly over time (${\theta_{\text{head}}}({t_{\text{drift}}}) = \int_0^{{t_{\text{drift}}}} {\omega_{\text{bias}}} \, d{t'} = {\omega_{\text{bias}}} {t_{\text{drift}}}$). Integrating forward velocity ${v}$ then yields a cumulative lateral deviation that compounds quadratically in time:

$$
{y_{\text{base}}}({t_{\text{drift}}}) = \int_0^{{t_{\text{drift}}}} {v} \sin({\omega_{\text{bias}}} {t'}) \, d{t'} \approx \frac{1}{2} {v} {\omega_{\text{bias}}} {t_{\text{drift}}}^2
$$

 This quadratic growth resembles the ${S}^2{\epsilon}$ imitation bound, but the two characterize distinct phenomena. The kinematic derivation integrates a systematic yaw-rate bias through continuous dynamics, whereas the imitation bound accounts for accumulated discrete decision costs under distribution shift. Both show small, bounded errors accumulating polynomially over an operational horizon. Neither excludes the worse case that **[[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/02 Teaching the Machine/05 Physical Data/V4-05.04 Recovery and Compounding Error#^sec-data-curse-dimensionality-recoverability|Recovery and Compounding Error]]** described, in which a smooth imitator of a stable expert can compound its errors exponentially with horizon; the aisle example simply does not need that mechanism to fail.

For the constant $1.0^\circ$ heading bias, the mobile base spans 100 cm within a 130 cm aisle. This geometry leaves ${W_{\text{clear}}} =$ 15 cm of lateral clearance to the storage racks on either side. At full drive velocity, the kinematic drift consumes this safety buffer at:

$$
{t_{\text{impact}}} = \frac{{W_{\text{clear}}}}{{v} \sin {\Delta}{\theta_{\text{head}}}}, \tag{2}
$$

 which evaluates to 5.7 s, after just 8.6 m of forward travel, less than a third of the way through the aisle. The same heading offset that replay scores at an angular mean squared error of $3.05 \times 10^{-4}\text{ rad}^2$, because each next input returns to the recorded centerline, spends the entire 0.15 m side clearance in the physical loop ([[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/02 Teaching the Machine/07 Closed-Loop Evaluation/V4-07.04 Closed-Loop Dynamics#^fig-07-open-vs-closed-loop|figure 1]]). The computed point is the unmitigated collision horizon. To halt before impact, the runtime permission path must intervene earlier, allowing for qualified detection, command and actuation response, and stopping motion. A protective stop makes strict proposer autonomy fail while preserving protection; an unprotected rack strike fails protection as well. Both expose a limitation of the policy that offline evaluation rated as near-flawless.

^eq-eval-clearance-time

This warehouse scenario highlights the two structural mechanisms that cause open-loop validation metrics to decouple from physical performance:

1.  **State integration across time**: Static evaluation cannot integrate persistent control errors into physical displacements. By resetting the robot’s input state to an expert demonstration at every clock cycle, offline scoring obscures error accumulation. In closed-loop deployment, however, the state at timestep ${t}$ represents the unrolled composition of all prior control actions:

$$
{\mathbf{x}}_t = {f}({f}(\dots {f}({\mathbf{x}}_0, {\mathbf{u}}_0)\dots, {\mathbf{u}}_{t-2}), {\mathbf{u}}_{t-1})
$$

 A persistent heading bias does not require an algorithmic flaw. Physical phenomena like asymmetric tire wear, mast vibration, uneven payload distribution, or floor camber routinely introduce systematic offsets that accumulate across steps.
2.  **Covariate shift into unmodeled states**: Static benchmarks cannot evaluate how a policy behaves once early errors steer the plant away from nominal trajectories. The very first uncorrected action shifts the base from the expert state ${\mathbf{x}}_1^*$ to an unvisited state ${\mathbf{x}}_1$. Once the base drifts even a few centimeters off the centerline, it enters operating states absent from ${\mathcal{D}_{\text{demo}}}$. Because offline metrics are evaluated exclusively along demonstrator states where ${y_{\text{base}}} = 0\text{ m}$, they provide no evidence about policy behavior when ${y_{\text{base}}} \neq 0\text{ m}$. Lacking training on corrective maneuvers, the policy often generates larger prediction errors in these off-nominal regimes, precipitating rapid divergence.

↩︎ Compounding covariate shift, and the recovery experience a fixed demonstration archive lacks, are established in **[[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/02 Teaching the Machine/06 Policy Training/V4-06.03 Policy Training Regimes#^sec-training-recovery-experience|Compounding errors and recovery experience]]**.

### Contact Discontinuities and Metric Masking

^sec-evaluation-non-lipschitz-contact

In the warehouse aisle, a two-millimeter tracking residual spends a tiny fraction of lateral clearance; at the door latch, that same two-millimeter residual commands the gripper into rigid steel. The moment an end-effector touches a latch, unconstrained free-space kinematics give way to constrained contact mechanics governed by impact, stick, and slip. In classical rigid-body mechanics, collision produces discontinuous velocity jumps (${v_n^+} \ne {v_n^-}$), while Coulomb friction creates set-valued reaction forces (${F_{\text{friction}}} \in [-{\mu} {f_{k, \perp}}, {\mu} {f_{k, \perp}}]$). Because these transitions violate Lipschitz continuity (a bound on how much the dynamics can change per unit change in state), infinitesimally small variations in spatial state produce disproportionately large, discontinuous changes in physical dynamics. Although mechanical compliance and structural damping distribute impact forces across small time windows in real hardware, aggregate offline loss metrics obscure the critical physical consequences of crossing contact boundaries:

1.  **Action cosine similarity ignores operational magnitude**: In representation learning and behavior modeling, action cosine similarity:

$$
\text{Cosine}({\hat{\mathbf{a}}}, {\mathbf{a}^{*}}) = \frac{{\hat{\mathbf{a}}}^\top {\mathbf{a}^{*}}}{\|{\hat{\mathbf{a}}}\| \|{\mathbf{a}^{*}}\|}
$$

 evaluates directional alignment between predicted and expert commands. At a physical contact interface, however, directional alignment alone is insufficient because cosine metrics discard vector magnitude entirely. A policy proposal achieving 0.999 cosine similarity that commands 25 percent insufficient force fails to overcome the latch’s mechanical preload and static friction, stalling against the mechanism and halting the task. Conversely, a command with identical 0.999 similarity that commands 150 percent of the required force overloads the contact interface, risking an immediate trigger of the 15 N protective tripwire. Whether hardware damage occurs depends on structural compliance, pre-contact kinetic energy, and joint impedance response. A trip invokes the protective response, whose ability to prevent damage requires qualification under those conditions. A stopped, incomplete run fails the task despite the favorable cosine score.

2.  **Action mean squared error equates disparate physical regimes**: In offline supervised regression, Mean Squared Error:

$$
\text{MSE} = \frac{1}{{d_a}} \sum_{j=1}^{{d_a}} ({\hat{a}}_j - {a}_j^*)^2
$$

 penalizes every millimeter of tracking residual identically, regardless of physical context. During free-space transit, an end-effector tracking discrepancy of 2 mm represents a benign perturbation that downstream control easily accommodates. Consider, by contrast, a stiff environmental interface modeled with contact stiffness ${k_{\text{contact}}} \approx$ $5 \times 10^{5}\text{ N/m}$. When an unmitigated position controller commands an apparent surface penetration ${\delta}$, the resulting normal reaction force scales as:

$$
{\Delta} {f_{k, \perp}} = {k_{\text{contact}}} {\delta}
$$

 For an uncorrected 2 mm penetration residual, this linear relationship demands a reaction force of 1,000 N. Realized loads depend on structural compliance, link elasticity, and active torque limits rather than bare stiffness alone. Even so, the calculation shows the dimensional mismatch. Offline MSE treats a benign 2 mm tracking error in free space identically to a contact error capable of tripping joint protection or damaging gearboxes.

3.  **Hardware instance variance amplifies contact bifurcations**: Non-smooth contact mechanics compound the unit-to-unit manufacturing variance analyzed in [[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/02 Teaching the Machine/07 Closed-Loop Evaluation/V4-07.03 What a Physical Success Rate Measures#^sec-evaluation-what-measured|section 7.2]]. Subtle physical discrepancies across production robots, such as transmission backlash, harmonic drive compliance, and millimeter-level camera-mounting tolerances, shift the physical boundary of the contact interface in Cartesian space. A policy trained on demonstrations recorded on Unit 01 will encounter the latch surface slightly earlier or later when deployed on Unit 02. Because contact transitions are discontinuous, this millimeter-scale spatial displacement triggers immediate operational bifurcations. The manipulator either halts prematurely in free space or collides aggressively with the mechanism frame, causing mechanical jamming that static evaluation over historical logs cannot detect.

The door manipulation benchmark illustrates this failure mechanism directly. An end-effector positioning error of a fraction of a millimeter per step registers as negligible Cartesian deviation on static logs. Against the rigid latch mechanism, however, that small offset acts as a mechanical wedge. The same stiffness relation, ${\Delta} {f_{k, \perp}} = {k_{\text{contact}}} {\delta}$, now acting sideways, transforms this lateral displacement into a steep reaction force that quickly crosses the 15 N contact tripwire, triggering an immediate safety abort before the door unlatches.

Conversely, offline benchmarks can also fail through false pessimism when tasks support multimodal solution paths. If a human demonstrator avoided an obstacle by steering left, an open-loop regression metric penalizes an autonomous policy that navigates safely to the right (the multimodal distribution dilemma), even though both trajectories satisfy the closed-loop objective. Static action loss measures divergence from a single historical recording, not functional task completion.

↩︎ Mode averaging over multimodal demonstrations is established in **[[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/02 Teaching the Machine/06 Policy Training/V4-06.03 Policy Training Regimes#^sec-training-multimodality-breakdown|Mode averaging when demonstrations split]]**.

These asymmetries frequently produce rank inversions across candidate models. A policy whose small residual errors concentrate precisely at critical contact boundaries will score lower validation loss than a competitor with larger, yet harmless, errors distributed across free space. When deployed, however, their operational reliability reverses:

$$
{\mathcal{L}_{\text{val}}}(\pi_{{A}}) < {\mathcal{L}_{\text{val}}}(\pi_{{B}}) \quad \text{yet} \quad \text{ACR}(\pi_{{A}}) \ll \text{ACR}(\pi_{{B}})
$$

 Evaluation campaigns must therefore measure autonomous completion rates, force profiles, and contact stability on physical hardware rather than ranking models by average offline loss. Each standard offline metric conceals a specific physical failure mode, and [[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/02 Teaching the Machine/07 Closed-Loop Evaluation/V4-07.04 Closed-Loop Dynamics#^tbl-07-offline-closed-loop-disconnect|table 1]] maps each metric to its implicit open-loop assumption, its physical failure mechanism, and the corresponding closed-loop metric required to expose it. Most of the closed-loop measures in its last column are physical quantities the record already logs. The one new measure is time to intervention (${T_{\text{TTI}}}$), the elapsed duration from the granting of policy authority to the first permission-path override or safety operator takeover, which measures how long a drifting policy runs before something must stop it. Alongside ACR, MTBI, and ${D_{\text{int}}}$, these execution measures characterize real-world autonomy in ways that static loss calculations cannot.

**Systems Perspective 7.1: The offline evaluation illusion**

Offline validation loss, Mean Squared Error (MSE), and action cosine similarity score predictions exclusively along frozen demonstrator trajectories where errors are artificially erased at every clock cycle. Physical closed-loop evaluation exposes accumulated trajectory drift, state distribution shift, and dynamic contact transitions absent from recorded logs. While the worst-case imitation bound (${\mathcal{O}(S^2 \epsilon)}$) models decision divergence under distribution shift, kinematic integration and contact mechanics (${f_{k, \perp}} = {k_{\text{contact}}} {\delta}$) govern physical clearance and reaction forces. A score computed against recorded states cannot see a feedback loop that recording removed.

### The Proposal Boundary in Two-Processor Architectures

^sec-evaluation-proposal-boundary

Drift and contact are failures of the plant. A third class of failure lives in the machine’s own hardware, and replay misses it for a different reason, because the replay harness runs on the wrong processor. In the running machine, the proposal boundary of **[[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/00 Introduction/01 The Causal Boundary/V4-01.08 The Machine in Five Levels#^sec-boundary-machine-model|The Machine in Five Levels]]** is also a processor boundary. The learned proposers run on an application processor, the permission path runs on a separate safety microcontroller, and every candidate proposal crosses the inter-processor bus before it can move the arm. Replaying sensory traces on the host application processor therefore verifies only whether neural inference completes within allocated memory bandwidth and latency budgets.

Evaluating an unverified neural policy strictly offline, without the physical plant, real-time bus transport, and safety monitors active in the loop, benchmarks isolated tensor computation rather than operational survivability. What replay on the host cannot see is everything the bus and the microcontroller add: transport delay, a late proposal arriving after its chunk lease has lapsed, a clip that changes the motion the policy expected, and a fallback that takes over mid-task. Shadow operation exposes the first two on real buses without letting the candidate move the machine, and constrained physical testing exposes all four.

↩︎ The two-processor split, stated as a hypothesis that placement must later test on silicon, is established in **[[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/01 Machine Anatomy/04 The Nervous System/V4-04.03 The Independence Requirement and the Two-Processor Hypothesis#^sec-nervous-silicon-partitioning|The Independence Requirement and the Two-Processor Hypothesis]]**.

↪ How the permission path admits a proposal, corrects it, or transfers authority to a resident fallback is developed in **[[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/03 Running the Machine/12 Safety Enforcement/V4-12.07 Refusing Proposals and Enforcing State Invariants#^sec-enforcement-authority-refuse|Refusing Proposals and Enforcing State Invariants]]**.

Because open-loop metrics cannot certify closed-loop reliability, the proposer must be evaluated in settings that restore parts of the loop. Each setting alters a different element of the sensorimotor loop (the plant dynamics, the fidelity of the sensor stream, or the actuator authority), so each exposes a different set of failures.
