# V4-07.10 Verification Ceilings and the Exposure Wall

[[Home|Home]] · [[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/Volume 4 Overview|Volume Overview]] · [[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/02 Teaching the Machine/07 Closed-Loop Evaluation/V4-07 Closed-Loop Evaluation|V4-07 Closed-Loop Evaluation]]

^sec-evaluation-limits

A clean record on a bounded manipulation task, such as cycling a warehouse door latch, supports a task-success claim under the sampled nominal operating conditions. It does not, however, establish that the system is safe against rare hazards or catastrophic structural overloads. Extending task success claims to rare physical hazards demands an explicit operating exposure budget, while bounding peak mechanical loads requires an explicit physical contact model.

### Rare-Event Exposure

^rare-event-exposure

Failing to pull a warehouse door latch open wastes a cycle, whereas crushing an obstacle or shearing a structural pin creates a physical hazard whose rate must be bounded across every hour the robot stays energized. Evaluating task success and evaluating system safety therefore require different statistical currencies. Repetitive manipulation tasks measure success across discrete trials, whereas physical hazard bounds measure event rates per unit of operating exposure, typically operating hours or travel distance. Engineers cannot conflate a task failure with a hazardous outcome, nor can they infer physical hazard severity from a trial success percentage alone.

Under the assumption of a constant hazard rate ${\lambda}$, an empirical test that logs an uninterrupted operational exposure ${T}$ without observing a hazardous event yields a record whose likelihood is $e^{-{\lambda} {T}}$, the continuous-time analog of the Bernoulli trial probability ${p}^{n}$. Setting this observation probability equal to the significance threshold ${\alpha_{\text{sig}}}$, exactly as [[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/02 Teaching the Machine/07 Closed-Loop Evaluation/V4-07.07 Confidence from a Finite Campaign#^eq-eval-zero-failure-bound|equation 3]] established for discrete trials, yields the minimum failure-free exposure time required to bound the hazard rate from above:

$$
{T} \ge \frac{\ln(1/{\alpha_{\text{sig}}})}{{\lambda}} \approx \frac{3.0}{{\lambda}} \tag{9}
$$

 evaluated at a 95 percent confidence level (${\alpha_{\text{sig}}} = 0.05$). This relationship is the continuous-time formulation of the rule of three, substituting continuous operating hours for discrete test cycles. In life-critical cyber-physical systems, mandated rate targets routinely reach $10^{-9}$ dangerous failures per operating hour.[[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/02 Teaching the Machine/07 Closed-Loop Evaluation/V4-07.16 Footnotes#^fn14|¹⁴]]

^eq-eval-exposure-wall

**Definition 7.2: Exposure wall**

***Exposure wall*** is the failure-free operating exposure, ${T} \ge \ln(1/{\alpha_{\text{sig}}})/{\lambda}$, that a zero-event test must accumulate before it can bound a hazard rate ${\lambda}$ at a stated confidence, a requirement that grows tenfold with each tenfold reduction in the target rate.

1.  **Significance**: At 95 percent confidence the wall sits near $3/{\lambda}$, so a claim of $10^{-9}$ per hour needs $3.0 \times 10^{9}$ failure-free hours, against the 33.3 h that the door’s 99.9 percent trial claim occupies. Past the wall, evidence for a hazard rate must come from the permission path and physical safeguards rather than from testing the learned proposer.
2.  **Distinction**: The exposure wall bounds a rate from event-free hours, and the zero-failure trial bound ([[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/02 Teaching the Machine/07 Closed-Loop Evaluation/V4-07.07 Confidence from a Finite Campaign#^eq-eval-zero-failure-bound|equation 3]]) bounds a per-trial probability from clean trials. Both invert a clean record; neither estimates a mean time between failures from observed failures.
3.  **Common pitfall**: A frequent misconception is that fleet hours carry a claim past the wall. Under the constant-rate model, parallel rigs do add exposure hours, which is why the notebook below divides the wall across a fleet. What they fail to add is diversity. Hours pooled across machines that share a facility, a production batch, and an operating protocol bound the rate only for the conditions those machines share, and simulated hours add no physical exposure at all.

**Napkin Math 7.3: Rig-years against the wall**

**Problem**: How much failure-free exposure does [[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/02 Teaching the Machine/07 Closed-Loop Evaluation/V4-07.10 Verification Ceilings and the Exposure Wall#^eq-eval-exposure-wall|equation 9]] demand to bound a hazard rate of $10^{-6}$ and of $10^{-9}$ per hour at 95 percent confidence, and what rate can one test rig bound in a year of continuous running?

**Variables**:

- $\ln(1/{\alpha_{\text{sig}}}) =$ 2.996 (Chosen; 95 percent confidence).
- ${\lambda} = 10^{-6}$ and $10^{-9}$ per hour (Chosen; analytical targets at the magnitudes of aviation and IEC 61508 bands).
- One rig-year $=$ 8,766 hours (Derived; continuous operation) and 100 rigs (Illustrative).

**Math**: \$= \$ 2.996 $/ 10^{-6} =$ $3.0 \times 10^{6}$ hours, or 342 machine-years; at $10^{-9}$, ${T} =$ $3.0 \times 10^{9}$ hours, which 100 rigs running continuously would need about 3,420 calendar years to supply. Inverting for one rig-year gives an upper confidence limit of 2.996 $/$ 8,766 $=$ $3.4 \times 10^{-4}$ per hour.

**Result**: A rig-year of clean running bounds the rate only to $3.4 \times 10^{-4}$ per hour, about 340 times short of $10^{-6}$ and 340,000 times short of $10^{-9}$.

**Systems insight**: A rig-year of testing reaches the order of a site’s mission-reliability target, such as the $10^{-4}$ per hour campaign sized below. Each further decade of rate costs a decade of machine-years. Claims at $10^{-6}$ and beyond must therefore rest on the permission path and physical safeguards, whose own faults can be injected and tested at the component level.

**Systems Perspective 7.3: Limits of testing for ultra-reliability**

The exposure wall is not a shortage of test budget that a larger program could cure. Butler and Finelli made this argument for fly-by-wire flight control software, whose catastrophic failure rate must satisfy ${\lambda} \le 10^{-9}$ per hour. The failure-free exposure such a requirement demands exceeds any feasible test program by orders of magnitude, so operational testing alone cannot validate it ([[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/02 Teaching the Machine/07 Closed-Loop Evaluation/V4-07.15 Source References#^ref-butler1993infeasibility|Butler and Finelli 1993]]). The evidence for a requirement past the wall must therefore come from the architecture, from what the design makes impossible, rather than from what testing failed to observe.

Butler and Finelli’s conclusion (perspective [7.3](https://mlsysbook.ai/vol4/07_evaluation/07_evaluation.html#psp-evaluation-untestable-reliability)) carries over to physical AI unchanged. Notebook [7.3](https://mlsysbook.ai/vol4/07_evaluation/07_evaluation.html#nbk-evaluation-rig-years) priced the wall in rig-years, and the definition’s pitfall explains why a fleet of parallel rigs buys hours but not the diversity of conditions a deployment claim needs. Autonomous road vehicles confront the same wall at scale. Placing one real fleet’s exposure against RAND’s mileage requirements ([[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/02 Teaching the Machine/07 Closed-Loop Evaluation/V4-07.10 Verification Ceilings and the Exposure Wall#^fig-07-av-miles-scaling|figure 5]]) shows that 56.7 million rider-only miles clear the zero-failure bars for injuries and crashes and the crash precision bar. The same exposure falls short of every fatality requirement, by more than two orders of magnitude for the precision and better-than-human rows. High-assurance physical AI systems must therefore establish safety through deterministic runtime constraints and structured hardware fault injection. While rare-event simulation and importance sampling can reduce the required sample count by orders of magnitude, their mathematical validity extends only as far as the simulator’s physical fidelity in those extreme tail regimes.

^fig-07-av-miles-scaling

Empirical road testing demonstrates concretely what fleet-scale operational exposure can and cannot substantiate. The first benchmark evaluated in the RAND study represents the exposure wall translated into driving mileage. Given an average human driver fatality rate of 1.09 per 100 million miles, an autonomous vehicle fleet must log 275 million failure-free miles to establish an upper bound matching the human baseline at 95 percent confidence. That single demonstration requires approximately 4.9 times the total rider-only commercial mileage accumulated by Waymo through January 2025 (56.7 million miles). Producing an empirical estimate of the fatality rate within a 20 percent margin of error requires 8.8 billion miles.

Nevertheless, tens of millions of operational miles can validate claims regarding higher-frequency events. Kusano et al. ([[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/02 Teaching the Machine/07 Closed-Loop Evaluation/V4-07.15 Source References#^ref-kusano2025comparison|2025]]) evaluated crashed-vehicle rates across 56.7 million rider-only miles against human driving benchmarks matched for vehicle class, road type, and operating jurisdiction. They demonstrated a 79 percent reduction in police-reported injury crashes and an 81 percent reduction in airbag-deployment collisions, both with confidence intervals that exclude zero. A finite operational exposure bounds only those event classes whose expected counts within that window are large enough to measure. Any rigorous comparative safety claim requires an exposure-matched baseline and confidence intervals over the incident rate ratio. The rarest and most severe failure modes remain untestable through on-road fleet mileage alone, and simulated driving mileage adds no true physical exposure to these empirical totals.

The exposure wall restricts verification even for target failure rates orders of magnitude less stringent than aerospace standards. Consider an industrial requirement for the warehouse mobile manipulator. The platform must certify a critical failure rate ${\lambda} \le 1.0\times 10^{-4}\text{ failures/hour}$, corresponding to a Mean Time Between Critical Failures of $\text{MTBF} \ge 10{,}000\text{ hours}$[[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/02 Teaching the Machine/07 Closed-Loop Evaluation/V4-07.16 Footnotes#^fn15|¹⁵]] at a 95 percent statistical confidence level (${\alpha_{\text{sig}}} = 0.05$). In this operational scenario, each warehouse retrieval mission takes ${T_{\text{mission}}} =$ 360 s (0.10 h) and covers 540 m at the platform’s 1.5 m/s maximum drive velocity. Automated docking, battery replenishment, and telemetry offloading introduce an overhead of ${T_{\text{reset}}} =$ 120 s, resulting in a total cycle duration of 480 s per trial. Each drive wheel uses a direct-drive hub motor with an outer diameter ${D_{\text{wheel}}} =$ 266 mm, eliminating mechanical gearbox reduction between the motor and the wheel rim.

Applying [[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/02 Teaching the Machine/07 Closed-Loop Evaluation/V4-07.10 Verification Ceilings and the Exposure Wall#^eq-eval-exposure-wall|equation 9]] reveals that establishing this $10^{-4}\text{ failures/hour}$ ceiling without observing a single failure requires 29,957 hours (3.42 years) of continuous, uninterrupted operation. Discretized across operational sorties, each mission has an allocated failure probability budget of ${p_{\text{fail}}} \le {\lambda} \cdot {T_{\text{mission}}} =$ $1.0 \times 10^{-5}$. Inverting the zero-failure binomial model from [[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/02 Teaching the Machine/07 Closed-Loop Evaluation/V4-07.07 Confidence from a Finite Campaign#^sec-evaluation-closed-loop-survival|section 7.6]] yields ${n} = \ln({\alpha_{\text{sig}}})/\ln(1-{p_{\text{fail}}})$, demanding 299,572 consecutive clean missions. This total dwarfs typical empirical validation budgets, which rarely exceed hundreds or thousands of runs. Conducting these trials sequentially on a single physical machine at 480 s per cycle requires 39,943 elapsed hours (4.56 calendar years). It also subjects each direct-drive wheel to ${N_{\text{rev}}} = ({n} \cdot {v} \cdot {T_{\text{mission}}}) / (\pi {D_{\text{wheel}}}) \approx$ $1.94 \times 10^{8}$ continuous revolutions.

Because the hub motor drives the wheel without an intermediate gearbox, every wheel revolution directly rotates the internal hub bearings. Their endurance is rated by the ${L_{10}}$ convention of **[[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/02 Teaching the Machine/05 Physical Data/V4-05.07 Teleoperation Demonstration Costs#^sec-data-yield-fatigue-budgeting|Operational yield and mechanical fatigue budgeting]]**, here the revolutions that 90 percent of an identical bearing cohort completes before fatigue flaking at a stated load, a population statistic that falls steeply as the carried load increases. Determining whether this test campaign exhausts the mechanical endurance of the running gear requires comparing ${N_{\text{rev}}}$ against the manufacturer’s internal bearing load rating and measuring the actual static and dynamic wheel reactions with corner-scale load cells. This is the stationarity problem of [[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/02 Teaching the Machine/07 Closed-Loop Evaluation/V4-07.08 Trial Budgets, Stopping Rules, and Comparisons#^sec-evaluation-trial-costs|section 7.7.2]] at the scale of a reliability campaign. The revolution count states how much wear the campaign itself inflicts, and only an ${L_{10}}$ rating at the measured load can say whether that wear reaches the fatigue regime. Because hub-motor datasheets rarely disclose that internal load rating, a campaign of this duration cannot be shown to stay clear of wear-out until the load is measured. Systems teams must therefore schedule, log, and isolate component refurbishments and sensor recalibrations before pooling runs across extended operating intervals.

The distinction drawn in [[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/02 Teaching the Machine/07 Closed-Loop Evaluation/V4-07.03 What a Physical Success Rate Measures#^sec-evaluation-what-measured|section 7.2]] between MTBF and the intervention-based metrics matters most in a supervised campaign. When operating with real-time safety guardrails, permission-path filters, software limits, emergency stops, and human supervisor overrides can intercept hazardous trajectories before physical damage occurs when their sensing, response, and physical margins are qualified for those conditions. Such interventions censor the unassisted physical outcome while remaining recorded autonomy failures. The campaign measures an empirical intervention rate, ${\lambda_{\text{int}}} = {N_{\text{int}}}/{T_{\text{active}}}$, whose inverse is the MTBI.

Consider an evaluation run in which the permission path or human supervisors intervene 38 times during 100 retrieval missions configured identically to the scenario above. With each sortie lasting 360 s and covering 540 m, the campaign aggregates 10 h of active physical movement across 54 km. These trials yield an MTBI of approximately 15.8 min and an intervention spatial density of 0.70 per kilometer. Because interventions occur frequently throughout the campaign, the resulting rate parameter supports rigorous statistical inference. If interventions are modeled as a Poisson arrival process, the exact 95 percent Garwood confidence interval for ${\lambda_{\text{int}}}$ spans 2.7 to 5.2 interventions per active operating hour around the empirical mean of 3.8 ([[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/02 Teaching the Machine/07 Closed-Loop Evaluation/V4-07.15 Source References#^ref-garwood1936fiducial|Garwood 1936]]).

A system can achieve zero hardware damage while simultaneously imposing an intolerable intervention overhead. Aggregate intervention counts do not reveal ACR, because multiple interventions can cluster within a single difficult run, while other missions might abort without triggering a formal intervention. Determining autonomous completion requires inspecting the cycle-by-cycle mission log. Furthermore, observing zero hardware damage across 10 h of supervised testing provides no statistical evidence that the unassisted policy possesses an MTBF of 10 h. From the standpoint of unassisted physical reliability, every intervened mission represents a right-censored observation. The safety layer terminated the trial before its unconstrained physical trajectory could unfold. The log captures neither a confirmed physical disaster nor an autonomous success. Interpreting these censored runs as demonstrations of unassisted task survival commits the censored trial fallacy detailed in [[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/02 Teaching the Machine/07 Closed-Loop Evaluation/V4-07.05 Evidence Regimes#^sec-evaluation-evidence-regimes|section 7.4]]. In deployment budgets, the reset and recalibration latency following each intervention represents an unavoidable operational penalty.

### Peak Loads and Unvisited Conditions

^peak-loads-and-unvisited-conditions

Operating exposure represents only one dimension of the verification ceiling. A second boundary arises because safety-constrained testing censors the physical regimes that pose the greatest operational risk ([[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/02 Teaching the Machine/07 Closed-Loop Evaluation/V4-07.05 Evidence Regimes#^sec-evaluation-evidence-regimes|section 7.4]]). Because a destructive physical failure cannot be undone (the first law, irreversibility), supervisory safety interlocks must truncate trajectories before structural damage occurs. The empirical record terminates exactly where the intervention fires.

↩︎ The first law, irreversibility, which holds that work delivered to the plant cannot be rolled back, is established in **[[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/00 Introduction/01 The Causal Boundary/V4-01.11 The Four Bedrock Laws#^sec-boundary-bedrock-laws|The Four Bedrock Laws]]**.

The corner cases an engineering team most needs to characterize, such as loss of traction on slick surfaces at maximum transit speed, dynamic wheel slip, or imminent collisions in tight aisles, are the physical regimes the system cannot be permitted to explore. Allowing an automated mobile manipulator to skid into structural shelving damages the drive chassis, knocks calibrated lidar and camera constellations out of alignment, and sidelines the entire test cell for mechanical repair. Physical cages, virtual geofences, and human operators with emergency stops truncate trials at a conservative safety boundary. The test log records an intervention event and counts it in the denominator, but the physical consequences of the policy’s unconstrained commands remain unobserved.

When evaluating physical hardware survival, average metrics can mask structural overloads. Consider a learned mobile manipulation policy whose average latch contact force across one hundred nominal trials is only ${\bar{F}} =$ 12 N, below the 15 N contact tripwire threshold. However, if the gripper strikes the strike plate at a slight angular offset, the tail of the empirical peak-force distribution reaches a 99th-percentile impact of 115 N. This transient peak breaches the structural latch limit of 100 N despite the benign average. A peak of that size in a campaign guarded by a 15 N tripwire is not a contradiction. The first impact peak arrives faster than the tripwire can act, so these trials record the overshoot first and are ended by the tripwire afterward, as failures. The rise-time argument below shows why no reactive monitor can prevent it. While a single force peak does not directly equate to total impulse or plastic deformation (which depend on the full force-time profile, structural load paths, and material yield criteria), it reveals how central tendencies conceal destructive tail behavior.

![[90 Assets/Volume IV/07 Evaluation/Margin Latch Peak Force Envelope 5c0c9430d6.svg]]

*Two horizontal bars of latch contact force against a red dashed latch limit of 100 newtons. The mean of 12 newtons sits left of a dotted tripwire at 15 newtons, and the 99th-percentile peak of 115 newtons runs past the limit into the shaded region.* *A 12 N mean hides a 115 N peak past the 100 N limit.*

The undamped spring-mass model of **[[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/02 Teaching the Machine/06 Policy Training/V4-06.05 The Simulator as a System Component#^eq-training-impact-peak|equation 6.7]]**, which in **[[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/02 Teaching the Machine/06 Policy Training/V4-06.05 The Simulator as a System Component#^sec-training-contact-exploit|The sim-to-real contact exploit]]** exposed how a soft simulated spring understated the latch force, bounds this initial impact. Written for evaluation, its moving mass becomes the reduced mass of the colliding bodies, ${m_{\text{red}}} = (1/{m_{\text{eff}}} + 1/{m_{\text{obj}}})^{-1}$, which reduces to the arm’s effective inertia against an immovable frame. Its spring becomes the local contact stiffness ${k_{\text{contact}}}$. The peak force is then ${v_{\text{approach}}}\sqrt{{m_{\text{red}}}\,{k_{\text{contact}}}}$, and it arrives a quarter of the natural oscillation period after touch, a rise time of ${\Delta} {t_{\text{rise}}} \approx (\pi/2)\sqrt{{m_{\text{red}}}/{k_{\text{contact}}}}$. Inverting the peak for a structural threshold ${F_{\text{lim}}}$ gives the quantity an evaluation campaign can actually enforce, a precontact speed limit:

$$
{v_{\text{approach}}} \le \frac{{F_{\text{lim}}}}{\sqrt{{m_{\text{red}}}\, {k_{\text{contact}}}}}, \tag{10}
$$

 This physical relationship governs the transient contact velocity limits in ISO/TS 15066 Annex A for power- and force-limited collaborative robots ([[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/02 Teaching the Machine/07 Closed-Loop Evaluation/V4-07.15 Source References#^ref-isots15066|International Organization for Standardization 2016]]). The collaborative application requirements of ISO/TS 15066 have since been incorporated into ISO 10218-2:2025, and ISO/TS 15066:2016 remains published while it is revised into the international standard ISO 15066-1 ([[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/02 Teaching the Machine/07 Closed-Loop Evaluation/V4-07.15 Source References#^ref-iso10218part2|International Organization for Standardization 2025]]).

^eq-eval-impact-speed

Evaluating this bound on a physical door latch requires measuring both the effective mass of the arm at the end-effector and the local contact stiffness of the latch mechanism. These parameters depend heavily on joint posture and kinematics, and they are never found in high-level hardware datasheets. The systems takeaway, however, is structural rather than numerical. If a software monitor’s sensing, compute, and actuation latency exceeds the mechanical rise time ${\Delta} {t_{\text{rise}}}$, no reactive software policy can attenuate the initial force spike, regardless of model inference speed. Software intervention comes too late. Physical protection against initial impact must therefore rely on two pre-contact mechanisms: passive mechanical compliance, which lowers effective stiffness ${k_{\text{contact}}}$ and extends rise time ${\Delta} {t_{\text{rise}}}$, and strict pre-contact kinematic velocity caps, such as the platform’s 0.03 m/s guarded approach limit.

Extreme value theory (EVT), developed in **[[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/04 Governing the Machine/15 Adversarial Verification/V4-15.11 Interpreting Campaign Statistics#^sec-verification-extreme-value-theory|Extreme value theory and heavy-tailed safety distributions]]**, models such tail extremes by fitting a limiting distribution family to many independent block maxima, yet the physical mechanics of contact do not prescribe which family applies.[[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/02 Teaching the Machine/07 Closed-Loop Evaluation/V4-07.16 Footnotes#^fn16|¹⁶]] In the absence of thousands of destructive physical trials, [[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/02 Teaching the Machine/07 Closed-Loop Evaluation/V4-07.10 Verification Ceilings and the Exposure Wall#^tbl-07-extreme-value-tails|table 6]] pairs measurable physical disturbance channels with concrete architectural safeguards rather than relying on unverified tail extrapolations.

The door latch campaign shows why the contact shock entry in [[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/02 Teaching the Machine/07 Closed-Loop Evaluation/V4-07.10 Verification Ceilings and the Exposure Wall#^tbl-07-extreme-value-tails|table 6]] pairs a measurement with an architectural safeguard rather than with a fitted tail. Each trial yields only one block maximum, its peak latch contact force, and twenty maxima cannot fit a generalized extreme value tail that would predict the probability of exceeding 100 N. The safeguard therefore comes from the rise-time argument above. The guarded 0.03 m/s approach is a candidate precontact limit whose adequacy depends on the measured effective mass, contact stiffness, and structural threshold. Reactive force monitoring addresses the sustained load that follows.

Deploying parallel robots across a test fleet does not establish statistical independence, as the pitfall in definition [7.2](https://mlsysbook.ai/vol4/07_evaluation/07_evaluation.html#dfn-evaluation-exposure-wall) warns. Ten identical machines operating concurrently in a single warehouse share the same manufacturing batch, firmware revision, concrete sealant finish, heating and ventilation airflow, and operator handling protocols. Environmental disturbances absent from that facility, such as low-angle winter solar glare, surface water pooling, or floor polishing glazing, affect none of the machines. Standardized physical test fixtures, such as the response-robot test bays captured in [[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/02 Teaching the Machine/07 Closed-Loop Evaluation/V4-07.10 Verification Ceilings and the Exposure Wall#^fig-07-nist-test-arena|figure 6]], ensure repeatable benchmark comparisons by strictly constraining the test environment to known, fixed obstacle geometries.

The standardized obstacle courses documented in [[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/02 Teaching the Machine/07 Closed-Loop Evaluation/V4-07.10 Verification Ceilings and the Exposure Wall#^fig-07-nist-test-arena|figure 6]] enable repeatable comparisons across robotic platforms under ASTM E54.09 test methods. However, their controlled geometry, uniform surface traction, and regulated indoor lighting also define the perimeter of empirical evidence. Validating system performance against factory chemical spills, erratic pedestrian crossings, or blinding solar glare requires intentionally introducing those environmental distributions into the test matrix.

These physical and statistical verification ceilings lead to an architectural conclusion. Safety in physical AI cannot rest on statistical confidence derived from testing learned weights. The exposure that failure rates between $10^{-6}$ and $10^{-9}$ per hour demand, from $3.0 \times 10^{6}$ to $3.0 \times 10^{9}$ failure-free hours, shows that empirical test volume cannot serve as the primary safety argument. Deterministic runtime constraints must supply their own independent verification evidence, including explicit physical assumptions under which hazardous transitions are prevented and rigorous bounds on worst-case execution time. Empirical policy trials measure task competence and quantify intervention rates inside a nominal operational envelope; they do not prove that the system can contain arbitrary out-of-distribution failures. The burden of safety certification therefore shifts from the learned proposer to the deterministic permission path and verified physical safeguards, each of which must be evaluated through independent verification pathways.

A model-based stability certificate, such as the Lyapunov argument developed in **[[05 Reference/Volume IV - Physical AI/V4 Control and Dynamics#^sec-appendix-control-lyapunov|Lyapunov stability and invariant sets]]**, provides structural guarantees that empirical finite trials cannot establish. When the learned proposer lacks a validated certificate for the declared plant and observation model, the embodied computing platform must provide architectural containment and targeted fault injection:

1.  **Runtime Containment**: The system enforces hard physical bounds on the learned policy by delegating collaborative force thresholds, kinematic acceleration boundaries, and emergency stop triggers to an independent, deterministic permission path situated beneath the proposal boundary. The system’s safety claims then rest on the permission path and its associated fallback strategies. These controllers rely on validated state estimation, conservative plant and actuator dynamics models, worst-case execution timing, and guaranteed stopping margins, all verified independently of the neural network proposer.
2.  **Physical Fault Injection**: Rather than passively waiting for rare environmental disturbances or hardware faults to manifest during operational testing, systems engineers deliberately inject them on instrumented test benches. Because fault injection artificially alters the operational failure rate, these trials must be recorded in a separate, explicitly labeled log rather than pooled with nominal operational draws.

**Checkpoint 7.3: Sample size and MTBF evidence target**

Before moving on, review sample size scaling and physical test limits:

**Exposure-wall estimate**: Under a stationary Poisson failure model, estimate the failure-free hours a relaxed 1,000 h MTBF target needs at 95 percent confidence, and determine whether one rig running continuously for a year could supply them.

**Mechanical fatigue exhaustion**: Explain why a campaign’s wheel revolutions cannot be compared with a bearing ${L_{10}}$ rating without knowing the load the bearing carries.

**Architectural containment vs. statistical sampling**: Explain why a task campaign needs separate evidence for runtime constraints and physical safeguards.

Even under automated test cells and rigorous qualification protocols, statistical sampling of learned policies cannot demonstrate ultra-reliable autonomy. The integrity of an operational release decision depends on identifying precisely what remains unvisited by a finite test campaign and deploying verified runtime monitors to supervise those uncharacterized boundaries.

### Turning Coverage Gaps into Runtime Obligations

^turning-coverage-gaps-into-runtime-obligations

Those uncharacterized boundaries come from every stage of the pipeline: offline datasets omit recovery states, physics simulators omit real-world contact and stator phase lag, and finite hardware campaigns sample only a handful of cells in the condition matrix. Because the physical states a machine traverses depend on the specific sequence of actuator commands admitted by the permission path ([[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/02 Teaching the Machine/07 Closed-Loop Evaluation/V4-07.04 Closed-Loop Dynamics#^sec-evaluation-closed-loop-dynamics|section 7.3]]), small discrepancies between modeled and real-world dynamics compound over closed-loop horizons. They drive the platform into state regimes that never appeared during training or simulation. Neither a static dataset nor a simulator can match the physical plant’s closed-loop state distribution, and an empirical hardware test bounds nothing beyond the specific operating conditions it directly sampled.

The operational conclusion of the door campaign is correspondingly narrow. The physical trial records establish that a single software build deployed on a specific hardware platform (Unit 01) bounded its per-cycle failure probability below 13.9 percent at 95 percent confidence. That bound was evaluated from the sampled initial poses under the specific ambient illumination and temperature present during testing. This record provides no assurance about system behavior on a cold morning that thickens gearbox lubricant or derates actuator drive current. It provides none on an aging transmission whose compliance and backlash have increased through gear tooth wear, or against a door whose strike plate has shifted several millimeters out of true. Testing across multiple physical units broadens the declared operating population, whereas executing additional runs solely on Unit 01 with a nominal strike plate merely tightens the binomial confidence interval ([[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/02 Teaching the Machine/07 Closed-Loop Evaluation/V4-07.08 Trial Budgets, Stopping Rules, and Comparisons#^tbl-07-confidence-bounds|table 3]]). Logging two thousand additional nominal cycles measures that single hardware unit with high statistical precision while leaving displaced strike plates, payload variations, unit-to-unit manufacturing variations, and real-time bus jitter unsupported by empirical evidence.

Because finite evaluation cannot characterize behavior outside the sampled operational distribution, every unverified boundary of an empirical safety claim must be translated into an explicit runtime monitoring obligation. Each boundary requires an identifiable sensor signal, a calibrated detection threshold, a validated mitigation response, and deterministic enforcement by the permission path. Because operational distribution support is only partially observable and software out-of-distribution (OOD) detectors have nonzero false-negative rates, environmental conditions that cannot be sensed with high confidence require hard ODD constraints, passive mechanical guards, or independent physical barriers.

The evaluation record captures these obligations in its `runtime_monitor_specs` field ([[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/02 Teaching the Machine/07 Closed-Loop Evaluation/V4-07.09 The Evaluation Record#^sec-evaluation-logs|section 7.8]]), across five mandatory monitoring channels whose detection coverage, latency, and mitigation responses must be independently validated:

1.  **Input Support and Out-of-Distribution (OOD) Monitor:** Quantifies whether incoming sensor observations remain within the support of the training and verification distributions using a calibrated score, with measured false-negative rates on held-out OOD test sets.
2.  **Execution Latency and Freshness Monitor:** Tracks sensor observation age and neural inference execution time against separate hard deadlines. It verifies proposal renewal against nominal controller cadence (for example, verifying that the latch policy meets its 40 ms p99 inference target inside its 50 ms chunk period at 20 Hz), logs deadline misses, and ensures that an expired chunk lease immediately engages a validated fallback. A p99 latency is an empirical percentile; it does not replace a hard real-time execution bound.
3.  **Constraint Margin Monitor:** Continuously tracks the distance between the current physical state and hardware protection boundaries, monitoring joint torque limits, motor winding Joule heating, and physical obstacle clearances against calibrated stopping-distance models.
4.  **Intervention Rate Monitor:** Computes the running frequency of permission-path interventions and trajectory overrides as a warning of growing supervisory burden or behavioral drift. Preventive detection of a specified instability requires a declared window and threshold, with qualified detection-plus-response lead time. This warning does not replace instantaneous clearance and constraint checks or a feasible fallback.
5.  **Environmental Assumption Monitor:** Verifies whether current physical conditions comply with operating design assumptions, tracking surface traction through wheel slip ratios and ambient illumination via calibrated photometric sensors.

A runtime monitor that merely records an anomaly to a diagnostic log provides zero physical protection. Under active control, if a newly generated policy proposal fails validation, the permission path rejects the replacement command without revoking the currently executing chunk, whose chunk lease is still valid. If the active command expires, encounters a safety violation, or lacks sufficient clearance, the permission path overrides the proposer and commands a validated fallback, such as a conservative speed restriction or a controlled deceleration to rest. The fallback is valid only if current state estimates, actuator torque headroom, and braking distances render it physically feasible. Even when a neural policy produces timely, high-confidence proposals, every candidate control action ${{\mathbf{u}_{\text{nom}}}}_t$ must pass independent geometric clearance and actuator dynamic limits before the low-level hardware bridge applies torque to the plant.

↪ The barrier condition that keeps braking feasible within the remaining clearance is developed in **[[05 Reference/Volume IV - Physical AI/V4 Control and Dynamics#^sec-appendix-control-cbf|Safety and Control Barrier Functions]]**.

Certain catastrophic failure modes cannot be detected by any software runtime monitor. A software out-of-distribution monitor operates exclusively on digitized sensor telemetry and latent neural representations. When a physical transduction failure occurs, such as a clean glass wall, a blinding specular laser reflection, or a camera occluded by uniform mud, the sensory telemetry itself is corrupted or absent. The perception pipeline may extract seemingly normal visual features from the reflection, reporting benign obstacle clearance while the physical chassis is on a collision course with the glass. Software cannot detect physical reality that hardware transduction failed to capture. In such failure modes, the proposal can satisfy every execution deadline, respect all motor torque envelopes, and produce familiar latent embedding vectors, yet drive the platform directly into an invisible obstacle. Physical safety against transduction failures cannot come from statistical evaluation or software-based OOD monitors. It requires architectural physical zoning, hardware current limiters, sensitive tactile bumpers, and physical safety barriers validated under explicit mechanical assumptions independently of the learned policy.

↪ Observational indistinguishability, in which a hazardous state yields the same telemetry as a safe one until the response deadline, is developed in **[[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/04 Governing the Machine/18 Engineering Physical AI/V4-18.04 The Epistemic Frontier#^sec-frontier-observational-indistinguishability-sub|Observational indistinguishability and the action deadline]]**.

These verification ceilings bind every evaluation campaign, no matter how rigorously executed. The empirical evidence preserved in an evaluation record is only as trustworthy as the measurement protocols that produced it. Procedural flaws, silent sensor drift, and statistical fallacies can corrupt an evaluation campaign long before its summary metrics reach the evaluation record.
