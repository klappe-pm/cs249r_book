# V4-07.08 Trial Budgets, Stopping Rules, and Comparisons

[[Home|Home]] · [[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/Volume 4 Overview|Volume Overview]] · [[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/02 Teaching the Machine/07 Closed-Loop Evaluation/V4-07 Closed-Loop Evaluation|V4-07 Closed-Loop Evaluation]]

^sec-evaluation-trial-limits

While a million software unit tests consume only processor cycles, each closed-loop trial of a physical robot consumes test-cell time, exhausts bearing and linkage fatigue life, burns thermal headroom in motor windings, and risks costly hardware damage. Statistical theory dictates how many trials a reliability claim demands, but on a physical machine the plant, not the compute budget, determines what each trial costs. Sizing a qualification campaign therefore means reconciling the trial count required by statistical confidence with the operational time and fatigue life of the testbed that must supply it. That reconciliation separates a rigorous qualification argument from an anecdotal demonstration. The same budget pressure then motivates the two refinements that close the section: stopping a campaign early without inflating its error rates, and deciding whether a retrained checkpoint is better than the one it would replace.

### Sample Size Requirements per Empirical Claim

^sec-evaluation-trials-per-claim

Reconciling confidence with testbed capacity starts by fixing the statistical claim before scheduling the cell, because the reliability target, not the length of an afternoon shift, dictates the required sample size. For a zero-failure campaign the count is [[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/02 Teaching the Machine/07 Closed-Loop Evaluation/V4-07.07 Confidence from a Finite Campaign#^eq-eval-zero-failure-sample-size|equation 5]], with the rule of three as its rare-failure approximation. [[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/02 Teaching the Machine/07 Closed-Loop Evaluation/V4-07.08 Trial Budgets, Stopping Rules, and Comparisons#^fig-07-confidence-bounds|Figure 4]] plots it across confidence levels, and [[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/02 Teaching the Machine/07 Closed-Loop Evaluation/V4-07.08 Trial Budgets, Stopping Rules, and Comparisons#^tbl-07-confidence-bounds|table 3]] tabulates it. The claim (target and confidence) is fixed first, the count follows from it, and if a testing facility cannot afford that count, the engineering team must narrow its claim rather than present an underpowered test as formal qualification. If a campaign records any failure, it must apply the general binomial bound of [[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/02 Teaching the Machine/07 Closed-Loop Evaluation/V4-07.07 Confidence from a Finite Campaign#^sec-evaluation-bounds-with-failures|section 7.6.1]]. An engineering team cannot salvage a zero-failure claim by appending clean runs after inspecting intermediate data.

↪ The same bound, counted in operating hours rather than trials, and the population such a statistic describes are developed in **[[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/04 Governing the Machine/15 Adversarial Verification/V4-15.11 Interpreting Campaign Statistics#^sec-verification-exposure-wall|Exposure and the population a statistic describes]]**.

### Test-Cell Time and Wear per Trial

^sec-evaluation-trial-costs

The door-opening campaign illustrates how the physical cost of a single trial accumulates. During a nominal execution run, the mobile base navigates inward from its 2 m standoff position and stops. The manipulator arm then executes a guarded unlatching sequence at 0.03 m/s and swings the door open. These physical motions occupy 25 s of active test-cell time. Preparing the testbed for the next statistically independent draw requires an automated reset: re-latching the door mechanism, navigating the base back to its standoff position, and flushing onboard telemetry buffers. This reset sequence adds 15 s, bringing the total wall-clock budget for each trial to 40 s. At this cycle duration, the initial twenty clean runs take 13.3 min. Qualifying a 99.9 percent success lower limit (notebook [7.2](https://mlsysbook.ai/vol4/07_evaluation/07_evaluation.html#nbk-evaluation-statistical-scaling-high-reliability-assurance)) demands a far larger campaign, even before accounting for the thermal cooldown intervals required by the actuator windings.

↩︎ The recovery time a periodic duty cycle forces between loaded bursts, before the windings reach their temperature ceiling, is established in **[[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/01 Machine Anatomy/02 The Physical Body/V4-02.06 Thermal Duty Cycles#^sec-body-transient-thermal-bursts|Transient thermal bursts and periodic duty cycles]]**.

**Napkin Math 7.2: The nines tax**

**Problem**: An evaluation target calls for a one-sided success lower limit of ${p_{\text{target}}} = 0.999$ at 95 percent confidence. How many zero-failure door runs does it need, and how long does the latch cell take to supply them?

**Variables**:

- ${p_{\text{target}}} = 0.999$ and ${\alpha_{\text{sig}}} =$ 0.05 (Chosen; the claim).
- ${t_{\text{run}}} =$ 25 s and ${T_{\text{reset}}} =$ 15 s (Illustrative; drive-in, guarded unlatch, swing, then re-latch and return), so ${T_{\text{trial}}} =$ 40 s.
- two safety operators per run (Illustrative).

**Math**: From [[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/02 Teaching the Machine/07 Closed-Loop Evaluation/V4-07.07 Confidence from a Finite Campaign#^eq-eval-zero-failure-sample-size|equation 5]], ${n} = \lceil \ln(1/{\alpha_{\text{sig}}}) / \ln(1/{p_{\text{target}}}) \rceil = \lceil$ 2.996 $/$ 0.0010005 $\rceil =$ 2,995, close to the rule of three’s $3/(1-{p_{\text{target}}})$. The cell time is 2,995 $\times$ 40 s $=$ 119,800 s, or 33.3 h (1.4 days) of continuous testing, and 66.6 h of operator time, over eight eight-hour shifts.

**Result**: 2,995 clean runs and 33.3 h of cell occupancy, against 13.3 min for the opening twenty.

**Systems insight**: The denominator $\ln(1/{p_{\text{target}}}) \approx 1 - {p_{\text{target}}}$ shrinks tenfold with each added nine, so each nine multiplies the runs, the cell hours, and the wear by ten. A longer cycle scales the hours in proportion. A campaign this long also outlives the stationarity it assumes, and a component replaced midway may require a new population definition rather than pooling incompatible runs.

The physical plant also degrades as trials accumulate. Statistical confidence bounds assume iid draws from a stationary process, but physical wear breaks stationarity. Transmission backlash (lost motion), effective wheel radius, tire surface friction, and joint damping all drift at rates that engineers must monitor directly on the test hardware. Pooling a thousand trials without that monitoring treats an evolving, degrading machine as an unchanging platform. A failure on trial 950 may reflect testbed wear, a policy limitation, or their interaction; configuration and condition logs are needed to distinguish these causes. The wear itself is budgeted as it is for demonstration collection, by charging each trial’s cycles against the rated life of the drivetrain components.

↩︎ Sizing a physical campaign against operational yield and the fatigue life of drivetrain components is established in **[[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/02 Teaching the Machine/05 Physical Data/V4-05.07 Teleoperation Demonstration Costs#^sec-data-yield-fatigue-budgeting|Operational yield and mechanical fatigue budgeting]]**.

Trials this expensive make stopping early attractive. A fixed-sample, zero-failure protocol already permits safe early rejection: testing halts immediately upon the first observed failure, avoiding pointless exposure to a hazardous policy. Early acceptance, however, presents a different statistical challenge. Halting testing prematurely simply because intermediate results appear favorable inflates the false-positive rate unless the stopping threshold was calibrated in advance. To balance test efficiency with rigorous error guarantees, [[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/02 Teaching the Machine/07 Closed-Loop Evaluation/V4-07.08 Trial Budgets, Stopping Rules, and Comparisons#^sec-evaluation-sequential-sprt|section 7.7.3]] develops sequential boundaries for both acceptance and rejection.

### Sequential Probability Ratio Testing

^sec-evaluation-sequential-sprt

Wald’s sequential probability ratio test (SPRT)[[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/02 Teaching the Machine/07 Closed-Loop Evaluation/V4-07.16 Footnotes#^fn9|⁹]] ([[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/02 Teaching the Machine/07 Closed-Loop Evaluation/V4-07.15 Source References#^ref-wald1945sequential|Wald 1945]]) provides those pre-calibrated stopping boundaries by evaluating the cumulative log-likelihood ratio after each successive trial, comparing an unacceptable failure rate against an acceptable one. For a strict autonomy campaign, the Bernoulli outcome records unassisted task completion; for a full-system service evaluation, it records the separately specified operational outcome. While the mathematical formulation accommodates either metric, the outcome definition must remain strictly frozen throughout data collection.

↪ The full SPRT derivation for Bernoulli trials, including Wald’s boundary approximations and their overshoot calibration, is developed in **[[05 Reference/Volume IV - Physical AI/V4 Machine Learning Background#^sec-appendix-ml-sprt|Sequential Probability Ratio Test]]**.

Following the convention of **[[05 Reference/Volume IV - Physical AI/V4 Machine Learning Background#^sec-appendix-ml-sprt|Sequential Probability Ratio Test]]**, this subsection states its hypotheses in failure probability. Within it, ${p_{\text{fail}}}$ denotes the per-trial failure probability (the ${p_{\text{fail}}}$ of [[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/02 Teaching the Machine/07 Closed-Loop Evaluation/V4-07.07 Confidence from a Finite Campaign#^sec-evaluation-closed-loop-survival|section 7.6]]), not the success probability of the preceding sections, and ${p}_0$ and ${p}_1$ are failure rates. The comparison of two policies that follows returns to success rates. For hypotheses ${H}_0: {p_{\text{fail}}} = {p}_0$ (unacceptable failure rate) and ${H}_1: {p_{\text{fail}}} = {p}_1 < {p}_0$ (acceptable reliability), observing ${k}$ failures across ${m}$ independent trials yields the log-likelihood ratio:

$$
{\Lambda_m} = {k} \ln \left(\frac{{p}_1}{{p}_0}\right) + ({m} - {k}) \ln \left(\frac{1 - {p}_1}{1 - {p}_0}\right) \tag{7}
$$

^eq-eval-sprt-llr

Each success increments the ratio toward acceptance, while each failure drives it sharply toward rejection. With the illustrative hypotheses ${p}_0 = 0.10$ and ${p}_1 = 0.01$, a success adds 0.0953 while a failure subtracts 2.303. This metric tracks cumulative evidence for the declared outcome; it never grants permission to continue an unsafe physical run when a safety tripwire is breached.

Wald’s approximate boundaries on the log-likelihood ratio are:

$$
{\Lambda_{\text{up}}} \approx \ln \left(\frac{1 - {\beta}}{{\alpha_{\text{sig}}}}\right), \quad {\Lambda_{\text{lo}}} \approx \ln \left(\frac{{\beta}}{1 - {\alpha_{\text{sig}}}}\right) \tag{8}
$$

 where ${\alpha_{\text{sig}}}$ represents the allowed probability of accepting ${H}_1$ when ${H}_0$ holds, and ${\beta}$ represents the allowed probability of rejecting ${H}_1$ when it holds. For nominal targets of ${\alpha_{\text{sig}}} = 0.05$ and ${\beta} = 0.10$, the boundaries evaluate to ${\Lambda_{\text{up}}} \approx$ 2.890 and ${\Lambda_{\text{lo}}} \approx$ -2.251. Following each physical rollout ${m}$, the evaluation supervisor executes one of three rules:

^eq-eval-sprt-boundaries

1.  If ${\Lambda_m} \ge {\Lambda_{\text{up}}}$: Halt testing and accept ${H}_1$ under the declared test (documenting certified evidence within the target ODD).
2.  If ${\Lambda_m} \le {\Lambda_{\text{lo}}}$: Halt testing and reject ${H}_1$ (policy rejected; abort the campaign immediately to prevent hardware damage).
3.  If ${\Lambda_{\text{lo}}} < {\Lambda_m} < {\Lambda_{\text{up}}}$: Continue sampling; execute rollout ${m}+1$.

Tracing an evaluation run reveals the operational efficiency of this sequential framework. Starting from a neutral score of zero, a failure on the first run crosses boundary ${\Lambda_{\text{lo}}}$ immediately, halting testing. Conversely, reaching acceptance from zero requires 31 consecutive clean runs, found by dividing threshold ${\Lambda_{\text{up}}}$ by the per-success increment and rounding up. Unlike a rigid zero-failure protocol, a failure mid-campaign does not force immediate termination. After 25 clean runs and one failure, the score settles at 0.08, remaining between the thresholds, so testing continues. Compare this with a fixed-sample zero-failure test for a 90 percent success lower limit, which requires 29 clean runs and rejects upon any failure. While both designs demand comparable trials on a spotless record, the fixed test rejects an acceptable policy with ${p_{\text{fail}}} = {p}_1$ in 25.3 percent of campaigns, whereas the sequential test explicitly bounds that Type II error. The sequential design earns its place on the testbed by controlling both error modes, not simply by promising fewer trials.

The error guarantees follow directly from the likelihood ratio itself. Under ${H}_0$, the likelihood ratio $e^{{\Lambda_m}}$ forms a nonnegative martingale with unit mean, a sequence whose expected next value, given everything observed so far, equals its current value. Because no run is expected to raise it, no rule for choosing when to stop can raise its expected size either. By Ville’s inequality,[[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/02 Teaching the Machine/07 Closed-Loop Evaluation/V4-07.16 Footnotes#^fn10|¹⁰]] the probability that it ever reaches $e^{{\Lambda_{\text{up}}}}$ is bounded by $e^{-{\Lambda_{\text{up}}}}$ ([[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/02 Teaching the Machine/07 Closed-Loop Evaluation/V4-07.15 Source References#^ref-ville1939etude|Ville 1939]]). Under Wald’s boundary, that bound evaluates to ${\alpha_{\text{sig}}}/(1-{\beta}) =$ 0.056, slightly above nominal ${\alpha_{\text{sig}}}$ because the chosen Wald threshold is an approximation. An analogous argument under ${H}_1$ bounds false rejection by ${\beta}/(1-{\alpha_{\text{sig}}}) =$ 0.105. These are upper bounds, not the realized error probabilities of this discrete test ([[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/02 Teaching the Machine/07 Closed-Loop Evaluation/V4-07.15 Source References#^ref-wald1945sequential|Wald 1945]]). Establishing its actual operating characteristics requires calibration that accounts for score-step overshoot. A campaign that must guarantee false acceptance at or below ${\alpha_{\text{sig}}}$ adopts the conservative threshold ${\Lambda_{\text{up}}} = \ln(1/{\alpha_{\text{sig}}})$, requiring only a few additional clean runs. A complete qualification record archives the hypotheses, outcome definitions, stopping thresholds, the full trial trace, and the final decision. Even when those stopping rules hold, a sequential design only reduces expected testing time for a single policy against fixed thresholds. It cannot compensate for an ill-defined operational domain, a filtered evaluation denominator, or unverified policy transfer to another physical unit. Deciding between two candidate checkpoints requires a comparative test design.

### Comparing Two Policies

^sec-evaluation-policy-comparison

Most robot-learning evaluations do not qualify a single policy against a static regulatory target; they compare competing models. An engineering team retrains the door-latching policy and asks whether the new checkpoint outperforms `latch-bc-01`. Such comparisons inherit every constraint of single-policy evaluation while adding three distinct systems requirements: experimental trial design, exact non-parametric testing, and statistical power analysis.

The first requirement is experimental trial design. Physical testbeds drift while a comparison proceeds: actuator windings warm, latch springs relax, ambient lighting shifts across the day, and tire surfaces wear. If all runs of one checkpoint precede all runs of the other, that physical drift corrupts the comparison, masquerading as a policy effect. The two policies must therefore execute in an interleaved, randomized sequence on the same physical unit and fixture, initialized from matched states sampled from the target ODD. Furthermore, the human operator who resets the cell and annotates borderline outcomes must remain blinded to which checkpoint executed. Kress-Gazit et al. ([[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/02 Teaching the Machine/07 Closed-Loop Evaluation/V4-07.15 Source References#^ref-kressgazit2024robot|2024]]) established these practices as a reporting standard for physical robot evaluation, requiring explicit run counts, stated initial conditions, unambiguous success criteria, and qualitative failure analyses. Toyota Research Institute adopted this discipline when evaluating large behavior models, executing physical comparisons as blind A/B trials ([[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/02 Teaching the Machine/07 Closed-Loop Evaluation/V4-07.15 Source References#^ref-trilbm2025careful|TRI LBM Team et al. 2025]]).

The second requirement is selecting an appropriate hypothesis test. Each policy arm yields a count of binary outcomes, and the analysis asks whether the two observed totals could plausibly arise from a single shared success rate. Fisher’s exact test[[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/02 Teaching the Machine/07 Closed-Loop Evaluation/V4-07.16 Footnotes#^fn11|¹¹]] resolves this for small physical samples by calculating the hypergeometric probability of every contingency table matching the observed margins ([[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/02 Teaching the Machine/07 Closed-Loop Evaluation/V4-07.15 Source References#^ref-fisher1922interpretation|Fisher 1922]]). Suppose, for illustration, that `latch-bc-01` completes all 20 interleaved nominal runs and a candidate completes 16. Evaluating significance requires only combinatorial counting. Under the null hypothesis that both checkpoints share the same success probability, the 40 total runs produced 4 failures. Assigning policy labels under the null is an arbitrary partition of the 40 runs into two arms of 20, making every configuration of 4 failures equally probable. The probability that all 4 failures land exclusively in the candidate’s arm is $\binom{20}{4}/\binom{40}{4} =$ 0.053. Accounting for an equally extreme split in the opposite direction yields a two-sided ${p}$-value (a probability computed under the null hypothesis, not a success rate) of 0.106. Because a disparity this large occurs by chance more than 10 percent of the time at this sample size, the campaign cannot declare either checkpoint superior at ${\alpha_{\text{sig}}} = 0.05$. Robot-learning evaluations frequently rest on samples this small, where genuine performance gaps remain statistically undetectable ([[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/02 Teaching the Machine/07 Closed-Loop Evaluation/V4-07.15 Source References#^ref-snyder2025policy|Snyder et al. 2025]]).

The third requirement is statistical power, which fixes the sample size before executing the first run. Cohen’s power analysis determines the necessary trials per arm. The analyst specifies the significance level ${\alpha_{\text{sig}}}$, the Type II error rate ${\beta}$ (or power $1 - {\beta}$), and the minimum effect size worth detecting ([[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/02 Teaching the Machine/07 Closed-Loop Evaluation/V4-07.15 Source References#^ref-cohen1988power|Cohen 1988]]).[[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/02 Teaching the Machine/07 Closed-Loop Evaluation/V4-07.16 Footnotes#^fn12|¹²]] For two binomial success rates, ${p_{\text{high}}}$ for the better policy and ${p_{\text{low}}}$ for the worse, the effect size is Cohen’s ${h} = 2\arcsin\sqrt{{p_{\text{high}}}} - 2\arcsin\sqrt{{p_{\text{low}}}}$. Because the raw binomial variance of an arm’s sample proportion, such as ${p_{\text{high}}}(1-{p_{\text{high}}})/{n_{\text{arm}}}$, depends on the underlying success rate and shrinks near 100 percent, a fixed percentage-point gap carries a different sampling variance near the top of the probability scale than near the middle, and the arcsine transformation stabilizes that variance. The sample transform $2\arcsin\sqrt{{\hat{p}}}$, computed from the success fraction ${\hat{p}}$ across ${n_{\text{arm}}}$ runs, has asymptotic variance approximately equal to $1/{n_{\text{arm}}}$ regardless of the underlying rate. Consequently, the difference between two arms has variance $2/{n_{\text{arm}}}$. Requiring the expected difference ${h}$ to span the two-sided critical value ${z_{1-\alpha_{\text{sig}}/2}} =$ 1.96 plus the power buffer ${z_{1-\beta}} =$ 0.84, both expressed in units of standard error $\sqrt{2/{n_{\text{arm}}}}$, gives ${n_{\text{arm}}} = 2\left(({z_{1-\alpha_{\text{sig}}/2}} + {z_{1-\beta}})/{h}\right)^2$ runs per arm. Distinguishing a 90 percent policy from a 80 percent one (${h} =$ 0.28) at ${\alpha_{\text{sig}}} = 0.05$ with 80 percent power requires about 195 runs per arm. The Toyota team reached this same conclusion in real-world policy comparisons: measuring subtle performance differences required substantial sample sizes and rigorous hypothesis testing rather than informal demonstrations ([[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/02 Teaching the Machine/07 Closed-Loop Evaluation/V4-07.15 Source References#^ref-trilbm2025careful|TRI LBM Team et al. 2025]]).

Physical comparisons also invite the pitfall of optional stopping, because engineers monitoring live rollouts face strong temptations to halt testing as soon as a favored checkpoint pulls ahead. Sequential tests designed specifically for policy comparisons, such as STEP, allow an evaluator to stop as soon as evidence is decisive while holding the Type I error strictly at its declared level ([[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/02 Teaching the Machine/07 Closed-Loop Evaluation/V4-07.15 Source References#^ref-snyder2025policy|Snyder et al. 2025]]). They are the comparative counterpart to the single-policy test of [[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/02 Teaching the Machine/07 Closed-Loop Evaluation/V4-07.08 Trial Budgets, Stopping Rules, and Comparisons#^sec-evaluation-sequential-sprt|section 7.7.3]]. In parallel, simulators can rank candidates before committing to physical rollouts only after paired sim-and-real trials have validated their rank correlation ([[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/02 Teaching the Machine/07 Closed-Loop Evaluation/V4-07.05 Evidence Regimes#^sec-evaluation-proxy-transfer-law|section 7.4.2]]). **Checkpoint 7.2: Bounds, budgets, and stopping rules**

A clean record buys a confidence limit, and the claim, not the facility’s schedule, fixes what that limit costs.

**Reading the bound**

**One-sided lower limit**: Explain why the 86.1 percent lower limit from twenty clean runs is a property of the procedure rather than a 95 percent probability that this policy’s success rate exceeds it.

**Failures in the record**: Formulate [[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/02 Teaching the Machine/07 Closed-Loop Evaluation/V4-07.07 Confidence from a Finite Campaign#^eq-eval-bound-with-failures|equation 6]] for a twenty-run campaign with two failures and state which way its limit moves relative to one failure.

**Pricing the claim**

**Trial-budget estimate**: Using the rule of three and the 40 s door cycle, estimate the clean runs and test-cell hours that a 99.5 percent success lower limit at 95 percent confidence needs, then compare the count with [[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/02 Teaching the Machine/07 Closed-Loop Evaluation/V4-07.07 Confidence from a Finite Campaign#^eq-eval-zero-failure-sample-size|equation 5]].

**Sequential vs. fixed sample**: Contrast the guarantees that the sequential test’s 31 clean runs provide against the fixed test’s 29.

**Policy comparison**: Explain why 20 of 20 against 16 of 20 does not separate two checkpoints, and what must be fixed before the first comparison run.

Whether an evaluation campaign qualifies a single policy against a target specification or compares two competing models, the findings become reviewable only when the trial count, stopping rule, and reset-adjusted time budget are established in advance. That empirical outcome must remain explicitly bound to the tested configuration and carried forward as an evaluation record.
