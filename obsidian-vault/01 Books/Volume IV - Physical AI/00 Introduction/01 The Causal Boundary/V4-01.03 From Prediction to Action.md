# V4-01.03 From Prediction to Action

[[Home|Home]] · [[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/Volume 4 Overview|Volume Overview]] · [[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/00 Introduction/01 The Causal Boundary/V4-01 The Causal Boundary|V4-01 The Causal Boundary]]

^sec-boundary-prediction-to-action

The acting line begins with feedback. Wiener’s cybernetics described animals and machines alike as systems that measure the result of their own action and use it to correct the next one ([[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/00 Introduction/01 The Causal Boundary/V4-01.16 Source References#^ref-wiener1948cybernetics|Wiener 1948]]), and control theory soon learned to estimate a moving system’s state from noisy measurements ([[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/00 Introduction/01 The Causal Boundary/V4-01.16 Source References#^ref-kalman1960new|Kalman 1960]]). The first industrial robots needed little of this at the level of the task. In 1961 a Unimate arm began stacking hot die-cast parts at General Motors by replaying step-by-step commands stored on a magnetic drum ([[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/00 Introduction/01 The Causal Boundary/V4-01.16 Source References#^ref-chm1961unimate|Computer History Museum 1961]]). Nothing in that program could notice a person in the arm’s path, so protecting people meant keeping them out of its reach. The machine acted, but it did not perceive, and nothing in it was learned. Robotics and control learning to act and machine learning mastering prediction run as the upper and lower lanes of [[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/00 Introduction/01 The Causal Boundary/V4-01.03 From Prediction to Action#^fig-01-evolution|figure 1]] until they merge.

Shakey, developed at SRI from 1966 through 1972, joined perception to planning ([[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/00 Introduction/01 The Causal Boundary/V4-01.16 Source References#^ref-nilsson1984shakey|Nilsson 1984]]). It took camera images and range-finder readings, kept a model of its surroundings, and used a planner called STRIPS to compose sequences of actions that would achieve a stated goal. This organization, later summarized as sense-plan-act (S·P·A), treats acting as the last step of a computation. Shakey moved slowly, and its designers treated its motion as running asynchronously to the programs directing it ([[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/00 Introduction/01 The Causal Boundary/V4-01.16 Source References#^ref-nilsson1984shakey|Nilsson 1984]]). The world was assumed to wait while the computer thought. Shakey also carried a detail that anticipates this book’s architecture. When one of its catwhiskers touched an obstacle, the robot stopped by a reflex action beneath the planner.

↪ The sense-plan-act paradigm, and the failure taxonomy it organizes, is developed in **[[05 Reference/Volume IV - Physical AI/V4 The Sense–Plan–Act Paradigm#^sec-appendix-spa|The Sense–Plan–Act Paradigm]]**.

Brooks inverted that organization. His subsumption architecture built control from layers of simple, asynchronous behaviors, each coupling sensing to action. Higher layers could suppress the outputs of lower ones, but the lower layers continued to run as higher ones were added ([[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/00 Introduction/01 The Causal Boundary/V4-01.16 Source References#^ref-brooks1986robust|Brooks 1986]]). A fast layer that keeps working while a slow one is busy or wrong is an ancestor of the permission path this book develops. Neither organization, however, made perception or dexterous motion easy to write by hand, and the planners and behavior layers alike stalled on exactly the skills a toddler has. That difficulty has a name. **Moravec’s paradox**[[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/00 Introduction/01 The Causal Boundary/V4-01.17 Footnotes#^fn1|¹]] observes that sensorimotor skills people perform with little conscious effort can be difficult to reproduce computationally, which is why learning became attractive for physical tasks.

Learning had already entered the loop. In 1989 ALVINN, a small neural network, mapped camera and laser range images to the direction a vehicle should steer, and followed real roads under some field conditions ([[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/00 Introduction/01 The Causal Boundary/V4-01.16 Source References#^ref-pomerleau1989alvinn|Pomerleau 1989]]). Through the 1990s robotics also made uncertainty explicit. Probabilistic robotics represents what a machine believes about itself and its surroundings as probability distributions, updated as actions and measurements arrive ([[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/00 Introduction/01 The Causal Boundary/V4-01.16 Source References#^ref-thrun2005probabilistic|Thrun et al. 2005]]). The DARPA Grand Challenge tested these ideas at scale. In the first race, in 2004, no vehicle covered more than a small fraction of the desert course. In 2005 five vehicles finished, and the winner, Stanford’s Stanley, relied predominantly on machine learning and probabilistic reasoning ([[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/00 Introduction/01 The Causal Boundary/V4-01.16 Source References#^ref-thrun2006stanley|Thrun et al. 2006]]). In systems of this kind, learning mostly estimated what the world was like, and hand-written planners and controllers decided what to do. A decade later, the DARPA Robotics Challenge Finals asked robots to work at a simulated disaster site, driving a vehicle, opening a door, turning a valve, and climbing stairs. The organizers deliberately degraded the communications link to the remote human operators, so that each robot had to carry out parts of every task under supervised autonomy rather than direct teleoperation ([[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/00 Introduction/01 The Causal Boundary/V4-01.16 Source References#^ref-krotkov2017darpa|Krotkov et al. 2017]]). Even at that stage, a person remained close to the loop.

↪ ALVINN’s missing recovery examples, and the compounding error they expose, are developed in **[[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/02 Teaching the Machine/05 Physical Data/V4-05.04 Recovery and Compounding Error#^sec-data-curse-dimensionality-recoverability|Recovery and Compounding Error]]**.

Deep learning then changed how much a machine could perceive. In 2012 a deep convolutional network trained on GPUs classified ImageNet photographs with error well below the previous state of the art ([[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/00 Introduction/01 The Causal Boundary/V4-01.16 Source References#^ref-krizhevsky2012imagenet|Krizhevsky et al. 2012]]), and learned detectors spread into the perception systems of robots and automated vehicles. The architecture around them usually stayed modular, with a learned percept feeding a planner written as rules or as an optimization. The learned outputs were still predictions, yet no person stood between them and the brakes. The Tempe vehicle examined in [[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/00 Introduction/01 The Causal Boundary/V4-01.09 When the Responsibilities Fail Together#^sec-boundary-when-fails|section 1.8]] was a machine of this kind.

Learning reached the actuator itself in the following years. Levine and colleagues trained convolutional networks that mapped raw camera images directly to the torques at a robot’s motors, for tasks such as screwing a cap onto a bottle ([[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/00 Introduction/01 The Causal Boundary/V4-01.16 Source References#^ref-levine2016endtoend|Levine et al. 2016]]). Grasping systems learned from hundreds of thousands of their own attempts, gathered by groups of robot arms working in parallel ([[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/00 Introduction/01 The Causal Boundary/V4-01.16 Source References#^ref-levine2018learning|Levine et al. 2018]]; [[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/00 Introduction/01 The Causal Boundary/V4-01.16 Source References#^ref-kalashnikov2018qtopt|Kalashnikov et al. 2018]]). Deep reinforcement learning (training a network by trial, error, and a numerical reward) in simulation produced controllers for a legged robot and a dexterous hand that transferred to hardware ([[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/00 Introduction/01 The Causal Boundary/V4-01.16 Source References#^ref-hwangbo2019learning|Hwangbo et al. 2019]]; [[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/00 Introduction/01 The Causal Boundary/V4-01.16 Source References#^ref-akkaya2019solving|OpenAI et al. 2019]]). With that step the data changed character. A robot that learns from its own attempts chooses the states it will see, so its training data depends on its own behavior.

Imitation learning, which trains a policy to reproduce recorded demonstrations, made human demonstration a practical route to new manipulation skills, and two ideas shaped the policies now in use. Action chunking predicts a short sequence of future actions from each observation, a design its authors motivated partly by the way a cloned policy’s errors compound over time ([[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/00 Introduction/01 The Causal Boundary/V4-01.16 Source References#^ref-zhao2023learning|Zhao et al. 2023]]). Diffusion policies generate action sequences by iterative denoising, which lets one policy represent the several different ways a demonstrator might complete the same task ([[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/00 Introduction/01 The Causal Boundary/V4-01.16 Source References#^ref-chi2024diffusionpolicy|Chi et al. 2025]]). The learned output had become a short plan of future motion, executed by controllers beneath it.

The latest step changed where learned proposals come from. In digital software systems, foundation models matured as agentic reasoning engines capable of decomposing abstract goals, generating plans, and invoking external tools. Bringing that agentic reasoning into physical embodiment required grounding multimodal representations in spatial geometry, continuous dynamics, and motor coordination. Large multitask datasets collected on many robots prepared the ground ([[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/00 Introduction/01 The Causal Boundary/V4-01.16 Source References#^ref-brohan2023rt1|Brohan, Brown, Carbajal, Chebotar, Dabis, et al. 2023]]; [[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/00 Introduction/01 The Causal Boundary/V4-01.16 Source References#^ref-open-x-embodiment-collaboration2024open-c3f8bd04|Open X-Embodiment Collaboration et al. 2024]]), and between 2023 and 2025 several groups showed that a model pretrained on web-scale images and text can be fine-tuned to emit robot actions directly. These **vision-language-action (VLA) models** share one mechanism. A pretrained vision-language backbone encodes the camera images and an instruction, and an action head converts the backbone’s output into motor commands. The head either decodes actions as discrete tokens, the way a language model decodes words ([[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/00 Introduction/01 The Causal Boundary/V4-01.16 Source References#^ref-brohan2023rt2|Brohan, Brown, Carbajal, Chebotar, Chen, et al. 2023]]; [[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/00 Introduction/01 The Causal Boundary/V4-01.16 Source References#^ref-kim2024openvla|Kim et al. 2024]]), or produces a continuous *action chunk*, a short sequence of future setpoints from one inference ([[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/00 Introduction/01 The Causal Boundary/V4-01.16 Source References#^ref-black2024pi0|Black et al. 2024]]). A backbone with billions of parameters cannot run at a controller’s rate, so recent designs split the work across rates. A slow model interprets the scene, and a fast action model or on-robot decoder emits chunks that controllers execute ([[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/00 Introduction/01 The Causal Boundary/V4-01.16 Source References#^ref-bjorck2025gr00t|Bjorck et al. 2025]]; [[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/00 Introduction/01 The Causal Boundary/V4-01.16 Source References#^ref-geminirobotics2025|Gemini Robotics Team 2025]]). These systems are dated instances of one step, in which a pretrained general-purpose backbone, rather than a policy trained for one task on one robot, proposes the motion. The sizes and rates their authors report are collected later in **[[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/01 Machine Anatomy/03 The Cognitive Brain/V4-03.06 Chunk Supply and Evidence Validity#^tbl-03-state-of-practice|State of practice in learned proposers, as of 2026]]**. The running machine adopts the same hierarchy with two proposers: an *intent model*, the slow vision-language model that reads the scene and chooses the goal, and a *chunk policy*, the fast learned policy that emits action chunks toward that goal. Their rates are fixed on its machine card ([[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/00 Introduction/01 The Causal Boundary/V4-01.05 Defining the Causal Boundary#^sec-boundary-running-machine|section 1.4.2]]). Because a general-purpose learned model can now propose motion for a general-purpose machine, deciding which component may *permit* that motion becomes a design question for every machine that adopts one.

^fig-01-compute-by-domain

The scale of that transfer from web-scale pretraining is easy to overstate. In the Epoch AI database, the open robot action policies trained on hundreds to thousands of times less compute than the largest language models of their year ([[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/00 Introduction/01 The Causal Boundary/V4-01.03 From Prediction to Action#^fig-01-compute-by-domain|figure 2]]) ([[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/00 Introduction/01 The Causal Boundary/V4-01.16 Source References#^ref-epochai2026models|Epoch AI 2026]]). The gap is about 350 times less for a 2024 vision-language-action model and about 7,000 times less for a 2025 open robot foundation model, and only 25 robotics models in the database carry a compute estimate at all. Physical AI is therefore not a smaller copy of the language-model scaling story. Its learned proposers are bounded by what can be collected from machines acting in the world and by what can run on the machine itself, two constraints that Parts I and II of this book treat as first-class.

The name *physical AI* is recent, and industry usage since 2024 applies it broadly to almost any AI that perceives and acts in the physical world ([[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/00 Introduction/01 The Causal Boundary/V4-01.16 Source References#^ref-nvidia2026physicalai|NVIDIA 2026]]). The history above runs through two research traditions that cover much of the same ground. **Embodied AI** descends from Brooks’s argument that intelligent behavior arises from a body situated in and interacting with its environment rather than from abstract representation alone ([[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/00 Introduction/01 The Causal Boundary/V4-01.16 Source References#^ref-brooks1991intelligence|Brooks 1991]]). **Cyber-physical systems** name the integration of computation and networking with physical processes ([[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/00 Introduction/01 The Causal Boundary/V4-01.16 Source References#^ref-lee2008cyber|Lee 2008]]), and they carry the timing and verification discipline of embedded engineering ([[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/00 Introduction/01 The Causal Boundary/V4-01.16 Source References#^ref-leeseshia2017embedded|Lee and Seshia 2017]]). Embodied AI supplies much of the learning this book uses, and cyber-physical systems supply much of its timing and verification practice. The book’s subject is where the two meet at an actuator, and the definition given below is deliberately narrower than industry usage, narrow enough that the scope test in [[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/00 Introduction/01 The Causal Boundary/V4-01.07 The Physical AI Scope Test#^sec-boundary-scope-test|section 1.6]] can apply it to a given machine.

Read as one story, this history moves the learned model step by step toward the actuator, as the band beneath the time axis of [[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/00 Introduction/01 The Causal Boundary/V4-01.03 From Prediction to Action#^fig-01-evolution|figure 1]] records. At first nothing was learned. Then learned percepts fed hand-written planners, learned policies emitted motor commands, and finally pretrained general-purpose models began to propose motion for many kinds of machine. At each step, a guarantee that separation or human inspection used to supply had to move inside the machine. The familiar concerns of machine learning systems engineering come along, and each acquires physical stakes. Data stops being a fixed corpus because the machine’s own actions choose what it will observe next. Compute stops being a matter of throughput and cost alone because a late answer is paid for in stopping distance. Evaluation stops being a score on held-out examples because the competence that matters appears only in the closed loop. Deployment stops being a routine version rollout because a release grants a machine authority over motion near people. When a learned output becomes an irreversible action, none of these concerns can be settled inside the model. They belong to the whole machine, and they meet at the boundary where its commands become physical work.
