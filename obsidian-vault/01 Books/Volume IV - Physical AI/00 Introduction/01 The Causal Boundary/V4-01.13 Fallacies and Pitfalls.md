# V4-01.13 Fallacies and Pitfalls

[[Home|Home]] · [[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/Volume 4 Overview|Volume Overview]] · [[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/00 Introduction/01 The Causal Boundary/V4-01 The Causal Boundary|V4-01 The Causal Boundary]]

^sec-boundary-fallacies-pitfalls

The misconceptions that follow arise when assumptions from pure software engineering, statistical learning, or classical control theory are imported into embodied machines. In digital systems, an erroneous state can typically be trapped, rolled back, or restarted. Across the causal boundary, however, software outputs become physical forces that alter the world irrevocably. Preventing physical harm requires hard deterministic guarantees that learning objectives, human operators, and asynchronous recovery routines cannot provide on their own.

**Fallacy**: *Safety constraints can be trained into the policy.*

Engineering teams often attempt to enforce physical safety by adding penalty terms, barrier penalties, or negative rewards to the training objective. While penalty shaping suppresses undesirable actions over the training distribution, it provides no mathematical guarantee on the actions emitted during deployment. Deep neural networks remain uncalibrated function approximators when exposed to out-of-distribution inputs, sensor degradation, or novel environmental dynamics. A statistical loss function optimizes expected performance, but safety requires hard invariants that hold on every control cycle. For this reason, deterministic safety enforcement must sit downstream of the policy, operating as an independent permission path that filters proposed commands before they reach actuator driver registers, exactly where the third law places the boundary gate.

**Pitfall**: *Treating human supervisory intervention as instantaneous in shared-autonomy systems.*

In shared-autonomy designs, such as a robotic surgical tool providing haptic guidance from a learned anatomical model, system designers sometimes treat the human operator as a failsafe catch-all. This assumption ignores the physiological limits of human reaction time, which ranges between 150 ms and 250 ms for simple tactile or visual reflexes and grows under cognitive load. Once console video and a handshake are added, the running machine’s card budgets an in-loop takeover at 605 ms. Over that interval, an actuator running on a 1 kHz control loop can execute hundreds of unchecked cycles, generating enough force to tear soft tissue or damage a mechanical workpiece before the operator can intervene. Delegating authority to a human supervisor does not eliminate the machine’s capacity for harm during the supervisory lag. The permission path must enforce local force, velocity, and displacement envelopes autonomously, preserving the established physical envelopes under their operating assumptions without relying on timely human overrides.

**Pitfall**: *Assuming software latency merely degrades performance gracefully.*

In purely digital systems, inference latency or scheduler jitter increases response time or degrades service quality. In physical systems, timing is a functional correctness requirement. If a learned policy stalls, whether from accelerator kernel contention, memory allocation pauses, or network serialization delays, allowing its previous command to persist across subsequent control cycles turns stale intent into physical danger. For example, a mobile manipulator that repeats its last forward-velocity command during a 100 ms inference hiccup can collide with an unmapped obstacle. Actuation commands must therefore carry bounded temporal validity, enforced by hardware or real-time software watchdogs. When that validity window (the chunk lease of the stopping budget) expires without a verified update, the low-level controller must begin a fallback within its established response budget. The selected stop or hold must remain feasible for the current mechanism, state, and surviving resources.

**Fallacy**: *Assuming physical actuation is reversible through subsequent software compensation.*

Transactional software architectures rely on rollbacks and compensation routines to undo erroneous operations. In embodied systems, thermodynamics and materials science break this equivalence. If a robotic arm applies excessive torque to a workpiece, issuing a reverse motor command cannot unbend plastically deformed metal, repair fractured composite, or recover the electrical energy dissipated as heat in the motor windings (${I_{\text{phase}}}^2 {R_{\text{phase}}}$). Anomaly detection routines that react after physical contact can only truncate ongoing damage; they cannot undo the mechanical work already transferred across the causal boundary. Preventive verification, such as checking proposed trajectories against workspace boundaries, torque limits, and kinetic energy caps before dispatch, must serve as the primary defense. Reactive recovery strategies remain necessary for unexpected disturbances, but they function as a secondary backstop rather than a substitute for preventive enforcement.
