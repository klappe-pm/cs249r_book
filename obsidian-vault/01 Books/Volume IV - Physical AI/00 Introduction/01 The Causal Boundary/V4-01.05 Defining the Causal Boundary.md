# V4-01.05 Defining the Causal Boundary

[[Home|Home]] · [[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/Volume 4 Overview|Volume Overview]] · [[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/00 Introduction/01 The Causal Boundary/V4-01 The Causal Boundary|V4-01 The Causal Boundary]]

^sec-boundary-causal-boundary

That difference between a proposed action and its physical effect separates advisory computing from physical AI. An advisory model that renders an inference on a dashboard or returns a JSON payload across a network has no direct electrical or mechanical coupling to physical actuators: an operator or downstream software filter can inspect, validate, or discard its output before any physical energy is expended. Digital errors are not benign (an operator who acts on flawed guidance can cause serious damage), but advisory software cannot directly alter physical state. Once an automated system acquires the delegated authority of definition [1.1](https://mlsysbook.ai/vol4/01_boundary/01_boundary.html#dfn-boundary-physical-artificial-intelligence-physical-ai) to turn a computed proposal directly into physical motion, safety arbitration cannot remain an external afterthought. It must live inside the machine architecture, at the causal boundary between digital intent and physical consequence.

### From a Proposed Action to Motion

^sec-boundary-tensors-to-silicon

In modern computational frameworks, executing `action_chunk = policy(observation)` produces a floating-point tensor. That tensor represents an uncommitted proposal; converting it into physical motion requires an entire chain of intermediate controllers, bus interfaces, and power electronics. The causal boundary is the exact interface through which an admitted digital command begins driving physical change in the plant.

A learned policy typically proposes a trajectory of future joint positions, target velocities, or motor torques. Those numerical arrays remain purely informational descriptions until a low-level controller validates, accepts, and begins executing them. The internal representation and predictive horizon of such action sequences are developed in **[[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/01 Machine Anatomy/03 The Cognitive Brain/V4-03.06 Chunk Supply and Evidence Validity#^sec-brain-action-chunking|Action chunking]]**; here the systems distinction is between proposing an action and authorizing its irreversible consequences.

That authorization is governed by the permission path, an independent mechanism defined in [[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/00 Introduction/01 The Causal Boundary/V4-01.08 The Machine in Five Levels#^sec-boundary-machine-model|section 1.7]] that alone holds the authority to pass commands to motor drives. The permission path verifies whether a proposed trajectory satisfies physical kinematic constraints, obeys velocity and acceleration bounds, and can complete safely within current time allowances. Once admitted, trajectory setpoints feed into dedicated motor controllers. A position loop compares each setpoint with the measured joint position and computes a correction, and a current controller sets the motor current that produces the torque the correction needs.

↪ The bus timing and jitter bounds on command transport from the permission path to the drives are developed in **[[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/01 Machine Anatomy/04 The Nervous System/V4-04.06 Real-Time Command Transport and Bus Determinism#^sec-nervous-deterministic-fieldbuses|Real-Time Command Transport and Bus Determinism]]**.

Consider an electric motor driven by pulse-width modulation. A software write updates a compare register in a microcontroller timer, which sets how long each power switch stays on in every switching period. The resulting on-off pattern drives the inverter’s transistor switches, which turn the DC supply into current in the stator windings (the stationary coils). That current sets up a magnetic field that pulls on the magnets of the rotor (the spinning part) to produce torque, which accelerates the rotor shaft, transmission gears, and mechanical payload. The progression is asymmetric. Rewriting a planned setpoint in memory requires negligible energy and can be undone before the next clock cycle, but modifying a command that has already energized a winding requires physical work performed against mechanical inertia over finite time. A subsequent command can apply brakes or reverse drive current, but that corrective maneuver requires its own physical distance and must obey strict actuator limits.

**Definition 1.2: Causal boundary**

***Causal boundary*** is the interface through which a digital command produces physical forces, currents, or flows.

1.  **Significance**: A proposal can be rejected before it changes the plant. Once executed, it can alter the environment, dissipate energy, and change subsequent observations.
2.  **Distinction**: Restoring a command in memory does not restore the machine’s prior physical state. Recovery requires another physical action, with its own limits and consequences.
3.  **Common pitfall**: Assuming that canceling a software task stops the motion it initiated. The machine needs a feasible stopping or holding response even when its proposer stops computing.

Offline supervised learning evaluates models against static datasets where predictions never influence future test inputs. An embodied robot cannot rely on this decoupling. When the mobile base steers its chassis, its onboard cameras immediately capture a different visual field. When its manipulator nudges a carton, the object’s spatial coordinates and contact forces change. When the vehicle cruises along an aisle, its closing distance to personnel shifts in real time. The closed-loop feedback introduced in [[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/00 Introduction/01 The Causal Boundary/V4-01.03 From Prediction to Action#^sec-boundary-prediction-to-action|section 1.2]] applies directly to the learned model, because an acting machine reshapes the very environment that produces its future training and inference inputs.

In any embodied system, the environment evolves through closed-loop dynamics: the subsequent physical state ${s}_{t+1}$ and incoming sensory observation ${\mathbf{o}}_{t+1}$ depend directly on the action ${\mathbf{a}}_{t}$ issued by the policy inference:

$$
{s}_{t+1} = {f}({s}_{t}, {\mathbf{a}}_{t}), \quad {\mathbf{o}}_{t+1} \sim {p}({\mathbf{o}} \mid {s}_{t+1})
$$

 Here ${f}$ represents the physical plant and environmental dynamics (formulated without process noise for this introductory overview), and ${p}$ denotes the observation model transforming underlying physical states into discrete sensor measurements. Because each state transition feeds the next observation, a flawed action proposal can steer the machine into configurations its training data never covered. In those out-of-distribution states the model’s outputs degrade further, and the machine departs faster from its nominal trajectory. Advisory output stops at an informational interface. In physical AI the loop closes through the permission path, the motor actuators, and the plant, so the model’s own actions irreversibly reshape the observations ${\mathbf{o}}_{t}$ it receives next ([[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/00 Introduction/01 The Causal Boundary/V4-01.05 Defining the Causal Boundary#^fig-01-causal-loop|figure 3]]).[[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/00 Introduction/01 The Causal Boundary/V4-01.17 Footnotes#^fn2|²]] Imitation learning formalizes this compounding divergence as **covariate shift**. Whereas independent errors in open-loop prediction simply add up across the task horizon ${S}$ (the number of control steps the task lasts), a single mistake in closed-loop execution can push the machine into an unfamiliar state that causes elevated error rates across every remaining step of the rollout, so the expected cost of mistakes can grow quadratically with the horizon, ${\mathcal{O}(S^2 \epsilon)}$, rather than linearly ([[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/00 Introduction/01 The Causal Boundary/V4-01.16 Source References#^ref-ross2011reduction|Ross et al. 2011]]). **[[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/02 Teaching the Machine/05 Physical Data/V4-05.03 Mechanics of Endogenous Covariate Shift#^sec-data-covariate-shift|Mechanics of Endogenous Covariate Shift]]** develops the mechanics of this endogenous covariate shift. Contractive feedback controllers (stabilizing low-level tracking loops that pull perturbed states back toward the reference trajectory) or well-damped passive mechanics can absorb minor trajectory errors, although verifying behavior over long horizons still requires closed-loop evaluation rather than static offline benchmark scoring.

↪ Corrective aggregation and the intervention data that defend a policy against covariate shift are developed in **[[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/02 Teaching the Machine/05 Physical Data/V4-05.09 Intervention and Recovery Data#^sec-data-intervention-recovery|Intervention and Recovery Data]]**.

The first margin to price is stopping clearance, and four rows of the card, the drive limit, the credible deceleration, the clear distance, and the pre-brake delay, are enough to price it.

### Delay Consumes Stopping Clearance

^sec-boundary-kinematic-roofline

When the loaded warehouse base cruises down an aisle at speed ${v}$ and a worker steps out from behind a rack, stopping requires clearance across two distinct phases. The first is the distance the base travels before braking forces engage. The second is the distance it travels while mechanical braking brings its momentum to zero. Assuming steady initial velocity ${v}$, uniform deceleration ${a_{\text{brake}}}$ once brakes engage, and a stationary obstacle, the elementary stopping distance satisfies the kinematic budget:

$$
{d_{\text{stop}}} = \underbrace{{v} {\tau_{\text{delay}}}}_{\text{travel during response delay}} + \underbrace{\frac{{v}^2}{2 {a_{\text{brake}}}}}_{\text{mechanical dissipation}} \le {D_{\text{clear}}} \tag{1}
$$

 Here ${v}$ denotes initial forward speed, ${\tau_{\text{delay}}}$ represents total pre-brake response latency, ${a_{\text{brake}}}$ is the sustained braking deceleration, and ${D_{\text{clear}}}$ is the usable line-of-sight clearance. The pre-brake delay ${\tau_{\text{delay}}}$ spans the time from when an obstacle first appears in a sensor exposure to the physical onset of retarding force. Specifically, it sums the age of the sensor observation upon handoff and the unexpired lease duration of any currently executing motion chunk (the lease is the longest an admitted chunk may drive before the permission path must act again). It also adds one discrete permission-path evaluation tick (the period at which the permission path re-checks the motion it has admitted), one bus communication cycle (the time to carry a command to the motor drives), and mechanical brake onset latency.

^eq-boundary-stopping-budget

The lease belongs in the worst case even though the permission path re-checks every tick, because of what that check can see. Each tick tests the admitted chunk against limits the permission path already holds (the chunk’s freshness, its lease, and the kinematic bounds, measured against the wheel and joint encoders). The permission path has no camera pipeline of its own. On the detection path this budget models, evidence that a worker has stepped out therefore reaches the brakes only through the learned perception path and a replacement proposal. If the proposer stalls or fails to answer the worker, the most recently admitted chunk keeps driving until its lease expires, and only then does the permission path command the stop. The term deliberately excludes one interval, the dispatch-to-admission interval (the time while the learned policy computes a replacement proposal and the permission path admits it). Its length depends on where the proposer and the permission path run and how they communicate, which are decisions this chapter has not yet made. **[[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/03 Running the Machine/13 Workload Placement/V4-13.07 Silicon Isolation Decisions#^sec-placement-dispatch-admission|The board-link budget and the dispatch-to-admission interval]]** therefore allocates it, and the finished budget pays for it from the spare this elementary budget leaves.

The budget has two terms that grow differently with speed: travel during the delay grows linearly (${v} {\tau_{\text{delay}}}$), and travel while shedding kinetic energy grows with the square of speed (${v}^2 / (2 {a_{\text{brake}}})$). Equating them gives the crossover speed ${v^{*}} = 2 {a_{\text{brake}}} {\tau_{\text{delay}}}$. Below ${v^{*}}$, delay travel is the larger term; above it, braking travel dominates. Shortening sensing, computation, and signaling reduces the delay term, while lowering speed reduces both terms: halving speed halves the delay travel and quarters the braking travel. Which change saves more clearance depends on the reductions available, not on term dominance alone. Solving [[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/00 Introduction/01 The Causal Boundary/V4-01.05 Defining the Causal Boundary#^eq-boundary-stopping-budget|equation 1]] for ${v}$ gives the fastest speed whose stop fits inside a verified clear distance, a ceiling **[[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/01 Machine Anatomy/02 The Physical Body/V4-02.04 Kinetic Momentum#^sec-body-dynamic-velocity-ceilings|Dynamic velocity ceilings and clearance budgeting]]** derives with the allowances this elementary form omits.

Every input to the budget comes from the running machine. Its speed, deceleration, clear distance, and pre-brake delay are rows of the machine card ([[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/00 Introduction/01 The Causal Boundary/V4-01.05 Defining the Causal Boundary#^tbl-01-machine-card|table 2]]), and the five terms of that delay come from the same MLSysIM registry, so the notebook’s givens are the ones every later chapter refines.

**Napkin Math 1.1: Does the base stop inside its clearance?**

**Problem**: The loaded base cruises toward a rack end at its drive limit, and a worker may step out at the end of the rack. The permission path must stop the base inside the rack-end clear distance on the machine card. Does the elementary stopping budget of [[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/00 Introduction/01 The Causal Boundary/V4-01.05 Defining the Causal Boundary#^eq-boundary-stopping-budget|equation 1]] fit, which term dominates, and how much of the delay belongs to the learned model?

**Variables**:

- Speed ${v} =$ 1.5 m/s, the drive limit (illustrative).
- Credible deceleration ${a_{\text{brake}}} =$ 2 m/s², loaded on a dry floor (illustrative).
- Clear distance ${D_{\text{clear}}} =$ 1.10 m (illustrative).
- Pre-brake delay ${\tau_{\text{delay}}} =$ 133.6 ms (derived). It is the sum of five registry terms. The first is the observation age of the camera frame that first shows the worker, 51.6 ms, which includes the 22 ms perception backbone (the rest is half the exposure, sensor readout, transfer, image processing, and the handoff to the proposer, itemized in **[[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/03 Running the Machine/08 Sensor Perception/V4-08.05 Bounding Observation Age#^sec-perception-latency-waterfall|The latency waterfall]]**). The second is the chunk lease of 60 ms, the longest an admitted command may drive before the permission path must act. The remaining terms are one permission tick of 1 ms, one bus cycle of 1 ms, and a brake onset of 20 ms. It excludes the dispatch-to-admission interval, for the reason given with [[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/00 Introduction/01 The Causal Boundary/V4-01.05 Defining the Causal Boundary#^eq-boundary-stopping-budget|equation 1]].

**Math**:

$$
\tau_{\text{delay}} = 51.6 + 60 + 1 + 1 + 20 = 133.6\ \text{ms}
$$

$$
v\,\tau_{\text{delay}} = 1.5\ \text{m/s} \times 0.1336\ \text{s} = 200.4\ \text{mm}
$$

$$
\frac{v^2}{2 a_{\text{brake}}} = \frac{(1.5\ \text{m/s})^2}{2 \times 2\ \text{m/s}^2} = 562.5\ \text{mm}
$$

$$
d_{\text{stop}} = 200.4 + 562.5 = 762.9\ \text{mm} \le D_{\text{clear}} = 1100\ \text{mm}, \quad D_{\text{clear}} - d_{\text{stop}} = 337.1\ \text{mm}
$$

$$
v^{*} = 2 a_{\text{brake}} \tau_{\text{delay}} = 2 \times 2\ \text{m/s}^2 \times 0.1336\ \text{s} \approx 0.53\ \text{m/s}
$$

**Result**: The elementary budget fits, with 337.1 mm to spare. The drive limit sits above the crossover speed, so braking dominates: it needs 2.8× the travel of the delay, and the delay adds 35.6 percent to the braking distance. At half speed, 0.75 m/s, delay travel halves to 100.2 mm while braking quarters to 140.6 mm, for a stop of 240.8 mm.

**Systems insight**: The learned model’s backbone accounts for 22 ms of the delay, or 33 mm of travel; the chunk lease alone costs 90 mm. Each added millisecond of delay anywhere in the chain costs 1.5 mm at this speed, so a slower but more accurate model is a distance cost the budget must absorb. The spare is not yet a safety margin. **[[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/03 Running the Machine/12 Safety Enforcement/V4-12.04 Stopping Envelopes#^sec-enforcement-composite-budget|Sizing the composite stopping budget]]** adds the localization bound, a protective margin, tracking error, and the longer stop of a jerk-limited brake (one that limits jerk, the rate of change of deceleration, by ramping its braking up instead of stepping it). Together they exceed the clear distance at the drive limit, which is why the card sets the aisle speed at 1.3 m/s.

The running machine’s drive limit lies well above the crossover speed the notebook computed ([[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/00 Introduction/01 The Causal Boundary/V4-01.05 Defining the Causal Boundary#^fig-01-kinematic-roofline|figure 4]]), so braking, not delay, sets most of its stop, and the sum still clears the dashed clear-distance line before any later allowance is added. These values are representative parameters and a derived latency profile, not measured stopping distances for a real chassis, but they already show the two levers a designer holds. Shortening response latency contracts the linear term, whereas lowering cruising speed contracts both, the braking term quadratically. Selecting a model purely on offline accuracy overlooks the first lever, because added inference latency is added travel.

The budget just computed models one particular detection path, and naming it shows which learned computation it charges. The worker is seen by the learned perception stack, so the perception backbone sits inside the observation age. The chunk policy’s own compute time does not appear, because the lease, not the policy, bounds how long an admitted chunk may keep driving once the proposer falls silent. A machine can also carry a second, independent protective path with its own sensing, timing, power, and fallback logic, such as a certified safety scanner wired to the drives. A stop that path triggers bypasses perception dispatch and the lease entirely. The backbone and lease terms leave its delay, and its own sensing and switching latency take their place, while the braking travel and the allowances stay. Under that decoupled architecture, the protective path’s latency, rather than the execution time of the neural network, governs the protective stop. Industrial automated guided vehicles (AGVs) implement this separation today, with safety standards mandating certified safety scanners that halt the machine before human contact, independent of fleet management or guidance computers ([[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/00 Introduction/01 The Causal Boundary/V4-01.16 Source References#^ref-iso3691part4|International Organization for Standardization 2023]]). This book layers learned sensorimotor proposers atop deterministic safety kernels and analyzes the architectural contracts required between them.

↪ The scanner path’s own timing budget, and how the software stopping budget maps onto certified safety functions, are developed in **[[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/03 Running the Machine/12 Safety Enforcement/V4-12.10 The Fallback Ladder#^sec-enforcement-certified-functions|Certified safety functions beneath the permission path]]**.

Deploying this budget in real-world facilities requires several additional physical allowances. Beyond the allowances the notebook’s systems insight names (localization, a protective margin, tracking error, and the longer jerk-limited stop), real-world controllers must also account for unmodeled initial acceleration and degraded tire-floor traction. Dynamic obstacles complicate the clearance budget even further: if a pedestrian walks toward the mobile robot at speed ${v_h}$, the relative closing distance contracts during both the pre-brake delay and the mechanical deceleration phase. For this reason, functional safety standards mandate adding a pedestrian approach-speed allowance to the vehicle’s own braking distance ([[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/00 Introduction/01 The Causal Boundary/V4-01.16 Source References#^ref-iso13855|International Organization for Standardization 2024]]). Later chapters formalize these dynamic constraints and allocate delay budgets across compute hardware. Even before they do, a learned policy’s behavior is acceptable only inside the real-time envelope that plant momentum sets.

### Irreversibility and Recovery

^sec-boundary-thermodynamic-irreversibility

The stopping budget prices one consequence of irreversibility. The other is what recovery becomes. A database transaction that aborts restores the state it started from, but a gripper that has permanently deformed a workholding fixture, or a winding whose insulation has degraded from overheating, has no earlier state to return to. Recovery on a physical machine is therefore a new trajectory, not a reversal.

Corrective commands can brake the motors, release a grasp, or plan a diversion, but each is itself motion, limited by the torque the actuators can deliver, by friction, and by the speeds and accelerations the drives can reach. Which limit governs depends on what the machine is doing. For the mobile base it is stopping clearance under momentum. When the arm closes its jaws on a fragile component or braces against an assembly fixture, it is contact force, the flex of the gears and belts between motor and gripper (transmission compliance), and heat in the windings. Because recovery is constrained motion, the permission path must check before it admits an action that a feasible recovery from the resulting state will still exist; checking afterward is too late.

↪ The contact loads a transmission delivers to a gripped part or a latch, the arm’s limiting quantity here, are developed in **[[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/01 Machine Anatomy/02 The Physical Body/V4-02.05 Actuator Transmission Limits#^sec-body-transmission-compliance|Transmission compliance, contact impedance, and tooth fracture]]**.
