# V4-01.09 When the Responsibilities Fail Together

[[Home|Home]] · [[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/Volume 4 Overview|Volume Overview]] · [[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/00 Introduction/01 The Causal Boundary/V4-01 The Causal Boundary|V4-01 The Causal Boundary]]

^sec-boundary-when-fails

A warehouse model that recognizes an obstacle too late cannot recover the clearance already consumed. In a road vehicle, the same timing problem can combine with tracking errors, delayed intervention, and missing redundancy. The 2018 Tempe collision provides a documented example of these interacting failures; it does not establish that one prescribed hardware architecture would have prevented them. The National Transportation Safety Board (NTSB) attributed the crash primarily to the vehicle operator’s distraction. The design choices traced below explain why the automated system had made that single, unmonitored person the last line of defense.

On a March night in 2018, a modified Volvo XC90 operating Uber Advanced Technologies Group’s developmental automated driving system (ADS) struck and fatally injured a pedestrian pushing a bicycle across a road in Tempe, Arizona ([[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/00 Introduction/01 The Causal Boundary/V4-01.16 Source References#^ref-ntsb2019uber|National Transportation Safety Board 2019]]).[[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/00 Introduction/01 The Causal Boundary/V4-01.17 Footnotes#^fn4|⁴]] The vehicle carried a roof-mounted lidar, forward and side radars, surround cameras, and GPS antennas, all routed to ADS compute and data-storage racks in the cargo bay ([[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/00 Introduction/01 The Causal Boundary/V4-01.09 When the Responsibilities Fail Together#^fig-01-tempe-vehicle|figure 8]]). The ADS first detected the pedestrian 5.6 s before impact, but detection alone did not provide a correct path prediction ([[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/00 Introduction/01 The Causal Boundary/V4-01.16 Source References#^ref-ntsb2019uber|National Transportation Safety Board 2019]]). The failure ran through perception and path prediction, a response rule that withheld braking, and a human operator the design relied on as its last countermeasure.

### Tracking History Loss Under Classification Churn

^sec-boundary-tracking-churn

Despite continuing to track the detected object, the ADS did not brake in response to it because its classification kept shifting among vehicle, bicycle, and other before ${T}-1.2\text{ s}$ ([[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/00 Introduction/01 The Causal Boundary/V4-01.16 Source References#^ref-ntsb2019uber|National Transportation Safety Board 2019]]). Whenever the classification changed, the ADS path predictor did not use the object’s earlier locations, and for an “other” object it could assume a static path.[[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/00 Introduction/01 The Causal Boundary/V4-01.17 Footnotes#^fn5|⁵]] The loss of earlier locations matters because successive positions provide evidence of motion across the roadway; a changing semantic label must not silently discard that evidence. Because each relabeling shortened the track, the resulting path predictions did not place the pedestrian in the SUV’s lane until ${T}-1.2\text{ s}$ ([[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/00 Introduction/01 The Causal Boundary/V4-01.16 Source References#^ref-ntsb2019uber|National Transportation Safety Board 2019]]).

### Action Suppression and Lost Stopping Distance

^sec-boundary-action-suppression

The ADS included an action-suppression interval intended to reduce responses to false alarms while the system checked the situation and allowed operator intervention ([[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/00 Introduction/01 The Causal Boundary/V4-01.16 Source References#^ref-ntsb2019uber|National Transportation Safety Board 2019]]). That software choice directly consumed time available for a physical response: at ${T}-1.2\text{ s}$, with the vehicle traveling at 43.2 mph, the ADS recognized an emergency and entered its one-second action-suppression period with braking withheld. At ${T}-0.2\text{ s}$, suppression ended with the vehicle at 40.5 mph. The ADS then initiated a plan for gradual slowdown and sounded an alert; it did not request emergency braking. The NTSB notes that communication delay leaves unclear whether that slowdown began before the operator disengaged the ADS at ${T}-0.02\text{ s}$ ([[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/00 Introduction/01 The Causal Boundary/V4-01.16 Source References#^ref-ntsb2019uber|National Transportation Safety Board 2019]]).

*Recognition came seconds after detection; suppression then spent 18.7 m with the brake withheld.*

The first term of the warehouse stopping budget applies directly. Averaging the two recorded speeds over the 1 s of suppression, the vehicle traveled about 18.7 m while emergency braking was withheld, distance that no later action could recover. The speed still fell during that second because a slowdown for the planned right turn at the next intersection was already under way; what the suppression withheld was the emergency response ([[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/00 Introduction/01 The Causal Boundary/V4-01.16 Source References#^ref-ntsb2019uber|National Transportation Safety Board 2019]]). The emergency itself was defined by a chosen limit. The ADS treated a situation as an emergency when avoiding a collision required more than 7 m/s² (0.71 g) of deceleration or a jerk[[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/00 Introduction/01 The Causal Boundary/V4-01.17 Footnotes#^fn6|⁶]] beyond its limit, and it capped its own automated braking at that deceleration. Police determined that the vehicle could decelerate at an average of 0.92 g, about 9.0 m/s², on that road surface ([[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/00 Introduction/01 The Causal Boundary/V4-01.16 Source References#^ref-ntsb2019uber|National Transportation Safety Board 2019]]). The difference between a chosen permission limit and a physical one is the distinction the book’s numbers keep throughout. The calculation stops short of a counterfactual stop, because the report does not establish when a different design would have begun braking. Detection, correct path prediction, hazard recognition, and physical braking are distinct events; reporting the first detection time alone conceals the failures between them.

**War Story 1.1: The disabled safety redundancy (2018)**

**Context**: On March 18, 2018, a developmental ADS on a 2017 Volvo XC90 struck and killed a pedestrian crossing a road in Tempe, Arizona ([[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/00 Introduction/01 The Causal Boundary/V4-01.16 Source References#^ref-ntsb2019uber|National Transportation Safety Board 2019]]).

**Mechanism**: As traced above, classification changes discarded the object’s tracking history, and the one-second suppression withheld braking until only a gradual slowdown was planned. The design did not use maximum braking solely to mitigate an unavoidable collision ([[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/00 Introduction/01 The Causal Boundary/V4-01.16 Source References#^ref-ntsb2019uber|National Transportation Safety Board 2019]]).

**Impact**: The vehicle struck and fatally injured the pedestrian. The late gradual-slowdown plan could not recover the lost stopping opportunity.

**Response**: The NTSB determined that the probable cause was the vehicle operator’s failure to monitor the driving environment and the ADS because she was visually distracted throughout the trip by her personal cell phone. It found three contributing factors at Uber ATG, all a consequence of an inadequate safety culture: inadequate safety risk assessment procedures, ineffective oversight of vehicle operators, and a lack of adequate mechanisms for addressing operators’ automation complacency. The impaired pedestrian’s crossing outside a crosswalk and the Arizona Department of Transportation’s insufficient oversight of automated vehicle testing also contributed. The NTSB further found that deactivating the Volvo collision warning and automatic emergency braking, which Uber ATG had done over possible radar interference, removed a layer of safety redundancy, and it recommended stronger safety risk management for testing automated vehicles ([[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/00 Introduction/01 The Causal Boundary/V4-01.16 Source References#^ref-ntsb2019uber|National Transportation Safety Board 2019]]).

**Systems lesson**: Classification uncertainty, a delayed response rule, and reliance on an inattentive operator can combine. The design made the operator the primary countermeasure in an emergency, yet no mechanism monitored her attention in real time: authority rested on evidence nobody collected. A design needs timely hazard assessment and a response that can mitigate impact when avoidance is no longer possible; the investigation does not prescribe a particular hardware architecture.

The investigation supports a broader architectural requirement. A developmental ADS needs safety redundancy and a timely mitigation path even when perception or path prediction fails, and simulation tests by Volvo that the NTSB cites suggest that the factory emergency braking might have prevented or mitigated this collision. The report does not establish when a separate permission path would have triggered or where it would have stopped the vehicle.[[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/00 Introduction/01 The Causal Boundary/V4-01.17 Footnotes#^fn7|⁷]]

Tempe illustrates failure *before* impact, where classification changes and a software suppression delay prevented timely braking. The 2023 Cruise robotaxi incident marks the opposite pole *after* impact. A hit-and-run driver’s car threw a pedestrian into the path of the driverless vehicle, which braked hard but struck her and stopped. It then began a pullover toward a minimal-risk condition[[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/00 Introduction/01 The Causal Boundary/V4-01.17 Footnotes#^fn8|⁸]] and, because its sensing did not detect a person beneath the vehicle, moved about 20 ft (6.1 m) at up to 7.7 mph while she was pinned beneath it. This account comes from a review commissioned by the company’s boards, whose focus was the company’s disclosures to regulators ([[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/00 Introduction/01 The Causal Boundary/V4-01.16 Source References#^ref-quinnemanuel2024cruise|Quinn Emanuel Urquhart and Sullivan, LLP 2024]]).

↪ The Cruise pullover, and the fallback rule that forbids renewed motion while the space beneath the vehicle is unverified, are developed in **[[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/03 Running the Machine/12 Safety Enforcement/V4-12.10 The Fallback Ladder#^sec-enforcement-four-rungs|The four rungs of the fallback ladder]]**.

Neither failure reduces to an inaccurate model. The first spent stopping distance before braking began, and no later action could recover it. The second chose motion after the impact without fresh evidence about the space it moved through. Together they show what must hold before the machine acts and what a fallback may do once harm has begun. Both also had to be reconstructed afterward from records of what the machine perceived and when, so accountability, like safety, depends on the path that authorizes motion.

↪ Who answered for the Cruise collision, and the decisions behind an admitted action that no per-tick log records, are developed in **[[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/04 Governing the Machine/17 Responsible Physical AI/V4-17.02 Who Answered for the Cruise Collision#^sec-responsibility-who-answered|Who Answered for the Cruise Collision]]**.

**Checkpoint 1.2: Forensics of boundary and authority failures**

Verify how the Tempe mechanisms connect to the architecture:

**Classification changes and path prediction**: Explain how classification changes caused the ADS path predictor to omit earlier object locations, despite continued tracking.

**Software suppression vs. kinematic clearance**: Distinguish the ${T}-1.2\text{ s}$ recognition time from the one-second suppression period, and explain why the gradual-slowdown plan at ${T}-0.2\text{ s}$ could not restore the time already spent.

**Safety redundancy**: Explain why disabling factory collision warning and emergency braking removed a layer of protection from a developmental ADS.
