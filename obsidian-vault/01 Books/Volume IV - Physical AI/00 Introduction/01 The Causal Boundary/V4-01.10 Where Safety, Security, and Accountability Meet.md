# V4-01.10 Where Safety, Security, and Accountability Meet

[[Home|Home]] · [[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/Volume 4 Overview|Volume Overview]] · [[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/00 Introduction/01 The Causal Boundary/V4-01 The Causal Boundary|V4-01 The Causal Boundary]]

^sec-boundary-safety-convergence

Tempe and Cruise both turned on what the machine knew when it moved or failed to brake, and both had to be reconstructed afterward from what it had recorded. The same three questions (whether the evidence behind a motion was sound, whether anything could refuse it, and whether the decision can be traced) also describe an attack and an audit. In enterprise software and distributed systems, safety, security, and accountability usually live in separate tiers. Security defends network boundaries with cryptographic tokens, reliability frameworks mitigate component failures through retries and circuit breakers, and accountability records transactions to append-only databases. In physical AI, all three converge on the causal boundary, the interface where software commands meet physical actuators. Consider the running machine crossing a warehouse aisle. It needs a valid stopping decision whether its forward cameras are obscured by dirt, an attacker has injected spoofed packets onto its internal Controller Area Network (CAN) bus, or a vision-language-action policy has hallucinated an unobstructed path. In each case the hazard is kinetic, a collision with racking or a worker. Because momentum cannot be rolled back like a failed database transaction, admission control cannot simply confirm who dispatched a command. It must determine whether executing that command now would violate the machine’s physical limits.

An ordinary environmental change challenges this boundary as directly as an attack. The running machine’s credible deceleration of 2 m/s² rests on the premise, stated on its card, that the floor supplies a friction coefficient of at least 0.204. When the floor cannot supply the friction the brakes ask for, traction rather than the brakes caps deceleration, and braking distance grows in inverse proportion to the available friction. A film of spilled hydraulic fluid can drop tire-floor friction from about $0.60$ on the aisle’s dry concrete to $0.05$ (an illustrative pair). A vehicle that brakes at the traction limit would see its braking distance grow by a factor of 12. The running machine asks far less of a dry floor than it supplies, but $0.05$ falls below its premise. Its credible deceleration therefore no longer holds, and every clearance envelope and speed profile computed from that deceleration a moment earlier is invalid. The learned planner, processing camera streams that do not reveal the transparent slick, may continue to output an aggressive velocity command with high nominal confidence. Safety verification cannot treat the model’s internal confidence as proof of physical feasibility. The machine needs independent, real-time evidence from the plant and its surroundings to detect the divergence. Such evidence includes wheel-slip telemetry, disagreement between commanded and inertial-measurement-unit (IMU) acceleration, and motor current transients. The machine can then restrict motion using a response feasible under the updated traction estimate. If the machine has already consumed the clearance that response needs, detection can support harm mitigation but cannot establish collision avoidance.

Security threats target this same permission machinery by corrupting the evidence used to authorize motion. An adversary does not need to crack cryptographic keys or modify model weights to destabilize a physical agent; distorting the sensory inputs that inform admission decisions is sufficient. Projecting structured light patterns onto camera lenses, spoofing ultra-wideband (UWB) localization beacons, or injecting rogue packets into sensor buses can deceive the controller into approving destructive commands, even while the neural network executes its inference pipeline without numerical fault. Isolating the proposer from the permission path offers little defense if both subsystems ingest the same corrupted telemetry. Securing the causal boundary therefore requires validating the evidence itself. The validation consists of cross-checking heterogeneous sensor streams (for example, corroborating camera-based visual odometry against wheel encoders and inertial sensors), enforcing cryptographic message authentication across bus transceivers, and maintaining fail-operational plausibility checks that reject sensor readings deviating from physical conservation laws.

↪ A well-formed packet that carries a false measurement becomes a fault trial in the injection campaign developed in **[[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/04 Governing the Machine/15 Adversarial Verification/V4-15.07 Deriving the Fault List#^sec-verification-transduction-attacks|Physical sensor spoofing and proposer injection]]**.

In digital systems, a cryptographic capability token or access control list entry grants authority to modify a file regardless of processor load or ambient conditions. In physical AI, **physical authority is state-dependent**. Cryptographic credentials establish identity and organizational rights to request an action, but physical admissibility depends entirely on the instantaneous operational state of the machine and its workspace. A joint velocity command of 2 rad/s may represent a routine transit when an industrial manipulator operates in open free space, yet that identical command becomes unacceptable if the arm is within 2 mm of a mechanical hard stop or a human coworker. Evaluating physical authority requires testing the proposed action against instantaneous kinematics, payload inertia, motor thermal headroom, and state estimation uncertainty. Admission control must also remain coupled to an executable fallback. If the permission gate rejects a proposed command because the machine approaches an operational boundary, the controller must immediately dispatch a dynamically feasible contingency maneuver, such as controlled regenerative braking or a compliant hold that yields to contact. The maneuver halts or redirects the plant without exceeding mechanical stress limits.

Accountability demands an audit trail that reconstructs the causal chain connecting sensory evidence to physical outcomes. When an embodied machine causes a collision or near-miss, post-incident analysis requires timestamp and clock-conversion uncertainty small enough to distinguish the relevant sensing, admission, dispatch, and physical-response events across multiple data streams. These streams include the raw observations ingested by the planner and the action proposed by the learned policy. They also include the state estimates and dynamic constraints evaluated by the admission gate, the filtered command dispatched to the motor drives, and the measured response from joint encoders and force-torque sensors. Command logs record the intended trajectory, while sensor telemetry measures the plant’s actual mechanical response. Recording dispatched setpoints alone does not confirm that an actuator executed them; an open-circuit fault, a tripped thermal breaker, or mechanical gearbox jamming will cause the physical plant to diverge immediately from commanded behavior. Both streams must be logged against synchronized hardware clocks (such as the IEEE 1588 Precision Time Protocol) so that investigators can distinguish between a planning failure, an admission gate error, and an electromechanical actuator breakdown. Reconstruction is necessary but does not by itself say who answers for an action. The decisions that set the machine’s envelope, its staffing, and its permission to operate are made before any tick, and they have to be recorded on purpose (**[[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/04 Governing the Machine/17 Responsible Physical AI/V4-17.03 Decisions Behind an Admitted Action#^sec-responsibility-many-hands|Decisions Behind an Admitted Action]]**).

↪ **Protocol:** The IEEE 1588 Precision Time Protocol exchange that aligns these clocks is developed in **[[05 Reference/Volume IV - Physical AI/V4 Systems and Hardware#^sec-appendix-systems-ptp|Clock Synchronization and Network Timestamps]]**.

Safety, security, and accountability ultimately converge on three unified demands at the permission path guarding the causal boundary:

1.  **Fresh, authenticated physical state**: The permission gate must operate on telemetry fresh enough to bound physical uncertainty and robust enough against tampering or degradation to justify admitting an action.
2.  **Deterministic authority to intervene**: The admission mechanism needs uncompromised physical authority to veto or clamp infeasible proposals and inject safe contingency maneuvers, regardless of the learned model’s internal confidence or identity.
3.  **Synchronized causal telemetry**: The system must keep an immutable record of what was proposed, what state was evaluated, what commands were dispatched, and how the machine responded.

The incidents above map onto these demands. Tempe’s predictor discarded evidence it already had, and the factory emergency braking that could have intervened independently had been disabled; Cruise moved without fresh evidence about the space beneath it. Both investigations then depended on the third demand, the recorded telemetry from which the events were reconstructed. The permission path is therefore where a physical machine’s safety, security, and accountability obligations are discharged, and anchoring probabilistic proposals to checks against physical limits is what lets learned capability operate within justified bounds. What those checks must respect, and why, is the subject of four laws.
