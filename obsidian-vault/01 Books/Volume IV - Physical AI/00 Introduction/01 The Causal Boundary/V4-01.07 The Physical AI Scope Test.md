# V4-01.07 The Physical AI Scope Test

[[Home|Home]] · [[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/Volume 4 Overview|Volume Overview]] · [[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/00 Introduction/01 The Causal Boundary/V4-01 The Causal Boundary|V4-01 The Causal Boundary]]

^sec-boundary-scope-test

A robotic arm in a fulfillment center can follow a fixed motion profile under a proportional-integral-derivative (PID) controller (a classical hand-tuned feedback law that corrects in proportion to the present error, its accumulated sum, and its rate of change). It can also execute an offline trajectory pre-screened and loaded by a human technician, or stream joint torques directly from a learned policy running on real-time sensor frames. While all three regimes actuate identical electric motors and gear trains, they impose different architectural obligations on the computing system. The scope test establishes the operational boundaries that define physical AI throughout this book.

The scope of physical AI is defined by the simultaneous intersection of three structural criteria:

1.  **Learned Statistical Model:** The system relies on a policy, perception encoder, or world model whose runtime behavior emerges from training data rather than an inspectable closed-form specification, as seen in deep neural networks or diffusion policies ([[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/00 Introduction/01 The Causal Boundary/V4-01.16 Source References#^ref-chi2024diffusionpolicy|Chi et al. 2025]]). Correctness cannot be formally proven across all state configurations from source code alone; engineers must establish operational validity empirically across a defined operating domain. By contrast, a linear plant model fitted through classical system identification,[[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/00 Introduction/01 The Causal Boundary/V4-01.17 Footnotes#^fn3|³]] where parameter uncertainty yields provable stability margins, falls outside this category. A high-capacity neural policy falls within it.
2.  **Consequential Physical Feedback:** The system’s actions alter the material state of the surrounding physical world, and those state changes directly govern what its sensors measure in subsequent time steps under classical mechanics, thermodynamics, or electromagnetism. The environment cannot be paused or rolled back, and energy dissipated into it cannot be recalled.
3.  **Delegated Authority:** The system’s outputs reach execution without synchronous human approval for each control cycle. In a machine that also meets the second criterion, that execution is actuation, so the authority is physical. A learned component holds this authority whenever its output (a raw percept, a semantic state estimate, or a proposed motion trajectory) can propagate to live actuators without such approval. The learned network need not write directly to motor driver registers; the system possesses delegated authority whenever learned signals actively shape downstream actuation commands. Adding downstream safety checks or deterministic rate limiters does not vacate this authority, provided accepted proposals still drive hardware motion.

Together, these three criteria delineate the domain of physical AI. The three-set Venn diagram in [[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/00 Introduction/01 The Causal Boundary/V4-01.07 The Physical AI Scope Test#^fig-01-scope-venn|figure 6]] formalizes this boundary, locating physical AI at the central intersection where a learned model, consequential physical feedback, and delegated authority converge. Each pairwise overlap holds systems that meet only two of the three criteria. Advisory systems place a learned model inside a physical process, as when a technician inspects learned toolpaths before loading them onto a mill, but a person still approves each action. Digital autonomy executes learned outputs without human approval, as in an automated trading engine, but its actions land in software rather than in physics. Classical control closes a physical feedback loop automatically, as in a PID speed governor, but relies on an analytical control law rather than a learned model. Excluding these pairwise overlaps restricts the design problem to systems where statistical uncertainty collides directly with physical irreversibility.

A learned model alone does not make a machine a physical AI system. Consider an offline trajectory generator that optimizes smooth paths using a learned diffusion model. If a manufacturing engineer must inspect, validate, and load those toolpaths onto a CNC mill prior to cutting metal, the statistical model operates only as an advisory tool. The human retains ultimate physical authority. Similarly, a vision model that highlights defects on an assembly line display assists human inspection without closing the physical loop.

Conversely, delegated authority does not require end-to-end neural control. Most production autonomous systems adopt modular architectures where deep neural networks handle perception while deterministic trajectory planners compute actual actuator setpoints. These systems satisfy the delegated-authority test through perception: if an unvetted object detection or bounding-box estimate triggers an automated swerve or emergency braking maneuver, the learned representation directly dictates plant dynamics. The autonomous test vehicle in the Tempe collision examined in [[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/00 Introduction/01 The Causal Boundary/V4-01.09 When the Responsibilities Fail Together#^sec-boundary-when-fails|section 1.8]] is such a machine: changing classifications in its modular perception fed the path predictions that decided whether it would brake.

Shared autonomy falls within physical AI whenever a learned sub-system modifies live motion before a human operator can intervene. In a robotic surgical console equipped with learned haptic virtual fixtures, for example, the assistive policy exerts real forces on the master tool manipulators to guide the surgeon around delicate anatomical structures. Even if the surgeon holds administrative responsibility for the procedure, high-bandwidth force cues alter physical tissue contact well within normal human neurological reaction latency (typically 150 ms to 250 ms). Because the machine enforces learned spatial guidance dynamically without requiring the surgeon to review and acknowledge each micro-correction, it exercises delegated authority over physical motion.

When classifying an ambiguous machine, engineers must evaluate each of the three criteria independently. Implementing an independent deterministic veto, such as an analog brake relay triggered by a certified safety lidar, does not strip a machine of delegated authority, because learned proposals still govern all nominal motion up to the intervention threshold. In contrast, requiring a human operator to screen and authorize every trajectory command before transmission reliably removes that authority. Once a design satisfies all three criteria, it qualifies as physical AI, and its computing architecture must respect the hard timing and causal boundaries established in [[01 Books/Volume IV - Physical AI/00 Introduction/01 The Causal Boundary/V4-01.05 Defining the Causal Boundary#^sec-boundary-causal-boundary|section 1.4]].

**Checkpoint 1.1: Binding budgets and the scope test**

**Delay vs. braking**: Explain why response delay and active braking consume separate portions of stopping clearance, and identify which one dominates above the crossover speed.

**Stopping budget estimate**: Using the machine card, estimate how far the elementary stop at the 1.5 m/s drive limit grows if the 60 ms chunk lease doubles, and determine whether it still fits inside the 1.10 m clear distance.

**Binding margins**: Identify the relevant physical margin when the warehouse machine travels, presses a latch, and holds a load.

**Scope criteria**: Apply all three scope criteria to a new machine, including one whose learned output passes through an independent veto.

Satisfying the scope test binds statistical uncertainty directly to hardware consequences. To design systems that operate safely under these demands, computer systems engineers must map how timing, state estimation, and control authority distribute along the execution path from raw sensor frames to physical actuator motion.
